Han Qin · Dream of the Red Chamber: Thirty New Chapters

Chapter 87Peppery Xi-feng falls from power and feels the world turn cold; the foolish young master stands at Amaryllis Eyot with a question

Original continuation by Han Qin, not a recovered manuscript by Cao Xueqin.

For several days Lady Xing had been looking into Xi-feng’s old accounts. Little by little she had begun to make something of the money from Jing-xu, the old affair of Zhang Jin-ge, You Er-jie’s prescription and the whereabouts of Zhang Hua. Early one morning she ordered Wang Shan-bao’s wife to gather up several ledgers and statements and put them in a black lacquer box, then carried it herself to Jia She’s rooms.

Jia She had just taken his medicine and was reclining on the kang. When Lady Xing came in, he asked, ‘Have you got to the bottom of it?’

‘You had better judge for yourself, sir,’ Lady Xing said.

She set the box on the kang table and took out the books one by one. The first recorded the allowances received and paid out over the years; the next item was the receipt for the silver sent by Jing-xu. There were also statements from Wang-er, Bao Er’s wife and an assistant at the old apothecary’s. A dated paper seal lay slantwise across the mouth of the box. At the very bottom were several incomplete letters, yellow at the edges, with patches where water had blurred the writing.

Jia Lian had been sent for too. He stood below the kang, his hands at his sides, while Lady Xing read through the entry on the lending out of the allowances at interest. ‘There can be no denying that she put the household’s silver out at interest,’ she said. ‘Some of the interest did go to make up shortfalls in the common accounts later. But there was no express approval from the master or mistress.’

‘How much is missing?’ asked Jia She.

‘Counting capital and interest, there are upwards of four thousand taels still out on loan,’ Wang Shan-bao’s wife said quickly. ‘In a year of bad harvests people can’t pay, and I’m afraid we shan’t get it back in a hurry.’

‘The bitch has some nerve!’ Jia She swore. ‘Taking the household silver to do business on her own account!’

‘Then there are the three thousand taels from Jing-xu at the Mantou Nunnery,’ Lady Xing said. ‘That silver went into the common accounts afterwards, but it came from the breaking-off of Miss Zhang’s engagement. Xi-feng took the three thousand and had Wang-er write a letter in Lian’s name to Yun Guang, the military commissioner at Chang’an. She used his influence to break off the original match. Afterwards the girl died, and so did the young man she’d been promised to.’

At the name Yun Guang, Jia Lian went suddenly pale. ‘How dared she drag my name into the military commissioner’s office!’ he cried. ‘Loans outside, money held back inside, that’s all household business. At the very worst we lose face. This means bringing in the military commissioner. It’s colluding for private gain! It puts the whole house at stake.’

‘As for You Er-jie,’ Lady Xing went on, ‘Doctor Hu disappeared long ago, and only a scrap of the prescription is left. We still can’t be certain who recommended him.’ She had Bao Er’s wife brought in. The woman had waited on You Er-jie in Little Flower Lane; she dropped on both knees as soon as she entered. ‘When Er-jie was ill you went to her room often enough,’ said Lady Xing. ‘What do you remember?’

Bao Er’s wife was white with fear. She kowtowed again and again. ‘Your Ladyship knows best. I never dared make a decision in that room. I took in tea and water, that was all; I did as I was told. All I remember is that Er-jie was with child. Then she took a dose of the wrong medicine and miscarried in the night. She grew weaker by the day after that. Miss Autumn went in every day to say spiteful things, and Shan-jie wouldn’t look after her properly. Those were orders from above; it truly had nothing to do with me.’

‘And what do you know of You San-jie?’ asked Wang Shan-bao’s wife.

Bao Er’s wife hastily kowtowed again. ‘I daren’t say too much. I only heard people say that one sister swallowed gold and the other cut her throat. The poor things, neither came to a natural end. Beyond that I know nothing at all.’

No one in the room asked another question. Jia She merely took up the prescription and turned it over. The back was blank. He tossed it into the box.

‘Wang-er has confessed that Zhang Hua didn’t die,’ said Lady Xing. ‘Xi-feng told him in those days to make a clean job of it. He couldn’t bring himself to do it. Instead, he spent twenty taels lying low outside for a few days and came back saying Zhang Hua had died on the road. Whatever else she did or didn’t do, she meant to have him silenced.’

‘What are we keeping a daughter-in-law like that for?’ Jia She said with a cold laugh.

‘If you have something to ask her, sir,’ Jia Lian said at last, ‘ask her to her face.’

‘Of course I shall,’ said Lady Xing. ‘Then no one can say afterwards that we quarrelled as mother- and daughter-in-law and I laid false charges while she was ill.’

The box was soon sealed up again, and Autumn and Shan-jie were ordered to go along. Jia She had himself helped there too and sat in the outer room. Xi-feng was still bleeding and lay in her old room in the main quarters. When she heard the sound of many footsteps in the courtyard, she had Patience help her sit up and put a bolster behind her.

Lady Xing came in and sat down without asking how she was. She merely told Wang Shan-bao’s wife to set Jing-xu’s receipt, Wang-er’s statement and the remains of You Er-jie’s letter on the kang table.

Xi-feng glanced at them and smiled. ‘Since Your Ladyship has looked into it, let the books speak.’

‘Did you take these three thousand taels from Jing-xu?’

‘I did. It’s in the accounts for the third month of that year, Your Ladyship. Turn the page.’

Lady Xing turned a few pages and closed the book. ‘The next page says it made up a shortfall in the common funds. I’m not asking where the money went; I’m asking where it came from.’ She pointed to the torn letter. ‘And what went on in You Er-jie’s room?’

‘Once she was in the house, did I stint her of anything I ate or wore? I sent for a doctor when she fell ill. If the servants fell short, that’s for the servants to answer for.’

Autumn had gone white with fright. She dropped to her knees with a thud. ‘It really, truly had nothing to do with me! It was my mistress who sent me to Er-jie’s room. She told me outright to abuse her, and promised me privately I should be the better for it later. I only did what I was told. How has it all come to be my doing now? Your Ladyship must see me right.’

‘Like hell I did!’ Xi-feng gave a cold laugh. ‘When did I ever tell you to do her harm? You couldn’t bear the sight of her and were forever fighting for favour. Who in this house doesn’t know it? And now you dare turn round and take a bite at me.’

Seeing Autumn kneel, Shan-jie hurried forward and fell on her knees too, as though afraid of being left behind. Looking eagerly up at Lady Xing, she poured out her story. ‘Your Ladyship, when I was waiting on Second Mistress, my own mistress taught me what to say, one thing at a time. If she asked for hair oil, I was to say my mistress was too busy. If she asked for hot water, the stove had been banked. If she wanted a doctor, we’d send for one tomorrow. If she wanted to see Second Master, he was busy outside. If she cried, she was breaking a taboo; if she didn’t cry, she was putting on an act. If anyone above us asked whose orders those were, we were to say we servants had used our own judgement. I haven’t added a word or left one out, Your Ladyship. They’re my mistress’s very words.’

‘You foul-mouthed wretch!’ Xi-feng said. ‘If any such thing was done, it was done behind my back. What’s that to do with me?’

When Shan-jie heard Xi-feng shifting the blame onto the servants again, she grew desperate. ‘Did Your Ladyship hear that? “It has nothing to do with me.” I’ve heard my mistress say it so often I’ve grown calluses on my ears. It’s the very thing she taught me to say to Er-jie. Now she uses it to pass the blame to me, I can only give her the same answer back. If there was any such thing, Autumn did it in secret. It had nothing to do with me. So this is what one does with the words my mistress taught me!’

‘Hold your tongue!’ Patience said sharply.

‘Don’t be angry, Miss Patience.’ Shan-jie smiled. ‘My mistress used to say no one was to interrupt when a mistress was being questioned. I’m only following the old rule.’ She stepped forward and moved the hand-warmer from the edge of Xi-feng’s kang. ‘Her Ladyship said there was too much heat in the room. An invalid oughtn’t to sit roasting.’

‘Who told you to touch my things?’ said Xi-feng.

‘When Second Mistress said she was cold, that was just what my mistress told me to say. An invalid already has too much false heat in the body. We weren’t to put on any more charcoal.’

Far from losing her temper, Xi-feng laughed. ‘How good of you to remember so well.’

‘I was taught by you yourself, ma’am.’ Shan-jie lowered her head. ‘How could I dare forget?’

From the moment he entered, Jia Lian had stood at the window. Xi-feng looked at him, but he looked at the window. Outside, the wind blew a dead pomegranate branch against the lattice. It tapped twice, softly.

‘There’s one thing more,’ said Lady Xing. ‘Trinket confessed the other day. She says that you made her take Patience’s bracelet and told her to put it on Bao-yu’s bed so that Patience would take the blame.’

Xi-feng gave a cold laugh. ‘Whose nonsense has Your Ladyship been hearing now? Patience lost that bracelet herself on her way to see my elder sister-in-law. It had slipped open and fallen among the grass roots. She picked it up herself when the snow melted. She told me so with her own lips. Am I now supposed to have made someone steal it?’

Everyone looked at Patience. She lowered her head and said not a word.

‘Since Patience has nothing to say, Trinket’s statement can’t be wrong.’ Lady Xing turned to Wang Shan-bao’s wife. ‘Repeat what she actually said that day.’

‘The day they got Trinket to leave by the corner gate, I followed her out,’ said Wang Shan-bao’s wife. ‘I asked her privately why Mrs Lian wanted her to do such a thing. She said it was all over the house at the time that Miss Patience was with child. That was what had upset Mrs Lian. “I’ll see she can’t keep her place in this house,” was what she said. I daren’t add a word.’

The room went quiet for a moment. Xi-feng leaned on her bolster without a word.

Patience suddenly raised her head and looked at Xi-feng. ‘You asked me about it yourself once, ma’am. I told you I wasn’t.’ Xi-feng turned her eyes to the bed curtains and said nothing. Patience stood a little while, then lowered her head again, her hands still folded in front of her.

‘What else have you to say?’ asked Lady Xing.

‘Where there are accounts, go by the accounts,’ said Xi-feng. ‘But I won’t confess to things there’s not a scrap of proof for just because a few servants say so.’

Jia She heard this from the outer room. ‘Still talking back at this hour, are you!’ he said angrily. ‘Take her keys and seals first. Seal up her main rooms and move her to the west side courtyard.’

‘She’s still bleeding, sir,’ Patience put in quickly. ‘No one has lived in the west side courtyard for a long time. It’s dark and cold. Let me send someone to put it in order first.’

‘A suite of three rooms isn’t big enough for one person?’ said Lady Xing. ‘She moves today. There’s no need to choose a day for it.’

Xi-feng threw off the covers and got to her feet with one hand on the edge of the kang. Patience hurried over to help her. ‘Don’t hold me,’ said Xi-feng. ‘I can still walk.’

She had scarcely stepped down from the footstool when she swayed. Her hand pressed hard on the kang table. She waited until she had her breath back before going towards the door. Zhou Rui’s wife had come running when she heard the news. She knelt before Lady Xing. ‘Mrs Lian is so ill, Your Ladyship. Do please allow her a few more days.’

‘You’ve come at just the right moment,’ Lady Xing said with a smile. ‘Hand over that bunch of storeroom keys as well. From today you’re not to handle the money or provisions for the inner household. Your son-in-law Leng Zi-xing trades outside, and your family has had a good deal of help from Xi-feng these last few years. Where your names are in the books, there’ll be questions to answer.’

Zhou Rui’s wife went pale. ‘I’ve served the Second Lady for years. When did I ever dare fiddle with the accounts?’

‘I didn’t say you had,’ said Lady Xing. ‘If you haven’t, what’s there to fear from an inquiry?’

Wang Shan-bao’s wife untied the keys from her waist. Zhou Rui’s wife knelt a while longer, but no one took notice of her and at last she rose and withdrew to the verandah. Joker had been standing at Xi-feng’s side. Seeing Jia Lian leave, he hurried ahead to raise the door curtain. ‘Shall I have your horse made ready, sir?’

‘Joker,’ Xi-feng called from behind, ‘fetch me my medicine chest.’

Joker checked his step and looked only at Jia Lian. ‘Go and wait in the outer study first,’ Jia Lian said.

Joker said he would and followed Jia Lian out. Xi-feng heard him go and did not call again.

The west side courtyard had indeed stood empty for a long time. There were two holes in the window gauze, and the mat on the kang was clammy. Patience hurried to have the floor swept and the bedding laid out. Xi-feng sat in an old chair and asked for a brazier, then for a doctor, then for Second Master. Shan-jie stood at the door and said she could not oblige her in any of the three, then stood very properly with her arms at her sides. Xi-feng looked at her. ‘Very good,’ she said at last. ‘You may go.’ Shan-jie acknowledged the order, turned and let the curtain fall.

Patience tucked her own hand-warmer into Xi-feng’s lap. ‘I’ll fetch charcoal from the kitchen.’

‘No need.’ Xi-feng gave a cold laugh. ‘If he’s willing to do this, let him see it through.’

Patience could only paste some old paper over the holes in the window. The wind still crept in at the edges, blowing the steam from the medicine bowl to one side.

Now when Jia Lian left the west side courtyard he did not go back to the outer study. He went alone to his own courtyard and made for the east wing. You Er-jie had once occupied the three rooms there. The door had been locked these two years, and even the keyhole had rusted. Joker fetched a flask of oil; it took him a long while to get the door open.

The tables and chairs were covered with dust. The bed curtains had long since been taken down, leaving only a few hooks on the wall. Jia Lian told Joker to wait outside and went in alone. Beneath the window stood an old sewing basket. It held a few skeins of silk thread, a thimble and half a piece of tailor’s chalk. At the bottom lay a tiny garment of patterned silk. The body was sewn; there was still a needle pinned at one cuff.

Jia Lian lifted the little garment. The silk had yellowed with age, and the thread in the needle had faded. Three or four stitches would have finished the cuff. The stitching stopped there and had never gone any further.

Jia Lian held the little garment without moving for a long while. At last he pulled the needle out of the cuff, held it up against his finger for a moment and put it back just where it had been. Then he folded the garment, put it in his sleeve and went out.

Joker hurried to lock the door when he saw him come out. ‘No one is to touch anything in these rooms,’ Jia Lian said. Joker said he understood.

That evening Jia She sent for Jia Lian again. A sheet of blank paper lay ready on the table, and the ink had already been ground in the inkstone. ‘Jealousy, theft, no son,’ said Lady Xing. ‘Wouldn’t any one of them be enough to repudiate her? Write it out in the usual form. Tomorrow her family can come and take her home.’ Jia Lian kept one hand pressed to the cuff of his sleeve.

‘A daughter-in-law who ruins the house,’ said Jia She. ‘What are we keeping her for? To turn it upside down again?’

Jia Lian sat down at the desk, took up the brush and dipped it in the ink. He held the tip suspended over the paper. A drop of ink slowly gathered, but it did not fall.

‘Why don’t you write?’ asked Lady Xing.

Jia Lian put down his brush. ‘She’s ill, and the mourning for Grandmother isn’t over yet. We can leave the letter of repudiation unwritten for now. We’ll speak of it again in a few days.’

‘Useless thing!’ Jia She swore.

Jia Lian merely folded the blank sheet once, opened it out again and held it down with a paperweight. Lady Xing saw that he would not write and did not press him. ‘The main rooms are sealed already,’ she said. ‘She’s to live in the west side courtyard and get half her allowance in everything. Someone else will run the household from now on.’

The next day Jia Lian called Patience to the outer study. He pushed the keys of the inner household, the paired tallies and the allowance book towards her. ‘You’re to take charge for the time being, starting today.’

Patience did not reach for them yet. She looked up. ‘What does Her Ladyship say?’

‘Her Ladyship knows,’ said Jia Lian.

Only then did Patience take up the keys and count them, one by one. She looked through the allowance book as well. ‘The kitchen, the sewing room and all the gates can stay under the people who have always managed them. I only want a daily count of what goes out and what comes in. No one is to use my mistress’s illness as an excuse to replace anybody, or stint her of food or medicine.’

‘Do as you see fit,’ said Jia Lian.

‘I’ll stay in the same outer room as before,’ said Patience. ‘The main rooms are sealed, so no one is to go in.’

Jia Lian looked at her. ‘As you please.’

Patience went out with the keys in her hands. First she went to the kitchen to make up the west side courtyard’s supply of charcoal, and then she had a separate account kept of Xi-feng’s medicine. Now that Patience had charge of the household, several of the married servants came up with smiles. ‘We once lost a whole month’s wages over a mistake of half a string of cash,’ said one. ‘At last there’s someone we can reason with.’ Another quickly put in, ‘If we can’t make up the full amount one day, miss, I hope you’ll give us a little time.’

‘I know nothing of what happened before,’ said Patience. ‘What you draw today, you account for to the last cash tomorrow. If there’s a shortage you make it good, and if there’s anything over you don’t keep it for yourself.’

She had not raised her voice, but the women dared say no more and dispersed. She had the paper seal set straight on the doors of Xi-feng’s main rooms. Seeing a little maid peer in through a crack, she said, ‘There may be no one living in there, but it’s no place for you to go rummaging.’ The girl hurried off. Patience locked the doors herself and put the keys away in her old purse. She did not get a new one.

Lady Wang, for her part, had heard what had happened to Xi-feng. Xi-feng was her brother’s daughter and had run the household for her all these years. Now the ledgers and statements were all in the hands of the elder branch. When the person who had brought the news had finished, Lady Wang merely fingered her prayer beads and waved the messenger away.

One day Cai-xia’s mother came in. She had scarcely kowtowed before her eyes reddened. ‘Have pity, Your Ladyship,’ she said, choking on the words. ‘Please take my Cai-xia’s part. Two years ago Mrs Lian insisted on promising her to Wang-er’s boy. He’s no good, and Cai-xia never wanted him, not from the start. Now that Wang-er’s people have heard what’s happened to Mrs Lian, they’re pressing us harder than ever. They say Mrs Lian gave her word herself, so we can’t go back on it. Cai-xia has been desperate these last two days, threatening to take her life and refusing food or drink. I truly don’t know where else to turn, so I’ve come to beg Your Ladyship.’

Lady Wang was turning a string of prayer beads that Grandmother Jia had left. She thought a moment. ‘If this ends badly, it’ll be another life lost,’ she said at last. ‘We haven’t heard the last of Xi-feng’s troubles yet. This would make things worse still.’ Then she said, ‘Enough. I was the one who sent Cai-xia out. As to her marriage, I’ll decide the matter for her. Tell Wang-er’s people to come to me. Whoever gave them their word, it gives them no claim. Go home and tell Cai-xia to set her mind at ease and eat a proper meal. She mustn’t try to do away with herself.’

Cai-xia’s mother hastily kowtowed and withdrew. Cai-xia had come in with her and was sweeping the outer room. When she saw her mother come out, the broom in her hands paused only a moment. Her mother gave her a look, said nothing and went straight out. Cai-xia lowered her head and went on sweeping. The grey bricks were already clean, but she swept them again, first across and then lengthwise. At last she set the broom against the wall, fetched water and rinsed out the joints between the bricks. Of this we need say no more.

Scarcely was Cai-xia’s affair settled when someone carried word to Lady Wang of what Trinket had confessed a few days earlier at the First Lady’s: how Xi-feng had made her take Patience’s bracelet and told her to put it on Bao-yu’s bed. When Lady Wang heard this, she was silent a long while. She slipped a few beads through her fingers. ‘You needn’t bring me any more news from that quarter,’ she said. After that she would have nothing to do with Xi-feng’s affairs.

Once the story spread, people high and low in the house no longer held back from mentioning Xi-feng. Patience brought her food and medicine at the proper hours every day and would not let her go short by a mouthful. ‘Are the accounts settled?’ Xi-feng asked one day. ‘Her Ladyship says the case is closed,’ Patience replied. ‘Just as well,’ was all Xi-feng said. Patience put down the medicine, set the stove to rights and pulled the door to behind her. She no longer stood by the kang a moment longer as she used to. Once she would have shielded her at every turn, defended her at every word. Now she said none of those things.

Now Feng Zi-ying had lately acquired some fine horses and put an archery ground to the west of the city back in order. He sent to invite Wei Ruo-lan, Chen Ye-jun, Han Qi and Bao-yu to try their skill with the bow. Bao-yu was still in mourning and had no wish to go. But when Jia Zheng heard it was only archery practice, with no banquet or theatre, he smiled. ‘A man ought to know something of bows and horses. Our house owes its standing to military service. You can’t idle away whole days in the Garden.’ Bao-yu had no choice but to put on a plain pale-blue robe and go, taking Tealeaf with him.

The archery ground had earth walls on three sides and a low wood on the fourth. Several rows of straw targets stood in the field. At the foot of the wall a cluster of goose feathers showed above the mouth of a quiver. The red cloth at the centre of several targets had faded in the wind and sun. There was no bustle; the only sound was the occasional clink of an iron ring on a horse’s bit. With no music laid on, there were just a few plates of fruit and two flagons of light wine. Feng Zi-ying wore a deep-blue riding jacket with archers’ sleeves and met his guests himself at the gate. Wei Ruo-lan arrived on a grey horse with a bow case at his waist. Newly married as he was, he wore nothing bright but a red tassel tied to his leather belt. Chen Ye-jun and Han Qi arrived soon after.

Once seated, they talked first about bows and horses. Before long the talk came round to how each family was faring. Han Qi tapped the end of an arrow twice against the edge of the table and sighed. ‘My uncle’s been waiting three years for a posting. At last a place fell vacant, and now, just when it seemed settled, they’ve held the appointment back again.’

Chen Ye-jun turned his cup with a cold laugh. ‘You’ve only had a posting held up. It’s my family that’s in a fix. A censor impeached my father and even dug up the salt duties from ten years ago. The money shop caught wind of it and stopped cashing our drafts yesterday.’

Feng Zi-ying picked up the wine and filled a cup halfway for the man beside him. ‘A visiting card in the capital these days is worth more than a handful of drafts,’ he said with a smile. ‘Send it to the wrong door and all the silver in the world will do you no good. If this goes on, we’d do better taking ship once or twice for the Southern Seas. When the goods come ashore, what counts is what they weigh.’

‘That’s easily said, Brother Feng.’ Han Qi frowned. ‘Do you really mean families like ours ought to go and scramble with merchants for a profit?’

Feng Zi-ying waved a hand and laughed. ‘You may turn up your nose at merchants. I don’t mind the sea breeze.’

Chen Ye-jun looked up at last. ‘The proper road is through study. If they hold the examinations next year, I’ll go and sit them myself. The more we rely on our family’s old protégés and relations, the less there is to rely on.’

They spoke now of promotion, now of impeachment, now of silver they could no longer move. Wei Ruo-lan ran his fingers along his bowstring and put in a word now and then. Bao-yu, seated at the foot of the table, spent a long while looking at a goose-feathered arrow, then began to finger a clump of foxtail grass gone to seed in a corner by the wall.

Seeing that he said nothing, Feng Zi-ying smiled. ‘Brother Bao, did you follow what Brother Han said about that vacancy at the Board of Civil Office?’

‘What vacancy?’ Bao-yu asked.

They all laughed. ‘A fortunate man indeed,’ Han Qi said, smiling. ‘You don’t even have to understand these things.’

Bao-yu did not ask again. He simply put the arrow back in the quiver.

After a round of wine they went down to the field to try their skill, calling on Han Qi to draw first. He tried twice, but the string came no farther than his chest. His first arrow fell short of the target, and the second stuck slantwise in the grass. The others laughed at him for knowing how to judge essays but having no strength in his arms. Then they pushed Bao-yu forward. He could hold the bow well enough, but his hand had no strength when he drew it. The arrow skimmed past the edge of the target and buried itself in the earth wall behind. ‘At least you didn’t shoot yourself in the foot,’ Feng Zi-ying said, laughing. Bao-yu laughed too and handed back the bow.

Chen Ye-jun shot three arrows in succession. Two landed in the red, and one near the centre. Feng Zi-ying took his turn next. He shot once to get his eye in and put his next three arrows inside the red ring. He put away his bow and turned back to talk and joke with the others, making no show of it.

After watching them all, Wei Ruo-lan suddenly called to the pages to bring over two square tables from the side of the ground. ‘What do you want tables for when we’re having a perfectly good shoot?’ Feng Zi-ying asked with a smile.

‘There’s no sport in shooting from level ground,’ Wei Ruo-lan said, smiling. He had the tables stacked one on the other. Their legs were uneven and the stack rocked as soon as they put it together. The others called out that it was dangerous, but Wei Ruo-lan had already stepped onto the lower table with his bow in his hand. Using a corner for purchase, he swung himself onto the top one.

Feng Zi-ying looked up and laughed. ‘Ruo-lan, if you fall, don’t expect me to pay for a new bridegroom.’

‘Just keep your eyes on the target,’ Wei Ruo-lan said.

One of the bottom table’s legs sank into the earth, and the top table rocked harder still. Wei Ruo-lan stood in the middle, his left foot a little forward and his right foot a little back. He let the table sway once before he nocked his arrow and drew. The bow curved like the full moon, the arrow went like a star and struck the very heart of the red.

The whole field broke into cheers. Han Qi clapped his hands. ‘First with bow and horse, and a bridegroom into the bargain! Brother Wei carries off all the honours today!’

Wei Ruo-lan leapt down and tightened his leather belt as he landed. Bao-yu suddenly caught sight of a gold ornament fastened to it. It flashed in the sun, and its shape was so familiar that he looked twice.

‘What are you looking at, Brother Bao?’ Feng Zi-ying asked, smiling. ‘Brother Wei’s qilin,’ Bao-yu said. ‘It looks like Cousin Xiang-yun’s.’

‘Hers is a little smaller,’ Wei Ruo-lan said with a smile. ‘I got this one the other day from someone I know outside. He said it was an old piece that had once passed out of some family’s hands.’ He unfastened it and gave it to Bao-yu to look at.

Bao-yu took it in both hands. It lay heavy in his palms, pure gold with kingfisher-feather inlay. This was the very one he had taken from Daoist Zhang’s tray at the Temple of Pure Emptiness, a size larger than Xiang-yun’s. He looked a little longer and handed it back. ‘It is a little bigger than hers,’ was all he said. Wei Ruo-lan had his horse brought up, swung into the saddle and rode once around the ground, loosing three arrows one after another. The first hit the target to his left and the second the one to his right. For the third he turned in the saddle and shot back. That arrow too stuck in the centre.

The cheers went on and on. Feng Zi-ying carried over a cup of wine himself. ‘Here’s to your bow and to your qilin,’ he said with a smile. Wei Ruo-lan drained it at once and clasped his hands to Bao-yu without dismounting. Bao-yu stood by the straw targets watching the gold qilin rise and fall with the horse. He looked at Wei Ruo-lan, so spirited and beaming with happiness, and heard them all wish him joy of his marriage. He stared, quite lost in the sight. After a long while he gave the front of his robe a little tug.

After they parted, Tealeaf went back to the city with Bao-yu. All the way he kept asking questions. ‘What was Master Han saying about a vacancy at the Board of Civil Office, and Master Chen about money from the salt duties? I didn’t understand a word. And how did they come to say you were fortunate, Master Bao? Did you make anything of it?’

‘I didn’t understand it either, and I can’t be bothered to,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. After a while he added, ‘Those two tables rocked terribly. And he wasn’t a bit afraid to stand up there.’ A little later he asked, ‘That gold qilin I had you take to Nuageuse, did you ever hear what became of it?’ ‘I took it to the Budding Grove that day and came straight back,’ said Tealeaf. ‘I never heard anything after that.’ Bao-yu asked no more.

By then they had entered the west corner gate of the Rong mansion. It was still early, so Bao-yu did not go straight to the House of Green Delights. He walked slowly along the paved path through the Garden and found himself turning into Amaryllis Eyot.

The old gate at Amaryllis Eyot stood ajar, its bronze ring gone green. The reeds before the steps had grown as high as the windows. Bao-yu went in. The curtains of the main rooms had been taken down, and the paper in one corner of the window was torn. Beneath it lay the dark zitan go board with its mother-of-pearl flowers and birds and its crossing lines of ivory inlay. Not a single stone remained on it. a few flecks of mud from a swallow’s nest on the board had long since dried. Beside it was a sewing basket with half a skein of blue-green thread, an unfinished tracing for a pattern and an old thimble, all under a thin layer of dust.

Half a sheet of old paper showed beneath the board. On it was a drawing of an uncoloured water-caltrop flower. Insects had eaten into the edge in two places.

‘Master Bao,’ Tealeaf called from outside, ‘it’s been empty a long time. There’s a chill about the place. Let’s go back.’

Bao-yu stood quite still under the window. ‘Were you there the day Second Sister was married?’

‘I went along with the carriage outside,’ Tealeaf said. ‘I didn’t go in.’

‘It was plain that day she didn’t want the marriage,’ Bao-yu said. ‘Why was the family so set on pretending she wanted it?’

Tealeaf lowered his head and looked at the threshold. He did not answer.

‘Everyone said it was her happy day,’ Bao-yu went on. ‘Did she ever once say she wanted it? How did everybody know she wanted it?’

‘In young ladies’ affairs it’s the master and mistress who decide, sir.’

‘I know they decide,’ said Bao-yu. ‘What I’m asking is, if they did the deciding, why did they have to pretend Second Sister wanted it herself?’

Tealeaf opened his mouth. All he said was, ‘The wind’s getting up, sir. Mind you don’t catch cold.’

Bao-yu picked up the thimble, put it back in the basket and turned to go. Just as he reached the door, a gust came through the torn window paper and blew a scrap from the side of the window onto the go board. Bao-yu looked back once more before he finally went out. At the edge of the pond the wind blew across the water and curled a withered water-caltrop leaf up onto the stone steps. The water was down by more than half, leaving a half-ring of black mud with a few broken reeds sticking out of it. The pond was the same pond as before, the reeds at the water’s edge as white in flower as in other years. Only there was no one in the room to call him to a game, or to ask through the window who had come.

Leaving Amaryllis Eyot, Bao-yu went straight to Naiad’s House. Nightingale saw his dusty sleeves and smiled. ‘Where have you been poking about now, Master Bao?’

‘I’ve just come from the archery ground,’ he said with a smile. ‘I went to have a look in Second Sister’s rooms. Is your mistress in?’

‘She’s just taken her medicine and is lying back,’ said Nightingale. ‘Stay only a little while, sir, and don’t get her talking too much.’

Bao-yu went in softly. Dai-yu was resting by the window in a plain lined jacket, a bowl of medicine at her side. When she saw him she smiled. ‘You found the energy to go out today, then.’

‘Brother Feng asked me to go shooting,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘I didn’t hit a thing. Wei Ruo-lan was the best.’ Then he said, ‘Something odd happened. That gold qilin I gave Nuageuse has passed through I don’t know how many hands and ended up at Wei Ruo-lan’s waist. I saw it with my own eyes today. I even held it. He says he got it from someone he knows outside; all he said was that it was an old piece some family had parted with.’

Dai-yu gave a cold laugh. ‘It was your own. And now you have to ask other people how it got into somebody else’s hands.’

Bao-yu could think of no answer. He sat a while before saying quietly, ‘Cousin Dai-yu, they say that old affair of Trinket’s has been brought up again, and it has something to do with Patience’s bracelet. I can’t get a straight account of it, and I daren’t ask. I just keep thinking how angry Skybright was when she sent Trinket away. She took her for a little thief, that was all. Now Skybright is gone, and there’s nowhere to say any of this.’

The wind stirred a few slender bamboos outside the window. One side of the leaves was green, the other white, and they turned this way and that. Nightingale came in with a fresh cup of warm tea and took away the medicine that had gone cold beside Dai-yu.

Bao-yu stared at the medicine bowl for a long time. ‘My dear cousin,’ he said. ‘I’m muddled about everything else. There’s only this one thing I’m sure of: whatever happens later, you and I are bound to be together.’

Dai-yu had been straightening an old handkerchief. She did not answer. Her head bent, she straightened it over and over before saying slowly, ‘It’s late, and the wind has come up. Put something warmer on when you get home and go to bed early.’

Bao-yu said only that he would. Bamboo shadows moved across the window gauze. There was just a little cold ash in the incense-burner on the table. After sitting a while longer, Bao-yu suddenly asked, ‘Is Eldest Sister really doing well in the palace?’

Dai-yu looked out of the window. No one in the room answered him.

Bao-yu waited a moment, then stood up. ‘You rest. I’ll come back tomorrow.’

Dai-yu turned to him. ‘Go carefully.’

It was already late when Bao-yu left Naiad’s House. When he came to the passage hall, two little maids were carrying out an old chest. The lid was open, showing some worn things made at the palace: a palace fan, a string of red musk-scented beads and several sealed incense boxes, all gifts Her Highness had bestowed in those years. ‘What are you doing with these?’ Bao-yu asked. ‘Her Ladyship says there’s been a great deal going on at the palace lately,’ one of the girls answered. ‘These old things draw the eye if we leave them about. She told us to put them in the storeroom.’ Bao-yu stood a while. He took the palace fan out of the chest and looked at it. One rib had already split. He put it back.

An old woman tended a lantern at the Garden gate. The wind rattled its shade softly. Crossing the bridge outside Amaryllis Eyot, Bao-yu looked that way again. The windows of the empty rooms were dark, with not a glimmer of light. To learn what followed, listen to the next chapter.
