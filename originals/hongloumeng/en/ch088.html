Han Qin · Dream of the Red Chamber: Thirty New Chapters

Chapter 88An old case comes to light and sets husband against wife; two young hearts bind their hair and pledge themselves in secret.

Original continuation by Han Qin, not a recovered manuscript by Cao Xueqin.

Ever since the old lady’s death, hardly a day’s peace had been known in the Rong mansion. One affair followed hard upon another, within the gates and without. Jia Zheng was in his outer study one day attending to some letters when a boy announced from beyond the curtain, ‘Mr Xia, chief eunuch of the Six Palaces, is here again.’ Jia Zheng put down his brush, straightened his cap and robes, and hurried out to receive him. He showed him into the hall and offered him tea. Xia Shou-zhong did not sit down. He began with a few ordinary civilities, asking how the family had been faring since its recent bereavement and saying that Her Highness was well. Then he sent the junior eunuchs who had come with him and the household servants out to the verandah, stepped a little closer to Jia Zheng and lowered his voice. ‘Her Highness has one more word of a private nature. She told your servant to pass it on to you quietly, sir.’

Jia Zheng bowed. ‘Pray tell me what it is.’

‘Her Highness said: “The family must on no account do anything more just now to bring itself to notice.”’ Beyond that Xia Shou-zhong would not say a word. Jia Zheng started inwardly and was about to ask what she meant by bringing themselves to notice when the eunuch waved a hand. There was a curious haste in his expression, as though the words had burnt his tongue. Jia Zheng took the hint, did not press him, and ordered fresh hot tea. Xia drank half a cup in a hurry and rose to go. At the main gate he turned and added casually, ‘Nor need you be counting quite so much on His Excellency Wang Zi-teng just now, sir. He’s still inspecting the frontier. His official business won’t let him get away, and he won’t be back just yet. The vine’s still there; I fear you can’t lean on it just now.’ With that he got into his sedan and was off. Jia Zheng saw him past the main gate and stood for a while at the foot of the steps, lost in thought. Then he turned and went slowly back in. He took the sheet on his desk that read ‘Be guarded in speech and cautious in conduct’ and slipped it under the inkstone case with his own hands.

Now a curious case had lately come before the authorities. In a house of modest means in the south of the city someone had been ill for days, and nothing the doctors prescribed did any good. The family suspected evil spirits and searched and dug at the foot of the walls, under the bed and behind the stove. They unearthed two things: male and female demons carved in ivory, and scarlet embroidery needles. Greatly alarmed, they reported the matter at once. The authorities followed the trail to a sorceress named Ma and sent officers to search her quarters. In one cupboard, crowded with strange objects, they found clay figures of evil spirits, Seven Stars lamps still alight, and several straw figures with bands clamped round their heads, nails through their chests and little locks tied round their necks. Another cupboard held male and female figures cut out of paper. Eventually they turned up several small account books, with a careful entry for each household that had called her in to cast a spell or had called her in to see about an illness over the years, the silver still to be collected from each, and the charges for incense and lamp oil besides. But Mother Ma had long made a trade of such things and heard what was coming in time to abandon the whole business and slip away. They did not catch her. The only things that came into official hands were those account books.

The books passed from one authority to the next until reports of them reached the Prince of Zhongshun’s mansion. What the Prince wanted, as it turned out, was not the sorceress who had got away, but those few books. Here was a genuine case of witchcraft, and he used it to investigate the households in the ledgers, one by one. In a few days a pretext had been found to raid several houses in the city, and quite a number of people were taken away. Of that there is no need to say more here. Every family of any standing in the city spent those days in dread, wondering when the net would come down over its own head. That evening Lin Zhi-xiao came to the study and told Jia Zheng all he had heard outside, from beginning to end. ‘There is one more thing your servant dare not leave unreported,’ he said at last. ‘There is an entry for our own household. Under it are the words: “Woman surnamed Zhao. Requests a spell to bring down young Master Bao. Received: one slip with particulars of birth; one lock of black hair; fifty taels in incense money. Bond for five hundred taels unpaid.”’ Jia Zheng held a bowl of tea in his hands. The lampwick gave a little pop. For a long time he did not move.

Jia Zheng remembered the year when Bao-yu and Xi-feng had both gone out of their minds at the same time, for no apparent cause. Everyone had thought then that they had chanced upon something unclean, and no one had gone into the matter very deeply. But if the book recorded both the particulars of birth and a lock of hair, someone in the household must have had a hand in it. His suspicions fell on Jia Huan. He at once summoned the boy to the study and, sitting very still, gave nothing away as he questioned him closely about what had happened that year. At first Jia Huan denied it for all he was worth and swore he knew nothing. But Jia Zheng put the words about the birth particulars and the hair to him, one by one, until he could deny it no longer. He stiffened his neck and gave a cold laugh. ‘Suppose I did pass them on. What of it? I wrote out the birth particulars myself, and I told a maid to take the hair from his comb.’

Jia Zheng’s face darkened. ‘You wicked, unfilial creature! Do you know this was a plot to take your brother’s life?’

Jia Huan tossed his head. ‘Of course I do. A life is a life. Where’s the difference? His counts, does it? And mine doesn’t? He falls ill and the whole household runs round him. Let my lamp be half a measure short of oil, and someone says I am wasting things. We are both your sons, sir, but my whole life they’ve treated me as less because I wasn’t born to your wife.’ Having gone so far, he pressed him with one question more. ‘Since that is what you think of me, sir, tell me this. Who, exactly, is my own father?’

At those words Jia Zheng began to shake all over with rage. ‘You animal!’ he thundered, and got up to strike him. But the fury rose suddenly and caught in his chest. Everything went black before his eyes, and he fell back in a faint. The room was in an uproar. Some pinched him, others tried to get him to swallow something, and it was a long while before they brought him round. Jia Huan neither cried nor pleaded. He took advantage of the commotion to slip straight out, and no one thought to stop him.

Jia Zheng got his breath back. Someone had already sent in haste for a doctor. The doctor took his pulse, shook his head and said, ‘You have grieved too deeply for the old lady in these first days of mourning, sir, and the pulse of your heart is already weakened. On no account must you give way to such anger again. If there is another attack like this one, I fear we may not be able to bring you round.’ No one in the room dared speak. Jia Zheng rested a little, then sent for Lin Zhi-xiao and ordered Jia Huan to be turned out of the house that very day. His allowance was to be stopped and his rooms sealed, and every gatekeeper was to be told that he was never to be let in again.

When Aunt Zhao heard the news she came rushing in with her hair all loose, crying and making a scene. She flung herself on the ground and rolled about, shouting, ‘What right have you to turn my Huan out? Where is he to go? Are you trying to make him beg in the streets?’ She shouted that she would go out with Jia Huan. Jia Zheng, thinking of Tan-chun married so far from home, quickly ordered them to hold her back. ‘You stay,’ he said. Aunt Zhao shouted a while longer and had her cry on the ground. Then she got up of her own accord, brushed the dust off her clothes and went back. All this time only Cai-yun said not a word. She quietly put her few clothes and ornaments together, tied them in a small bundle, and followed Jia Huan out of the mansion. Whether she came or went, not a soul was willing to ask her to stay. A woman at the gate looked at her and bent over her shoe sole again, stitching away as though she had seen nothing.

Meanwhile the household had also taken up the question of Bao-yu’s marriage. They went over it again and again without coming to anything definite. In her lifetime the old lady had said before them all, ‘Bao-yu’s marriage must be arranged soon. There is no need to wait for me. If anything should happen to me, you must not let the mourning observances hold you back and delay this important business of his.’ At the end she added to Jia Zheng, ‘There is no need to wait three years. Do it within the hundred days.’ In neither speech did she say whose daughter it was to be.

One day, sitting in Lady Wang’s rooms, Jia Zheng brought up Bao-yu’s marriage. ‘We cannot put it off any longer,’ he said slowly. ‘While she was alive the old lady told us to see to it early and not stand on the rules of mourning. The Lin girl has grown up in this house. The old lady loved her best, and there is no fault to find with her looks or her disposition. Her father, our brother-in-law Lin, had that one daughter and entrusted her whole future to us. What I would do first is send the birth particulars of the two to be matched.’

Lady Wang did not contradict him to his face. She looked down and stirred the tea leaves in her bowl, then said, ‘What you say is true, of course. But the Lin girl’s health is another matter. She falls ill every few days, and the imperial physician says she is already much too run down. However fine a girl she may be, I fear she will not be fortunate. The Bao girl, now, has everything in her favour: looks, disposition, family. There has been talk for years of the happy match of gold and jade. Besides, we’ve just had this matter of witchcraft in the family, the Prince of Zhongshun’s people are still summoning people from house to house, Her Highness is ill and my brother cannot get back from his post. If we hurry to celebrate a wedding now, won’t we be doing one more thing to bring ourselves to notice? There is no reason to rush it in any case. Let the old lady’s affairs be over first, and we can think about it at leisure.’

‘What Her Highness said was not to make a show,’ Jia Zheng replied. ‘She did not tell us to put our children’s futures aside altogether.’

Lady Wang did not argue further. She put the matter gently to one side with another ‘We can think about it at leisure,’ and added, ‘If you are set on going ahead, you must ask Her Highness first and wait for my brother to return to the capital.’ Jia Zheng, seeing her take this line, could hardly press her further. There the matter stuck, neither settled nor dropped, and we need say no more of it here. After Jia Zheng had gone, Zhou Rui’s wife came in and asked with a deferential smile if Miss Lin’s birth-date card was to be sent to the astrologer to see how it matched. ‘Find it first and keep it by you,’ Lady Wang said. ‘There is no hurry to send it. Miss Lin is ill. We can speak of it when she is a little better.’

Since their betters could come to no decision, the servants’ gossip soon flew in every direction. In the tea room, under the verandah eaves, by the kitchen fire and on the well platform, they gathered by twos and threes. Master Bao’s marriage was ready-made news for them, and each had a word to add. ‘I hear they have Miss Lin in mind upstairs,’ said one with a smile. ‘She and Master Bao grew up together. Who else should it be?’ Another laughed. ‘Not necessarily. Our lady has always had Miss Bao in mind. Haven’t you heard the story about the happy match of gold and jade?’ Someone more seasoned smiled and said, ‘No use arguing. When could people like us ever guess what they’re doing upstairs? We’ll find out soon enough when the day comes.’ Simple, who had heard them from the side, laughed. ‘Let him marry both of them, and there’ll be no one left to disappoint.’ They all laughed and told her not to talk nonsense. She still had a basket of old coloured threads in her hand and went on asking everyone she met when there would be wedding wine to drink. The little maids carried it all back to the rooms, one repeating what she had heard and another adding her part, so that Bao-yu and Dai-yu both heard it.

Dai-yu heard every word and would not ask a single question. She sat leaning by the window with a book she had not read, rolling the corner of a page between her fingers. Bao-yu had scarcely sat down when she said with a cold smile, ‘You are a very busy man these days. The whole household is making a match for you. One day it is settled that it’s this girl, the next day it’s settled that it’s that one. Which one they have really settled on, how are outsiders like us to know?’ As she spoke she turned her face away. The thin edge of the page had curled under her fingers.

At the word ‘outsiders’ Bao-yu flushed at once. ‘Who’s an outsider? Say that word again and I won’t go on living!’ In his agitation he pointed to the heavens, ready to swear an oath.

Dai-yu quickly turned her face away. ‘Who wants to hear you swear such useless oaths?’ she said in a hurry. ‘The other day I heard you went to Second Sister’s rooms and asked why the family had insisted on saying she was willing. Now it’s your own turn and all you can do is swear.’ Bao-yu sat there stunned, without a word. Dai-yu lowered her voice. ‘You needn’t swear. I was only teasing.’

Bao-yu sat for a little while. Then he noticed that the medicine bowl on the table had little left in it but a layer of dregs, and that the draught was much paler than usual. He picked it up and took a closer look. ‘Why have they changed the medicine?’

‘The prescription calls for good ginseng,’ Nightingale said quietly from beside him. ‘The house can’t provide it just now. The accounts office says to make do with a lesser sort for the time being.’

Bao-yu held the bowl without a word and did not set it down for some time. Back at the House of Green Delights, he opened an old chest of his. At the bottom lay a little protective Buddha of sandalwood. The Duke of Qing had given it to him alone that year, and he had kept it there ever since without touching it. He took it out, looked at it in his hand, and called Tealeaf. ‘Take this out and get silver for it. Don’t go to any shop where we have an account, and don’t let anyone from the household see you. With the silver, go to the old-established shop on the west side and buy a fine ginseng root. It must be whole, not just the rootlets. If anyone asks, say it’s for the medicine of someone ill in your own family.’

Tealeaf took it and had a look. ‘But the Duke of Qing gave this to you especially, Master Bao. Can you really bear to part with it?’

‘Don’t ask,’ said Bao-yu.

Musk, who was standing by, said with a smile, ‘That Buddha is a costly thing. What it fetches will be more than enough to buy one ginseng root. What do we do with the rest?’

‘You keep what’s left,’ Bao-yu said. ‘It will come in useful some day.’

Seeing that Bao-yu meant it, Tealeaf had no choice but to slip the Buddha into his clothing and go out. He was away half the day and did not return until evening, bringing a mature ginseng root wrapped in soft paper. Bao-yu unwrapped it quickly. The neck was long and the markings fine, the rootlets were all intact, and the colour inclined to yellow. Though he knew little about ginseng, he could see that it was far better than the packet from the storeroom. He told Musk to take it over to Naiad’s House that very night and say only that the apothecary had just sent a fresh supply. Nightingale prepared the medicine the following day. Dai-yu drank half a cup and said, ‘This tastes different today.’ Nightingale looked at Snowgoose. ‘They say they’ve only just got it,’ she said. Dai-yu asked no more and drank the other half as well. She had a little more colour that day than she had had for several days.

A few more days passed in this way. Bao-yu heard all the uncertain talk outside and saw Dai-yu looking worse by the day, eating and drinking less. One night he tossed and turned in bed and never closed his eyes. Two days later, finding an opportunity, he went to Naiad’s House and sent the attendants away. He sat before Dai-yu for a time before speaking softly. ‘The other day all I could do was swear oaths. That was no answer. We’ve been together since we were children. What’s in our hearts is ours to acknowledge. Shall we bow to each other?’

Dai-yu was both glad and startled. She said nothing, but sat with her head bowed. The candle on the table was nearly spent. She picked up the little silver scissors of Western make beside it and clipped the wick. The flame brightened at once. She put the scissors back, then nodded with a smile. Bao-yu only nodded in return. On his way out he said, ‘Wen-guan knows how they do these things on the stage. I’ll ask her.’ ‘There needn’t be much ceremony,’ Dai-yu said quickly. Bao-yu laughed. ‘Don’t worry, Mrs Bao. Just one stick of incense.’ And off he ran, before Dai-yu had time to scoff at him.

Back in the House of Green Delights, Bao-yu sent quietly for Wen-guan. Finding only a little lamp burning in the room, she smiled and asked, ‘What did you want me for, Master Bao?’ Bao-yu told her. Wen-guan thought a moment. ‘If you mean the way we do it on the stage, I can still remember several ways. But if you want the proper ceremony of the house, I don’t know it.’ ‘We don’t want the house ceremony,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Just do it the way you know.’ Wen-guan smiled back. ‘That’s easily done, then. All we need is a stick of incense, a pair of scissors and the moon. Let’s do it at Green Crest Hall. There’s room in the hall and the moon is close. Bring two of your own attendants, Master Bao, and let Miss Lin bring two of hers. Ou-guan can keep watch for me.’ ‘Better still,’ said Bao-yu, smiling. ‘We’ll do as you say. You get the things ready and I’ll bring what I ought to bring.’ When she had gone, he took the old purse out from inside his underjacket, looked at it and tucked it back against his breast.

But let us leave them for a while. Since getting to the bottom of Xi-feng’s old affairs, Lady Xing had scarcely let a day pass without bringing up You Er-jie in front of Jia Lian. One day she called him to her room, spread several volumes of old testimony on the table and said coldly, ‘That Second Sister You of yours was a pretty girl and good-natured, too. She was with child, and things came to such a pass that she swallowed gold. Yet not one person would say half a word in her defence. Now that the old case has come to light, the people at the Prince of Zhongshun’s mansion are raking up old accounts house by house. If the affairs of Zhang Hua and Jing-xu and You Er-jie are taken together, do you think they’ll proceed against Xi-feng and leave you alone?’

Jia Lian sat in the lower seat. He clenched both hands before he spoke. ‘It was I who put my wife aside and took another while we were in mourning both for the Court and in our own family. Once that petition reached the magistrate, I was still the first person they had to take up and call to account. If we lay it all at her door now, does that make our own hands clean?’

Lady Xing gave a cold laugh. ‘If you know your own hands aren’t clean, all the more reason to break with her soon. Or are you waiting for the magistrate to do it for you?’

Jia Lian picked up the blank sheet on the table. It had been laid ready two days earlier. Its corners were still flat and there was just one crease down the middle, not a word on it. He turned the sheet over. ‘The break will come, of course. But not today. We’re still in mourning for Grandmother, and Xi-feng is ill. If I put it in writing now, people outside will say all the more readily that we waited till she was ill to turn her out.’

Lady Xing gave a cold laugh. ‘So now it’s her good name you’re thinking of.’

Jia Lian put down the blank sheet. ‘Not hers. Mine.’

Lady Xing eyed him. ‘Well, now you’ve said that, I shall wait.’ Jia Lian made no answer. He rose and went out. The blank sheet lay where it was on the table, the crease still down the middle.

Since her fall, Xi-feng had been moved out of the main courtyard into a quiet place on the west side. With her allowance cut, everyday provisions were poorer still. Even the breakfast gruel was thinner than before. An old woman in charge of charcoal came to report. ‘Her Ladyship says every branch is cutting back. You’re allowed only one brazier of charcoal a day now, ma’am.’

‘Did Her Ladyship say that herself?’ asked Xi-feng.

The woman lowered her head and said nothing. Asked again, she said, ‘Wang Shan-bao’s wife passed on the order.’

Xi-feng asked about her medicine. ‘The accounts office says Second Master gave orders to fill the old prescription as before, but not to pay for the costly new ingredients,’ said the woman.

‘Did Second Master say that himself too?’ The woman could find no answer. Xi-feng laughed. ‘The charcoal comes from Her Ladyship’s mouth, and the medicine from Second Master’s. Any other mouths? You might as well tell me all at once.’

The woman put on a placating smile. ‘Don’t blame me, ma’am. I’m only carrying out my orders.’

‘So Her Ladyship’s mouth and Second Master’s both grow on the likes of you. Very good. Leave the charcoal and get out!’

A little later someone came to fetch the pair of silver candlesticks she used to use, saying they were to go back to the common stock. Seeing a copper foot-warmer as well, he made to carry that off too. ‘Who ordered you to fetch the foot-warmer?’ asked Xi-feng. ‘I suppose it belongs to the common stock too.’ Xi-feng laughed. ‘“I suppose” will pass for a tally now, will it? Take the candlesticks. Leave the foot-warmer.’ He did not dare touch it again, but gathered up the candlesticks and went.

Xi-feng lay on the kang. She neither argued nor wept. She merely said to Felicity, ‘Remember the names of everyone who came today. Don’t leave out a single one. The day I’m up again, I’ll settle this account with them, entry by entry.’ Felicity said she would and wrote the names on a scrap of old paper.

Of all of them, only Patience still came regularly to see her. She brought no crowd with her that day, merely telling a little maid to set down two baskets of silver-frost charcoal, a packet of medicine and a flask of lamp oil on the verandah. She went in herself and sat down beside the kang. Xi-feng looked at her. ‘Your name carries weight now, too.’

‘I didn’t bring my name today,’ said Patience, smiling. ‘Only two baskets of charcoal.’

‘Whose account is the charcoal charged to?’ asked Xi-feng.

‘My own monthly allowance,’ said Patience.

‘You’re running a whole household now, and you use your allowance to plug this hole of mine.’

‘A hole that size I can still afford to plug,’ said Patience. She got up and patched the torn places in the window, poured the dregs out of the lamp and filled it with fresh oil. Seeing a crust at the spout of the medicine pot, she washed it off with hot water. Xi-feng let her get on with it. Patience sat down again without a word about the inquiry into the accounts or talk of sending Xi-feng away. She only asked, ‘How much have you had to eat today?’ ‘Half a bowl of gruel.’ ‘Have another half this evening.’ Before going, Patience stirred up the charcoal in the brazier and set a pair of newly stitched soft-soled shoes beside the pillow. ‘I’ve no use for those,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Keep them for now,’ said Patience, and went out. Xi-feng told Felicity to put the shoes under the bed. Felicity asked if she would like to try them on. ‘Leave them there,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Someone will have a use for them.’

Now that day, as Bao-yu came out of the outer study and passed through the hall, he heard Zhou Rui’s wife telling one of the married servants that the birth-date cards had already been exchanged in the main apartments. ‘Doing such a thing in mourning, won’t people outside have something to say? Which young lady is it?’ the woman asked. ‘It’s the master and mistress’s business,’ said Zhou Rui’s wife. ‘Is it for you or me to ask? The old lady wanted it done within the hundred days, but there was a delay earlier on. Now the master is pressing them to choose a day.’

Bao-yu stood a while behind a pillar of the verandah and then went off into the garden. When he reached Naiad’s House, Dai-yu was leaning against the warming basket, unable for a time to stop coughing. He sat down beside her and waited till the fit had passed. ‘Cousin,’ he said at last, ‘the birth-date cards have been exchanged. They’ll be choosing a day soon.’

‘I’ve heard,’ said Dai-yu.

Neither spoke. After a long while Dai-yu asked, ‘Did you hear which young lady it was?’ ‘I didn’t,’ said Bao-yu. She asked nothing more.

Bao-yu sat a little longer, then said suddenly, ‘With the moon to witness it, it comes to the same thing.’ Dai-yu looked up at him. ‘Have you thought it through?’ she asked slowly. ‘I have.’ Dai-yu said nothing more. She only smoothed the handkerchief in her hands, then smoothed it again.

Over the next few days the two made their preparations separately, and Wen-guan sent word to the Kshitigarbha Convent. On the day they had agreed upon, Ou-guan came into the Garden at dusk, saying she was going to see Dai-yu. That night the sky was full of moonlight, the wind bitterly cold. All the Garden gates had long since been locked. The Green Crest Hall stood at the highest point in the Garden. There was usually only one old woman to look after it; as soon as it grew dark she went down to sleep in her little room at the foot of the slope. Wen-guan and Ou-guan had come early and were waiting in the hall with a little box containing a plain porcelain censer, a bundle of thin incense-sticks and a square of white silk damask. Ou-guan looked round the front and back of the hall, then sat down to keep watch on the lower stone steps. A wind came off the water and lifted a corner of her plain gown.

Before long Dai-yu came up by the eastern bamboo path with Nightingale and Snowgoose, and Bao-yu by the western slope with Musk and Tealeaf. The two parties met in front of Green Crest Hall. Dai-yu stopped first; Bao-yu stopped too. The wind up on the hill was colder than in the Garden. Dai-yu had barely come to a standstill when she coughed again. Her clothes were thin. Bao-yu unfastened his peacock-gold cloak to put about her shoulders. Dai-yu looked up at him and did not refuse. ‘Won’t you be cold?’ she asked. ‘If you aren’t cold, neither am I.’ He put the cloak round her and was left in his plain robe. Musk hurried to offer her own cloak, but Bao-yu waved her away and went straight into the hall. The cloak was wrapped about Dai-yu. Its gold threads caught the moonlight for an instant, then went dark again.

Inside, a little table had been set in the middle. On it were only the plain porcelain censer, a cup of clear water and the square of white silk. There were no wedding candles, no scarlet silk. Snowgoose came forward, drew the little silver scissors of Western make from her sleeve and laid them beside the censer. Bao-yu took the old purse from inside his robe and set it on the silk. Dai-yu knew it at once. Her fingers pressed against the front of the cloak. Wen-guan lit a stick of incense. The hilltop wind nearly put it out several times, and only when Musk cupped both hands round it did it catch properly. All about them there was the faint sound of water. Nightingale, Snowgoose, Musk and Tealeaf stood to either side without a word. Down on the lower steps, Ou-guan still kept watch, turning her head to peer down the hill when she heard a watch-drum far away.

When the incense had burnt a little over an inch, Wen-guan handed the scissors first to Bao-yu. He lifted a lock of dark hair at his temple, cut it off and laid it on the white silk. Wen-guan handed the scissors to Dai-yu. She reached for them and cut a lock of her own. It seemed too little; she cut another, and only then had enough to lay beside Bao-yu’s. Wen-guan put down the scissors and gathered their hair, winding the strands together once and then again. She divided them into two, each with hair from both, and tied each into a tight knot. The first came loose; she tied it again before it would hold. The old purse on the white silk was the one Dai-yu had made with her own hands and given to Bao-yu long ago. Its colour had faded, but the orchid embroidered on it could still be made out. Dai-yu took a purse of her own from her sleeve. Wen-guan put one knot in each purse, drew the strings tight on both purses and handed one to each of them. Bao-yu took his in both hands and slipped it back close to his breast. Dai-yu took hers and folded it back into her sleeve.

‘I don’t know any words to bring good luck, Master Bao, miss,’ said Wen-guan. ‘I only know a few lines from the plays.’ ‘Go on,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Say them.’

Wen-guan stood in the middle. She did not sing, but spoke slowly the lines she had once been used to singing:

‘So vivid purple, lovely red have blossomed everywhere,
Yet all are given over to broken wells and crumbling walls.

In this fine hour, before this lovely scene, what can I do?
Whose courtyard has the joys to gladden the heart?

Only for you, my flower-fair love,
And these years that flow like water.

I have wandered everywhere in search of you;
You pity yourself in your secluded chamber.’

At the third couplet Dai-yu’s hand stirred inside her sleeve.

When Wen-guan had finished, Bao-yu spoke suddenly. ‘I have something to say too.’ And he recited:

‘No one knows where love begins. Once it takes hold, it grows ever deeper. The living may die of it; the dead may come to life through it. Where the living cannot die for love, nor the dead come back to life for it, that is not love at its utmost.’

Dai-yu listened and looked at him for a little while. ‘Those are words from somebody else’s book,’ she said at last. ‘The words I spoke were mine,’ said Bao-yu.

Only then did Wen-guan add a word. ‘In life or death, may this bond never break. Let Heaven and the spirits bear witness.’

Everything fell quiet. The full moon stood directly above Green Crest Hall, and the stick of incense had not yet burnt out. Bao-yu and Dai-yu faced one another, each took half a step back, and bowed low. A first bow. A second. A third. Then they straightened, took each other’s hands and smiled.

Wen-guan bent her head and poured the clear water into the censer. The last half-stick of incense went out at once. Snowgoose hastily wrapped the scissors in her handkerchief, tucked them into her sleeve and picked up the gauze lantern. Nightingale drew the collar of the cloak closer about Dai-yu, and Dai-yu did not take it off. Ou-guan came up from the stone steps and nodded to Wen-guan. There was no one below the hill, she said. Wen-guan put the censer, the cup and the white silk back in the box, and she and Ou-guan went down the northern slope first. A few specks of ash had fallen on the stone steps; the wind did not blow them away at once. With Nightingale and Snowgoose supporting her, Dai-yu went back to the Naiad’s House by the eastern bamboo path. Bao-yu, with Musk and Tealeaf, went back towards the House of Green Delights by the western slope. Where the paths divided, Bao-yu stopped. Dai-yu did not look back, but walked slowly on, leaning on Nightingale. The edge of the cloak brushed lightly against the stone, then she turned round a clump of green bamboo and he saw her no more. Bao-yu stood a moment longer, pressed the purse against his breast, and turned to go down the hill.

Back at the Naiad’s House, Nightingale helped Dai-yu out of her things. When she came to the cloak, she asked, ‘Shall we send it back tomorrow?’ ‘Keep it here for now.’ Nightingale straightened the cloak and draped it over the stand beside the bed. Dai-yu drank half a cup of warm water and lay back against her pillow. She coughed only two or three times that night and was quieter, after all, than she had been for several days. At the House of Green Delights, Musk saw how thinly Bao-yu was dressed and hastened to have more charcoal brought. Bao-yu sat by the lamp. He took out the old purse and looked at it, but did not open it. He put it back close to his breast. Tealeaf asked whether he would go to the study again in the morning. ‘I will,’ said Bao-yu. He did not open his Zhuang-zi again that night. He simply sat there until the lamp ran out of oil.

Early the next morning the old woman who looked after Green Crest Hall came up to unlock it. She found patches of night dew on the paving outside and took her broom to them, grumbling as she swept that the wildcat had been back in the night, trampling all over her steps. When she was done, she shut the hall door again and went back down the slope. If you would know what followed, listen to the next chapter.
