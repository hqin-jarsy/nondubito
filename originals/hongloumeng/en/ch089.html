Han Qin · Dream of the Red Chamber: Thirty New Chapters

Chapter 89Xiang-yun and Dai-yu find a new rhyme by the cold pond; Yuan-chun brings her father a warning in a dream

Original continuation by Han Qin, not a recovered manuscript by Cao Xueqin.

The morning after that night at Green Crest Hall, the Garden opened at dawn and shut at nightfall as usual. Meals were fetched, medicines brewed, accounts checked and doors locked. Outside the mansion, though, a rumour had sprung up: everyone was saying that Master Bao-yu was to marry Miss Bao-chai of the Xue family.

One day, when Jia Zheng had finished his work at the government offices and was about to go home, a colleague he was on easy terms with smiled and said, ‘I hear there’s a wedding in your household. How is it you’ve kept so quiet about it?’ Jia Zheng was taken aback. ‘There’s no such thing. What made you ask?’ ‘It’s all over town,’ said the man. ‘They say it’s the Xue girl.’ Jia Zheng could hardly ask more and made some vague reply. At home a letter was brought in from the gate. It had come from his protégé Fu Shi, who began with congratulations, went on about this and that, and brought up his sister’s marriage, still a worry to him. In the end it came to the same question.

Jia Zheng put down the letter and sent for Lin Zhi-xiao. He was still in plain mourning clothes, with two official documents on the desk awaiting his attention. Lin Zhi-xiao came in with a lacquer tray of slips from several shops and stood before the desk. Jia Zheng did not look at them. ‘Where did these stories start?’ he asked.

Lin Zhi-xiao bowed. ‘I’ve made inquiries, sir.’ He ventured a look at Jia Zheng before continuing. ‘In the last fortnight, Lady Wang’s rooms have sent Zhou Rui’s wife out four or five times. At the silk shop she asked about dress material and said it was to be made up to Miss Bao-chai’s old measurements. At the jeweller’s she asked about sizes for bracelets, and at the candle shop about candles for a wedding feast. The shopmen heard half the story and passed it on to their employers. The employers told their relations and friends. By the time it had gone round, the marriage was settled. A couple of shops have even sent people to ask when the wedding is, so they can lay in stock.’

‘Did Lady Wang tell her to say it was Miss Bao-chai?’

‘Zhou Rui’s wife only said that Lady Wang had told her to have an outfit made to Miss Bao-chai’s old measurements. As for a marriage, Her Ladyship said nothing expressly.’

Jia Zheng shoved a paperweight half an inch aside. After a moment he said, ‘Put it about that the household is still in mourning and no marriage has been settled for any of the young people. If the shops send anyone to ask, turn them away. Don’t say a word more.’

‘And if they ask whom he really is to marry, sir, what am I to say?’

Jia Zheng looked up at him. Lin Zhi-xiao hastily lowered his head. ‘I understand, sir.’ He withdrew with the tray.

Jia Zheng sat alone for a while. On his desk lay a pair of sample candles the shop had sent a day or two before. Four characters were painted in gold down each candle: ‘May the Hall Be Filled with Gold and Jade.’ He looked at them for a time, then had a boy bring in a brazier and burned them there before him. He watched the wax drip, drop by drop, into the ash.

Bao-yu had heard the rumour in the inner apartments too. His hand went straight to the purse against his breast. He pressed it through his clothes and kept his hand there. There was nowhere he could go to say a word about it.

Within a few days the rumour had reached the Wei household. The following afternoon word suddenly came from the gate of the Rong mansion that young Madam Shi had arrived. Xiang-yun came through the second gate with Kingfisher, wearing a pale-blue figured satin jacket and a dark-blue coat lined with black fox fur. In her hair were just two plain flowers. A woman at the gate smiled at her. ‘Have you come to offer congratulations, young Madam Shi?’

Xiang-yun stopped. ‘To whom?’ she asked with a smile.

‘Why, to Master Bao-yu and Miss Bao-chai, of course,’ the woman said, still smiling.

‘You haven’t got it straight yourselves, and you’re asking me!’ Xiang-yun laughed. ‘I’ve come to see the girls. The next person who asks if I’m here to offer congratulations can tell me where the wedding wine is being served.’ She started in. Behind her Kingfisher laughed. ‘My mistress said she didn’t believe a word of it all the way here. Now she’s barely in the gate and already asking about the wedding wine.’ The woman took her at her word. ‘It’ll be any day now,’ she said with an obliging smile. ‘When the day comes, we’ll be sure to ask you to have a cup, young madam.’ Xiang-yun turned back and spat. ‘It’s your wagging tongues I’m asking about. Who said there was a wedding?’ And off she went into the Garden.

Bao-qin and Xiu-yan had been with Aunt Xue, and Li Wen and Li Qi were in the Garden delivering some needlework. Hearing of Xiang-yun’s arrival, all four went to see her at Naiad’s House. Bao-chai had sent Oriole over with some fresh skeins of thread for Dai-yu. She was sitting under the window, knotting a cord in the phoenix-tail pattern out of pale-blue and rose-pink silk, and Xiang-yun would not let her go either.

Dai-yu was feeling a little better that day. In a light-grey lined jacket under a dark satin waistcoat, she sat by the window reading a volume of old lyrics. Her face was pale, but there was a faint flush on her cheeks. Nightingale had just brought her medicine. It smelled of stale ginseng, and Dai-yu had pushed it away after drinking only half a cup.

Xiang-yun looked round at everyone before going over to the couch and taking Dai-yu’s hand. ‘The whole world’s turned upside down out there,’ she said with a smile, ‘and here you sit as quiet as can be.’

‘Let them make their noise,’ said Dai-yu, smiling. ‘What’s it to me?’

Xiang-yun was about to say more when Bao-qin gave her sleeve a little tug. ‘Never mind,’ Xiang-yun said with a smile. ‘I came to see the girls, not talk about all that.’

Xiu-yan lifted the medicine cup beside Dai-yu and looked at it. ‘The ginseng smells stale. How can you drink it?’

‘The storeroom says all the good ginseng has been given out,’ said Nightingale. ‘We’ve had to make do with ginseng rootlets these last few days. There’s still a little of what came the other day, but Miss Lin told us to save it and not touch it.’

Xiang-yun took the cup and held it to her nose. ‘A house this size,’ she said with a frown, ‘and it can’t supply a single good root of ginseng?’ She put it back beside Dai-yu.

‘Ginseng isn’t rice,’ said Dai-yu with a smile. ‘I shan’t starve for going a day without it.’

‘You’ve grown remarkably easy to please,’ said Xiang-yun, laughing. ‘There was a time when one petal out of place would keep you talking half the day.’

‘That was then. Now the flowers bloom as they please. Why should I mind what they do?’ Dai-yu raised the cup again and slowly drank what was left.

‘That’s more like it,’ said Xiang-yun. ‘I haven’t brought any good ginseng today, only my mouth. I suppose I’ll have to make it up to you with a few lines of verse.’

‘It’s close in here,’ said Bao-qin. ‘There’s hardly any wind. Why don’t we go and sit at Crystal Hollow Lodge? The air’s fresh by the water, and there’s good sun there.’

‘Miss Lin has only just taken her medicine,’ said Nightingale. ‘She can’t sit in the wind by the water.’

‘Bring a thick cushion and another hand-warmer,’ said Xiu-yan. ‘We’ll only stay for an hour. That needn’t do any harm.’

Xiang-yun was on her feet already. ‘If she coughs even once, I’ll carry her back myself!’

‘You’re a married woman now, and you still make such grand promises,’ said Dai-yu, laughing. ‘You’ll find you can’t carry me and fall into the water yourself.’

Xiang-yun turned and pointed with a laugh at Kingfisher. ‘If I fall in, Kingfisher can pull you out first, then me.’

Kingfisher waved both hands. ‘I won’t pull either of you out. I’ll stand on the bank and call for help.’ Everyone laughed. Dai-yu laughed too and stood up. Nightingale brought her a cloak. ‘Not that one,’ said Dai-yu. Nightingale went back for the peacock-gold cloak and put it round her shoulders. Xiu-yan handed her her own hand-warmer.

Xiang-yun had been talking to Bao-qin. When she saw what Dai-yu was wearing, she came over and felt the collar. ‘This one’s thick,’ she said with a smile, and went out ahead of the others.

Snowgoose carried the cushion to Crystal Hollow Lodge first, spread it under the covered verandah and brought a little brazier to warm the pear broth. The others came slowly after her. The pond had fallen so far that a stretch of stone showed beneath the bamboo railing. No one had pulled up the withered lotuses. They stood where they were, rasping against one another when the wind passed. A swathe of reeds along the bank had gone white. Frost lay on the stone steps under the roof. Where the sun reached it, it had melted; in the shade it was still there. Light shifted on the water, brightening and darkening the steps.

They took their seats. Oriole had brought her cord along and was threading it by the railing. Li Qi laid out the paper and Li Wen ground the ink. Bao-qin noticed that the two stools of mottled bamboo were still where they had been. ‘These two are still here,’ she said. ‘Only there aren’t as many of us.’

‘There may be fewer of us, but we’ve still got that evening,’ said Xiang-yun. She turned to Dai-yu. ‘Do you remember the Mid-Autumn before last? We got as far as “Across the cold pond passes a crane’s shadow; / The chill moon buries a flower’s soul.” Then Miao-yu came out from behind the rocks and wouldn’t let us go on.’

‘I only remember that you threw a stone and startled the crane,’ said Dai-yu with a laugh. ‘And I rather think it was you it frightened.’

‘It was you it frightened,’ Xiang-yun laughed. ‘Don’t put it on me.’

‘When you came back that night, madam, there was mud all round your shoes,’ said Kingfisher, laughing. ‘And you said you hadn’t been down to the water’s edge.’ Xiang-yun got up to pinch her, and Kingfisher darted behind Bao-qin. Everyone laughed again.

Snowgoose brought an old roll of verse from Naiad’s House. Xiang-yun unrolled it and found Miao-yu’s fair copy. The writing at the beginning was close-set: the lines the two of them had made together, followed after their last couplet by the many lines Miao-yu had added. Xiang-yun looked at it for a while, rolled it up and laid it on the table. ‘That rhyme is altogether too cold. Let’s have fresh paper and start with a brighter one. And no grabbing the brush. Whoever has it gets to write a line.’

‘You’re the one who says no grabbing,’ Dai-yu laughed. ‘When you grab it yourself, don’t blame me.’

Xiang-yun had already taken up the brush. ‘If I do, Kingfisher will have to take the next line as my forfeit.’

‘I can pour you another cup,’ Kingfisher said at once. ‘I can’t give you another line!’

Li Qi laid out a fresh sheet of blush-white flowered paper, and Li Wen ground more ink. Xiang-yun glanced towards the water and wrote: ‘After rain, the pond holds a silk of sunlight;’
Dai-yu took the brush and answered: ‘Through the open railing drifts the cool of night.’
‘I can only write what’s in front of me,’ said Li Wen with a smile, and wrote: ‘The last lotus leaves cup a little dew;’
Li Qi followed: ‘Fresh osmanthus scents the breeze.’

Bao-qin looked up towards the bank. ‘The osmanthus is long gone,’ she said, laughing.

‘Off the subject, then,’ said Dai-yu with a smile. ‘Let it stand for now. We can think it over tomorrow.’

Bao-qin had been itching to join in. She took up the brush: ‘Morning sun warms the dressing chamber;’
Xiu-yan thought for a moment, then answered: ‘A frosty wind chills the embroidery frame.’
Xiang-yun wrote: ‘A thin line of geese crosses the Milky Way;’
Dai-yu followed without stopping to think: ‘Gulls’ shadows dot the long ripples.’
Bao-qin offered another: ‘Swallows skim past the curtain;’
Xiu-yan answered: ‘Crickets chirr away beneath the grass.’
Xiang-yun wrote: ‘We brew our tea with moonlight in the steam;’
and Dai-yu: ‘The shadows of flowers fall across our sleeves.’

Oriole had a length of bead thread between her fingers. When she heard this line she laughed. ‘That one sounds true enough. There was just such a shadow on Miss Lin’s sleeve a moment ago.’ Dai-yu looked down and saw the shadow of a reed plume on her sleeve. A breath of wind and it was gone.

Li Wen took the moment to write: ‘Heavy dew dampens blue-green sleeves;’
Li Qi answered: ‘Idle clouds drift past the whitewashed wall.’
Xiang-yun wrote: ‘On old paper we set down a few short lines;’
Dai-yu answered: ‘A candle here and there lights a freshly dressed face.’
Bao-qin wrote: ‘Flakes like powdered jade fly past the steps;’
Xiu-yan matched it with: ‘The white jade hall begins to chill.’
Xiang-yun wrote: ‘Tonight, don’t ask how late it is;’
Dai-yu followed: ‘Tomorrow we’ll bring our cups again.’

‘We’ve only just had a few sips of pear broth,’ said Xiu-yan, laughing, ‘and you’re already making plans for tomorrow’s wine.’

‘It needn’t be wine in the cups,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘Pear broth will do.’

Last came Li Wen: ‘Let’s not let such a lovely sight go to waste;’
and Li Qi: ‘Let’s not forget this quiet outing too soon.’
Xiang-yun rested her chin in her hand and thought awhile, then wrote: ‘Years hence, when we come back here,’
Dai-yu lifted the brush. A long time passed before she wrote the last line: ‘We’ll sit in the old verandah once more.’

When they had finished, Xiang-yun took up the paper and read it through from the beginning. ‘The osmanthus is over and the swallows are gone,’ she said with a laugh. ‘Half this poem belongs to next year. Let’s leave it here for today.’

While they were still laughing and talking, Dai-yu reached for the poem. Her sleeve shifted and the gold threads on the front of her cloak caught the sun. Xiang-yun saw the flash and turned suddenly to Oriole. ‘You’ve been at that cord all this time. Haven’t you finished it yet?’

‘You were making verses and trying to pinch people, madam,’ Oriole laughed. ‘I got so busy watching that I fell behind.’

‘Make mine a good tight one, then,’ said Xiang-yun. ‘One that won’t come undone at a tug.’

‘If I tie it so tight you can’t undo it later, don’t blame me,’ Oriole said, laughing.

‘All the better if I can’t.’ Xiang-yun got up and went to the railing. Dai-yu glanced down at the front of her cloak, then looked out over the water too.

They read the new verses through several more times. The shadows lengthened and a breeze came up over the pond. Dai-yu coughed twice. ‘That’s quite enough for today,’ said Nightingale. ‘It’s time we went back.’

Xiang-yun put her hand on the poem. ‘Two little coughs and you’re sending us away,’ she laughed. But up she got, rolled the poem carefully and handed it to Dai-yu. ‘Keep it. Next time I come, we’ll take it up from “the old verandah”.’

‘If you do come, we will,’ said Dai-yu with a laugh. ‘Though I daresay the Weis will lock you in and not let you go running all over the Garden.’

‘I’ve got legs of my own. Who’s going to lock me in?’ Xiang-yun laughed. Then she added, ‘You needn’t pay any attention to those stories outside, either. A lie’s a lie. The whole city can say it’s true and it won’t make it so.’

‘When have I ever paid them any attention?’ said Dai-yu.

Xiang-yun only smiled. Her eyes rested for a moment on Dai-yu’s cloak, but she said nothing more and turned away from the railing. The others laughed a little longer, and then they all went back together.

That night Jia Zheng sat in his outer study well into the second watch. Official business had been piling up for days. On one side of his desk were the congratulatory cards that were to be sent back; on the other, several documents concerning the case of Mother Ma. There were papers, too, for Jia Huan’s expulsion from the family. At last his eyes began to smart, and he laid his head on the desk for a moment’s rest.

He did not know how much time had passed when he found himself standing in a very long palace corridor. Vermilion pillars rose one after another on either side, each clearly lit by the palace lanterns, yet not a voice could be heard. At the far end stood Yuan-chun in her court robes, a heavy phoenix crown on her head. ‘Your Highness,’ Jia Zheng called. Yuan-chun did not answer. She raised a hand slowly and took away the golden phoenix at the side of her crown, then removed both earrings and drew a pin from her hair. The gold beads fell without a sound. Slowly she went on, removing one layer and pausing before the next. At last she took the crown in both hands, lifted it off and laid it beside the threshold. Only her black hair remained.

Yuan-chun turned and pushed open a vermilion door. It creaked open, only to show another closed door beyond it. She pushed that one open and there was another. Door after door, layer upon layer, and still she could not get out. When she looked back, every door she had opened had shut again without a sound. Beyond the doors it was quiet and deep, with no end in sight. At length Yuan-chun stopped and looked back at Jia Zheng from far away. She said only, ‘Father, perhaps it’s better that the road is long.’ Then she turned, pushed the door open and went in. It closed behind her, without a sound.

Jia Zheng woke with a start. His heart was pounding and he was cold with sweat. Outside the window it was not yet light.

Jia Zheng went over the dream from beginning to end, though he was not altogether willing to believe it. After sitting for a while, he rose and quietly summoned a trusted servant. Without telling anyone else, he had the man change into plain clothes and tuck a few taels of silver in his pockets. He was to go straight to the palace gate and contrive to bring out someone he knew among Master Xia’s attendants. All he was to ask was how Her Highness had been of late. He was not to present a visiting card from the Rong mansion or speak of the matter to anyone else. Whether or not he saw anyone, he must come back and report each day. The servant took his orders and left by the west side gate before daybreak.

His servant gone, Jia Zheng sat on. He read the same official paper twice without writing a word on it. Finally he put down his brush. ‘Whether the dream means anything or not,’ he said to himself, ‘Mother’s business can’t wait any longer.’ He went straight to the main rooms.

Lady Wang had eaten only half a bowl of porridge. Surprised to see Jia Zheng so early, she hastily called to Silver for more tea. Jia Zheng sat down. ‘We’re not waiting any longer to set the day for Bao-yu’s wedding.’

The beads stopped moving in Lady Wang’s hand. She waited a moment before answering. ‘The talk outside has only just died down. If we go ahead now, I’m afraid people will take it all the more seriously.’

‘The rumour was about Miss Bao-chai, not the Lin girl.’

‘You know what Miss Lin’s health is like. She cannot go a day without medicine, and we cannot even get the ginseng. And Bao-yu never knows when to put on a coat or take one off. They both need looking after. Which of them is to look after the other?’

‘The Lin girl can be treated in time,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘If we go on putting off the marriage, what good is the treatment?’

‘What the Old Lady said before she died was: “There is no need to wait three years. Do it within the hundred days.” The hundred days are long past now. Since we’ve missed them, all the more reason to take our time and see. Rushing it now will sound stranger still to anyone who hears of it.’

‘We did miss the hundred days. I should have seen to it. If we put it off again, it will be three years. Who can wait that long?’

‘But we ought at least to wait for a letter from my brother.’

‘It can’t wait.’

Lady Wang lowered her head and counted out her beads one by one. For a long while she did not speak.

Jia Zheng looked at her. ‘Before Mother died, she had a private message for me. She told Amber to pass it on where no one could hear, without adding a single word. She said, “My daughter is gone. Her daughter is my heart’s own flesh. The one who is my very life must always be with my heart’s own flesh.”’

‘She said that too?’ said Lady Wang. ‘I never heard it.’

‘Of course you didn’t hear that. But Mother also said, “Bao-yu’s marriage must be arranged soon. There is no need to wait for me. If anything should happen to me, you must not let the mourning observances hold you back and delay this important business of his.” She said that to your face. You heard her. You can’t listen to only half of what she said.’

Lady Wang gripped her beads. ‘Everyone knows how dearly Mother loved Miss Lin. But when an old lady speaks out of fondness for her children, she isn’t always thinking of how they’re to get on from day to day.’

‘That year when Nightingale said something in jest, you saw with your own eyes the state Bao-yu got into.’

‘He was a child. He took leave of his senses for a moment.’

‘If he isn’t allowed a word in his own marriage, does that make everyone else’s word count for him?’

Lady Wang fell silent. After a long while she loosened her hold on the beads. ‘If it was Mother’s private wish and it is your decision, I’ll see to it. But if a letter comes from my brother, have it sent to me at once.’

‘Of course.’

‘Well then,’ she said, ‘Miss Lin is frail. We had better not make too much of a stir.’

‘Do it soon and keep it simple. No music, no guests and no word of it outside. Have the man who chooses the dates find us the earliest one today.’ Jia Zheng got up and left.

Lady Wang sat on the kang until Jia Zheng was out of the room. Then she said to Silver, ‘The tea’s cold. Take it away.’ But she kept her hand on the cup and did not give it to her. After sitting another while, she sent for Zhou Rui’s wife. ‘If any news comes from my brother, there must be no delay about that either.’

That very day they consulted a man about the date. There was only one auspicious day in the coming month, and it was just nine days off. Jia Zheng looked at it. ‘That’s the day,’ he said. No sooner had the little slip of red paper reached the inner apartments than every room was busy.

Since there could be no fuss outside the house, the clothes and bedding had to be made in haste within it. The sewing women got out all the dark-red silk and satin they had. Old things were unpicked and washed; anything too short got an extra border. Li Wan checked the vessels, and Patience went from door to door for Lady Wang, checking what each room had drawn from the stores. Nightingale and Snowgoose were busier than anyone, day and night: measuring clothes, turning out trunks and running to the accounts office to hurry them up about cotton wadding.

Lady Wang sent Zhou Rui’s wife to Naiad’s House. Her message was simply that the day was set and Miss Lin should rest easy and look after herself. Dai-yu did as custom required and kept out of Bao-yu’s way for the time being, sitting quietly in her own rooms. Nightingale spread the old flowered mauve bed-curtain out on the couch. ‘The edges and corners are worn. There’s still time to take the border off and put on another. What do you think, miss? Silvery red, or mauve again?’

‘The old colour is still good. Why take it off? Just patch the worn places.’

‘You only do this once in your life, miss. Why make do with old things for everything? Cousin Feng sent this over the very first day you came to the mansion.’

Dai-yu smiled. ‘Would a new border make me new as well?’

Snowgoose came in with two garments in her arms. ‘The sewing women say there’s enough cloth for the jacket, but they’re half a width short for the skirt. They want to know if you’d like another colour.’

‘Unpick the old skirt in the trunk,’ said Dai-yu. ‘That’ll be enough.’

‘Don’t bother about these things, miss,’ Nightingale said with a smile. ‘Just sit and get your strength up. If you can get through the day without coughing, that’ll help us all.’ Dai-yu picked up the new poem from Crystal Hollow Lodge. While Nightingale measured her sleeves, she held out her arm when asked and turned when asked. When the medicine came it was still rootlets, and she drank it as before. Once, when her cough grew worse, she rested awhile, then spread the poem out flat again and weighted it down.

When Bao-yu heard the day was set, the first thing he did was go to Naiad’s House. He had only reached the bamboo bridge when Nightingale came out to meet him. ‘You must keep away for a few days, Master Bao. Her Ladyship says you’re not to see Miss Lin.’

Bao-yu stopped at once. ‘I won’t come in. I only want to know if Cousin Lin has taken her medicine today.’

‘She has.’

‘Is her cough any better?’

‘Much the same as yesterday.’

‘And that good root of ginseng?’

‘There’s only that little bit left. We didn’t use it today. Miss Lin said to save it for when it matters.’

Bao-yu gripped the railing. ‘What does she mean, when it matters? If she’s coughing like this today, doesn’t it matter now?’

‘The accounts office can’t give us any more. Miss Lin’s afraid there’ll be none to be had once it’s gone, so she’s making it last.’

Bao-yu stood at the end of the bridge. ‘I’ll think of something.’

‘You’re not to go running about outside just now, Master Bao. Miss Lin asked me to tell you to do as your father and mother say, wait quietly and not make more trouble.’

‘Did she say that herself?’

‘Yes,’ was all Nightingale said. Only then did Bao-yu turn back. He sat awhile at the Drenched Blossoms Pavilion, looking towards Naiad’s House. When he got back he had Musk fetch the silver left over from last time and gave it to Tealeaf, with orders to ask for ginseng at the same shop again. The next day he sent Musk over with a box of pear paste and followed at a distance. Nightingale took the box but still would not let him past the bamboo bridge. Three times Bao-yu asked, ‘Is she well?’ Nightingale answered with a single word: ‘Well.’

That evening Bao-yu was sitting alone by the lamp when he felt the jade at his breast go icy cold. He put a hand over it. In a moment it began to warm, until it felt hotter than his palm. He drew it out and turned it over in the lamplight. There was no crack on either side, and the inscriptions were just as they had always been. Musk saw that something was the matter. ‘What are you looking at, Master Bao?’

‘It went cold just now. Then it grew hot.’

Musk took it and felt it. ‘It’s only a little warm,’ she said with a smile. ‘You feel your own heart beating and say the jade is moving.’

‘It wasn’t my heart. It’s right here against me. I can tell.’

Musk examined the silk cord. ‘The knot hasn’t come loose, and there’s nothing wrong with the jade.’ Two old women on night duty in the outer room overheard. One said the cord must be too tight. The other came in rubbing her eyes, looked for herself and said that in cold weather a thing worn next to the skin was bound to feel warm one moment and cool the next. Then she went out again. Bao-yu paid neither of them any attention. He tied the jade back at his breast, pressing it with his hand from time to time.

For several days the servant sent to the palace gate had come back with the same report: he could get no word inside and had not seen anyone he knew. He went again that day, but by lamplighting he had not returned. Under the inkstone in the outer study lay a fresh red slip with the auspicious date written on it. After the lamps were lit, wheels could be heard now and then beyond the west side gate, and the gatekeeper went out with a lantern. The first time it was a cart delivering rice. The second time it was a boy from the Ning mansion. The third time it was only an empty cart passing along the street.

As for Xi-feng in the west side courtyard, at first after she fell ill she could still sit up for a little while. Now she did no more than lie propped in bed, with Felicity quietly bringing her something to drink or a little broth. That day an old woman passing outside the courtyard lowered her voice to say to someone, ‘They say in the front rooms that His Lordship woke with a start in the night and sent a man to the palace gate before it was light. What for, nobody knows.’ The voices faded into the distance.

Xi-feng heard this from her pillow and slowly closed her eyes. Felicity took her mistress’s stillness for tiredness and gently let down the bed-curtain. The medicine at the bedside grew cold.

A long while later a voice came from behind the curtain. ‘The medicine’s cold. Pour it away. Brew tomorrow’s stronger.’ Felicity promptly answered, then asked through the curtain, ‘Would you like to sit up a little, madam?’ The voice said, ‘I can’t move.’ To find out what followed, you must read the next chapter.
