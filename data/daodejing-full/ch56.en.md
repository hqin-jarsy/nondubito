# A Person Who Cannot Be Bought

## I. Something Praise Cannot Purchase

You probably know someone who is difficult to control by the usual means. Praise pleases them, but does not buy their agreement. A cold shoulder hurts, but does not immediately send them scrambling to make amends. Offer them a better position and they will consider it carefully. There are still things they will not do to get it.

It is tempting to imagine that such a person has no vulnerable points: no attachments, no needs, perhaps not much feeling. But that would be a rather bleak kind of freedom. What is worth admiring is something else. They care about people and want things from life without placing their entire existence in the keeping of any one person or reward.

Chapter 56 of the *Daodejing* describes someone who cannot readily be captured by the forces that ordinarily move us. It ends with six conditions arranged in three pairs: intimacy and estrangement, benefit and harm, honor and disgrace. These are familiar instruments of social power. Come closer. Go away. Here is your reward. Here is what you stand to lose.

Can a person live among these forces without letting them decide everything? That is the question worth bringing into our lives. It is not a promise that injury will cease to matter. Losing an income changes what you can do; violence causes real damage; bereavement can break your heart. People need protection, support, and practical ways out. Needing them is not a failure of wisdom.

The freedom at issue is therefore not the achievement of needing nobody. It is the possibility of retaining some judgment of your own while loving, needing, and dealing with others.

## II. Not Every Conversation Needs to Establish Your Position

> Those who know do not speak; those who speak do not know.

Taken as a rule about word counts, the opening is easy to admire and hard to use. How would anyone teach, explain a misunderstanding, or testify about an injustice? Why would a book leave so much to say if its entire recommendation were silence?

For this reading, the more useful target is a particular pressure behind speech: the need to establish yourself. You have barely heard a question before announcing your position. Someone is halfway through an account of their experience, and you already have a more sophisticated interpretation. The purpose is less to understand what is happening than to make sure everyone knows where you belong in the room.

Social media makes that pressure familiar. A subject appears and seems to demand an immediate public response. Wait too long and you may miss the opportunity to demonstrate that you understand it. Soon you are spending more time defending your identity as a knowledgeable person than acquiring further knowledge.

There is relief in being able to say, “I don't know enough about this yet.” There is relief, too, in allowing a conversation to go well without becoming its central figure. Nothing essential has disappeared because your contribution was not the most memorable one.

This connects with the restraint of speech explored in [Chapter 23](ch23.html). It is not a certificate of wisdom awarded to quiet people. Silence can conceal indifference, and speaking can be a responsibility. What matters is whether your words help something become clearer or mainly protect your standing. Once they no longer have to prove your existence every time, they may become more useful.

## III. Light That Leaves Room for Other People

The chapter offers six actions: stop the openings, close the doors, soften the light, share the dust, blunt the sharpness, and untangle the knots. Together they suggest a person gradually loosening a posture of constant outward strain.

The first two recall [Chapter 52](ch52.html). Closing the doors need not mean shutting out criticism or withdrawing from the senses. Think instead of the openings through which you continually seek reassurance. A message remains unanswered, so you check again. A colleague seems distant, so the afternoon becomes an inquiry into what they think of you. Closing one of those doors can mean allowing your attention to return to something that does not require an immediate response from someone else.

Softening the light and sharing the dust are particularly generous images. Someone who understands a difficult subject can explain it without making the listener feel stupid. Someone accomplished can be fully present without turning the company into an audience. The light is not extinguished. It simply stops being trained on the difference between your brightness and everyone else's supposed dimness.

Imagine a successful person visiting old friends. Every topic could be turned toward an achievement; every ordinary story could become an opportunity for advice. Yet another possibility is available: listen to a small event in a friend's life that has nothing to do with your own success. Your accomplishments remain intact. Someone else gets a place at the table.

Blunting sharpness and untangling knots bring this attitude into conflict. Sharpness sometimes has a purpose, and disagreement will not vanish if ignored. But when every difference must produce a winner, we tighten knots that might otherwise be loosened. Dropping an insult, distinguishing what happened from what we assumed, admitting the part we have not understood—these are ways of untangling rather than merely pulling harder.

None requires staying in an unsafe relationship or abandoning a necessary boundary. The chapter calls the resulting condition *xuantong*, a deep or mysterious accord. Here I read it as a way of sharing a world without erasing differences and without continually using them to establish rank. [Chapter 55](ch55.html) explored softness; this chapter asks what softness might look like between people who remain themselves.

## IV. The Three Pairs

The closing sequence refuses capture through intimacy or estrangement, benefit or harm, honor or disgrace. [Wang Bi's commentary](https://www.chineseclassic.com/LauTzu/LaoTzu_wongbee/ch56.htm) notices the dependence between the paired terms: if closeness can secure your compliance, distance can become a means of moving you too; if advantage controls you, the prospect of losing it acquires a corresponding power.

This series develops that thought as an invitation not to let either pole occupy the whole of a life. That is an interpretation of the sequence, not a claim that the word *ke*, “can,” literally means “carried to an extreme.” Nor does it require remaining at a comfortable midpoint between every pair. The question is whether any single relationship, transaction, or verdict has been allowed to decide who you are.

Intimacy can be deep. You can depend on a partner, give generously to a friend, and grieve when someone leaves. “I love you” still differs from “You therefore have authority over every part of my life.” Distance, meanwhile, can be necessary protection. Freedom from these levers includes refusing to remain where you are being hurt merely to prove how forgiving you are.

Benefit need not be despised. Wanting fair pay or needing a job does not make a person shallow. The difficult moment comes when an attractive offer asks for something in return that deserves a second look. Must you injure someone else? Must you keep contradicting your judgment? Not being for sale does not mean wanting nothing. It means that not every price can purchase every action.

Harm is more difficult, because losses and threats can exceed what an individual can bear. That is why independence needs social as well as personal supports: people to turn to, enforceable protections, an actual possibility of leaving. Someone unable to escape coercion should not receive another accusation of insufficient detachment. The task is to increase the conditions under which refusal becomes possible, not to pretend those conditions do not matter.

Honor and disgrace reach another vulnerable point. Praise can tempt you to perform the approved version of yourself indefinitely. Contempt can persuade you that someone else's low opinion has settled your worth. There may be useful criticism to hear, but humiliation is not a final assessment of a person. When the chapter calls this person precious to the world, we can hear the value of a life not wholly priced from outside. We should not turn it into a prediction that the world will always recognize that value.

## V. Keeping a Little Authority Over Your Life

There is no need to master the language of *xuantong* before trying something small. Start with a relationship in which you are about to agree to everything to recover someone's approval. Do you want to do what they ask, or are you trying to close off the fear that they will leave?

Security can come from closeness, but it can also be supported by friends, practical abilities, and interests of your own. That does not make love less serious. It keeps love from becoming the only place where you are allowed to breathe. A relationship often has more room to flourish when neither person must serve as the other's entire world.

With an opportunity, take enough time to look at the terms. Some compromises make cooperation possible. Others gradually make it harder to recognize yourself. Negotiate what can be negotiated and name what you cannot accept. If your present circumstances leave little choice, you have not thereby forfeited your worth. Keeping your judgment alive and looking for support are also ways of recovering room.

With an evaluation, try separating “I handled this badly” from “I am a failure.” The first can help you change something. The second closes over an entire life. The same restraint applies to praise: doing well today does not bind you to an unbroken performance of excellence tomorrow.

Return to the person who cannot readily be bought. They are not beyond sadness, need, or fear. They have learned, imperfectly, to remain someone within intimacy, to retain judgment while receiving a benefit, and not to surrender immediately when approval is withdrawn.

This freedom is rarely spectacular. Sometimes it is a reply you do not rush to give, a conversation you do not need to win, or the ability to sit comfortably beside someone else's light. You are still in the world. You simply have not handed it the whole task of deciding your life.
