# Take the Difficulty Seriously Before It Becomes Difficult

## I. The Promise Made Too Quickly

You have hardly finished explaining the task when the answer arrives: no problem, easy. You feel relieved and hand it over. Several days later, when you need the result, you discover that nobody asked about a crucial condition and the slowest part has not even begun.

Someone else is less immediately reassuring. They ask questions, check a few unglamorous details, and say which part they cannot yet guarantee. At first this seems laborious. Over time, however, their estimates become the ones you can plan around. When something changes, they tend to tell you before the deadline arrives.

Laozi captures the contrast in Chapter 63: treating too many things as easy produces many difficulties. The wise person gives difficulty its due. This is not the talent for making every task sound daunting. It is the willingness to give a task its actual weight before promising to carry it.

The chapter also closes this series' group of nine essays, from 55 through 63. A sequence that began with an infant's softness ends with something very ordinary: thinking for a moment before saying yes.

## II. Do the Work Without Manufacturing More Work

The opening offers three apparent paradoxes: act through non-action, attend to affairs without generating affairs, and taste what has no flavour.

They become less mysterious in a familiar scene. Two people need to clarify who is responsible for a task. Instead, the organization adds three forms and holds five meetings. Activity multiplies, but the conversation that would settle the matter still does not happen.

Acting through non-action can mean doing what is needed without adding a performance whose main purpose is to prove that you are doing something. Attending without generating affairs means not creating complications merely to manage them. Neither phrase abolishes labour, planning, or responsibility. They ask which part of our effort helps and which part only makes the room look busy.

Tasting the flavourless brings the same restraint into life outside work. An experience need not be intense, novel, or conspicuously exceptional to be worth having. An ordinary meal, company in which no one has to perform, an evening without new information: these can acquire a depth that constant stimulation keeps us from noticing.

The thought recalls the unassertive flavour of [Chapter 35](ch35.html), the subtraction of [Chapter 48](ch48.html), the lighter intervention of [Chapter 57](ch57.html), and the patient cooking of [Chapter 60](ch60.html). Do not turn off the heat that the fish needs. But do not keep turning it simply because waiting makes you restless.

Less fuss may be unimpressive to watch. It can nevertheless give useful action room to occur.

## III. Responding to Resentment Without Surrendering Yourself

The chapter asks us to attend to the small and the few, then says to respond to resentment with *de*: virtue, integrity, or the quality of one's conduct. This has a long history of interpretation. Announcing that everyone has misunderstood it does not settle that history.

There is a related exchange in the *Analects*. Asked about repaying resentment with kindness, Confucius speaks of responding to resentment with uprightness and to kindness with kindness. These voices can illuminate one another, but the passage does not establish that Confucius was personally replying to this particular chapter of Laozi.

The reading developed in this series places special emphasis on an earlier moment: before resentment becomes a self-sustaining exchange of retaliation, can we avoid adding fuel? A mistake can be identified without an extra humiliation. A request can be refused without condemning the person who made it. Some hostility really is manufactured by what we needlessly add.

Yet the word for responding can also mean answering or repaying. It is not legitimate to insist that the sentence concerns prevention alone and says nothing about harm already done. Nor can we promise that good conduct makes resentment unable to reach us. A non-aggressive person may still be bullied; someone who did not start a conflict may still be drawn into it.

Responsibility for that harm does not pass to the victim. Refusing needless retaliation does not require remaining in danger, concealing another person's wrongdoing, or presenting one's pain as graciousness. Stopping the harm, seeking accountability, and leaving can all be proper responses.

The practical question is whether the wrong done to us must now author every action we take. Can we protect ourselves without allowing the other person's cruelty to become the only available model of power?

Earlier discussions of self-preservation in [Chapter 50](ch50.html), softness in [Chapter 55](ch55.html), independence from manipulation in [Chapter 56](ch56.html), and mutual non-harm in [Chapter 60](ch60.html) belong within this limit. They help us reduce avoidable opposition. They do not issue certificates of invulnerability.

## IV. Give the Small Problem Its Appointment

Laozi advises addressing the difficult while it is still easy and working on the great while it is still small. Large difficulties, he says, begin in easier conditions; great undertakings begin in fine details.

A small defect in a system may take little time to repair. Left until it affects other parts, the same defect can require interruptions and repeated work. A misunderstanding about responsibilities may initially need a short conversation. After months of accumulated irritation, the conversation is no longer only about responsibilities.

The problem is often not that the early action was impossibly hard. It is that the problem was not yet impressive enough to earn attention. We keep postponing its appointment until it becomes important by becoming expensive.

The positive side is just as practical. Writing a book still requires making a paragraph intelligible today. Building a team still requires saying clearly who will do what. A large ambition cannot complete these small acts on our behalf.

But not every disaster arrives with a visible warning, and not everyone who attends carefully to details obtains the result they hoped for. Chance, resources, and other people's decisions matter. Turning every failure into proof that someone neglected an early sign would make a useful reminder into an instrument of blame.

The more defensible invitation is modest: where you can already see a problem and can reasonably act, do not ignore it merely because it is small. Where you cannot yet see clearly, leave room for testing and adjustment. Small things deserve care not because they guarantee everything, but because some burdens that will become heavy can still be set down lightly now.

## V. Stop Adding the Task of Looking Great

The wise person does not set out to be great, the chapter says, and can therefore accomplish something great.

An additional task attaches itself to everything when we need to look important. A minor error cannot be admitted. Asking for help seems diminishing. Ordinary work feels beneath our position. By the time our image has been protected, little room remains for the thing we were supposed to do.

Laozi's phrase can be read as not setting oneself up as great, and it also follows the advice to work on greatness in small details. Both readings move attention away from the image of the actor and toward the requirements of the action.

This continues [Chapter 34](ch34.html) and the discussion of external honour in [Chapter 62](ch62.html). It does not mean that self-contempt produces achievement. You can recognize your abilities and take pleasure in completed work without making every collaboration demonstrate that you are its most important member.

The test is often unremarkable. When a detail needs redoing, will you look at it? When a newcomer notices something you missed, which do you protect first: the project, or the picture of yourself as someone who never misses that sort of thing?

Setting the picture aside does not make you smaller. It allows work previously blocked by your image to continue.

## VI. Seriousness Is Not Permanent Alarm

The chapter closes its main argument by pairing light promises with weak trust, and an excess of easy assurances with an excess of difficulty. Taking the difficulty seriously is what makes the outcome less difficult.

A careless promise costs more than the speaker may realize. “Definitely by Friday” can determine someone else's whole week. They stop looking for alternatives. They make commitments of their own. By the time you explain on Friday that the task was harder than expected, more than a date has been lost.

Seriousness might mean checking before agreeing. It might mean saying, “I can finish this part; that part depends on something we have not confirmed.” The answer is less exhilarating than an unconditional yes. It gives the other person something real to work with.

Taking difficulty seriously does not mean imagining disaster behind every minor task. If preparation must be perfect before action begins, caution becomes another way of never starting. Reasonable preparation, checks along the way, and correction when new information arrives are more useful than an endless rehearsal of possible failure.

Laozi's final freedom from difficulty expresses the point sharply; it is not a guarantee that conscientious people will have trouble-free lives. Care cannot remove every obstacle. It can keep us from manufacturing some obstacles unnecessarily and leaving them for other people to discover.

Give the thing its weight, then bring it back to a scale at which you can act. The point is not to stare anxiously at a mountain. It is to see where today's footstep belongs.

## VII. One Actual Piece of Attention

There is no need to turn this chapter into another elaborate self-improvement programme. Look instead at a small matter you have been postponing. Is it genuinely too early to address it, or is it repeatedly displaced by things that make more noise?

Then look at a promise already made. If you now know you cannot keep it, saying so early is more reliable than covering it with a larger promise. Trust does not require plans never to change. It requires not leaving others inside assurances you already know to be misleading.

If you are angry, two tasks may also need separating: resolving what happened and making the other person feel diminished. The first may deserve vigorous attention. The second does not necessarily have to be completed alongside it. Less retaliation need not mean less justice.

And allow an ordinary day to count. A problem clarified, a paragraph made readable, a meal eaten with attention: none needs to become a grand achievement before deserving your presence.

The practical force of the chapter lies in such small changes of direction. Add fewer complications. Add less resentment. Attend to something already visible. Leave a little room in which a plan can still change.

## VIII. From Softness to Care

Looking back over this series' nine-chapter group, [Chapter 55](ch55.html) began with an infant's softness and vitality. [Chapter 56](ch56.html) asked us not to surrender judgment entirely to closeness, distance, advantage, injury, or status. [Chapter 57](ch57.html) considered excessive intervention; [Chapter 58](ch58.html) kept change in view even during good fortune; [Chapter 59](ch59.html) attended to the roots that make continuation possible.

In [Chapter 60](ch60.html), a small fish in a pot helped distinguish care from incessant interference. [Chapter 61](ch61.html) asked the strong to take a lower position. [Chapter 62](ch62.html) kept a door open for people who had not yet become good. Finally, we arrive at the unshowy seriousness of today's task.

This grouping is the reading rhythm of the present series, not a claim that the ancient author demonstrably planned the book in these units. Nevertheless, a common reminder emerges: strength need not always become harder, fuller, faster. It can appear as the capacity to wait, conserve, notice, and leave space.

None of these attitudes guarantees an easy life. They may simply let us meet the difficulties life brings without so much extra weight produced by our own haste.

The next time “How hard can it be?” is about to leave your mouth, pause long enough to ask a question. Real care does not always make a dramatic entrance.
