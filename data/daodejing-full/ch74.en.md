# Before You Take the Carpenter’s Axe

## I. The person with the last word

The project lead is explaining why a seemingly simple change is not simple. There are dependencies, people already working to an agreed plan, and a constraint that will not disappear because the deadline is inconvenient. The senior manager listens for a while, then interrupts: “We’re overcomplicating this. Here’s what we’ll do.”

The discussion is over. The difficulty is not. The person with the last word has stepped into the place of the person who understands the work. The new decision may happen to be right, but authority has not supplied the knowledge needed to make it right.

Most of us recognize the impulse. A child is struggling, a colleague is slow, a professional gives an answer we dislike. From outside, we see the desired result so clearly that the route to it seems obvious. We reach for the tool.

Chapter 74 ends with an image of this temptation: taking over the master carpenter’s axe. But it begins with the power to kill. We should not hurry past that beginning to reach a comfortable management lesson. The stakes are human lives, and the question is not simply who can perform a task efficiently. It is who may exercise a power, under what authority, and within what limits.

## II. What an ultimate threat cannot settle

The chapter develops through conditional situations. If people do not fear death, how can killing frighten them? If they do fear it, and those who depart from the expected order are seized and killed, who will dare? If fear of death persists, there is one charged with killing; to take that place is to incur the danger illustrated at the end.

This is not a straightforward recommendation for capital punishment. The passage tests the ruler’s reliance on its threat and turns toward a warning against appropriating the function. Its language belongs to a political world in which such decisions had lethal consequences.

The people within that world are not switches that turn obedient when sufficient fear is applied. They have relationships, livelihoods, reasons for resistance, and unequal exposure to danger. A ruler who asks only how much intimidation will work has already narrowed the question disastrously.

The conditional structure matters, too. The text does not establish a universal sequence through which everyone passes, or prove that hardship necessarily makes people indifferent to dying. It exposes a possible limit of extreme coercion. A threat may fail to secure its intended response and still inflict terrible harm.

That distinction keeps the chapter from becoming a clever recipe for more effective intimidation. The question is what kind of relationship has been created when the threatened destruction of a person is expected to hold that relationship together.

## III. When pressure is the only remaining argument

[Chapter 72](ch72.html) warned about authority maintained through fear. Here the warning reaches its sharpest point: what happens when the sanction you regard as final no longer produces compliance?

The wording “恒且” does not, by itself, prove that people have already been made chronically and uniformly fearless. Oppression and desperation are plausible contexts for the passage, but neither allows us to read every person’s inner life from outside. Someone living under severe hardship may remain intensely afraid and eager for safety. Someone else may resist despite that fear.

The ordinary parallels are less extreme, but recognizable. A manager repeatedly says, “If you don’t like it, leave.” Eventually an employee does leave. Or the employee cannot afford to leave and remains, while trust and voluntary initiative disappear. A parent discovers that another ultimatum does not restore the relationship that previous ultimatums damaged.

Fear has no guaranteed expiration date. It can endure for years, along with the suffering it causes. The mistake is to assume that increasing it must always produce the desired result, and that outward compliance tells us what has happened to the relationship underneath.

At that point, searching for a larger threat avoids the harder inquiry. What would make cooperation possible without this pressure? What conditions have we ignored? What forms of protection, voice, and material security would allow people to respond as participants rather than as targets?

## IV. A role is not an unlimited license

Who is the “司杀者,” the one charged with killing or death? Interpretations have included a heavenly or natural order and a properly assigned human function. The phrase cannot establish, on its own, a developed ancient doctrine of modern judicial independence.

It can support a limited contemporary analogy: a ruler’s private will should not count as sufficient authority over another person’s life. Today, that concern leads us toward evidence, lawful procedure, scrutiny, defense, and avenues of review. Those safeguards are not all literally spelled out in the chapter. They are necessary commitments in the political application we make of it.

We also need to distinguish expertise from legitimacy. Being skilled at carrying out an action does not authorize it. Holding an office does not make every order issued from that office just. Finding a technically competent official is no answer to violence whose grounds are never examined.

The adopted text uses “为畸者”; other versions use “奇.” This difference does not prove a clean lexical distinction between condemning conduct and condemning an entire identity. Yet the ethical distinction remains valuable. Holding someone answerable for harm need not mean declaring them permanently disposable.

[Chapters 27](ch27.html) and [62](ch62.html) kept open the possibility of not abandoning people. That possibility must coexist with the protection of those they have harmed. Responsibility should rest on conduct, evidence, and fair procedures, not on the convenience of dividing the world into worthy lives and worthless ones.

## V. What the carpenter’s hand has learned

“代大匠斫”: taking the master carpenter’s place at the cut. An axe looks uncomplicated. Lift, swing, strike. The practiced hand, however, is responding to grain, resistance, angle, balance, and the moment when another blow would be a mistake. Much of what makes the action reliable is not visible in its outline.

Such knowledge is not mystical or forever unteachable. It can be learned through explanation, observation, practice, and correction. The danger lies in mistaking permission to intervene for having completed that learning.

The warning that a substitute rarely avoids injuring their hand does not logically prove that the master must also be injured. Nor does it promise that every abuse of authority will return neatly to its author. In practice, a senior person may impose a bad decision and leave other people to bear the cost. That is one reason to prevent the takeover, not wait for experience to punish the right person.

Parents encounter the temptation when they solve every difficulty a child could gradually learn to handle. Managers encounter it when they override a specialist without understanding why the existing arrangement was chosen. “It will be quicker if I do it” may be true for the next five minutes and false for the next five months. Dependence grows; the person responsible stops developing judgment; hidden errors accumulate.

Delegation is not withdrawal from responsibility. A leader still needs to ask questions, provide resources, review outcomes, and intervene appropriately in negligence, abuse, or danger. The difference is between making competent work possible and replacing it with a preference that rank makes compulsory. Good oversight requires enough understanding to ask useful questions, not a performance of knowing every answer.

## VI. A pause before taking over

Three signals deserve attention: impatience with an explanation, the belief that our position gives us a complete view, and the wish to end uncertainty by issuing an order. None proves that intervention would be wrong. Together they are a good reason to slow down.

Ask what the person doing the work knows that you do not. Ask which consequences they expect from the available options. If their explanation is unclear, request clarity. If a resource is missing, supply it. If there is a serious safety problem, establish protection. Listening does not eliminate decision-making; it gives a decision something more substantial than force of position.

It also helps to separate the responsibility that belongs to us from a task we are displacing. A team leader is responsible for how work is organized, not therefore obliged to carry out every step. A parent is responsible for safety, but also for making room for growing capability. Assistance can develop another person’s agency or replace it indefinitely.

Sometimes a handover really is necessary. Then make it explicit: why it is happening, how long it will last, what knowledge must be transferred, and who will answer for the result. Roles are not sacred boundaries that can never move. The problem is moving them merely because someone cannot bear not to be in control.

If our judgment is repeatedly displaced, we can propose concrete working boundaries: which decisions are shared, which belong to specialist judgment, and when a concern should be escalated. The specialist, in turn, must be willing to explain and submit their judgment to review. Trust in expertise does not remove the right to ask questions.

## VII. Authority with room inside it

The chapter joins a recurring warning in this book. [Chapter 29](ch29.html) questioned treating the world as an object to manipulate. [Chapter 64](ch64.html) examined the intervention that damages a process by disregarding its development. Chapter 74 adds the weight of expertise, assigned functions, and limits on power.

Good intentions do not remove the need to understand what we are doing. Nor do they make other people’s knowledge dispensable. In political life, accepting limits must take institutional form; it cannot depend entirely on a powerful person’s restraint. In an ordinary conversation, it may begin by letting an explanation finish.

[Chapter 75](ch75.html) will turn toward the conditions that make living difficult when demands from above become excessive. It will not ask suffering people to care less about their lives. It will ask us to examine what is being placed upon them.

Before taking someone else’s axe, we might offer something more useful: a stable bench, an appropriate tool, time to work, and a question that is not an instruction in disguise. Sometimes that is the clearest way to exercise the authority we actually have.
