# Let the Food on Your Table Taste Good Again

## I. A simple life or a closed one?

A small state, few inhabitants, equipment left unused, neighbors who hear one another but never visit. Read quickly, chapter 80 seems to purchase happiness by surrendering almost everything: technology, movement, exchange, and curiosity.

That impression is not an invention of careless readers. Archaic images and restricted contact really are present in the text. Yet they need not exhaust its possibilities. This series proposes another emphasis: the wish that ordinary life should not be subordinated to expansion, warfare, and permanent comparison.

The interpretation does not settle every textual difficulty or uncover a mistake made by everyone for two thousand years. It opens a conversation with the chapter. What would we stop doing if possessing a capability no longer obliged us to exercise it? What conditions would let us enjoy where we live without turning it into a prison?

The eventual question will be close to hand: being able to taste the food in front of us. Reaching it, however, takes us through weapons, migration, records, and borders. The simplicity of a table also depends on what happens beyond the kitchen.

## II. Having the means does not require using them

“小邦寡民”: a small political community with few people. We need not imagine it impoverished, or assume that smallness automatically protects it. Small communities can be conquered, suffer shortages, or fall under greater powers.

“使有什佰之器而毋用” refers to equipment associated with tens and hundreds. “什佰之器” has been interpreted in different ways: powerful implements, equipment serving many, or, in the antiwar reading followed here, means connected with military groupings. The military route is plausible in context, not the sole indisputable meaning.

The following lines mention carts and boats with no occasion to ride in them, and armor and weapons with no occasion for deployment. Transport also has civilian uses; its presence cannot prove that every object mentioned is exclusively military. The explicit appearance of arms does support attention to the wish not to need war.

A useful distinction emerges: a capability can exist without having to demonstrate itself through use. Possessing weapons does not require seeking battle. Having a tool does not make every application an improvement. Being able to expand an organization does not prove that expansion is what life needs.

Unused capacity is not always wasted capacity. Sometimes it means the emergency that would have required it has been avoided. Tranquility need not manufacture danger to justify its means of protection. The point is to judge use by the need it serves, rather than create needs to keep every instrument busy.

## III. Taking life seriously and leaving a way out

The adopted text reads “使民重死而远徙.” The received version commonly includes “不远徙,” not moving far away. We should retain that difference without claiming that all early manuscripts present a single uniform wording.

“重死” is ordinarily understood as taking death seriously, giving weight to what it means to lose life. The fact that “重身” can refer to pregnancy does not establish that “重死” therefore means natural reproduction and death. A resemblance between expressions cannot carry that definition.

We can nevertheless preserve the human wish motivating such a reading: generations able to be born, live, and grow old without being consumed by war. That is an aspiration brought to the passage, not a proven translation of the two characters.

“远徙” raises a further difficulty. It can be read in relation to moving far away, and it has also prompted readings concerned with keeping migration distant or avoiding it. This series’ interpretation makes room for leaving danger, seeking refuge, and refusing to be bound to a ruler’s ambition. That is a contested application, not a certainty imposed upon every reader by the wording.

In practice, neither leaving nor remaining should be compulsory for someone else’s convenience. Escape requires resources, routes, and protection that many people lack. Failure to escape is not consent to danger. A community that values life needs conditions for remaining safely and real exits when remaining becomes impossible.

## IV. Less complication need not mean less memory

“使民复结绳而用之”: have the people return to using knotted cords. The image evokes an archaic form of recordkeeping. We should not translate away its backward-looking element, or the question raised by having an authority determine how others ought to live.

A contemporary application can nevertheless concern reducing complications that add nothing useful to life: repeated forms, unnecessary approvals, reports whose only reader will be another report. Complexity sometimes serves an apparatus of control more than the need that supposedly justifies it.

But not every record is oppressive, and not every difficulty is caused by war. Writing, archives, services, and technical systems can protect rights, preserve agreements, support healthcare, and allow cooperation over distance. Removing them in the name of ideal simplicity can especially harm people who need evidence of a claim against power.

The useful question is not how to return to an age without tools. It is which complexities justify their cost, and who gets to decide. An imposed simplification that leaves people without information or services does not restore agency. It changes the form of dependence.

There is a simplicity that frees time for living, and another that forces people to live with less than they need. The chapter becomes productive when it helps us distinguish them. It becomes dangerous when its charm allows us to confuse them.

## V. The taste that comparison covers

“甘其食，美其服，乐其俗，安其居”: find the food sweet, the clothes beautiful, the customs enjoyable, and the dwelling a place of ease. The sentence does not begin from an external ranking. What matters is how the things sustaining daily life are experienced.

We can be eating something we like and suddenly lose pleasure in it after seeing a photograph of a more luxurious table. The food has not changed. Its relation to us has: it has stopped being an experience and become a position in a comparison.

Clothes, homes, and celebrations can undergo the same change. Something useful and enjoyable begins to seem deficient because another image tells us what it should represent. We need not prohibit every comparison to notice when comparison prevents contact with something already present.

Yet not all dissatisfaction comes from looking sideways. Hunger, unsafe housing, discrimination, and unavailable care are real deprivations. Telling someone enduring them to learn contentment would turn simplicity into an excuse to withhold help.

We can enjoy a meal while seeking better conditions. We can value a custom while changing an unjust part of it. Pleasure does not require freezing the world. It asks that improvement not make us incapable of recognizing any good before the next good arrives.

The bowl need not be the finest bowl anywhere for its contents to nourish us. It does, however, need something nourishing in it. Both parts matter.

## VI. Neighbors within hearing

“邻邦相望，鸡犬相闻，民至老死不相往来”: neighboring communities can see one another and hear one another’s animals, yet their people grow old without going back and forth. Literally, the image limits contact. It does not mean only noninterference, however much more comfortable that reading sounds today.

Our contemporary application can seek proximity without occupation: knowing another life exists nearby without needing to direct it, compete with it, or absorb it into our project. Distance need not mean hostility. Nearness does not confer authority over someone else’s existence.

Neighbors can also freely visit, trade, learn, and help. Dignified coexistence must respect those contacts and the possibility of movement. The ideal should not be used to enclose people in a community that others have declared sufficient for them.

Nor does noninterference require ignoring a request for help or serious harm. Imposing our preferred way of life differs from providing protection; monitoring out of curiosity differs from responding to danger.

Perhaps the image can teach us to be close without requiring constant access. The existence of a door does not oblige us to cross it every day. But a door someone freely closes is not the same as one that cannot be opened from inside. A peaceful boundary needs that distinction.

## VII. Simplify with the people who will live there

Begin with a capacity exercised merely because it exists: an available meeting, a possible expansion, an affordable purchase. Asking what real need it serves may let us retain the resource without inventing an obligation to use it.

Then examine a complication other people endure to make our oversight easier. Is it needed? Could its purpose be met more simply? The answer should include those who would suffer if simplification removed something that protects them.

In our own lives, we can reclaim a meal, a garment, or a walk from continual comparison. Not as training in accepting every deprivation, but as an occasion to enjoy something without turning enjoyment into a contest.

And when imagining a better community, ask about exits and connections. Can someone reject its ideal of life? Request help? Leave and return? A happiness that works only while nobody can choose otherwise has a cost its attractive portrait tends to hide.

The peace of this chapter needs adequate resources and less violence, not a population trained to want little by those deciding how much it should receive. A simpler arrangement earns its name by making life more livable for its inhabitants, not merely easier for an observer to admire.

## VIII. The ordinary life that war takes away

[Chapters 31](ch31.html) and [69](ch69.html) resisted the easy celebration of warfare. Chapter 80 lets us look at what warfare threatens: meals, clothing, customs, and places where life can proceed without constantly becoming material for a larger enterprise.

[Chapters 57](ch57.html) and [65](ch65.html) offer another connection: do not occupy everyone else’s capacity to act through plans that presume to know in advance what they should want. Simplicity should not be purchased by denying people knowledge or choice.

In [chapter 81](ch81.html), the last chapter of this reading route, we will return to trustworthy words, knowledge, and giving without accumulating control. The adopted final line will speak of a human way: acting without contending to impose ourselves.

For now, we can return to the table. Wanting to enjoy a meal seems modest. That enjoyment needs food, time, safety, and some freedom from comparison. This is not withdrawal from the world. It is a reminder of what we might want a better-functioning world to make possible.
