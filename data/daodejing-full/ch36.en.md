# This Chapter Is Not Teaching You Statecraft

## I. The Chapter That Has Been Read as Machiavellianism

Chapter 36 of the Daodejing is often read as a lesson in strategy. I want to explore a different way into it.

"What you want to draw in, first let it fully extend. What you want to weaken, first let it fully strengthen. What you want to eliminate, first let it fully flourish. What you want to take, first fully give it."

Want it to contract — first let it expand. Want it weak — first let it get strong. Want it gone — first let it rise. Want to take from it — first give to it.

These four lines have been read by countless people as political manipulation — "want to take down a rival? First flatter him, let him inflate, wait until he's at the peak of his arrogance, then harvest him." Draw and release. Rope-a-dope. Play the long game.

This is the Thick-Black-Theory version of Laozi — reading these four lines as an operator's manual for scheming.

## II. The Four Pairs Describe Structural Dynamics

Drawing in and opening out are opposite movements. So are weakening and strengthening, removing and raising up, taking and giving. Chapter 29 also puts contrasting movements beside each other. The arrangement invites us to look beyond the most visible side of an action.

I use it to consider counterproductive effects. Expansion financed by excessive debt can make growth a burden. Constantly deciding for a child in order to secure obedience can deprive the child of practice in judgment. Strength is not guilty by definition; the question is whether a particular way of using it undermines its supporting conditions.

None of this is an exceptionless law of nature. A bow can spring back or break. A growing business can decline or change course in time. Outcomes depend on circumstances. Recognizing risk still leaves us with decisions to make.

## III. Do Not Let the Plot Own You

The strategic reading has a history. In the *Hanfeizi*, “Illustrations of Laozi” explains these lines through stories of military advantage and gifts preceding attack. It is not simply a modern misunderstanding. [Read the early commentary](https://ctext.org/hanfeizi/yu-lao/zh).

I do not want to turn this chapter into that kind of manual. But rejecting manipulation is different from proving that manipulators always lose. Sometimes they profit, and someone else pays first.

Read alongside chapter 29, the passage raises a different question: what happens to your own life when making another person fall becomes its organizing project? You invest attention and resources in a script. When reality refuses the script, you have to invest more. Your opponent begins to set your agenda.

That is the danger worth naming. Treating another person as something to arrange also binds you to controlling the outcome. It creates exposure; it does not establish who must fail first. Refusing to plot is not waiting for the universe to take revenge for you. It is keeping revenge from choosing your direction.

## IV. This Is Called Subtle Illumination

“This is called subtle illumination.” The small turns are not always visible in the apparent victory or defeat. Alongside chapter 33’s knowing oneself, I read illumination as seeing one’s own position and blind spots as well.

A team is still announcing successes while resources grow scarce. A partner still cooperates but becomes afraid to speak frankly. Subtle illumination is not knowing who is destined to lose. It is noticing whether the conditions beneath apparent success are changing.

You may also discover that your suspicion was mistaken. Then keep asking and listening, rather than turning “I saw through it all along” into another image to defend. The point of seeing is to bring action closer to the situation, not to claim the status of a prophet.

## V. The Soft and Yielding Overcomes the Hard and Strong

“The soft and yielding overcomes the hard and strong.” Think first of a living branch. It bends in the wind and can recover. Something unable to bend must absorb the whole impact.

Flexibility here is not a lack of strength. Nor is weakness an instruction to keep yielding when someone is hurting you. The contrast helps us notice the value of room to respond. It cannot predict that every powerful opponent will lose or every vulnerable person will win.

If a competitor is expanding recklessly, you need not burn money trying to provoke even more expansion. Examine your own resources, product and capacity. The competitor may fail, succeed or change course. Your plan needs to survive more than one possible ending.

Protecting your team, refusing unreasonable demands, documenting harm and leaving a dangerous partnership may all require decisive action. What you decline is the project of controlling another person, not the work of judgment, preparation or self-protection.

## VI. The Fish Does Not Leave the Deep Pool

“The fish does not leave the deep pool.” A fish needs water. The plain image shifts attention from impressive ability to the environment that supports it. Read beside chapter 33’s not losing one’s place, it asks: what makes your life possible?

A practical skill, relationships in which you can speak honestly, a reliable income, sleep, time alone: any of these may belong to your answer. Spending them all to obtain a prestigious title may leave you with the title and a worse life.

But people are not fish, and the pool is not an identity assigned by somebody else. You can change jobs, leave a harmful relationship or move elsewhere. Protect not the old position as such, but the conditions that let you live, judge and turn. Leaving may be how you recover them.

## VII. A State's Sharp Instruments Cannot Be Shown to People

“The state's potent instruments should not be displayed.” Those instruments can lead into discussions of weapons, authority and means of government. We cannot claim that the ancient sentence has only one possible meaning: refusing to advertise yourself.

Here I take it towards restraint in the display of power. A manager may have authority to decide without turning every disagreement into a demonstration of rank. Having the power to discipline someone does not mean fear must organize the workplace.

Alongside the fish that needs its deep water, this suggests tending the conditions that sustain strength instead of constantly performing strength. But public responsibility must not disappear. Who decided, how resources were used and how mistakes can be challenged all deserve clear procedures. Accountability is not boasting; secrecy is not wisdom by itself.

## VIII. What This Chapter Gives the Reader

This chapter can change what we admire as cleverness. Not the ability to script everyone else's next move, but the ability to keep a direction of our own under pressure.

Facing an expanding rival, ask what you can sustain. Facing provocation, decide whether it deserves a response. Silence is an option, not an obligation when harm is being done. Facing the urge to display strength, distinguish public accountability from intimidation.

The fish's pool reminds us to care for the conditions that support a life. It does not forbid leaving an old job or relationship. In this reading, the state's instruments warn against flaunting power; they do not justify hiding public power from scrutiny.

Keep room to turn. Do the work and acknowledge that the ending is not entirely yours to decide. You do not need someone else's eventual collapse to prove that your life was worth living.
