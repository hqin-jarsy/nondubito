# Self-Respect Does Not Need Someone Beneath It

## I. Two kinds of confidence

Some people know what they can do. Ask them a question and they answer carefully; cross a boundary and they can tell you no. Yet in their company you do not have to monitor your rank. You do not have to make yourself smaller before you begin to speak.

Other people are capable too, but every conversation seems to establish a hierarchy. Mention a problem and they first correct your terminology. Ask a question and they make it clear that the question is not quite worthy of them. You may leave knowing something you did not know before, but also feeling diminished.

The difference is not simply between more and less confidence. It is whether confidence needs a lower position for someone else in order to secure its own. Chapter 72 draws a memorable distinction near its end: knowing oneself without displaying oneself, loving oneself without elevating oneself over others.

This chapter closes the group comprising chapters 64–72 in our reading route. Before turning inward, however, it asks those who possess power to notice where that power is pressing. The question of self-respect begins with a question about other people's room to live.

## II. Fear is not the same as trust

The opening warns that when people do not fear authority, a greater *wei*—a word associated with awe, threat or power—may be approaching. The line permits more than one interpretation. Here I take its warning as a challenge to the assumption that present obedience secures a lasting relationship.

A manager loses his temper and the room goes quiet. Something has certainly changed. Perhaps a problem has been clarified. Perhaps everyone has decided never to bring him a problem again. If he checks only whether anyone is objecting, he can easily mistake the second outcome for the first.

A child can fall silent for similar reasons. The explanation may finally have made sense; or the child may have learned that explanations of her own will not be heard. The outward behaviour becomes tidier while the relationship becomes more distant.

None of this means fear has a predictable depletion rate, or that oppressive power will soon defeat itself. Fear can last a long time. People can suffer without an uprising that puts matters right. The warning is not a promise of eventual victory to those being hurt. It denies those doing the pressing an easy inference: effective intimidation is not therefore legitimate, and an absence of resistance is not evidence of an absence of damage.

Rules, responsibilities and protective boundaries are not all intimidation. A useful test is whether disagreement can be voiced and bad news can get through. A relationship held together only by the fact that someone cannot afford to challenge you is different from one in which they can trust you with the truth.

## III. The inconvenience belongs to someone

The next phrase, *wu xia qi suo ju*, concerns people's dwelling or circumstances of residence. The verb has been approached through meanings such as treating lightly, crowding or intruding upon. We need not exclude those possibilities before asking what the passage might illuminate now.

Taking someone's difficulties lightly and intruding on their life are often close neighbours. Once a cost seems trivial to the person making a decision, it can disappear from the decision entirely.

Two extra hours look like a small adjustment on a schedule. For someone who must collect a child, they can require rebuilding an evening around favours that may not be available. Moving an office is one announcement for the person who approves it; for someone else it is an earlier alarm, a longer commute and a later meal every working day.

This does not mean nothing may change whenever change causes inconvenience. It means the cost should enter the discussion before the decision, rather than appearing afterward as evidence that some people are insufficiently cooperative. Their circumstances are part of the matter being decided, not an interruption to it.

Acknowledgment alone is not enough either. “That sounds hard” followed by exactly the same arrangement can become a polite substitute for attention. Say what can change and what cannot, and keep listening to the difference. A person's dwelling is more than an address. It is the set of conditions by which a day is made possible.

## IV. Who gets to say that life is good enough?

The following line, *wu yan qi suo sheng*, and its continuation have a range of interpretations involving oppression, weariness, rejection and satisfaction. The subjects of the clauses are also debated. We should not pretend that translating one word as “enough” settles the passage.

The contemporary question this series draws from it is more specific: do people with authority decide too readily that other people's lives are already satisfactory? That is an application, not the only defensible word-for-word rendering.

A manager says the team's compensation is already competitive, and the discussion closes. A parent says a child has everything, making the child's unspoken wishes sound ungrateful before they have been expressed. A platform points to high average satisfaction, while a particular group's continuing difficulty disappears into the number.

These remarks need not be malicious. The problem is that the speaker does not necessarily live with all the consequences of what counts as enough. His satisfaction cannot substitute for someone else's experience.

The reverse matters too. Respect does not mean endlessly announcing that another person could do better. Someone likes a quiet life; you keep devising the next improvement. “You must not want more” and “you must want more” sound opposed, yet both can prevent the person concerned from speaking.

Returning that voice does not abolish limited resources, negotiation or mutual responsibilities. It simply begins the discussion with actual needs and chosen satisfactions, instead of a reassuring summary prepared from above. Neither imposed contentment nor compulsory self-improvement is the same as respect.

## V. Valuing yourself without lowering someone else

The chapter's distinction between self-knowledge and self-display now becomes practical. You may know something and explain it clearly without turning every encounter into an opportunity to demonstrate how much more you know. Not every unfinished sentence needs your superior version of it.

Self-love, likewise, can involve rest, help, fair treatment and a clear refusal. Self-elevation needs a different kind of support: an audience that keeps acknowledging your higher place. It does not merely ask for room. It needs someone else to occupy less room.

Over the years in Silicon Valley, I have met people whose authority did not depend heavily on job titles or seniority. They could listen to a new colleague through to the end of a sentence, and could also say no when a demand went too far. Treating others as equals and protecting their own boundaries were not rival projects.

But it would be unfair to reverse this observation into a diagnosis: anyone who promotes their work must secretly lack self-respect. Some people have been overlooked for years. Some have to explain a contribution actively for it to be recognised at all. Visibility is not automatically vanity.

The more useful distinction concerns what an action does. Am I describing my work so that responsibility and credit are understood accurately, or making sure nobody else can hold their head up? Am I maintaining a boundary that lets me live, or demanding that everyone organise themselves around my position?

You can take yourself seriously while taking another person seriously too. Self-respect does not have to be assembled by deducting value from someone else.

## VI. Letting go can take practice

The chapter concludes, “discard that and take this”: let go of the compulsions of display and superiority, retain the clarity of self-knowledge and self-love. The direction may sound simple without being instantly available.

Sometimes seeing a cost clearly does reduce its attraction. Once you notice that every attempt to prove yourself clever leaves a conversation exhausted, you may feel less urge to do it next time. Understanding can alter desire, not merely restrain it.

Still, insight does not automatically dissolve habit. You know there is no need to interrupt, and your mouth moves first. You know you need rest, and stopping still feels like losing your place. Reminders, temporary restraint and repeated adjustments are not evidence that the insight was fraudulent. They can be part of learning it.

Try a small act: when describing shared work, give the other contributions their proper names. When someone asks a question, answer what they asked before displaying all the related things you also understand.

If your established habit is to erase yourself, the practice may run in the opposite direction. Say that you contributed too. Say that you cannot carry this arrangement. “Discard that and take this” is not an instruction for everyone to shrink. It asks us to distinguish actions that help a person stand from actions that require someone else to crouch.

## VII. Bring the distinction into the next encounter

When you have decision-making power, ask whose time a decision will consume. Do not wait for someone to become visibly distressed before treating them as a person with circumstances. The fact that a cost is not yours does not make it imaginary.

When someone complains, you need not immediately establish how well you have already done. Let the dissatisfaction be expressed. Recognising that someone has reason to be uncomfortable is not a promise that you can solve everything.

When you need protection, explain the limit rather than your rank: “This is more than I can take on.” Your refusal need not be made valid by first proving the other person inferior. And when things are going well, you may enjoy them. Sharing pleasure and accepting praise are not offences.

The question is whether every encounter becomes someone else's assignment to maintain your self-esteem. There is relief in no longer appointing everybody to that job. You may not become universally liked. You may simply spend less energy defending a pedestal that never needed to be built.

## VIII. A way of standing among others

Across chapters 64–72, our editorial reading route has moved from care near the completion of a task to the ways knowledge can leave room for people; from the low position of rivers and seas to care, restraint and not rushing to be first; from conflict to recognising what we still do not know.

The threads meet here: act without making everything a proof of yourself, help without replacing the person you help, see yourself clearly while allowing someone else a place of their own. This grouping is our way of reading, not a claim that an ancient author designed a nine-part course for us.

[Chapter 70](ch70.html) has already reminded us that understanding and practice remain some distance apart. Self-respect does not require pretending that distance has disappeared. [Chapter 73](ch73.html) will turn to courage in acting and courage in declining to act, as we enter the final nine essays in the received numbering.

Our adopted closing line will speak of the human way: doing without contending. We do not have to become perfect people before beginning to ask what that might mean among imperfect people like ourselves.
