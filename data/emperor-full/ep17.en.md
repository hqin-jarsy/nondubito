# Late Tang and the Five Dynasties — What Must Continue When Power Changes Hands

When the flag above a city changes, who receives the keys to the granary?

The question sounds small beside the fall of a dynasty. For those awaiting food, transporting it or surrendering part of their harvest, it may matter more immediately than the new ruler's title. A proclamation can announce a new order in a day. It cannot rebuild every working relationship in a day.

Between the end of the An Lushan rebellion, the Tang collapse in 907 and the Song foundation in 960, power repeatedly dispersed and regrouped. This was not simply a succession of shorter failures. Some arrangements were destroyed, some persisted, and some were created amid the uncertainty.

## I. An imperial appointment was not always an empty stamp

The military regions of late Tang China were not interchangeable. Some commanders in the northeast exercised extensive control over succession, revenue and troops. Other regions continued to supply the court. Imperial recognition sometimes confirmed an accomplished fact; elsewhere it could help change what happened next.[1]

Tian Hongzheng's adherence to the court in 812 and Wang Chengyuan's departure from Chengde in 820 were real changes. The renewed rupture in 821 also shows why an appointment was only a beginning. A newly appointed commander needed support, supplies and workable relations with people already there. The seal on his commission could not provide those things by itself.

Liu Yan's work on revenue and transport belongs to the same history. Grain did not travel from the southeast to the centre by invoking unity. Routes, warehouses, boats and responsible agents had to connect. The Two-Tax reform of 780 altered the basis of collection, but supplementary demands could still accumulate.[2]

A shared title could therefore have practical value without proving unanimous allegiance or complete control. Its significance depended on who needed recognition from whom, what each could deliver, and what refusal would cost. Tang survival for another century and more was not a broken vehicle coasting. People were repairing, financing and negotiating the connections that kept it moving.

## II. Those closest to the emperor had relationships of their own

Eunuch command of the Shence armies is central to this story. A familiar explanation says that eunuchs, unable to father children, had no families and therefore no interests separate from the emperor. Actual relationships make that explanation untenable: brothers, adopted sons, colleagues and local connections remained important.[3]

The military offices they held also had formal names and responsibilities. These were not merely private favours outside government. The danger lay partly in the offices themselves: men protecting the emperor could control access to him, restrict his actions and influence succession.

The Sweet Dew incident of 835 exposed the consequences brutally. An attempt to remove eunuch power failed and was followed by killings. This was not an abstract balance shifting from one group to another; people lost opportunities to explain themselves, escape or continue living.

There were subsequent changes, including intervention against an accusation in 842 and Qiu Shiliang's retirement in 843. A successful rebuttal did not create permanent security, but it remains different from an unsuccessful one. We should not erase every intermediate possibility because we know the eventual outcome.

No identity makes a person a perfectly dependable instrument. The task is to examine the powers people actually exercise, not to keep searching for a category of people who supposedly cannot acquire purposes of their own.

## III. Huang Chao could take Chang'an without being able to feed it

Huang Chao assumed an imperial title and appointed officials. Saying that he established no administration misses the harder question: could those appointments secure food, cooperation and continuing order?[4]

Capturing a gate changes who controls entry. It does not guarantee tomorrow's provisions. War damages roads and cultivation while the force holding the city must itself be supplied. Hunger does not pause for the announcement of a dynasty.

The *Zizhi tongjian* mentions Huang's unsuccessful examination attempts. That is evidence of a reported experience, not a complete psychological explanation of rebellion. Networks associated with salt trading, recruitment, regional conditions and changing alliances cannot all be replaced by a story of wounded ambition.

The fate of envoys from Wang Xianzhi's side opens another question. Men associated with negotiations for submission were reported instead as captured enemies and killed. To one participant they were a possible route out of war; to another they could become proof of military achievement. Which account reached the court affected what could happen next.

Zhu Wen's movement from Huang's forces into Tang service, his subsequent accumulation of power, and his foundation of Later Liang in 907 were likewise distinct steps. The rebellion did not automatically produce its final successor. Suppressing an external challenge redistributed power inside the Tang order. “Internal” and “external” are useful descriptions only when they do not replace the sequence of decisions.

## IV. Fifty-three years are not an argument by division

The Five Dynasties were five successive regimes at the northern political centre, not five governments that each unified the whole country. Other kingdoms persisted, and Song unification continued after 960.[5]

Dividing fifty-three years by five does not show that each dynasty lasted less time than its predecessor. Nor can that average sensibly be compared with an average derived from overlapping Sixteen Kingdoms. The arithmetic conceals the relationships it claims to explain.

The settlements of 936 and 947, for example, involved different exchanges of territory, military support and authority. Armed replacement of rulers was a recurring danger, but each transfer was not a performance of the same script.

Continuity, moreover, could preserve an injustice. A record for 953 describes charges associated with cattle that households no longer possessed. Here the hardship came not from interrupted administration but from a demand continuing after its basis had disappeared.[6]

We need to ask both what a change of government destroyed and what it failed to stop. A stable demand can be intolerable; an administrative change can sometimes provide relief. Neither condition is visible in the list of emperors alone.

## V. Civil government did not suddenly begin in 960

The project to print the Nine Classics passed through several changes of dynasty and reached completion in 953. Shu had a separate history of printing. Feng Dao's documentary and administrative work belongs to this landscape, however difficult his successive loyalties may be to judge.[6]

Efforts to distinguish local civil business from military responsibilities also preceded the Song. Regulations under Guo Wei addressed disputes and collection in particular regions. Recruitment rules attempted to organize recommendation, examination and the prevention of improper assistance.[7]

These arrangements do not prove universal success. A printed book cannot compensate for people killed in war, and the survival of an office cannot justify a massacre. But the claim that the period built nothing makes it impossible to see what later rulers inherited.

Continuity can be modest and exacting: someone preserves a document, finishes a set of printing blocks, or carries a dispute forward rather than forcing the complainant to begin again. Civilization is not transmitted only through celebrated declarations. Sometimes it depends on people continuing a task after the reign name at the top of the page has changed.

## VI. Chai Rong's reforms still needed objections

Chai Rong took the Later Zhou throne in 954 and reorganized his forces. Measures concerning abandoned land, monasteries, copper and law followed. Wang Pu's strategic advice connected personnel, trust, resources, agricultural work and timing. It was more than a fixed instruction to attack south before north.[8]

Yet commitment to reform did not make every decision sound. In 957 an order addressed officials who delayed litigation until agricultural restrictions supplied a reason to suspend it. Urgent cases of encroachment were not to be postponed merely because the calendar offered an excuse. The rule recognized that delay could become a weapon against the vulnerable.

That same year, Fu Lingguang was executed over delayed preparation of military clothing. Senior ministers attempted to intervene; the emperor withdrew, and the execution proceeded.[9]

These episodes belong together. A ruler making legal redress more usable in one setting could close the opportunity for explanation in another. Agreement with his larger programme does not remove the need to question a particular act.

Chai Rong's northern expedition made limited gains in 959 before illness and death intervened. We do not know what ten more years would have produced. We do know that troops, personnel and arrangements survived him. Zhao Kuangyin inherited resources and made further choices; later achievements belong neither wholly to the successor nor wholly to a plan supposedly completed in advance.

## VII. One banquet cannot contain the whole Song settlement

Zhao Kuangyin's elevation at Chenqiao in 960 was followed by his return to Kaifeng and the transfer of the throne. It was not universally accepted or free of deaths. Li Yun and Li Chongjin resisted during the founding year.[10]

The famous story of surrendering military power over wine makes a real problem intelligible. If officers could place the yellow robe on Zhao, why could another group not do the same to one of his commanders? Exchanging commands for wealth offered a different approach from killing the men who held them.

But the dialogue is not a transcript, and the changes cannot all be put on one evening. Central command changes in 961 must be distinguished from regional changes in 970. Li Tao explained how he combined accounts in his long chronicle; Shi Shouxin's biography preserves a different dating.[11]

Shi also participated in the campaign of 979. His wealth and the unpaid labour used in building a monastery complicate the apparent bargain. The emperor and commander might settle their relationship while costs fell on people who had no seat at the table.

The Bureau of Military Affairs was not an exclusively civilian institution from its beginning; careers such as Cao Bin's resist that simple division. Nor did the later scale of examination recruitment appear fully formed in 961, when eleven *jinshi* were selected.[12]

The achievement of avoiding a purge should be recognized. It need not be inflated into the permanent elimination of military risk.

## VIII. Preventing a coup does not teach an army to fight

Rotation and divided command can limit private control while creating difficulties of coordination. Their effects need to be examined in particular forces and campaigns. They do not allow us to deduce a century of irreversible weakness from a single preference for civil government.

The conquest of Southern Han in 971, the defeat of Northern Han in 979, and the Song reverse at Gaoliang River later that year were different outcomes. Around 1040, training methods were still being reconsidered; Han Qi questioned whether exercises measured what mattered in battle. People were attempting to alter military capability, not merely enacting a destiny fixed in 960.[13]

The negotiations at Chanyuan in 1004–1005 likewise involved a military situation, withdrawal, border conduct and annual transfers of silver and silk. For residents, peace also meant the possible end of emergency transport demands, return from displacement and renewed cultivation. “Buying safety” does not capture the whole transaction.[14]

From late Tang to early Song, duties were continually redistributed: fighting, provisioning, authorizing action and reporting that an order could no longer be carried out. No final settlement removed the need to revisit those relationships.

The flag is easy to see. A granary key, an unresolved petition and unpaid wages are less conspicuous. They may tell us more about whether life has improved. A change of ruler should mean more than making the same people pay again.
