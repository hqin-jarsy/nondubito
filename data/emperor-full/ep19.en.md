# Mongols and Yuan — Whom an Institution Connects, Whom It Excludes

A damaged banknote and a devalued banknote present different problems. The first needs recognition and replacement. The second may be perfectly intact while no longer buying the same bag of grain.

Yuan government made arrangements for exchanging damaged notes, maintaining reserves and relating new issues to old ones. These provisions do not establish reliable implementation. They do require a better question than whether the dynasty possessed institutions at all: how did those institutions work, who could use them, and who carried their costs?[1]

Acknowledging organization does not excuse conquest. If discrimination and extraction operated through rules, understanding the rules makes criticism more exact. Saying that there was only violence can conceal the arrangements through which domination continued in ordinary life.

## I. Conquest itself required organization

Early Mongol units reorganized relationships among people, commanders, service and supply. Their numerical names are not a verified census, and reorganization did not erase kinship.[2]

Succession was not simply awarded to whoever had won the latest battle. The account of Ögödei's accession in 1229 invokes both his father's intention and an assembly of princes. Military achievement mattered alongside descent, negotiation and recognition. This was neither popular electoral government nor an uncomplicated performance score.

Distribution also extended beyond one-off booty. Arrangements concerning silk in 1236 assigned recurring revenues. Once conquest had brought people and territory under authority, obtaining annual returns required registration, collection, delivery and accommodation among claimants.[3]

The image of an empire that must keep expanding or exhaust its fuel is memorable. It is not sufficient explanation. Spoils, regular income, landed claims and political promises are different relationships and need separate examination.

Mongol power was not a natural property of an entire people on horseback. People organized movement, reward and succession. Its subsequent difficulties cannot be understood as the sudden encounter of organization-free nomads with an entirely unfamiliar settled world.

## II. Fields needed governing before the last battle ended

The struggle for Xiangyang and Fancheng involved ships, siege techniques, supplies, local military knowledge and negotiation. Cooperation among Aju, Liu Zheng, Arigh Khaya and others cannot be reduced to timeless ethnic abilities or deficiencies.[4]

The adoption of the Yuan name in 1271 and the fall of the Southern Song in 1279 were separate events. Agricultural administration did not wait for the final victory. Government was already making continuing demands on areas under its control while the frontiers were moving.[5]

Agricultural provisions addressed local communities, mutual assistance, irrigation and differences of terrain. Changes to inspection in 1291 suggest a recognizable difficulty: visits intended to support cultivation could themselves impose burdens. A prescription does not prove good administration everywhere. It is still evidence that administration existed.

Examinations require a similar distinction among proposals, approval and actual sessions. Several discussions under Khubilai did not amount to straightforward continuation of the Song system. The decision of 1313 and the examinations of 1315 belong to a later stage.[6]

Institutions were not an outer shell installed once conquest was complete. They took shape during war, recruited existing people and methods, and changed. Criticism needs to enter those arrangements rather than dismiss them in advance as not really institutional.

## III. Shared books did not provide an equal entrance

Examination rules make inequality concrete. Mongol and *semu* candidates faced different sequences and requirements from *hanren* and *nanren* candidates. Common engagement with classical texts did not establish an identical threshold for office.[6]

Quotas of twenty-five for each of four categories also did not give individuals equal chances. The populations and candidate pools differed. A prescribed total of one hundred was not the outcome of every session; the record for 1315 gives fifty-six successful candidates. Rules and results must not be exchanged for one another.

Criminal provisions likewise made distinctions. Rules concerning tattooing for theft included exemptions for Mongols and women, while classifying Jurchens differently in that particular context. Rank and kinship also affected legal treatment. “Four classes” cannot describe every dimension of status, but qualifications to that shorthand do not make inequality disappear.[7]

The assertion that southerners could never hold anything above a deputy position is too absolute. Cheng Jufu's disputed appointment and Zhao Mengfu's later Hanlin office require a more precise account. An exceptional appointment can coexist with substantial restrictions; the exception does not make a route equally available to everyone.[8]

The more troubling possibility is not always that no route exists. It is that a real route sorts people unequally before their abilities are considered. What additional performance is demanded from one person to obtain what another can reach more easily?

People of different backgrounds also worked together, argued and supported one another. We cannot assign them a uniform inner life of either wholehearted acceptance or concealed rebellion. Institutions constrained their circumstances without exhausting their choices.

## IV. The existence of an office does not establish access to it

Yuan provincial administrations had recorded responsibilities and staffing arrangements. Their territories and powers changed; the Jianghuai reorganization in 1284–1285 is one example. Negotiation between centre and region does not imply the complete absence of rules.[9]

Paper currency involved distinct operations: issue, conversion, reserves, replacement and destruction of withdrawn notes. Later depreciation cannot establish that no monetary discipline ever existed. Conversely, prescribing a reserve cannot establish that a person needing exchange could obtain it.[1]

Legal compilations, including the *Zhiyuan xinge* and *Da Yuan tongzhi*, also make an institutional vacuum impossible to sustain as a description. The harder questions concern communication, jurisdiction, status and conflicts between procedures. Which office would hear a case, and what could someone do when two authorities disagreed?[10]

Religious plurality was not merely indifference. Promotion of an imperial script in 1269 was an intervention in communication. The order of 1281 to destroy Daoist books apart from the *Daodejing* shows active religious discrimination. Neither coexistence nor prohibition warrants an absolute claim: plurality was not unrestricted freedom, and an order did not ensure the destruction of every text or practice.[11]

An order can connect distant places while placing additional barriers before particular people. The test is not whether it resembles a tidy institutional model. It is what happens when someone needs to make use of it.

## V. Fast movement depended on people who stayed to provide

Relay stations connected long distances. Following the messenger, we easily overlook the households supplying horses, grain, boats and labour. An account in 1279 of relay families pledging or selling children, followed by an order to send officials to address their situation, brings the network back to the scale of a household.[12]

Orders concerning military households similarly addressed supplementary burdens and classifications that no longer matched their resources. Measures in 1282 and 1285 show that an enduring entry in a register could describe a life that no longer existed. Relief ordered was a real administrative act; relief received and maintained was another matter.

Land investigation beginning in 1314 aimed to establish holdings but produced reports of false declarations and coercion, followed by limitations in 1315. A real need for information did not excuse the way it was gathered.[13]

A shipbuilding report in 1281 gives an especially concrete example. Of two hundred vessels demanded, fifty had been completed; the burden on the population was reported and the work ordered stopped. This does not certify good government throughout the dynasty. It contradicts the claim that greater coercion was its only possible response.[14]

Why could a difficulty become grounds for stopping in one case but remain something a household had to absorb in another? The empire's connections depended on the people providing them. Entering a household in two administrative circuits did not give its members two bodies.

## VI. The ending was not a stock running out

Succession struggles damaged cooperation, but it is wrong to say that no ruler after Khubilai had time for sustained government. The last Yuan emperor at Dadu ruled there from 1333 to 1368. His reign included the restoration of examinations and consequential decisions about finance and public works.[15]

In the currency debate of 1350, Lü Sicheng objected and the policy proceeded. Jia Lu's river work in 1351 involved inspection, alternative proposals, genuine flood damage and extensive mobilization. The work could address a real danger while imposing serious costs. Recognizing the costs does not require pretending the river needed no attention.[16]

The movements associated with Han Shantong and Liu Futong in 1351, Zhu Yuanzhang's entry into Guo Zixing's forces in 1352, and Toghto's dismissal during campaigning in 1354 were distinct developments. Disaster, collection, organization, military command and court decisions interacted. “The cavalry grew soft and coercion ran out” is not an adequate substitute.[17]

Nor did the loss of Dadu in 1368 end every Mongol political formation on the same day. An ending needs a defined political and geographical scope.

Knowing the outcome does not mean a countdown was built into the dynasty at its foundation. Participants confronted situations that had not yet been resolved. Keeping their alternatives visible also keeps their responsibility visible.

## VII. Survival does not make a legacy just

Yuan provincial administration influenced later government, but the Ming changed offices, responsibilities and some boundaries. It was not the unchanged survival of one provincial system. Movement, translation, intermarriage and exchange also had lasting effects; the formation of present-day communities cannot be reduced to a single episode of mixture under conquest.[18]

The Ming foundation should not be projected backward as the common programme of every earlier rebel. The well-known northern-expedition proclamation belongs to 1367, not to all the Red Turban movements of 1351. It drew exclusions and offered conditional acceptance; it was not a declaration of modern universal rights.

Yuan discrimination and coercion do not become objectionable only because the dynasty fell. An unjust arrangement can last a long time and remain unjust. Useful administrative inheritances do not compensate those who suffered under them.

This distinction matters for the series as a whole. People should not be reduced to tax resources, military quotas or assigned statuses because they have lives of their own—not merely because mistreating them might eventually be bad for the ruler.

Acknowledging Yuan institutions lets the question become more exact. An institution is not judged only by its presence or longevity. It may organize many people together and still need to answer: can those people describe what the arrangement costs them, and obtain a chance to change it?
