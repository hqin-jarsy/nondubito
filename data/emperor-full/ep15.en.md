# Sui — The Time an Order Cannot Save

Grain in a storehouse is not yet a meal for a hungry person.

The History of Sui describes famine relief from the Yongfeng granary. Some people had to travel hundreds of li to reach it. They then faced demands for money and time spent waiting. Grain had been authorized for distribution. Lives could still run out before it reached them.[1]

One episode is not the whole dynasty. It does expose a question easily hidden behind great projects: after a plan has resources and an official order, how much work remains before it reaches a person?

Sui was founded in 581, conquered Chen in 589, and lost Emperor Yang in 618. Comparison with Qin can illuminate construction, coercion, and inheritance. Selecting different starting points to manufacture equal lifespans does not explain either dynasty. Nor does later use of an institution justify every burden originally imposed to create it.

## I. Naming the offices did not coordinate all their work

Under Yang Jian, Emperor Wen, offices including the Neishi, Menxia, and Shangshu divided work on documents and administration. The names of the subordinate departments also changed over time. Drafting, reviewing, and implementing could be separated without becoming three independent branches of government.[2]

Making scrutiny an official duty mattered. An objection need not depend entirely on someone's spontaneous courage. Yet the reviewer still faced the ruler, and a formal responsibility did not automatically protect its holder. Having authority to return a document and being able to exercise it in a particular confrontation were different conditions.

Recruitment likewise cannot be reduced to “no pedigree, no recommendation, only the examination paper.” Recommendations in 587 and 598, and the ten categories of 607, retained sponsors and eligibility requirements. The Tongdian attributes the jinshi category to Emperor Yang's reign. Recommendation, family privileges, examinations, and actual appointment did not suddenly become one procedure.[3]

A conversation between Niu Hong and Liu Xuan about appointments and document checking raises a practical complication: fewer administrative layers could coexist with more repeated verification. Removing an office's sign did not remove the problems its occupants had handled.

Legal work in 581 and 583 preceded several of the later recruitment measures. Wen's reforms cannot be arranged into a tidy sequence in which one was allowed to settle before the next began. The Kaihuang code organized and reduced provisions and altered punishments. A shorter code did not mean that everyone understood it, or that status differences had disappeared.[4]

Land, labor obligations, taxes, and registers made people more administratively legible. In 590, military personnel's local registration and land arrangements changed while military command continued.[5] A person counted as both cultivator and soldier still needed a household capable of reconciling cultivation, service, and collection. The two labels did not perform that work for him.

## II. Different projects could demand the same household

Large construction did not begin with Emperor Yang. Wen's reign included the Guangtong Canal in 584 and work on the Renshou Palace beginning in 593. Assigning all restraint to the father and all building to the son makes both reigns less intelligible.[6]

From 605, Yang's projects included Luoyang, the Tongji Canal, ships, journeys, and subsequent canal works. Their demands extended beyond laborers at a worksite. Timber, transport, boat crews, and provisions had to arrive; relatives remaining at home had to take over unfinished tasks. A register could count those taken away without counting every additional hour worked by those left behind.

A canal could later shorten a journey. During construction, crops could not be ordered to mature early, nor could a sick person recover faster because a requisition had a grand purpose. Dividing projects among offices did not prevent them from drawing on the same village or family.

The wars against Goguryeo also require separate accounts. Wen's expedition in 598 and Yang's in 612, 613, and 614 differed. In 612, the requirement to report offers of surrender affected commanders' room to act. In 613, Yang Xuangan's rebellion created a crisis in the rear. In 614, negotiations involving an offer of submission accompanied withdrawal. These were not three identical defeats.[7]

Goguryeo was not simply a piece of territory awaiting Sui's assessment of its value. It had a state, inhabitants, and armed forces of its own. The Sui court's strategic reasoning could not answer for those people, or exempt its war aims from examination.

Yang Xuangan also occupied a specific position before rebelling in 613: he supervised transport at Liyang and could use relationships, information, and supplies to mobilize.[8] Discontent does not organize itself into an army. Capacity acquired through an official assignment can be turned against the authority that made the assignment.

## III. Slower is not an answer to every question

Several demands arriving together can exceed what a household can manage. That insight remains valuable. “All the projects were right; only the speed was wrong” nevertheless grants every project an exemption from further scrutiny.

Some work can be delayed. Some should be reduced. Some goals need to be reconsidered. Who receives the benefits, who carries the risk, and who may refuse are related to timing without being reducible to it.

Return to Yongfeng. Grain had not all vanished, and no relief order was wholly absent. Obstacles arose between authorization and receipt: distance, waiting, and the extraction of payments. Another road or a larger reserve might improve some conditions without ensuring that the person at the gate received food.[1]

People's capacity is not a fixed axle identical across reigns and households. A family with reserves, care, and access to an appeal experiences a demand differently from one already indebted, short of workers, or struck by disaster. Policy can itself change that capacity.

Nor was every plan under Yang immune to alteration. Accounts of a proposed ceremonial hall and of vehicles include abandoned plans and reductions in quantity. But canceling one project and replacing it with another requisition does not demonstrate a reduction in total burdens.[9]

A later Tang example makes time concrete. In 631, the crown prince's coming-of-age ceremony was moved from the second to the tenth month of the contemporary calendar following consideration of agricultural work. That is evidence about one decision, not a certificate of benevolence for the whole dynasty.[10] It does show that “giving people time” could mean altering a real schedule.

## IV. Tang did not inherit an untouched machine

Li Yuan rose in 617 and assumed the imperial title in 618. Rival regimes did not all disappear then; Liang Shidu survived until 628. Revolt, dynastic foundation, and the completion of major conquest were separate events.[11]

In 621, Fang Xuanling was instructed to collect Sui maps and documents. The action matters. Records could reduce the work of learning about a territory, but someone still had to find, preserve, interpret, and use them. The survival of particular collections did not mean that every archive remained intact.

Legal continuity involved initial brief rules, additions, the Wude code of 624, and further revision. Disputes over replacement penalties also show that an intention to mitigate punishment did not make every proposed substitute reasonable. Inheritance was an activity of selection and rewriting, not the passive receipt of a finished device.[12]

Recruitment continued to involve schools, local presentation, assessment, and appointment. Details developed later in Tang cannot all be packed into Sui's beginning and then cited to show that Tang needed to do nothing.

Accounts from 627 and 628 of famine, assistance, and the recovery of family members sold in hardship further distinguish administrative continuity from household recovery.[11] A register might survive while the people named in it had become separated.

Han, likewise, did not wait seventy years before using anything Qin had left. Both transitions are worth comparing. Artificially making one prolonged and costly and the other immediate and lossless cannot establish a theory of superior successors.

## V. A restrained father and a hurried son are not enough

Wen's reign contained punishments outside the stated law as well as responses to objections. Implements for beating could be removed and later return. Yang's Daye code of 607 included mitigations and changes, while later years brought exceptions and reprisals after rebellion.[4]

This does not make the rulers identical. It asks us to locate the differences in actions. Who accepted which objection? When did a ruler depart from his published rule? Those questions explain more than giving one man the personality of patience and the other the personality of haste.

Officials and skilled workers could also serve across reigns. Knowledge held by men such as Yan Pi did not disappear at succession. Assignments, resources, scale, and deadlines changed. The same competence could be put to uses with very different consequences for other people.[9]

Innovation and maintenance were not incompatible human types. A government could revise a rule in the morning and address a failure under an existing rule that afternoon. It needed arrangements for checking costs, proposing a stop, and avoiding the temptation to replace an abandoned project with an even larger demonstration of resolve.

A successor who did not experience earlier hardship might underestimate the cost of accumulated resources. That remains an interpretation requiring evidence, not a complete biography or an automatic law of declining ability and patience with every generation.

## VI. Every inheritance brought unfinished questions

Administrative divisions left possibilities for review, along with questions about whether information could pass and reviewers could perform their duties safely.

Examinations and recommendations left entrances, along with questions about who could learn, qualify, and obtain an appointment. An exam could not answer for everyone unable to reach it.

Codes left more explicit provisions, along with questions about their application to powerful people, authorization of exceptions, and correction when enforcement caused harm.

Land, levies, and storage left calculable arrangements, along with the distance between recorded grain and a meal. The community-granary proposal of 585 was followed by changes concerning storage and contributions in 595 and 596. Reserves needed maintenance; the rules themselves continued changing.[13]

Military organization left mobilizing capacity, along with problems of supply and work transferred to relatives at home. A man who farmed in peace and served in war did not thereby make military service costless to others. Canals likewise needed maintenance, personnel, and decisions about which cargo moved first. They were not gifts requiring no care after completion.

These legacies outlasted Sui. They did not constitute one unchanged answer operating for a thousand years. Later people could use them partly because later people kept altering them.

The next essay follows advice, appointment, supply, and military organization in early Tang. Wu Zetian's later entry into the highest position of rule also requires its own account; the existence of examinations does not already explain it. Neither a golden age nor the next disaster should be written in advance and then filled with appropriate characters.

Sui's warning is finally simple. Something worth doing still requires an account of its costs. Possessing resources does not mean that those resources have reached the people who need them.

A canal can shorten the journey of grain. Whether a hungry person receives it in time remains work for the living.
