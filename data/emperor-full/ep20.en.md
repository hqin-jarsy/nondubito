# Early Ming — When a Life Changes but the Register Does Not

A grandfather served as a soldier. His son replaced him, earned promotion, and passed his military office to the next generation. The family had continued the service required of it.

Yet another register still listed the grandfather as a deserter. Repeated demands arrived for a replacement.

The *History of Ming* records Qian Xing reporting this problem in 1425. The failure was not three generations refusing an obligation. It was information that had failed to follow an obligation already fulfilled. In one set of records the family was serving; in another it still owed a man.[1]

This is a more useful entrance into early Ming government than diagnosing its founder's personality. Zhu Yuanzhang's poverty matters, but it cannot explain every subsequent order. The specific question is what happens when government fixes a person's place and that person's life changes. Who can amend the entry?

## I. Abolishing an office does not abolish its work

The removal of the chief ministership in 1380 changed relations between the emperor and central government. Ministries dealt more directly with the sovereign, but Zhu did not personally receive, investigate and execute every item of business. Agencies of communication, review and justice continued to work.[2]

Decision and coordination became more concentrated. When several departments touched the same matter, someone still had to establish priorities. Conflicting reports required resolution. Removing an office could not remove the needs it had addressed.

Adaptations began during Zhu's own reign, not only after his death. Assistants were appointed in 1380 and then discontinued; grand secretaries were established in 1382. These were not simply chief ministers under another name. They nevertheless show that assistance and coordination still needed personnel. A provision in 1393 permitting grain to be lent before a report was submitted also complicates the image of every action awaiting prior personal authorization.[3]

Under Yongle and later rulers, the role of secretaries in confidential government developed further, eventually including more elaborate preparation of proposed responses. Drafting a recommendation, authorizing it and commanding execution remained different powers. Calling the early secretaries restored chief ministers conceals that distinction.

What proved persistent was not a title but a set of tasks. Zhu might work intensely and successors might devise other arrangements. Imperial business still had to pass through human attention, with its needs for delegation, transmission and timely action.

## II. A family can inherit an error along with an obligation

The garrison system relied on continuing obligations attached to military households. Designated servicemen, supporting households and replacement arrangements need to be distinguished: not every son was simultaneously serving for life. Yet military registration imposed a serious constraint on descendants, before they could choose how to live.[4]

Military farming was not self-financing merely because land was assigned. Oxen, seed, tools, food, irrigation and transport required provision. Campaigning could remove labour from cultivation. An expense absent from one treasury account might be borne elsewhere.

Qian Xing's case exposes a further cost: proving that one has already complied. Families without an office or an effective route upward could have greater difficulty correcting a similar error.

Ming provisions did make distinctions concerning single adult males, replacements, elderly parents, mistaken demands and removal from registers. There were also attempts to prevent recruitment from spreading indiscriminately through people sharing a surname or kin connection. Such measures show that particular abuses could be recognized. They do not establish timely redress for every household.[1]

Desertion, appropriation, vacancies and supply problems subsequently accumulated, with varied responses. When Qi Jiguang recruited and trained troops in the sixteenth century, he did so through official appointments and state military arrangements. He was not an independent warlord operating a private army outside government.[5]

The history is therefore more complicated than an institutional army collapsing and private force taking its place. Could registers and requirements change when service and household resources changed? Or did leaving become the only way some people could make the official description encounter their actual lives?

## III. A maritime prohibition must specify whom it prohibits

Coastal attacks, the maritime presence of former rivals and defensive requirements shaped early Ming policy. Tang He's coastal defence work required local people, materials and provision. Protecting a coast meant assigning work, not merely drawing a line.[6]

Restrictions on private voyages did not end every form of overseas movement or exchange. Accounts concerning a Japanese mission in 1403 describe permitted trading through a recognized channel. Government could close one route while authorizing another.

For coastal residents the distinction mattered. Who could obtain permission? Who needed an intermediary? What could be traded, and where could a dispute be heard? The presence or absence of ships on a map does not answer these questions.

By the sixteenth century, merchants, protectors, disputed payments and armed groups were connected in further ways, with participants of different backgrounds. Neither “all Japanese pirates” nor “all Chinese merchants forced into piracy” is an adequate explanation. A prohibition could alter risks and rewards without being the sole cause of every participant's violence.

A restriction was not ineffective simply because it was broken. It could determine who traded legally, who paid extra and who feared asking for redress. The question is not only whether the sea was closed, but what relationships people had to enter in order to continue living beside it.

## IV. Accurate records also require people who can challenge them

The Yellow Registers and Fish-Scale Registers recorded different things. The former concerned households and obligations; the latter concerned land, its configuration, ownership and associated assessment. Household rotation and exceptions under the *lijia* arrangements cannot be combined with grain-head responsibilities into one uniform hierarchy lasting unchanged across the empire.[7]

Records mattered. Without them officials might make arbitrary demands, while powerful holders shifted burdens onto others. But if the record became the only admissible reality, sales, migration, deaths and altered resources could become administratively invisible. Maintaining a register meant more than preserving paper. It required the possibility of correction.

The *Grand Pronouncements*, or *Dagao*, intervened through cases, punishments and admonitions. Its demands reached ordinary inhabitants as well as officials. Selections in 1397 and subsequent uses also resist the idea of a single unchanged textbook.

The case of Shi Lingzhi makes the intrusion concrete. She had been betrothed at three; that intended husband died young. She later married another man and had three children. The emperor accused the minister Wang Shi of pursuing the childhood arrangement and preparing to summon its intermediaries. This is an imperial accusation, not a complete independent dossier. It nevertheless poses a question: when an inquiry becomes ever more detailed, how many additional lives does it needlessly draw in?[8]

Appeal upward had a similar double edge. The complaint drum allowed certain matters obstructed below to receive attention, while later restrictions constrained bypassing ordinary channels. A successful petition did not guarantee the safety of everyone considering one. Han Yike had objections accepted and also experienced imprisonment; the ability to speak was not a permanent on-off switch.[9]

The special judicial powers associated with the Embroidered Uniform Guard were restricted and restored at different stages. An organization's existence and its possession of a particular power must be distinguished. Retaining only the frightening name obscures the history of what it could do.[10]

An emperor's personal discovery of injustice might save someone. A durable arrangement needed an answer for when he did not notice. Could a person obtain review without staking everything on being heard at the very top?

## V. Relatives were not outside political relationships

The Hu Weiyong affair, the Lan Yu case of 1393 and their extensive repercussions exposed the dangers faced by meritorious founders. They do not support the claim that every founding commander was killed: Tang He lived until 1395. Restrictions on eunuchs and their later expanded assignments also belong to different stages.[11]

Imperial princes held titles and revenues; some commanded guards and performed military tasks. Kinship supplied special qualifications, not a guarantee against conflict. Difficulties involving princes already required attention while Zhu Yuanzhang lived. Unity did not suddenly turn into disagreement only after his death.

Jianwen's measures against princes and Zhu Di's uprising led to war from 1399 to 1402. This was a conflict between nephew and uncle, involving succession, forces, timing and fears for survival. Saying that the young emperor chose the right direction but moved too fast implies more certainty about alternatives than the evidence warrants.[12]

Zhu Di restricted some princely military resources after victory, but not every princely guard vanished at once. Zhu Chenhao's rebellion in 1519 prevents an assertion that such danger had been permanently eliminated.[13]

Princely incomes posed another set of questions. Allowances were graded and adjusted during Hongwu's reign, including reductions in 1395. A report in 1562 described hardship within the imperial clan. What government promised, what recipients obtained and what local populations paid were different amounts.[14]

Calling the whole group useless parasites obscures those differences. It also repeats a premise this series should resist: that a person merits consideration only by performing an assigned function. Political privilege needs scrutiny. The concrete possibilities for people to earn a living also deserve scrutiny.

## VI. Assigning a place does not settle a life

Early Ming history need not become a psychological portrait of a man determined to control everything. Four practical questions explain more.

When an office disappears, who inherits its tasks? When a register persists, who verifies that it still describes reality? When an arrangement saves money, who receives the burden? And when an individual's energy, judgment or patience fails, is there a procedure that does not depend entirely on his personal favour?

These questions also prevent an opposite simplification: that all the founder's arrangements worked equally well and then emptied out together within a century. Persistence, revision, violation and restoration did not follow one timetable. Different regions and households experienced different consequences.

Violence can temporarily silence opposition. That does not make it the cheapest method of government. Deaths, family losses and decisions distorted by fear remain costs even when they never appear in a treasury ledger.

Return to Qian Xing. An outdated line concerning his grandfather generated repeated demands upon a family. Correcting it was not forgiving a refusal to serve. It was acknowledging that life had already fulfilled what the register still declared outstanding.

A lasting institution cannot merely be good at placing people in categories. It needs to let them demonstrate that a category is wrong. People move, age, die and fulfil responsibilities in unexpected ways. Records can help us remember them. They should not become a reason to stop looking at them.
