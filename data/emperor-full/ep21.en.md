# The Later Ming — Waiting for an Answer

In 1617, senior officials brought the emperor a problem concerning prisoners. Posts in the security office were vacant. People had remained in custody so long that many had died. The official history records no reply.

Nearby, the same account records an order to relieve famine in Jiangxi.[1]

That juxtaposition is more disturbing than the familiar picture of a government that simply stopped working. Orders could still leave the palace. Some requests received attention. Others did not. For the prisoners, the important distinction was not between a functioning state and a failed one. It was between an answer in time and an answer that would never reach them.

The later Ming invites us to follow this distance: between an administration doing many things and a particular person waiting for the thing it alone could authorize.

## I. Abolishing an Office Does Not Abolish Its Work

The founding Ming emperor abolished the chancellorship in 1380. The central ministries now answered more directly to him. Coordination, however, survived the disappearance of the coordinator. Conflicting recommendations still had to be reconciled; several urgent matters could still arrive together. Grand secretaries were appointed in 1382, and their involvement in government developed further under subsequent emperors.[2]

The emerging Grand Secretariat was neither the old chancellorship under a new name nor a collection of private assistants without official duties. Preparing proposed responses to memorials became a defined part of its work. A secretary's substantive rank, additional appointments and practical influence also need to be distinguished.

Within the palace, authorization, the writing of responses in red and the onward transmission of documents involved further people. The Directorate of Ceremonial was not merely stamping a conclusion already settled elsewhere. Access and timing could matter as much as a formal job description.

The problem was therefore not that one man literally performed every administrative task. Many people worked, but certain decisions had to pass through a narrow opening. If that opening stayed shut, an official farther down the chain might know what needed doing without possessing the authority to do it.

An institutional need is not an institution. It does not recruit staff, confer authority or settle a dispute by itself. The Ming found ways to share the work; those ways left particular dependencies in place.

## II. Zhang Juzheng: Getting Things Done, and Hearing Objections

Zhang Juzheng made a deceptively simple question harder to avoid: had the assigned business actually been completed? His system of administrative assessment set deadlines according to urgency and scale and followed delays through successive levels. Official evaluation already existed. What changed was the pressure applied to the progress of particular tasks.[3]

This was a real intervention in the habits of government. Yet a completed report is not identical to a completed public service. A deadline can help a waiting person; it can also encourage an office to make the paperwork look finished. Reform has to remain interested in the difference.

The Single Whip reforms likewise brought earlier practices into a wider arrangement. They did not introduce silver into official taxation for the first time. The sources record specified grain-tax commutations in 1436 and local experiments before the broader extension of 1581. Nor did every tax and obligation become uniform overnight.[4]

Consolidating demands could make them more intelligible. It did not eliminate the taxpayer's practical problem. A household harvesting grain still needed a way to obtain silver. Sale, exchange and changing prices stood between the tidier assessment and the payment.

Land surveys could expose concealed holdings and reduce the burden on people paying for someone else's omission. The same historical account also records manipulation of the measuring standard to inflate assessed acreage. More land in the register was not, by itself, more fairness. Someone had to ask how the figure had grown and who would pay for it.

Zhang's position rested on cooperation within the palace and officialdom, not on competence alone. When his father died in 1577, he was retained in office rather than withdrawing for the customary mourning period. Several critics were punished with court beatings. Their objections cannot all be dismissed as the resentment of corrupt men: they concerned whether a norm imposed on others could be suspended for an indispensable minister.[3]

His death in 1582 did not produce one instantaneous reversal. Honors came first, then their withdrawal, then confiscation in 1584. Nor did every policy disappear with his reputation. Important fiscal arrangements continued.

The question is more useful when stated precisely: which changes had acquired a life beyond their sponsor, and which still depended on his access to power? Reform and the reformer's personal fate can diverge. That divergence is part of what needs explaining.

## III. Wanli Did Make Decisions

The Wanli emperor's prolonged withdrawal from regular audiences created serious delays. But saying that he conducted no government for thirty years makes those delays harder, not easier, to understand.

Military decisions concerning Ningxia, Korea and Bozhou continued. Mining and tax commissioners were dispatched. Zhu Changluo was finally designated heir in 1601. During the Liaodong crisis there were appointments, maritime supply arrangements and additional levies.[5]

Some doors opened while others stayed closed. Why could a new demand be authorized while a request to correct its effects remained unanswered? How could a tax increase be ordered while requests to release funds still waited? Raising an assessment and supplying an army were connected tasks, not the same act.

A delayed succession decision eventually made was still a delayed decision. Its eventual arrival did not undo years of uncertainty. The prisoners of 1617 make the point more painfully. We do not possess their complete individual files, and should not invent their biographies. But the record of deaths during prolonged confinement establishes that silence was not an empty interval.[1]

Routine administration could continue through familiar channels. That does not show that government had become unnecessary or that institutions could indefinitely run themselves. A routine can carry forward an established answer. It cannot decide on its own that the answer must change.

## IV. More Than Factional Quarrels

The Donglin figures are often reduced to moralists who talked while the country failed. Once they are placed opposite the eunuchs, it is easy to conclude that both sides merely consumed the state's energies. The symmetry is attractive and misleading.

Gu Xiancheng challenged particular claims associated with the philosophy of mind; Donglin cannot simply be described as a uniform radical extension of Wang Yangming. Zuo Guangdou proposed irrigation and agricultural measures in the north. His concerns included water, land and provisions, not only the allocation of moral prestige.[6]

Those proposals faced practical limits. Authorization did not remove problems of terrain, flooding, resources or implementation. But a proposal's limitations are not proof that its author never attempted useful work.

The detention, torture and deaths of Yang Lian, Zuo Guangdou and others in 1625 also resist a symmetrical account of factional conflict. Exercising coercive power and suffering under it are not interchangeable contributions to an unfortunate atmosphere.

Personnel instability under Chongzhen deserves attention without an invented statistic. Several grand secretaries could serve concurrently. Dividing all appointments by the number of years does not reveal the average tenure of a succession of chief ministers. A faulty denominator can make an inaccurate story look exceptionally precise.

The practical question is whether disagreement can still affect a decision. Does an objection remain something to consider, or does the ability to punish its author become the final answer?

## V. Collapse Was Not a Bill with a Predetermined Due Date

Fiscal and military pressures reinforced one another in the last decades of Ming rule. The Liaodong surcharge began in 1618; levies for suppressing rebellions and strengthening troops followed in 1637 and 1639. They had different purposes and histories. An assessed amount, a collected amount and money delivered to soldiers were three different quantities.[7]

A household unable to pay might already have lost its crop. An unpaid army might impose further demands on the locality. Flight, disrupted production, warfare and failed collection could intensify each other. That does not mean every region followed one identical course.

Nor were recruited armies simply private possessions outside the state. Qi Jiguang's sixteenth-century military work belonged to a world of official appointments, authorization and public resources. Personal loyalties mattered, but they did not replace the institutions within which armies were raised and supplied.

Yuan Chonghuan's execution in 1630 exposes a dangerous relationship between the court and a commander. It cannot establish the counterfactual that sparing him would certainly have saved the dynasty. Wang Aoyong's criticism of detailed military direction from a distance also reminds us that alternatives were discussed at the time. Later historians did not invent every pertinent question.

Drought and other disasters must be located by year and region. A failed harvest changes people's possibilities; it does not choose their subsequent actions for them. Li Zicheng suffered a major defeat as late as 1638. His eventual entry into Beijing was not the smooth unfolding of an outcome already contained in the first rebellion.

Beijing fell in 1644 and Chongzhen died. A Ming court was subsequently established at Nanjing. The rupture was immense, but it was not the simultaneous disappearance of every Ming institution.[8]

We can investigate accumulating consequences without claiming to have calculated a conserved balance of distress that necessarily fell due after 276 years. No such calculation has been made here.

## VI. The People Waiting at the Door

Four questions remain. Can ordinary successors maintain an arrangement? Who takes over work when an office is abolished? Who can revise an old obligation when circumstances change? After one reform succeeds, can someone still ask for the next correction?

These are not four ways of declaring every dynasty equally doomed. They move attention from whether a state functions to where its functioning reaches—and where it stops.

The remainder need not be imagined as a mysterious reservoir of future catastrophe. A report has been submitted, but its subject is still in prison. Taxes have been consolidated, but the household cannot obtain the means of payment. Land has been measured, but the error cannot be challenged. Something administratively accomplished has not yet answered the life it concerns.

Particular errors can be corrected. That possibility matters. It does not follow that a person can ever be exhausted by a tax entry, an office or a case number.

We should care about the prisoners not merely because neglecting them endangered the dynasty. Another century of Ming rule would not have returned their lost years. They were not indicators whose ultimate purpose was to tell us how long a state could last.

The next essay turns to competing claimants to that state. Removing an old decision-maker does not remove the work that needs doing. But keeping the work going is not a sufficient answer to the people asked to bear it.
