Perhaps you met Cook Ding long before you opened the Zhuangzi. A knife moves between joints. Nineteen years of work leave its edge apparently fresh. The scene becomes a lesson in craftsmanship, practice, or the remarkable ease of an expert doing what an expert does.

But another question can travel with it. How does Ding know where to move? How does he distinguish the configuration of the thing before him from the route his own habits would prefer?

Turn back one chapter from “Nourishing Life,” and you enter “Discussion on Making All Things Equal,” where the authority of our judgments repeatedly comes under examination.

The familiar cook is suddenly standing inside a conversation we have not finished reading.

## Putting the stories back beside one another

The seven Inner Chapters run from “Free and Easy Wandering” through “Discussion on Making All Things Equal,” “Nourishing Life,” “In the Human World,” “The Sign of Virtue Complete,” “The Great Ancestral Teacher,” and “Responding to Rulers.” English titles vary across editions; the order is what matters for this proposed route.

You can enter through an individual story. We have done that in the first ten essays. Now I would like to try reading the chapters as seven lessons that illuminate one another.

This is a reading proposal, not an established ancient syllabus. A coherent interpretation of the received sequence cannot, by itself, prove that one author designed the whole arrangement at one time.

We need not solve every question about the text's compilation before trying the experiment. A story may stand independently and acquire another dimension when returned to its neighbors. That possibility is the reason to read consecutively, not a reason to disqualify everyone who approaches the book differently.

Ding is one place to begin. Read alone, he shows us extraordinary ability. Read after a challenge to settled judgments, he can also raise a question about ability remaining responsive rather than becoming a demand that the world follow its preferred procedure.

## First, a larger field of view

For “Free and Easy Wandering,” my working prompt is: look up.

The enormous Peng flies at a height the small creatures consider unnecessary. Strange beings inhabit spans of time that dwarf ordinary human measures. At the end, the question returns to something close at hand: must a gourd too large for familiar household uses simply be smashed?

Across these scenes, I hear a recurring reminder. The range you know is not the whole of what there is.

This need not become an instruction to make every life immense. The small bird does not have to apologize for needing a nearby branch. The invitation is to pause before treating the limits of one's own purposes as the limits of every purpose worth having.

When I say something is unnecessary, unnecessary for whose life?

## Then, a look at the person looking

My prompt for the second chapter is: examine your own measure.

Having encountered possibilities beyond our habits, we can ask how those habits enter our understanding. Why can two people find different things obvious? What accompanies our conviction without appearing among the evidence we offer for it?

This does not mean abandoning truth or treating every mistake as an equally valid perspective. If judgment is situated, reasons and checks become more important, not less. A judgment needs a way to meet what might correct it.

The third chapter brings the question into action. Ding does not remain forever suspended above the ox because perception has limits. He works. His skill allows a difficult place to interrupt him.

Along this route, examining one's assumptions and doing something well are not enemies. The first can make the second less dependent on forcing a familiar answer onto an unfamiliar situation.

## Not every room is yours to arrange

The fourth chapter changes the setting. In “In the Human World,” the issue is no longer only how I see or how I act. What happens when another person holds power and the situation does not obey my intentions?

Yan Hui wants to go to Wei, hoping to influence a dangerous ruler. His good intentions do not remove the need to consider danger.

I call this lesson responding to circumstances. It is not a celebration of enduring injury without protest. It recognizes that what can be said, how it can be said, and who bears the consequences do not follow automatically from the strength of an argument.

The fifth chapter shifts attention toward the person being seen. Visible bodily differences and socially imposed descriptions can acquire enormous authority over how someone is treated.

Here my prompt is recognition. A bodily impairment does not make someone superior, any more than bodily conformity makes someone worth more. Nor do practical difficulties disappear because we refuse a hierarchy of persons.

The question is whether one visible feature has been permitted to finish the account of a life. Instead of asking only whether this person fits a use we have in mind, can we attend to how they are living?

## A life extends beyond successful performance

For “The Great Ancestral Teacher,” the sixth chapter, I use the whole course of life as a prompt.

That does not mean its figures possess graduation certificates guaranteeing permanent understanding. The chapter explores exemplary lives, but also illness, transformation, death, and relations among friends. A person does not exist only while successfully completing a task.

We may be composed in a meeting and discover the limits of that composure when someone close to us becomes seriously ill. The question reaches beyond an effective technique: how do our ways of seeing accompany us through changes we cannot arrange?

The seventh chapter turns the inquiry back toward our effects on others. “Responding to Rulers” includes questions of governing, responding, and refraining from imposed arrangements. It ends with Hundun's death.

By this point, the question is not merely how I avoid being defined by someone else. When I possess the ability to arrange another person's circumstances, what might I do with an understanding that remains incomplete?

You do not need a throne to meet this problem. Teaching a child, assessing an employee, caring for someone, and introducing a newcomer to an institution can all provide smaller versions of that power.

## The beginning and the ending

The opening chapter asks whether we can acknowledge a life beyond our familiar range. The final story asks whether we can let a different form of life continue.

Hundun is not an undeveloped being waiting to become a proper person. Before the visitors begin their improvements, he already welcomes them and participates in a relationship. The openings they drill answer a need they have assigned to him, not a request he has made.

The route is therefore not from looking upward at superior people to looking downward at inferior ones. It moves from recognizing the limits of our view toward resisting the temptation to impose that view as completion for someone else.

At the beginning, I may be the creature laughing. At the end, I may be the friend trying to help. These roles need not be a lifetime apart. They may occur on the same day: dismiss an unfamiliar choice in the morning; carefully arrange an unwanted life for someone in the afternoon.

Reading the chapters together can help those actions recognize one another. It does not make the reader immune to repeating them.

## What a portable quotation can leave behind

Wandering freely can shrink into a reason not to face anything. Equalizing things can become an excuse not to check a claim. Ding's skill can be recruited to tell an exhausted person to adapt more efficiently.

These reductions cannot all be blamed on skipping lessons. Two thousand years of interpretation will not fit inside one diagnosis, and an independent reading of a passage can be excellent.

Nevertheless, neighboring chapters can resist a conclusion that has become too convenient. An examination of judgment unsettles the claim that I already know what is natural. A dangerous political setting complicates the advice to act on whatever I consider right. Hundun interrupts the inference from good intentions to permission.

Sometimes the difficulty is not that we misunderstood a sentence. We made it the only sentence we would ever need.

Bringing another story alongside it gives the conclusion someone else to answer to.

## A map, not a ranking

Calling these seven lessons creates a danger of its own. I can start locating everyone on a ladder: I have reached lesson five; you are still in lesson two.

Then a route intended to return attention to ourselves becomes a device for evaluating other people. Every invitation to reconsider becomes evidence that I have progressed beyond the person beside me.

I would rather use a map that permits repeated returns. The same person can be responsive in one area and rigid in another. A sentence understood today may become difficult again in tomorrow's conflict.

Books can teach us. Study plans can help. Understanding a map is simply not the same as walking the ground. Conversely, understanding is not nothing: we need not wait for life to hurt us before reading can count as learning.

## Starting again with the cook

The essays that follow will move through the seven chapters, pausing at particular stories and exchanges. You need not accept the entire route before entering one of them.

Try it with a passage you already know. What surrounds it? What question precedes it? How far does the use you usually make of it travel from its original scene?

If the route reveals more of the story, it has earned some provisional use. If it compresses the text too tightly, the route should change.

Ding remains at work, his knife moving among the joints. We can still ask how he acquired such ability. We can also ask how someone already good at doing things continues to encounter what is actually there, rather than only recognizing his own method.
