The appointment has ended. Someone else has the office now, the meetings, the people waiting outside the door. You see the former occupant a few days later. He seems perfectly at ease. Is he hiding something? Has the news not reached him properly? Or does he know a way of living that you have not found?

Jian Wu brings something like this question to Sun Shuao in the Zhuangzi's chapter “Tian Zifang.” Three times Sun has held the highest ministerial office in Chu; three times he has left it. Neither elevation nor dismissal seems to have altered his bearing. Jian Wu notices even the ease around his breathing and asks how he manages it.

It is an intimate literary detail, not a reliable instrument for inspecting another person's mind. Quiet people can be devastated. The important evidence here is what Sun says next.

## Losing an office, not becoming nobody

Sun does not describe a technique for suppressing emotion. He describes his relationship to the office. Its arrival was not his alone to refuse; its departure was not his alone to prevent. He does not take its gain and loss as wholly his own doing.

The older subcommentary likewise emphasizes the limits of control. This is not a theory under which everything involving other people ceases to belong to you. A salary depends on an employer, but unpaid wages remain something you can claim. An assessment is written by someone else, but you can still challenge its accuracy.

The distinction runs elsewhere. A position can alter your circumstances without acquiring the right to define the whole of you. Someone may dismiss you from a job. It does not follow that they can dismiss you from being a person of consequence.

Things you have done do not vanish with the nameplate. Skills do not automatically expire when access to the building is revoked. There are promises that still matter, judgments that still deserve a hearing, relationships that do not report to the new director.

None of this makes dismissal painless. A job may support children, keep rent paid, or hold years of work together. Anger can have excellent reasons. Seeking redress does not prove that you are spiritually dependent on a title. The useful separation is between “This has hurt me” and “This has made me nothing.” You may need to say the first without accepting the second.

## A second kind of promotion

Twice Sun asks what makes him better than anyone else. Those repetitions keep another possibility at bay: turning freedom from rank into a superior rank of its own.

The official has lost his post, but the exceptionally detached man can still stand above the crowd. He can still need an audience beneath him. He has merely exchanged one uniform for another.

Sun declines that replacement. His answer continues by asking whether the distinction lies with others or with himself, then turns toward looking freely about rather than being absorbed in human rankings. The older explanations differ over the precise relations between self and other here. What remains vivid is a man who will not spend every hour finding his place in the queue.

The text later has Confucius praise him in exalted terms. It is not withholding all judgment, and it is not saying that admiration itself is forbidden. The question is what a judgment is allowed to become in the life of its recipient.

Can I receive praise without making its continuation a condition of being able to live? Can I dispute a dismissal without giving the person who dismissed me final jurisdiction over my worth?

## The fisherman who accepted the work

Another story in the same chapter offers a different angle. It appears earlier in the ancient sequence; bringing the two together is a reading choice, not a claim that they form consecutive lessons.

King Wen encounters an old fisherman at Zang. The man is fishing, yet seems strangely unoccupied with making a catch. Wen wants to put him in charge of government. He worries that ministers and relatives will object. He also worries that failing to appoint him will deprive the people of someone they need.

The king tells his officials about a dream in which a figure ordered him to entrust government to this old man. They identify the figure as a former ruler. Wen suggests divination to check the matter; the officials reply that an ancestral command needs no further confirmation. The fisherman is appointed.

This is more complicated than a gifted leader making an unfamiliar idea intelligible. The dream supplies authority that ordinary disagreement cannot easily reach. Cheng Xuanying's subcommentary explicitly reads it as a dream invoked to advance a worthy man. Later, Yan Hui questions why Wen needed a dream, and Confucius defends him as responding to the moment.

That defense belongs to the story. We should not delete it to produce a tale in which everyone freely weighs an open proposal. We can also ask a question the defense does not settle for us: how can people check a claim whose decisive source is available only through the ruler who wants their agreement?

Concern for the public does not make that problem disappear. Helping someone understand and inventing an unchallengeable endorsement are different actions, even when their intended destination is the same.

## Three years before the departure

The fisherman does not flee as soon as responsibility touches him. He governs for three years. The text says established laws are not replaced and exceptional commands are not issued. It then describes changes in political association, official self-display, and relations within and beyond the realm.

Some of these details have disputed glosses. They should not become a modern statistical report of universally improved welfare. Their narrative direction is nevertheless approving: the old man has done something valuable by entering government, not merely by remaining outside it.

That matters. Freedom from a position need not mean refusing every position. Accepting work, helping people, or keeping a difficult commitment does not automatically count as surrender. The fisherman can become involved without being absorbed into an endless career.

Only then does Wen ask whether this government can extend to the whole world. The old man does not answer. He takes his leave and disappears during the night, never to be heard from again.

The story gives him no exit interview. An older reading emphasizes withdrawal after accomplishment. We might also hear unease about turning a situated success into an unlimited mission. Neither reading lets us claim complete access to his motive. We should preserve both the three years of participation and the later departure.

## Must good work keep getting bigger?

You make one project work, and someone asks whether you can reproduce it ten times. You help one person, and more people are sent your way. The request may be sincere recognition. It need not conceal a villain.

Still, “This was worth doing” is not the same proposition as “You must keep doing more of it.” New scale brings different relationships, requirements, and costs. Yesterday's success is not advance consent to tomorrow's assignment.

The old man's disappearance gives that distinction a startling shape. Real departures are rarely so light. There may be work to hand over, people to inform, and consequences to take responsibility for. The story is not a recommendation to leave everyone else clearing up without warning.

But the need to leave responsibly does not abolish the possibility of leaving. Otherwise responsibility becomes a door that opens only inward: once you have proved useful, there is no permitted way out.

Sun Shuao and the fisherman leave us with two different movements. One keeps living as appointments arrive and depart. The other accepts work and later refuses to let enlargement determine his only remaining future. Neither needs to turn a useful role into the entire measure of a person.

Tomorrow you may still need to attend an interview, challenge a decision, or write an uncomfortable email. Those things matter. Before you begin, it may help to separate yourself a little from the card bearing your title.

The card tells something true. It does not have to hold everything true about you.
