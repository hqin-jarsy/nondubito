You know the moment. You are reading a long passage when one sentence catches you. You take a screenshot, send it to a friend, and never quite get back to the rest.

Steal a belt clasp and you face punishment. Steal a state and you become a lord.

The line, from “Rifling Trunks,” needs little explanation to sting. Anyone who has watched power rearrange the consequences of wrongdoing can recognize something in it. Small theft is a crime; large theft may acquire a title.

It deserves to be remembered. But the chapter is doing more than observing that important people get away with things. Turn back a few pages. Before the state, there is a box.

## He does not pick the lock

Someone has possessions to protect. He tightens the bindings and secures the locks. This is sensible enough: make the box harder to open, and a thief will have more trouble getting at its contents.

Then a different sort of thief arrives. He carries off the whole thing. Far from wanting the fastenings to fail, he hopes they will hold. The last thing he needs is his loot spilling onto the road.

The lock has not stopped working. That is the unsettling part. It is working perfectly well for someone else.

One precaution answers one kind of threat. Against another, it may help preserve the very thing being stolen. We had been asking who could open the box. We had not asked who could take the box away.

This is not a theorem that more locks produce more danger. Locks still prevent certain thefts; locking something does not, by itself, determine how possessions are gathered or stored. The story supplies a thief capable of carrying everything off in order to expose the boundary of the owner's precautions. Protection matters. So does noticing what it leaves outside its scope.

## Taking the right to call it right

The chapter then enlarges the box into a state. In its account, Tian Chengzi takes Qi and appropriates its inherited arrangements along with it. We need not treat every detail of this political narrative as verified history to follow its question.

A person who steals money may still be judged by institutions he does not control. Someone who takes over the institutions, the position from which orders are issued, and the language used to justify rule has taken something more consequential than a larger sum.

He may now help decide who gets called a thief.

The text moves through measures, weighing instruments, seals of authority, benevolence, and righteousness. Each can serve a legitimate purpose. Its challenge is that a conqueror can appropriate the apparatus along with the goods: the means of measuring, certifying, and presenting the seizure as rightful.

This is where the famous sentence belongs. In the wording preserved in the Collected Explanations used here, it continues by placing benevolence and righteousness at the lords' gates. The point is not that becoming a ruler miraculously makes a person good. It is that even the public credentials of goodness can travel with the winner.

Commenting earlier in the chapter, Guo Xiang warns that the sages' methods are used by people and cannot guarantee appropriateness in every case. A reassuring name does not settle the question of what an arrangement actually does.

Consider a contemporary example, not an episode from the ancient text. A few people work together. Their money is to be accounted for openly; major expenses require discussion. Eventually one person holds the money, controls every record, decides what counts as a major expense, and adjudicates every disagreement.

Nothing has necessarily been stolen. But “we have rules” no longer tells us enough. Can the records be checked? Can a decision be challenged? Can someone object without losing the standing to speak? These questions extend the passage into a present-day setting. They are not a ready-made system of governance hidden inside the ancient box.

## Where the argument asks for more

The chapter also gives Robber Zhi's followers a provocative question: does robbery have a Way? The answer assigns familiar excellences—judgment, courage, fairness in division—to the business of stealing. Admirable capacities do not automatically govern the purposes for which they are used.

There is an argument here from the beginning, not a pure story later spoiled by someone starting to explain. The declaration that great thieves will not cease until sages die actually comes before the belt-clasp sentence. Later comes the demand to abandon sageliness and knowledge. The argument grows more radical as it proceeds.

Its intensity does not make it empty. It attacks the comfortable assumption that power becomes good merely by surrounding itself with elevated standards. For someone oppressed in the name of those standards, forceful speech may be the first speech that makes the situation recognizable.

Still, I hesitate at the next step. Does the possibility of appropriating a good thing establish that abolishing it will end the appropriation? If the powerful capture the measures, will the less powerful necessarily fare better without any measures? Moral language can conceal wrongdoing. It can also give someone grounds for exposing it.

Both possibilities need attention. A sharper sentence cannot decide between them for us.

Later passages deploy fierce images of destroying instruments and damaging celebrated specialists' senses. Older commentators often redirect such language toward abandoning the external standards people compete to imitate. That is an interpretation, not a reason to pretend the language was gentle all along. The chapter does challenge a civilization's confidence in its own accomplishments. I can take that challenge seriously without treating it as permission to abolish knowledge and rules, much less to injure people.

Accepting the danger it reveals does not oblige me to accept every remedy it proposes.

Guo Xiang does not simply nod along either. In an earlier note he worries that removing the sages' wisdom while leaving other forms of cleverness intact could produce still greater harm. He imagines a more thorough transformation, but does not equate removing one defense with solving everything. Ancient readers, too, could occupy positions other than wholesale assent or rejection. Recognizing that an arrangement can assist wrongdoing is one question; deciding how to change it, and who might suffer in the process, is another.

## What happens after the screenshot

A memorable sentence is not a failure of thought. Sometimes we need one to keep a plain injustice from disappearing beneath elaborate qualifications. Sharing it may begin a reading rather than end one.

The difference emerges afterward. When we meet a particular case, can we still ask who took what, by what means, and with whose authorization? What evidence might change our judgment? Whose account has not yet been heard?

If the sentence helps those questions become visible, it is helping. If it brands every disagreement as an apology for thieves before anything can be examined, it is starting to close the room.

The issue is not how widely a quotation circulates. We cannot infer shallow understanding from popularity, or claim superior insight merely because we read the whole chapter.

This series needs the same scrutiny. “Leave room for the reader” is also an attractive phrase. So is “Do not finish another person's thinking for them.” If I use either to dismiss this chapter's political anger as inferior writing, I have found myself another convenient lock.

Essay 64 considered “Webbed Toes”; essay 65 examined explanation itself. Here I cannot evade the obligation to explain my own disagreement by declaring where Zhuangzi ought to have stopped. Why accept the observation about capture but resist the proposed cure? My reasons need to be available for inspection.

The guide promised to leave readers a question. That need not mean placing a question mark at the end of every essay. Questions can smuggle in compulsory answers. Statements can remain genuinely open to correction. What matters is whether you can return to the text, recognize another possibility, and decline my reading.

The box is still worth keeping in mind. Even an excellent precaution can be enlisted in another purpose. So can an excellent warning about precautions. Remember the story, then look at the situation in front of you. Possessing the sentence is not the same as having understood every lock.
