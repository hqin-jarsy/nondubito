Hui Shi has become chief minister of Liang. Zhuangzi comes to visit him. Someone tells Hui Shi that the visitor intends to take his place.

Frightened, Hui Shi has a search conducted for three days and nights.

When Zhuangzi appears, he does not begin by explaining his itinerary. He tells a story about a bird that flies from the southern sea to the northern sea. This bird has particular tastes: certain trees to rest in, certain fruit to eat, certain springs to drink from.

An owl below has acquired a rotting rat. As the other bird passes overhead, the owl looks up and gives a threatening cry.

Is that what you intend to do with your state of Liang, Zhuangzi asks—use it to give me that cry?

## The joke has teeth

Comparing a ministerial position to a rotten rat is hardly gentle. There is sharp satire here. To describe the story as nothing more than a kindly mirror offered to a friend would remove some of its force.

But if we finish laughing and retain only a ranking—Zhuangzi noble, Hui Shi vulgar—we may have completed the reading too quickly. We have joined the attractive side and left ourselves nothing more to examine.

I want to look at what the owl gets wrong.

The thing it holds matters to it. The passing bird, however, has another destination and shows no interest in competing for that meal. The owl's cry joins two separate propositions: I am afraid of losing this; therefore you must want it.

Something can be worth guarding without being the object of every approaching person's desire.

This does not require pretending that the story contains no contrast between the birds. It asks what becomes visible once we stop using that contrast only to assign ourselves the better species.

## Three days following a sentence

The text emphasizes fear and duration. It does not supply the operational details of the search, and we need not invent a street-by-street sweep. What it gives is already substantial. Hui Shi's fear becomes sustained action.

That cannot prove he had no doubts. People sometimes search desperately precisely because they are uncertain. But the search does show how much authority a suggestion has acquired.

The claim that Zhuangzi wants his job has not been established. It has nevertheless begun organizing events.

Why was the suggestion so readily available to him? One possible reason lies in how much the position means to Hui Shi himself. Someone hands him a guess about another person's motives, and his own familiar desire fills the unknown space.

We do this too. Another person's unexplained choice quickly receives an explanation in our heads. The more closely that explanation resembles something we value, the less it feels like a hypothesis still needing evidence.

## Whom does the cry describe?

The warning is directed at the bird overhead. What it reveals most clearly is the owl below: I have something, I fear losing it, and I believe you may take it.

Not every suspicion is projection. Competition exists, and people sometimes conceal their aims. The story is not an instruction to abandon judgment. Nor should it become a convenient way of telling someone with good reasons for concern that they are imagining everything.

It can slow one particular inference: what evidence takes me from knowing that I value this to knowing that you came for it?

The inference becomes especially suspect when nothing could change it. If the other person acts vigorously, they are maneuvering. If they withdraw, they are playing a longer game. If they explicitly deny interest, they are concealing it well.

At that point, the explanation is not growing more accurate. It has become impossible for the other person to answer it.

Even if the bird continues north, the owl can announce that the threat has retreated for now.

## A tortoise beside the river

Immediately before this story in “Autumn Floods,” Zhuangzi is fishing. Two envoys from the king of Chu arrive to offer him responsibility for the state.

Still holding his rod, he asks about a sacred tortoise whose remains have been preserved and honored for three thousand years. Would it rather be dead and revered for its bones, or alive, dragging its tail through the mud?

Alive in the mud, the envoys answer.

Then go, says Zhuangzi. That is what I intend to do.

Placed together, the two stories let the reader see someone actually refusing a celebrated appointment. Disinterest is not merely a consolation invented by people who were never offered anything.

There is no need to calculate whether Chu's offer was grander than the position in Liang. The passages do not provide the evidence for that comparison. The important contrast lies between an honor others are prepared to confer and a life the recipient wishes to live.

The envoys can answer for the tortoise. Believing that a person before them might genuinely choose accordingly is a further step.

## Not climbing becomes another way to climb

Some workplaces are exceptionally good at explaining people.

Someone helps with a problem that will never appear in a performance review: they must be cultivating useful relationships. Someone declines promotion: they must have a better offer elsewhere. Someone leaves: they must have lost an internal contest. Even a repeated statement that they prefer a particular kind of work is courteously received as a cover story.

Any one of these explanations might occasionally be correct. The suffocating part is that no other explanation can be accepted as true.

Not competing becomes a sophisticated competitive strategy. Every path is made to lead back to the same ladder.

The ruler of Fan, in the previous essay, refuses to let a position represent his whole existence. This story moves the question outward. My position cannot explain another person's life either.

If advancement is my only measure, a colleague's competence may feel threatening. A useful objection becomes a challenge to my authority. The colleague may simply care about the work, but I have already assigned them an ambition in my account of events.

For a manager, that account does not remain private. It influences who receives opportunities, who is excluded, and whose ordinary conversations are redescribed as alliance-building. The cost of suspicion is not paid only by the frightened person. People placed under suspicion may pay for it as well.

## Do not add the final verdict

Zhuangzi's closing question is pointed. We need not blunt it. But the passage does not continue with a complete diagnosis of Hui Shi's character.

It records no reply. We do not have to supply a shamefaced silence to certify that the lesson worked.

More importantly, Hui Shi does not live only in this anecdote. Elsewhere, Zhuangzi passes his grave and tells the story of the carpenter who can no longer perform his extraordinary feat because the person who stood beneath the axe has died. Hui Shi is the lost counterpart in that scene.

The text gives us different moments in a relationship. In one, he can look absurd; in another, his absence is irreplaceable. Seeing someone guard a rotten rat at one moment does not mean we now know everything about them.

If we use the anecdote to settle their character permanently, we repeat the movement it helps us notice: a ready-made explanation covers the person in front of us.

## After the birds, the fish

The next scene in “Autumn Floods” brings Zhuangzi and Hui Shi onto a bridge. Watching fish move easily below, Zhuangzi speaks of their happiness. Hui Shi asks how he can know that happiness when he is not a fish.

This is a real question about understanding another living being. It should not simply be equated with Hui Shi's earlier fear about his job.

Still, the three adjacent scenes make an interesting sequence. Can someone want a life different from the one you would choose? On what grounds do you assign them a motive? When you say that you know, how do you know?

In the search for Zhuangzi, an unverified account of another person rapidly becomes action. On the bridge, a dispute about knowing is brought into the conversation itself. The text does not permanently appoint one man to be right and the other to be wrong. They are still talking.

## What you hold may continue to matter

There is no need to finish this story by announcing that your own position is worthless. Work, money, and public responsibility can matter greatly. Admitting that is not embarrassing.

What needs loosening is a further claim: because this matters to me, it must be what everyone else really wants.

Next time I confidently say that someone is “really after” something, I might pause over the word really. Does it name a reason they gave, evidence I observed, or my most familiar desire wearing somebody else's name?

The thing in my hands does not disappear when I ask that question. The bird overhead may simply recover a direction of its own.
