## Seven sages lose their way

The Yellow Emperor sets out to visit a figure named Dawei in the Juci Mountains. Six companions accompany him. One drives, one rides alongside, others go before the horses or follow behind. The party has rank, ability, and a complete division of duties.

On the open country near Xiangcheng, all seven lose their way.

It is a quietly comic beginning. Put enough impressive people together and you still do not necessarily know where the road goes. Fortunately, they meet a boy tending horses. Does he know the mountain? Yes. Does he know where Dawei is? Yes again.

The emperor is astonished and asks about governing the world. A child who can give directions is suddenly invited to answer a sovereign’s question. The leap belongs to the fable. It is not evidence that competence in one field guarantees competence in every other. What it unsettles is the expected relation between status and knowledge: the grand party needs someone it would ordinarily have passed by.

## The boy says more than we remember

The famous answer comes near the end: remove what harms the horses. Remembering only that line can turn the encounter into a neat exchange between a puzzled ruler and an unusually sensible child.

The received passage is stranger. The boy first says that governing the world amounts to something like this, with no need for further business. He then describes wandering within the bounds of the cosmos, suffering an affliction, receiving advice from an elder, and traveling through the countryside in the chariot of the sun. His condition has improved somewhat; he intends to travel beyond those bounds.

This is mythic language, not a clinical case history or a treatment a reader can reproduce. It also gives the boy an existence beyond answering the ruler. He has had his own difficulties and guidance, and he has somewhere else to go.

The emperor acknowledges that governing is not the boy’s business but asks again. The boy declines. The emperor persists, and only then does the answer about horses arrive. This is not simply two questions and two short replies. Nor is the first “like this” unambiguously explained by removing everything except the horse-tending at the end.

Restoring the middle makes the child less like a device installed in the landscape to deliver a maxim.

## A different first question

What difference is there, the boy asks, between governing the world and tending horses? It is only a matter of removing what harms the horses.

He does not begin by describing the ideal animal. He does not give a training schedule. He directs the attention of the person in charge toward harm. Before you ask how to make them faster, more orderly, or more pleasing, ask what is preventing them from living well—including the arrangements you have imposed.

The force of “only” stops the manager’s list from expanding indefinitely. But an arresting maxim is not a complete program of government. The absence of instructions about water, grazing, and shelter does not establish that horses no longer need them.

I take the line as a reminder about where to begin: examine the damage caused by present arrangements before adding another arrangement. It is not a promise that every difficulty will disappear once one obstacle is removed.

## Remembering Bole

Earlier in this series, Bole claimed to know how to manage horses and subjected them to treatment that killed many of them. Reading the episodes together sharpens the boy’s answer. Danger may come not only from neglect, but from practices introduced under the name of care.

The connection is ours, however. The boy does not name Bole or list the brands, bridles, and punishments in the other chapter. We should not put a specific instruction to abolish all of Bole’s measures into this passage and then claim to have discovered it there.

Some restrictions injure. Some protections prevent injury. A cruel training regime does not prove that all training is cruel, and the favorable word “care” does not excuse whatever is done under it. Attention must return to the animal affected.

If there is already a wound, removing the thing that caused it is not the end of responsibility. Someone must attend to the wound. Stopping an injury and dealing with its consequences belong together. Announcing that the first has been accomplished can otherwise leave all the remaining work to the injured.

## Everyone has one more improvement

Consider a contemporary team whose work is slowing down. The obvious proposals are additions: training, software, reporting, approvals, clearer targets. Some may help. Yet improvements can accumulate into a difficulty of their own.

Imagine that a progress update must be entered in a form, sent by email, and repeated in a group chat. Every step began as a response to a genuine communication problem. Together they now consume attention that could have gone into doing the work. A manager who asks only how to improve reporting may never notice the cost of reporting itself.

The boy’s sentence makes another question available: what could I stop requiring?

Ending a procedure need not mean the person who introduced it was foolish. Circumstances may have changed; a temporary measure may have served its purpose. Revision becomes easier when it does not require a public trial of every past decision. Otherwise people keep spending today’s time to protect yesterday’s reputation.

## Subtraction can be checked

It is tempting to say that additions have visible results while removals have nothing to show. That captures a familiar organizational bias, but it is too absolute. Less waiting, fewer duplicate forms, fewer errors, and time returned to useful work are observable changes. They may simply be less suited to a launch event than a new platform.

The harder question is whether we have removed the right thing. An annoying step may protect someone who has little voice in the meeting. A slow review may prevent a serious mistake. Calling a procedure harmful requires asking who bears its costs, who receives its protection, and whether a lighter alternative is possible.

The modern application therefore needs a return visit. After removing the duplicate report, has time actually been saved? Is necessary information still available? Has the burden merely moved to another worker? Those are our practical questions, not a set of instructions already supplied by the ancient boy.

“Do less” can be just as arbitrary as “do more” when only the person at the top may define its meaning. People affected need a chance to say whether a burden has been lifted or essential support has quietly disappeared.

## People are not obstacles to clear away

One reading of the maxim needs particular resistance: if we should remove what harms the horses, why not remove the people who make governing difficult?

That turns a warning about harm into a license for exclusion. Critics, slower workers, and people with different needs can all be described as obstacles by someone invested in a plan. If no account of the injury is required, “harmful” comes to mean “inconvenient to me.”

Actual violence and abuse must be stopped, and responsibility can be established. But disagreement with a program does not make a person an object to discard. Removing harm is not the same as removing everyone who resists our wishes.

This distinction is work we must do when using the story today. Brevity does not make a saying immune to misuse. The sentence cannot automatically exercise judgment on behalf of the person repeating it.

## The emperor withdraws

At the end, the emperor bows deeply, calls the boy his heavenly teacher, and leaves. The narrative does not tell us which policies he later changes. It does not report that the world is thereafter well governed. We have a moment of instruction, not demonstrated institutional success.

I like the departure. The elaborate traveling party arrived with a question, and the ruler kept pressing for an answer. Finally the questioning stops. Within this scene, he neither retains the child as an adviser nor demands ten further points for implementation.

Outside the story, water, roads, tools, and care still require attention. The boy has not made that work disappear. He has interrupted a different urgency: the conviction that responsibility must always be proved by adding something of one’s own.

Perhaps the useful next task is to find an arrangement that is hurting people and stop it. Then remain available long enough to hear whether things have actually improved.
