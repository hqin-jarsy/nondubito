## The student has something the teacher lacks

Nanbo Zikui notices Nüyu's face. She is old, yet her complexion looks like a child's. How can that be? She answers that she has heard the Way. Can he learn it too? Her response is abrupt: no, he is not the person for it.

This is not a gentle introductory consultation. An extraordinary appearance and an unexpected refusal pull us into the question of how change happens. We need not take the youthful complexion as evidence of an anti-aging treatment, or the teacher's judgment as a final decree about her visitor's possibilities.

Nüyu then describes teaching someone else, Buliang Yi. He had a sage's aptitude but not a sage's Way, she says; she had a sage's Way but not his aptitude.

The distinction does not mean that she declares herself generally incompetent. It means that the student brings something she does not possess. Helping him need not consist of making him into another version of herself.

## Teaching without enlarging yourself

Educational relationships easily become staircases. The teacher occupies the higher step; the student follows the teacher's footprints. Every improvement confirms that the teacher was already there.

Nüyu's formulation loosens that picture. She can guide Buliang Yi without containing everything he might become. She has some understanding he needs. He has possibilities that are not simply copies of hers.

That does not abolish expertise. Teaching surgery, driving, or reading requires knowledge, explanation, and demonstration. Belief in a student's potential is not a substitute for competent instruction.

Beyond the transfer of skills, however, there is another danger. A teacher may begin to expect the student's whole life to vindicate the method. Choosing a different direction looks like disloyalty; arriving at a different understanding becomes proof of insufficient learning.

Nüyu's sentence interrupts that expectation. Are you helping someone develop his abilities, or hoping the world will acquire a younger edition of you? The two projects may look similar for a while. Eventually they ask very different things of the learner.

## Three days, seven days, nine days

Nüyu describes remaining with Buliang Yi and instructing him. After three days he could put the world outside; after another period of seven days, things outside; after nine days, life outside. Then come the clarity of dawn, seeing what is alone, the disappearance of past and present, and entry into what is neither dying nor being born.

The language is dense. It is tempting to turn it into a chart of levels and start locating ourselves on it. But the numbers are not a validated curriculum. Adding them up will not produce a dependable timetable for transformation, and their progression does not establish that each harder achievement requires exactly two more days.

The wording also includes instruction. We cannot turn Nüyu into a teacher who merely stands by in absolute silence. The text does not describe the daily lessons, what was repeated, or how each change was recognized.

Some details should remain unavailable. Otherwise we will quietly write a precise coaching manual into a story that has not supplied one. The presence of sequence and waiting is clear enough; the universal method is our addition.

## Outside does not mean thrown away

How might an ordinary reader approach putting the world, things, and life outside? One starting question is whether something that occupies the whole mind can gradually lose its total grip.

Consider other people's opinions. At first, one dissatisfied expression can reorganize your entire day. Later you may still notice it and want to hear the criticism, without treating each verdict as the final decision on your life. The world has not disappeared. Its voices no longer arrive only as commands.

Or consider possessions and achievements. A job, a home, a collection of books can matter deeply. Losing them may hurt and may justify substantial efforts to protect them. Yet there is still a difference between cherishing something and needing it to establish that you are someone.

These examples are entrances into the passage, not exhaustive definitions of its ancient terms. Putting something outside should not become an order to discard property, sever relationships, or rank every ordinary attachment as spiritually inferior.

Putting life outside especially does not mean ceasing to care for the body. Refusing food, abandoning necessary care, or courting physical danger cannot establish enlightenment. The same chapter opens by valuing the completion of one's natural span rather than premature death. Living is not simply an error to be discarded as quickly as possible.

## The genuine person is not a safety experiment

The Great Ancestral Teacher, this chapter of the Zhuangzi, describes the genuine person in extraordinary terms: unafraid of heights, unwetted by water, unheated by fire. These images carry the figure beyond ordinary constraints. They are not a guarantee that a body can escape injury.

Other phrases come closer to recognizable experience. The genuine person is not consumed by regret after going wrong, nor self-congratulatory when things go right. We might hear a life less continually dragged around by its own running evaluation.

But freedom from consuming regret cannot excuse harm. Apologies, restitution, and changes in conduct may still be necessary. A useful loosening of self-preoccupation creates room to do those things; it does not make injured people pay for someone else's serenity.

Nor is this figure simply without emotion. Joy and anger are described in relation to the passage of the seasons. To use the image to rebuke someone for grieving too long would impose another rigid calendar on life. A person may move slowly and need help. An ancient ideal need not become a test of whether someone's suffering is philosophically acceptable.

## A quiet that does not depend on no one touching you

After the apparently remote stages, Nüyu introduces a striking expression, yingning: tranquility entangled with, or arriving through, disturbance. Contact has not simply been excluded.

If we retained only the repeated putting-outside, the best life might seem to be one in which every relationship has been sealed away and nothing can reach the self. This later phrase brings encounters back into view.

Someone challenges your work. You hear the criticism, dislike it, and may need to revise. Circumstances change abruptly. You rearrange your plans without first proving that you were never affected. In such moments, steadiness might mean retaining room to respond after being touched, rather than remaining untouched.

That is an everyday way of borrowing the image, not a claim to have settled everything the chapter means by neither birth nor death. A quiet room can still help. Withdrawal may be necessary. But if peace requires everyone to stay away forever, life can become an endless job of guarding the entrance.

## A teacher does not own the declaration of arrival

Nüyu judges and waits. Bringing her story into present-day teaching should not give a guide exclusive authority over the learner's inner life. “Your discomfort proves you have not understood” can become an answer that no experience is allowed to challenge.

A trustworthy guide can allow questions, explain reasons, and accept that the relationship might end. A student may seek another teacher or another way of learning. Guidance is not made deeper by removing that possibility.

The refusal at the beginning of the story retains its sharpness. We can hear it without raising it into an educational ideal. Being rejected by one teacher does not establish that all your possibilities have been seen. Growth should not require the prior destruction of your trust in yourself as an admission fee.

Someone accompanying you can make a difference. Someone taking possession of the whole road is doing something else. The distinction remains important even when the person taking possession sincerely believes he knows where you ought to go.

## At the end of the lineage, another question

Zikui asks where Nüyu heard what she knows. A sequence of strange names follows, passing through associations with written traces, recitation, obscurity, and origins, until it reaches a figure whose name suggests the questioning of beginnings. Commentators interpret the names in different ways.

This is not an ordinary register of teachers that finally pins every source in place. Following the lineage carries the reader toward another question. Knowing where an idea comes from matters; asking about its beginning need not therefore be finished.

The essay, too, can stop without issuing a final certificate of growth. Buliang Yi is not Nüyu's duplicate. We do not need to line up behind them, anxiously calculating how many levels remain before we qualify as ourselves.

Try a smaller pair of questions. Which burden have you been holding more tightly than necessary? Which ability would still be worth patiently learning?

Carrying less and learning more need not conflict. You do not have to become someone whom nothing affects before you can begin finding your footing in a life that will continue to affect you.
