## Speaking about swords

Some advice ends the conversation as soon as it begins. Stop wasting time on that. Think about something important. The speaker may have a legitimate concern. The listener hears something else: whatever you care about must first be put away before anything you say can count.

The Zhuangzi offers a remarkable alternative opening. A king is obsessed with sword fighting. The man sent to change his mind arrives dressed as a swordsman and announces that, having heard of the king’s interest, he has come to discuss swords.

It is a brilliant entrance. But brilliance is not the same as an adequate judgment of the whole encounter. We can admire a speaker’s ability to enter another person’s world while asking what happens there—and who pays for the visit.

## This pleasure has victims

In “Speaking of Swords,” King Wen of Zhao keeps more than three thousand swordsmen around his court. They fight before him day and night. The opening reports more than a hundred killed or injured each year, not more than a hundred deaths alone. After three years, the state is weakened and neighboring rulers are making plans against it. The crown prince seeks Zhuangzi’s help.

This is not an innocent hobby being misunderstood by an impatient family. Other people’s bodies sustain the king’s entertainment. How to get through to him is one problem. What to do for the people being hurt is another, and it cannot simply wait for persuasion to succeed.

The visitor knows there is danger for him too. Before meeting the king, he explains to the prince that failing to satisfy either of them could end in punishment and death. We are inside a political maneuver under sovereign power, not a friendly conversation among equals.

The clothes, the swagger, and the boast about striking down a man every ten paces provide access. They also appeal directly to the appetite that has caused the damage. Entering someone’s vocabulary does not yet tell us what entering it will cost.

## The seven days we should not skip

An abbreviated retelling can move from the first audience straight to the three swords. Zhuangzi accepts the king’s interest, enlarges it, and the king sees the error of his ways. The result looks like an elegant method for changing a mind without confrontation.

The received text has an intervening event. The king spends seven days selecting swordsmen for the trial. More than sixty are killed or injured before five or six are chosen. Only then does he summon Zhuangzi to demonstrate his skill.

Those seven days belong to the story. They cannot disappear because we like the speech that follows. This does not require assigning every injury to the visitor personally. The ruler directs the selection, and the narrative does not supply the detail needed for a complete assessment of responsibility. It does, however, prevent us from treating the encounter as a technique whose harmlessness has already been established.

Admire the ingenuity, if you wish. Just do not turn that page too quickly.

## One familiar word, three different worlds

When the trial is about to begin, Zhuangzi asks to explain his three swords first. The king agrees.

The first is the sword of the Son of Heaven. Regions, mountains, and political territories become its point, edge, spine, guard, and hilt. The seasons and the ordering of the world enter its construction. Its reach extends far beyond a duel.

The second is the sword of a regional ruler. Here the parts are made of people distinguished by intelligence, courage, integrity, and other qualities needed for government. The account speaks of harmonizing the people’s wishes and bringing security to the surrounding country.

The third brings us back to the men in front of the king: striking necks and cutting into bodies, like fighting cocks. A life ends, and the spectacle contributes nothing to the affairs of the state.

The subject has not changed. The word “sword” remains available throughout. What changes is the range of things it can mean. The king begins with the question of who can defeat whom. He is brought toward another question: what is this admired power actually accomplishing?

That is the opening worth preserving. A familiar interest may contain more possibilities than the form in which someone is currently pursuing it. Admiration for skill need not be satisfied only by watching people injure one another.

## A larger scale is not automatically a better life

Yet the hierarchy in the speech deserves scrutiny. The greatest sword seeks submission across the world; the second seeks obedience within a ruler’s territory. This political imagination is not identical with an ideal we must accept today. Increasing the scale of an activity does not necessarily make it good. A larger instrument can also inflict larger damage.

Nor does Zhuangzi simply lay out three options and leave the conclusion untouched. He tells the king that possessing the position of a Son of Heaven while favoring the commoner’s sword is beneath him. There is explicit criticism in that sentence. It works partly through the king’s sensitivity to rank.

Calling an interest low-class or unworthy of someone’s status may be persuasive. It may also move a person from one contest for superiority into another. The ancient category of the “commoner’s sword” binds an objection to violence to a social hierarchy; we need not carry the whole bundle into our own relationships.

What I would borrow is the ability to reopen a subject, not the assurance that the higher rung must be right. Advice can have a direction, give reasons, and make an objection. It does not become respectful merely by hiding its intended destination until the listener repeats it as their own discovery.

The encounter is also a literary account, not a verified record of a meeting. Calling the king a Son of Heaven within the speech raises the stakes of the appeal; it does not establish his actual possession of that standing. Recognizing the rhetoric lets us see more clearly how the speaker uses identity and imagination to move his listener.

## The ending will not let us relax

The received chapter ends with the king remaining inside his palace for three months and the swordsmen dying where they are. Whatever questions arise around the precise wording, the narrative does not offer them a safe transition into another livelihood.

This is not an uncomplicated picture of a man gently led out of a destructive attachment. The central figure changes, but the people whose lives had been organized around his appetite are not given a future. Resolving the powerful person’s problem is not necessarily resolving the situation.

It is tempting to keep the speech and declare the disturbing ending a later addition. That may be a hypothesis worth investigating, but dislike is not evidence enough to establish it. Differences in tone or characterization can raise questions about composition; they cannot alone identify a writer. Until a case is made, the ending remains part of what we are reading.

Su Shi dismissed this chapter, along with “Yielding Kingship,” as falling short of the Way. We need not accept his judgment in full. We also need not erase the swordsmen to defend a passage we find illuminating.

## Where an interest might grow

Now leave the palace for a much smaller, contemporary situation. A child spends too much time gaming. Repeating that games are a waste may achieve little. Asking what holds their interest can reveal different things: puzzles, teamwork, stories, competition. Some might lead toward design, reading, sport, or a more manageable place for games themselves.

This is an illustration, not an educational method validated by the ancient story. The questions cannot merely extract information for a more efficient campaign against what the child enjoys. They must leave room for an inconvenient answer: perhaps the child genuinely likes playing games, rather than the improved substitute an adult has prepared.

Sleep, schoolwork, spending, and obligations to others still need discussion. Listening does not abolish limits. Setting limits need not first invalidate the interest. And when someone is being harmed, protection cannot be postponed until a perfect conversation has changed everybody’s mind.

The three swords suggest a question wider than “Why won’t you let go?” What else could the thing you value become? That is a valuable question. Its value does not require forgetting the annual casualties, the selection trials, or the final fate of the swordsmen.

Getting through to someone is one achievement. Making it possible for the people affected to go on living is another. Our admiration for persuasion should travel far enough to ask about the second.
