Opening something you wrote years ago, you may recognize the tone before you recognize the mistake. You were so certain. The sentences were firm, the examples carefully chosen. You can almost remember waiting for people to agree.

What troubles you now is not only the conclusion. It is how confidently the possibility of another circumstance was excluded.

Deleting the passage would be easy. Harder is admitting that you meant it then and no longer wish to say it now. “Zeyang” gives that difficulty a companion in Qu Boyu, whose sixty years are described as sixty transformations.

What he first affirmed, he later rejected. Within one short sentence, the same person judges and returns to reconsider his judgment.

## You were not necessarily pretending then

The initial affirmation matters. Qu Boyu really takes something to be right before coming to regard it otherwise. Later change does not automatically expose the earlier position as insincere.

We sometimes make honesty depend on permanent agreement with our former selves. You said the opposite before, so one of your statements must have been a lie. Under that rule, someone who discovers a mistake has to choose between correcting it and preserving a reputation for integrity.

Serious judgment and openness to revision can coexist. You can have sufficient reason to decide now while recognizing that further evidence may require another look. The possibility of change does not make the present commitment empty.

There is no need to caricature a skeptic as someone incapable of affirming anything. Doubt can lead to checking reasons, reaching a provisional judgment, and accepting responsibility for action. The important question is what can still reach the judgment after it has been made.

A relationship, a project, or a sustained practice may need precisely this kind of seriousness: not a guarantee of never changing, but a refusal to use possible change as an excuse for neglecting the present.

## Sixty is not an attendance sheet

The ancient sentence places sixty years beside sixty changes. Older commentary also describes renewal with the years. Yet we do not possess sixty annual records establishing exactly one reversal in each.

The later rejection of an earlier belief does not tell us what caused every revision. Qu Boyu might reflect, hear another person, or encounter consequences he had not foreseen. The wording cannot establish that all sixty changes occurred before reality or criticism forced the issue.

Being persuaded by evidence is not a lesser achievement. Some people avoid what is plainly before them. Others acknowledge it and return to explain their error to those affected. That can require considerable courage.

If we admire only insight generated entirely from within, we may exclude the very people and events through which learning happens. Such independence looks strong while making a mind harder to reach.

The passage need not rank every route by which people change. It can loosen the force of a simpler obstacle: I said it myself, therefore it must remain beyond revision.

## This year's certainty is in time too

The next clause places the current affirmation beside the preceding positions later rejected. Might what seems right now belong with those earlier errors?

The compressed reference to fifty-nine errors should not be turned into an exact ledger identifying the present view as a particular numbered mistake. Its force is to place today's conviction within a history of transformation, not to provide an accountant's chronology.

That may sound unsettling. If a judgment can change, how can anyone act on it? But revisability and groundlessness are different conditions. A bridge needing inspection is not necessarily a bridge no one should cross. A decision needing stated conditions is not necessarily a decision without present justification.

A usable conviction can say: given what I know, this is what I judge; if that condition changes, I will reconsider. It need not expel tomorrow before it can do work today.

The qualification should be real. Repeating that you might be wrong while refusing to identify anything worth examining can become politeness wrapped around a sealed conclusion. Modest language alone does not create an opening.

## What I do not know may be nearby

The passage continues beyond the memorable account of Qu Boyu. Things arise, it says, without their root or doorway being visible. It then questions our respect for what we know when we fail to recognize our dependence on what we do not know.

Guo Xiang offers a strikingly practical explanation: what I do not know, other things may know. Relying only on my own knowledge leaves that knowledge small. Cheng Xuanying develops a different contrast between discriminating knowledge and genuine knowing.

These are not simply one ancient theory that every judgment stands on an unknown foundation destined to collapse. That metaphor may stimulate thought, but it is neither the only interpretation nor a proof that every new fact must destroy an old conclusion.

Consider arranging a meeting for two in the afternoon. It looks convenient until you learn about another time zone or a colleague's school pickup. The original judgment has encountered circumstances it did not include.

You might change the time, make part of the discussion asynchronous, or explain why this occasion cannot accommodate everyone. New knowledge can refine an arrangement rather than make all arrangements impossible.

An accountant knows where the figures are fragile. A new employee knows where the instructions first become confusing. A quiet colleague may know why someone keeps missing the meeting. Intellectual humility need not always face the universe. Sometimes it means allowing the next person to finish a sentence.

## No certificate of permanent correctness

The paragraph does not end by giving Qu Boyu victory over everyone less flexible. Its final questions turn again toward what we call so, or right. The gesture of settling the matter is itself made available for examination.

“Borrowed Words” gives a related sixty-transformations passage to Confucius within a conversation between Zhuangzi and Huizi. The changed name does not verify the detailed biography of either man. It shows a literary thought being developed through more than one figure.

The ring discussed in essay 59 offers another connection. There we asked how change and continuity could belong together. Here continuity need not mean retaining every earlier answer. The passages can illuminate each other without becoming components of one fully demonstrated psychological mechanism.

Long-held convictions are not therefore automatically suspect. Some survive repeated testing and remain worth defending. The age of a judgment does not refute it, just as the number of changes does not establish wisdom.

The question is whether reasons still matter, including reasons for keeping something rather than replacing it.

## Say what has changed

Our difficulty is that old statements remain available: messages, articles, meeting notes, documents with dates attached. The ancient passage does not tell us whether Qu Boyu faced public challenges or whether change cost him anything. Modern records simply make the pressure familiar in a particular way.

Instead of claiming that you always meant the new version, explain the movement. What supported the earlier conclusion? What has become visible? Which parts remain useful, and which no longer apply?

People affected by the earlier decision may need more than news of your personal growth. If they spent time following advice or suffered a loss, revision may require repair. “People change” should not function as a button that closes every further question.

Listeners also shape the conditions of correction. Pointing out contradictions is legitimate; asking for an explanation is useful. But if every admission becomes an opportunity for humiliation, we reward the people who keep defending what they already know is wrong.

The old passage need not always disappear. A note beside it can tell later readers where the problem was and why it mattered. That is more useful than displaying change as another personal accomplishment.

The comfort in sixty transformations is not merely that everyone makes mistakes. A person need not sink while bound to every sentence they have ever spoken. They can return, put the old sentence in its place, and speak the next one with care.
