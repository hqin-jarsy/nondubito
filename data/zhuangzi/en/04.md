On his way with a funeral procession, Zhuangzi passes Hui Shi's grave. He turns to those walking beside him and tells a story.

A man from Ying used to put a film of white plaster, thin as a fly's wing, on the tip of his nose. He would ask the craftsman Shi to remove it. Shi swung his axe with a rush of wind and cut the plaster clean away without injuring the nose. The man stood there without a change of expression.

When the ruler of Song heard of this, he summoned Shi and asked for a demonstration. Shi replied that he had once been able to perform it. But the person who made it possible had long since died.

Since Hui Shi's death, Zhuangzi tells his companions, he too has had no such partner. He has no one with whom to speak.

The axe is memorable. So is the nose. The story has room for both; its afterlife has not always been so generous.

## A skill with someone missing

“Swinging the axe like the wind” survives in Chinese as an expression for consummate skill. All the attention falls on the craftsman: his hand, his blade, his precision.

The ruler hears much the same thing. Here is a man who possesses an extraordinary ability. Bring him here and have him perform it. The request makes sense if the ability is wholly contained in the performer.

Shi's answer reveals something the request leaves out. His own hands are not the only condition. The other person was not an interchangeable spectator.

This does not make skill irrelevant. An untrained person with an axe would not become a master by finding a trusting friend. It means the skill in this particular story belongs within a relation. Remove the relation, and something the craftsman could do is no longer available to him.

## The person who does not flinch

The man from Ying “stood without losing his composure.” The narrative gives us that outward stillness, not a report of every thought that passed through his mind.

We cannot tell whether he had once been afraid, how the two had learned to work together, or what made him so steady. I read the image as one of profound trust. He has entrusted this moment to a particular person, not to the abstract prestige of expertise.

That reading should not turn the scene into a test anyone must pass. Dangerous actions do not become safe because two people trust each other. Nor does fear make someone an inferior partner. The story's power lies in the extraordinary picture it offers, not in a demand to reenact it.

It lets the usually unnoticed person become indispensable. The one who seems to be doing nothing is helping make the action possible.

## What exists between them

The phrase about the axe evokes speed and mastery. It does not establish that Shi had to swing with every ounce of strength, or that holding back would make the plaster impossible to remove. We need no invented mechanics to see the mutual dependence.

If the man moved suddenly, the situation would change. If Shi lost his precision, the consequences would change. Each person's action belongs inside the other's conditions.

There is an everyday counterpart to this. A conversation can reach a degree of candor neither participant sustains elsewhere. An idea that sounds crude when spoken alone becomes workable because someone knows where to press, where to wait, and which question would open it further.

The result can be attributed to one speaker, as if it had arrived complete inside their head. Yet it came into being in the exchange.

Such a moment does not prove that the two people trust each other perfectly in every matter. A scene of extraordinary mutual confidence remains a scene. We can value it without turning it into a certificate for the whole relationship.

## The ruler's mistake

The ruler wants the visible result. He can summon the craftsman, supply a place, and request the act. He cannot summon the history between the two people.

Shi calls the dead partner his zhi, a word associated with the thing against which an action is performed—a target, support, or counterpart. In the story, that apparently passive position belongs to a person. The bluntness of the word helps expose what the ruler has missed.

My hand is here, Shi is effectively saying, but what you saw was not simply in my hand.

There is a question for the reader in the ruler's place. When you admire a remarkable performance, do you see only the person to whom it is credited? Or can you see the people whose presence allowed it to happen?

## At Hui Shi's grave

Zhuangzi tells this story because he has lost someone.

The book gives Hui Shi a familiar role as questioner, disputant, and friend. They argue about fish, gourds, trees, and the use of what appears useless. Read as contests with a predetermined winner, these exchanges can make Hui Shi look like a foil whose job is to be defeated.

The grave changes that picture. “I have no one with whom to speak” is a strange thing to say of an opponent who contributed nothing but mistakes.

In this scene, Hui Shi is someone who could receive Zhuangzi's words with enough force of his own to make the exchange worth having. We need not conclude that Zhuangzi spoke seriously to no one else. This loss is specific enough without making it exclusive.

The disagreement and the attachment belong together. Hui Shi was not valuable because he agreed. Something Zhuangzi could say existed in the way the two met each other.

## When a room stops arguing

In a workplace, the counterpart can disappear without anyone dying. Sometimes a person becomes successful.

The success is real. But people gradually change their posture around it. Those who once argued begin asking for advice. A meeting waits for the successful person to speak, then reorganizes itself around the answer. The person still sounds fluent. There are simply fewer occasions on which fluency is interrupted by something they have not considered.

Asking for advice is not the problem. The problem is treating someone as a store of completed answers while withholding the questions that might make new thought possible. One participant supplies wisdom; everyone else collects it. The relationship has been converted into a service.

The axe remains. The other person has stepped out of the scene.

## An invitation cannot do all the work

“Please challenge me” may be a good beginning. It is not yet a safe relationship. If disagreement damages a career, earns a grudge, or closes a future opportunity, the invitation asks the listener to gamble against the evidence of the room.

The person at the center cannot appoint an equal interlocutor into existence. But they can change some of the conditions: stop punishing unwelcome information, let an objection alter a decision, admit when another person's account is better. A history can begin where a command cannot finish the job.

This connects with the River Lord's difficulty. Success can reduce the occasions on which someone shows you a sea. It can also reduce the occasions on which your own thought has anywhere substantial to go.

Freedom from all challenge is not necessarily protection. Some relationships make us able to think, speak, or act in ways that isolation does not preserve.

## Two kinds of disappearance

One counterpart leaves the organization. A colleague who spent years catching your rough ideas and sending them back improved is gone. Your technical competence may remain intact, yet the work changes.

An institution can misread that as an individual decline. It assigns a replacement and waits for the old output, as though the missing condition were a job title rather than a history between particular people.

The other disappearance happens while both remain. The colleague still attends the meeting but no longer argues. They have learned that speaking is not worth the cost, or ceased to believe anything will change.

This is why the story belongs beside the essay on performance reviews. A table may assign credit to individuals while missing the relation in which the credited work became possible. What it does not record can be dismantled in a reorganization without anyone intending to damage the work.

## An absence that cannot be transferred

The listeners in the original scene are Zhuangzi's companions. The place is a grave. He does not give them a general theory of friendship. He tells them about a craftsman who can no longer do something, and then about his own inability to speak as before.

The image allows grief to remain particular. New friendships may form. New work may become possible. None of that turns this person into a vacancy anyone else can simply fill.

Think of the work you have done best. Who was across the table? Could they disagree without ceasing to matter to you? Were you that person for them?

If someone comes to mind, their presence is not a minor supporting fact in a story about your ability. It may be part of what the ability was.

And if a piece of your work now feels missing, perhaps the first place to look is not your hand, but the other side of the room.
