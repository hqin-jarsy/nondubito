These essays began with stories I thought I knew. Two rulers help a friend and kill him. A bird receives a magnificent welcome and cannot survive it. An axe passes a man's nose, and the man does not flinch. The familiar explanations came readily enough: mean well, do harm; respect differences; admire a master craftsman.

Then something in each story kept going after the explanation had stopped.

This series follows that movement. There are sixty-seven essays, with this introduction written afterward and placed at the front. Each essay can be read on its own. You do not have to begin at the beginning, finish the sequence, or go on to the theoretical books behind it. A few minutes with one story should give you something worth taking away. You owe the series nothing for having read it.

## What the stories do

The Zhuangzi is often approached as a store of ideas: spontaneity, freedom, the useless, the limits of knowledge. Those ideas matter. But this reading pays particular attention to something that can disappear when we summarize them: the way a passage brings its reader to a realization.

Sometimes it leaves a picture where another writer might put a moral. Sometimes a teacher answers only after the other person has found a question. Sometimes a conversation breaks apart without a winner. These are not merely attractive wrappers for a doctrine. They can be part of the thinking itself.

Throughout the series I ask where a familiar explanation runs out. That is not the same as declaring previous readers wrong. An explanation may be useful and still leave a detail unexplained. Why do the inn's guests stop giving up their seats? Why can the craftsman not repeat the performance for a ruler? What happens after the River Lord admits that he has not seen enough?

The aim is to put those details back where they can trouble us.

## A measure you can test

The essays keep returning to a related question about how one person affects another. The final essay turns that question back upon the writing itself. I would rather let you encounter it at work than ask you to memorize a formula first.

Read three or four pieces, if you like, and see what their judgments have in common. If you work it out before the closing essay explains it, the explanation may no longer be necessary. That would be a good outcome, not a missed lesson.

Nor does this measure claim to encompass the whole Zhuangzi. A text may be philosophically rich, historically valuable, or beautifully written for reasons this particular reading does not bring into view.

## Four ways in

To see the approach in action, begin with essay 1, on the death of Hundun, or essay 4, on the person who stands still beneath the axe.

For a sustained reading of the seven Inner Chapters, essay 11 introduces a route developed through essays 12–29. This is the teaching sequence proposed by this series, not a curriculum handed down intact from antiquity.

For questions about the text's different voices and possible layers, begin with essay 30, on Su Shi's doubts about four chapters. These discussions examine reasons for a reading; they do not offer a certificate of authorship.

For the people who do things, begin at essay 42: a cicada catcher, a swimmer, a woodcarver, a trainer of fighting cocks. No philosophical vocabulary is required to notice what occupies their attention.

The website identifies which essays are available as editing proceeds. The original numbering stays in place even where a later essay returns to an earlier thematic group.

## A few promises, and a limit

The essays have positions. They do not pretend to say nothing. But a position need not occupy the reader's last word. Each ending offers a question to try against a life, rather than an answer to repeat back.

Contemporary examples generally leave companies and public figures unnamed. The question is what a familiar arrangement does to the people in it, including ourselves, not whether we approve of a particular institution's reputation.

Writing, value, and authorship also need to be kept apart. A passage that explains too much for my taste may still be excellent philosophy. A passage that leaves a beautiful silence is not thereby proved to be Zhuang Zhou's own work. Style can support an argument about a text's formation; it cannot settle that argument on its own.

Early catalogues record a fifty-two-chapter collection. The complete received version has thirty-three. That history involves editing and compilation, not simply nineteen chapters missing from a known author's finished manuscript. New evidence and better arguments may change what we think we know.

This series is one way of reopening the book. It is not the last one.
