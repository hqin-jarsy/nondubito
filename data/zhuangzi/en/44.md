The finished wooden object looks so extraordinary that people suspect the work of spirits. The ruler of Lu sends for its maker. What special technique did he use?

It is a reasonable question. Faced with something beautifully made, we want access to the making: a tool, a movement, a sequence, some small advantage the rest of us have missed.

Woodcarver Qing answers that he is only a craftsman. What mysterious technique could he possess? Then he adds a qualification. There is, nevertheless, something he can describe.

What follows is not a demonstration of carving. It is an account of what has to happen before the blade reaches the wood.

## A room already full of spectators

The object is called a jù. Its exact form is disputed: older commentaries describe a musical implement and also record a tradition of tiger-shaped carved wood. We need not settle its design to notice the reversal in the story. The ruler asks how Qing produces something astonishing. Qing begins with how he avoids exhausting himself before the work starts.

He undertakes a period of purification to quiet his mind. After three days, rewards and official emoluments no longer occupy him. After five, praise and blame, skill and clumsiness, no longer trouble him. After seven, he forgets his limbs and body.

These are successive stages in the narrative, not three separate retreats. Nor does the passage establish a seven-day regimen guaranteed to improve creativity. Its numbers give shape to a gradual withdrawal. First the reward leaves. Then the reviews. Finally, the person who keeps looking back at himself stops demanding an uninterrupted report.

Before Qing has looked at the wood, a crowd can already have assembled in his mind. Will the ruler approve? Will his fellow craftsmen admire the result? Can he live up to the thing he made last time? Is this stroke not only the right stroke, but evidence that he is still a good craftsman?

The wood has not yet been heard from. The evaluation of the woodcarver is well under way.

It is a familiar room. You begin a letter and, two sentences in, start composing the recipient’s disappointed expression. You cook for friends while imagining their comparison with another dinner. Wanting to do something well becomes watching yourself appear to do it well. The task still needs attention, but the spectators keep taking the better seats.

## The court is not here

Qing says something remarkable to the ruler: at this point, there is no court.

Not that the government has vanished, or that Qing has declared himself exempt from obligations. Guo Xiang’s commentary takes the line as treating the court as absent, no longer straining toward it. While he works, the next movement does not have to be submitted simultaneously to the wood and to the ruler’s anticipated expression.

This is a craftsman describing his working conditions to a sovereign. In this story, the excellent result does not come from imagining the sovereign more intensely.

It would be cruel, however, to turn that into a rule that genuine artists never think about money. Payment, deadlines, and ownership matter. Someone who depends on the work is not shallow for needing to know whether it will pay the rent. An unpaid invoice is not a spiritual failing. Neither is concern about the next month.

A more useful distinction is between attending to those conditions and letting anticipated rewards or punishments interrupt every movement. Agree on the terms. Establish what can realistically be delivered. Then, where possible, make room in which the work is not continuously auditioning for its own permission to exist.

Sometimes the conditions themselves prevent that room from opening. Improving them is part of the preparation. We cannot give the patron unlimited power to interrupt and threaten, then assign the craftsman sole responsibility for achieving serenity.

The temporary absence of the court is not necessarily a refusal of accountability. It may be one of the conditions under which there is eventually something worth accounting for.

## Forgetting the body without discarding it

On the seventh day, Qing forgets his limbs and body. Read carelessly, this can sound as though the body is an obstacle to craftsmanship.

But the eyes that examine the wood and the hands that carve it remain indispensable. Seven quiet days would not turn an untrained person into Qing. The passage describes a craftsman preparing to use an ability; it does not manufacture that ability out of stillness.

Consider an ordinary conversation. At first you monitor your posture, your voice, the strange business of where to put your hands. Then something genuinely interesting is said. You listen, answer, ask another question. Later you realize that for a while you had stopped inspecting those hands.

They had not disappeared. They had stopped being an examination you were required to pass every few seconds.

This is one way to approach the image without making a doctrine of self-erasure. It does not require ignoring pain or fatigue. Nor does a good result certify that someone has attained a superior spiritual state. The description of absorption need not become another scale on which to rank people.

The particularly unhelpful version would be to keep asking, while working, whether you have successfully forgotten yourself yet. The room of spectators would simply gain a new supervisor.

## Stillness leads outside

The crucial next step is easily missed. Qing does not finish quieting himself and immediately begin to carve. He enters the forest to examine the wood’s natural character.

The wording around the form of the wood and the envisaged object varies between transmitted texts. The older commentary nevertheless offers a clear practical reading: he identifies suitable material before putting his hand to it. If the material is not right, he stops.

Here the story leaves the territory of an exclusively inward exercise. An empty mind does not make every piece of timber suitable for every design. Reduced distraction gives Qing a better chance of noticing what this piece can sustain and what it cannot.

His knowledge is still needed. So is the encounter. A perfectly calm person who never looks properly at the wood remains capable of ruining it.

There is a close counterpart in writing about another person. You begin with a beautiful conclusion, then collect the episodes that support it. Awkward details can be put aside. Hesitant statements can be made more decisive. The resulting essay may read smoothly while the person slowly disappears from it.

Another way of writing takes longer. Listen for what the person returns to. Notice where an answer breaks off. The excellent opening you prepared may have to go. The conclusion may need to become smaller. This is not the abandonment of craft. It is craft beginning to answer to its material instead of merely defending a plan.

The analogy also has a limit that matters. A person is not wood. Timber cannot withdraw an interview or tell you that you have misunderstood it. Applied to human relationships, the story requires a further step: someone must remain free to object, to correct us, or not to become part of our work. “Following their nature” cannot license us to decide in advance what they are allowed to become.

## The skill of not proceeding

If the wood is unsuitable, Qing leaves it. This quiet qualification gives substance to everything that precedes it.

If every encounter has to end with the original plan being carried out, inspection is barely inspection. It becomes a search for authorization. Qing’s ability to recognize that he should not cut belongs with his ability to cut well.

That does not make indefinite delay a virtue. In ordinary work, other people may already be relying on a promise. A difficulty with the material should be explained, not used to justify silently abandoning them. Perhaps the design needs changing; perhaps a deadline needs renegotiating. And the power to stop is unevenly distributed. Not everyone can refuse a commission on equal terms.

Still, there is something worth protecting here: the ability to say that continuing would make the thing worse. Having begun is not, by itself, a reason to finish badly.

Qing describes the meeting of his own capacities with the wood as joining what is natural with what is natural. Older commentary does not remove human workmanship from that meeting. The work changes the wood while remaining responsive to its character. The result is neither pure imposition nor the absence of making.

The ruler wanted a secret technique. Qing has described the clearing of a space in which a technique can finally meet what it works on.

We are not told how long the carving itself takes, so we cannot conclude that preparation occupies more time than execution. What we can notice is where the narrative chooses to linger: before the first cut.

Sometimes beginning the work does not mean making the hands busy. It means finally looking properly at the wood.
