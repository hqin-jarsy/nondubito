The ruler of Lu has been trying. He studies the ways of earlier kings, continues his predecessors' work, honors spirits and worthy people, and attends to these duties without letting up. Trouble comes anyway.

When Shinan Yiliao asks why he looks anxious, the ruler lists what he has done. Beneath the ancient responsibilities is a recognizable question: I have made every effort. Why is this still happening?

The answer does not begin with an instruction to try harder. Yiliao says his approach to removing trouble is shallow, then talks about foxes and leopards.

This conversation occurs near the beginning of “The Mountain Tree,” not at the chapter's end. We return to it here because it leaves a useful question at the edge of these stories about living: what if greater self-discipline is not addressing the source of the difficulty?

## Someone wants the skin

The animals shelter in woods and rocky dens, keep out of sight, and seek food cautiously. The passage describes stillness, vigilance, and steadiness. Yet nets and traps can catch them all the same.

What crime have they committed? None. Their skins bring disaster upon them.

That sequence matters. The skin is not the crime. Being wanted by a hunter does not make the animal guilty, and having something another creature covets does not justify being harmed for it.

People in trouble often examine themselves repeatedly. Was it something I said? Should I have been more careful, more tactful, less conspicuous? Such questions sometimes help. They cannot establish in advance that every injury results from the injured person's inadequacy.

Sometimes what attracts attention is a resource, an ability someone wants to use, or a position they want to possess. Further politeness may not end the competition. That does not make precautions useless. It means precautions cannot provide the guarantee we sometimes demand of them.

## The kingdom as a skin

Is Lu not your skin, Yiliao asks its ruler?

The thing that gives him standing also exposes him. His office is where identity, power, and fear become entangled. He keeps improving his conduct as a ruler without necessarily examining how tightly he has bound himself to being this ruler.

The proposed remedy is radical. Yiliao speaks of stripping away form and skin, cleansing the heart of desire, and leaving the state and its conventions. We cannot omit that language and pretend the text never recommends relinquishment. Older commentary reads it figuratively as forgetting self and state, not as bodily injury.

We also need to notice whom the counsel addresses. A sovereign holding power is not in the same position as a person with few options who is being exploited. Asking someone powerful to possess less of other people is different from asking a vulnerable person to abandon protection.

Before advising detachment, it helps to ask who has hold of whom. Otherwise a word meant to loosen a ruler's grip can become another demand placed on those already in it.

## A country that is not a change of address

Yiliao imagines a country called Jiande, far to the south. Its people live simply, work without hoarding, and give without requiring repayment. It is a place where life can be lived and death can be met.

The ruler hears a destination and raises practical objections. There are rivers and mountains. He has no boat or carriage. Yiliao offers a different vehicle: relinquish arrogance and the insistence on remaining fixed. The ruler asks about neighbors and food; the answer again turns toward reducing expenditure and desire.

The exchange gradually makes the geography less literal. The older commentary notices the ruler's mistake in taking the journey as an actual relocation. What Yiliao proposes is another way of inhabiting a life, not a travel booking.

Then comes an expansive image: crossing rivers and seas without seeing the far shore, while those who accompany you turn back at the bank. You have gone a long way from where you began.

There are changes no one else can undergo on our behalf. But this image need not become a law that every serious change must be lonely. Friends can accompany us in meaningful ways. The passage imagines distance from old attachments; it does not require people in difficulty to refuse companionship.

## Holding people and being held

Those who possess others are burdened; those possessed by others are anxious. Guo Xiang's gloss makes the possession pointedly private: treating people as one's own.

There is a line here between relationship and ownership. Shared work requires commitments. Care may place demands on us. Neither requires one person to become another's accessory. The story invokes Yao, and the commentary does not present him as simply abandoning the world. It pictures affairs entrusted to others without the ruler privately claiming every part of them.

A manager concerned about a project is not necessarily controlling. But if a different method immediately feels like insubordination, or a colleague's departure can only mean betrayal, concern may have acquired a lock.

The lock operates in both directions. If every decision must pass through you, every departure must receive your consent, and every success must bear your name, you must remain permanently at the gate. Reducing your burden may require allowing other people a genuine door.

That is not indifference to what happens. It can be a more demanding form of participation: helping something exist without requiring it to remain an extension of you.

## The empty boat

At the end of his answer, Yiliao turns to boats. A man is crossing a river when an empty boat drifts into his vessel. Even a quick-tempered person, the story says, does not rage. Put someone aboard the approaching boat, however, and the shouting starts. One call goes unanswered, then another; by the third, curses follow.

First there was emptiness; now there is someone. Yiliao asks who could harm a person able to move through the world with an emptied self.

The comparison draws attention to something added to a collision: he could hear me, he meant to do this, this is an affront to me. A physical event rapidly acquires a personal story.

But the passage does not measure equal impacts or prove identical losses. In life, an occupied boat may have a different capacity to avoid collision, and responsibility matters. An empty boat can cause harm. Someone who fails to respond may not hear, may be unable to act, or may genuinely be negligent. The relevant facts still need examination.

The final move toward emptying oneself is also more than a trick of imagining every other person innocent. It asks how we enter a shared world. Must every encounter preserve an untouchable image of ourselves? Must the people around us be brought under our control before we can feel secure?

Loosening that demand may prevent needless collisions. It does not make a person disappear, provide an excuse for evading accountability, or guarantee immunity from harm.

## A boundary can remain

You receive an abrupt message and prepare an even sharper reply. After a pause, you notice that the sender is asking about a particular unfinished task, not issuing a total verdict on you. Separating the task from the imagined insult may produce a shorter, more useful answer.

Another message may actually be insulting, threatening, or part of a repeated violation of boundaries. Its answer need not be further self-erasure. Stating a limit, keeping a record, seeking support, or leaving may be ways of attending clearly to what is happening.

Anger sometimes protects something worth protecting. We can examine its object and decide what to do without first declaring every angry person insufficiently wise.

The episode does not tell us what the ruler does afterward. We need not claim certainty about why the author leaves this unwritten. For us, the unfinished business remains useful. The question travels back into a life that cannot be settled by one picturesque answer.

Someone may want your skin. Someone else may merely have bumped into you. A third person may have a legitimate claim on your responsibility. These situations do not all require the same response.

Becoming a little emptier need not mean becoming less of a person. It may mean having less armor made of rank, possession, and compulsory deference, while retaining the right to protect the person inside it.
