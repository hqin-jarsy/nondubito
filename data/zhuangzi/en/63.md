Every hour has a purpose. Your calendar contains no gaps, so the day ought to work efficiently. Then a call runs ten minutes late, a small task becomes complicated, and the appointments begin falling into one another.

You have not forgotten to schedule important work. You have removed the time that seemed to be doing nothing.

In “External Things,” Huizi raises a similar question about usefulness. Your words are useless, he tells Zhuangzi. Instead of listing his contributions, Zhuangzi directs attention toward the ground underfoot.

## The ground you are not standing on

The earth is vast, but a standing person occupies only enough room for their feet. Suppose everything around that patch were excavated down to the depths. Would what remained still be useful?

Huizi says no. Zhuangzi draws the conclusion: now the usefulness of what seemed useless is also clear.

The older subcommentary imagines the person trembling and unable to move. Using your feet requires surrounding space. We do not need to add a mechanical law declaring that the pillar of soil must immediately collapse. The thought experiment distinguishes occupying a small patch from being able to live and move there.

Not being directly underfoot is not the same as doing nothing. Define use too narrowly and you may remove the conditions that make the useful thing usable.

This scene is not chiefly about pretending to lack ability so that no one will cut you down. It asks what an immediately useful thing depends on beyond the area marked as useful. Older commentary can connect it with preserving life, but the image need not be reduced to a technique of concealment.

## Two participants, two different steps

The exchange is brief and beautifully timed. But saying that Huizi supplies the whole conclusion and Zhuangzi adds only a few words would conceal how it works.

Huizi's answer concerns the excavated situation: it would be useless. Zhuangzi then turns that answer toward the usefulness of the apparently useless. He explicitly states a conclusion, not merely a neutral acknowledgment.

Both people participate, but they do not perform the same step. Zhuangzi chooses a directed question and identifies what follows from the reply. Admiring Huizi's involvement does not require erasing the guidance.

Guidance is not necessarily a problem. A useful question often comes from someone who has already noticed a connection. What matters is whether the listener can inspect that connection and say that the example does not fit, rather than merely supplying the expected response.

The difference between clarification and indoctrination cannot depend only on whose mouth finally pronounces the answer. Carefully sequenced questions can make someone repeat a conclusion chosen in advance. Conversely, a plainly stated reason can leave room for genuine assessment.

The exchange gives us an image to try, not a guarantee that every question-shaped lesson respects its listener.

## The fish has already made its point

Another episode in the chapter begins with Zhuangzi borrowing grain. The official promises a large sum once revenues arrive. Zhuangzi responds with a fish stranded in a cart rut.

The fish needs a little water to survive. Instead it receives a grand promise to bring the waters of a distant river. Better look for me among the dried fish, it replies.

The story does not require an additional sentence announcing that help must meet the present need before it can be understood. Zhuangzi's anger, the fish's anger, and the two magnificent promises already illuminate one another.

Readers may notice different things. That does not justify assuming that most see only an irritated complaint about borrowing. Guo Xiang's comment explicitly concerns appropriateness: something fitting is not too small; something unfitting gains nothing by being large.

The mismatch remains familiar. Someone asks whether you can arrive this afternoon, and you offer an account of your long-term commitment. Someone needs help completing one immediate step, and you provide a comprehensive plan they have no strength to begin.

The goodwill may be real. The proposed gift may be substantial. Yet it has not arrived at the time or in the form in which it is needed.

Long-term help need not therefore be dismissed. The two times can be distinguished: water today, a discussion of rivers tomorrow. A large future promise should not cancel the smaller present need it was supposed to answer.

## The great fish comes with an argument

Nearby, the young lord of Ren prepares an enormous hook and line, using fifty cattle as bait. He waits a year before catching a fish whose struggle raises mountainous waves. Once divided and preserved, it feeds people across a great region.

This is extravagant literature, not a reliable forecast of investment and return. A long wait does not guarantee a great catch. A large undertaking does not automatically have more value than a small one.

The passage also continues beyond the impressive waves. It compares the undertaking with fishing for small creatures in narrow waterways, then explicitly discusses the distance between embellished minor discourse and larger understanding. Its ending concerns engagement with the world.

We can question the argument without pretending that the story lacks an interpretive conclusion. Guo's commentary, moreover, allows that large and small have different appropriate uses. That gives us a reason not to reduce the whole passage to the superiority of scale.

A contemporary reader might find encouragement for patient preparation while still examining the goal, conditions, and costs. Patience need not become permission to stop checking whether a project makes sense.

These neighboring episodes do not all need tails of the same length. Some connections are already vivid. Others benefit from explanation. An additional paragraph should be assessed by what it contributes, not condemned merely for arriving after a story.

## What an empty hour supports

Return to the crowded calendar. An unassigned hour may allow a conversation to settle or prevent one delay from being passed to the next person. It has no separate achievement to display, but it helps the scheduled achievements remain possible.

A book that has not produced an obvious application may change how you ask questions. A practice with no immediate financial return may restore some flexibility to your attention. Such things cannot always issue a certificate of usefulness on demand.

But this does not mean that everything unused must be kept forever. Accumulation can crowd out life too. An old arrangement may no longer support anything. The ground underfoot is not a permanent exemption for every item in storage.

Before removing something, look at its relationships with the rest. What will become harder without it? What new room might its removal create? The answer can differ from one case to another.

People, especially, should not become a reserve inventory of possible future uses. A friend is not valuable because they might eventually introduce you to someone important. They need not prove that they are the ground beneath your feet to deserve a place in your life.

The image helps us notice conditions. It should not quietly turn other people into nothing but conditions for our own activity.

## The sentence to add, the place to stop

When revising an explanation, ask what the final paragraph actually does. Does it supply missing background, correct a consequential misunderstanding, or simply demand a more solemn acceptance of a point already made?

The fourth repetition may add little to the first three. Yet a familiar term may be entirely new to a reader. Omitting its meaning is not necessarily elegant. What bores an experienced person can be the sentence through which a beginner finally enters.

There is no need to choose between saying everything and explaining nothing. Necessary material can remain available without crowding every turn of the main account. Say enough to make the next step possible, then let the reader take it.

An essay need not live the reader's life on their behalf. It should not use that limitation to excuse avoidable obscurity either. Leaving space is different from removing the steps.

Sometimes a clear judgment is exactly what should be written. Give its reasons, identify where the comparison stops, and allow someone not to follow. Readers do not gain freedom merely by having to guess what an author means.

The small patch beneath Huizi's feet has not grown. The ground around it has become visible again. Before clearing every supposedly useless part from a day, it may be worth pausing: some places are not being stepped on because they are what make another step possible.
