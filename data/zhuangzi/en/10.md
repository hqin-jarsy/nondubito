Zhuangzi is approaching death. His disciples want to give him an elaborate burial.

He replies that heaven and earth will be his coffin, the sun and moon his paired jade discs, the stars his jewels, and all things his funeral offerings. With such complete arrangements, what could they possibly add?

The disciples remain worried. Birds might eat their teacher.

Above ground, he says, the birds eat me. Below ground, the insects do. Taking from one group to give to the other—why are you so partial?

This is a scene in the chapter “Lie Yukou,” not a verified transcript from a deathbed. Still, something vividly personal survives in its tone. The disciples are preparing for solemnity. Their teacher is apparently concerned about fair treatment for birds and insects.

## More than being at peace

An account of Zhuangzi's attitude toward death often begins with equanimity. He accepts change; he does not cling to life. The passage can certainly be read in that direction.

But reducing it to acceptance makes the conversation disappear.

The disciples have not come to examine his philosophical credentials. They want to do something. A substantial funeral would express their affection and respect. Zhuangzi is not simply reporting his state of mind; he is answering their proposed arrangement.

The manner of the answer matters.

He does not instruct everyone to stop crying or require them to demonstrate equal detachment. The narrator does not inventory his feelings. We need not infer from a joke that a dying person experiences no fear or sorrow.

What the scene does show is that even here, another angle of conversation remains possible. Death is enormous, but it has not crowded every other way of speaking out of the room.

## Not too little, but already so much

The straightforward response to an expensive burial would be to say that it is unnecessary. Zhuangzi does something stranger. He enlarges the funeral beyond anything the disciples could arrange.

You fear that I will not have enough, his image suggests. Yet I am already within a world this vast. Your coffin and ceremonial objects, however fine, do not exhaust my relationship with what surrounds me.

The disciples' care is not simply dismissed as wastefulness. Instead, an elaborate funeral loses its claim to be the only sufficient expression of that care.

He changes the picture before arguing over its details.

Of course, a person is also entitled to say no directly. A refusal does not become legitimate only after being delivered with exquisite wit. We can appreciate the possibility this scene offers without making eloquence another duty imposed on someone who wants to decline.

Sometimes another person plans something for us so thoughtfully that it becomes difficult to explain we do not want it. The thoughtfulness is real. Whether the arrangement fits remains a question for the person who must inhabit it.

Zhuangzi does not withdraw from that conversation merely because his disciples mean well.

## What the ground conceals

The fear of birds is understandable. When someone close to us dies, their body does not instantly become an ordinary object. Wanting to protect it can be part of love.

Zhuangzi directs attention below the ground. Burial does not remove a body from natural change. It places some of that change out of sight.

The disciples' apparent choice—protect the teacher or allow him to be eaten—is recast as a choice between creatures above and creatures below.

This is not practical guidance for contemporary funerals. Nor does it establish that all arrangements are equivalent in emotional, social, or public-health terms. There are real differences between them.

The joke reaches a more specific illusion: that sufficiently careful arrangements could keep a body permanently outside change.

Once that impossible promise is withdrawn, there are still things to do with care. They no longer have to accomplish everything.

## A way to remain together

I like the final joke for more than its audacity.

Zhuangzi does not leave the disciples classified as spiritually inadequate. He brings birds and insects into the conversation as though they were neighbors waiting for a share. The determination to protect him absolutely encounters a small opening through which it might loosen.

That does not mean the disciples laugh. The text does not say they do. One of them might hear the point and remain miserable. Understanding another person's meaning does not instantly remove the pain of losing them.

The scene therefore need not become a debate with a winner. Zhuangzi expresses his wish; the disciples' concern remains visible. People approaching separation do not have to arrive at identical feelings before they can understand one another.

The voice is moving. It is not, for that reason, proof of authorship. Literary force gives us a reason to read closely; identifying a historical author requires other evidence. Our response to a passage cannot serve as its certificate of origin.

We can let it affect us without pretending to know more than we do.

## What we leave behind

A workplace handover is a long way from a deathbed. Leaving a team is not dying. But a smaller farewell can contain a recognizable anxiety: what will they do to this after I leave?

We write notes, explain procedures, record risks. Much of this is essential. Safety requirements, unfinished promises, and obligations to others cannot be abandoned under the elegant pretext of letting go.

Something else can enter the handover alongside that necessary work: the wish that every method we used should remain unchanged.

The two kinds of legacy can look similar. One equips the next person to understand why something was done, so they can judge a new situation. The other continually asks them to prove that they are faithfully reproducing the person who left.

We have always done it this way can convey valuable experience. It can also shelter an arrangement from questions after the reasons for it have ceased to apply.

Zhuangzi's joke does not teach us to leave no instructions. It prompts a different distinction. Am I trying to help this work continue well, or to ensure that the particular shape I gave it never changes?

A good record can preserve reasons while permitting revision. A bad legacy can preserve a gesture while making its reasons inaccessible. The amount of documentation alone will not tell us which we have made.

## Love has more than one proof

Returning to the disciples makes the question gentler.

Their wish for a grand funeral need not be a wish to display their own devotion. It need not be a scheme to imprison their teacher in ritual. The story does not supply those motives. We can first allow their reluctance to lose him to remain what it is.

Even love, though, can ask whether the form it insists upon is wanted by its recipient. If it is not, what other expression remains possible?

This recalls the ruler of Lu's attempt to care for a seabird. A magnificent arrangement and a life suited to its recipient do not automatically coincide. Here, unlike the bird, the person being provided for can answer.

Zhuangzi states his preference and offers another picture. The disciples need not become incapable of action. They can stop treating one particular form as the sole acceptable proof that they care.

We should also resist inventing the rest of the record. Because this conversation does not describe practical arrangements, it does not follow that the historical Zhuangzi left none. We are reading the selection made by a story, not a complete file of last wishes.

## The assumption inside the choice

There is a small action here that can travel into everyday life. When a choice is presented, look for what both sides already assume.

A grand funeral or a modest one. Accept the gift or reject the giver. Follow my method or ruin everything. Sometimes the alternatives differ decisively. Sometimes they share an unexamined premise that is doing much of the work.

Ding meets difficulty by looking again at where his blade must go. Fan's ruler separates an event from a verdict. Here Zhuangzi asks whether the thing the disciples hope to preserve can be permanently secured in the way they propose.

Noticing common ground does not erase differences. We must still choose, care for survivors, and keep commitments. But the choice may be relieved of a task it could never complete.

A funeral can help people say goodbye without guaranteeing that death has somehow been defeated. A handover can transmit experience without making the future repeat the past. A gift can contain genuine care even if the recipient declines its particular form.

## A place to pause

This scene is not the final sentence of the entire Zhuangzi. Nor need it be treated as the philosopher's last historically established utterance. It is the place I have chosen to pause after these first ten encounters.

From Hundun onward, we have met people trying to do things well, and watched some of those efforts do too much on another person's behalf. There are deaths, departures, and conversations that continue. The stories do not all offer the same answer.

I do not want to fold them into ten rules for life. Explanation can be useful; there is no need to pretend otherwise. But after an explanation, you can still disagree, return to the original, and find something I have missed.

Zhuangzi offers heaven and earth as his coffin. His disciples worry about the birds. He replies by mentioning the insects.

Solemnity has not vanished. It simply no longer occupies the entire room. There is still a little space for a joke, and for someone else to go on thinking.
