# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (20): The Fake Fossil That Lasted Forty-One Years

## 1. Brown Flint

On December 18, 1912, a paper by Charles Dawson and a colleague was read at the Geological Society of London. Dawson recalled walking along a farm road near Piltdown Common in Sussex, southern England, some years earlier. The road had been made with what he called peculiar brown flints.

He followed them to a nearby pit where men were digging gravel. The paper concerned pieces of a skull and a jaw from the gravel beds there. The new scientific name meant Dawson’s dawn man. In the newspapers it was Piltdown Man.

On November 21, 1953, the Natural History department of the British Museum issued a paper by three researchers. They found that the jaw and canine tooth had been faked. **From the reading of the paper on December 18, 1912, to the exposure on November 21, 1953, it took almost forty-one years.**

A few other things happened in the years leading up to and during those forty-one years. In 1866, the German scientist Ernst Haeckel put a genus meaning ape-man into a table. In 1891, an excavation overseen by the Dutch army doctor Eugène Dubois brought up a skullcap by the Solo River in Java. In 1906, a man was exhibited in the monkey house of a New York zoo. In 1925 a child’s skull from South Africa was published.

From 1866 to 1953 is eighty-seven years, about the span of a long life. **Over those years the name “missing link” was attached to the skullcap from Java. In New York, while a living man was in the news, someone said the supposed link would never be found alive. Two men also argued over whether one of them had first used the name for the South African skull.**

## 2. A Chain

A chain starts with God and the angels. Below come humans, beasts, birds, fish, insects and things neither eye nor instrument can see. Remove any link and the chain fails. This is the picture in *An Essay on Man*, the long poem whose installments were complete in London by 1734. One line calls out to the vast chain of being, which begins with God.

*On the Origin of Species* used a chain in 1859 to explain the incompleteness of the fossil record, but not to put living species in a row. Darwin referred to a nearly complete chain of intermediate links. In *The Descent of Man* in 1871, he recounted an objection that there was a great break in the organic chain between humans and their closest relatives.

In 1933, an American historian of ideas gave a series of lectures at Harvard. The book that came out of them in 1936 was called *The Great Chain of Being*. He described an infinite number of links disposed in hierarchical order. **Before a link can be missing, there has to be a chain. The eighteenth-century chain ran all the way from God to nothingness.**

## 3. A Name in a Table

On February 16, 1894, Haeckel turned sixty. A painter gave him an oil painting. In the wild stands a thick-haired, apelike man, upright. Beside him his companion nurses a child. The original is at the Ernst Haeckel memorial in Jena.

The family in the picture corresponds to a name Haeckel put into a table twenty-eight years earlier. He published *General Morphology* in 1866. A classification table at the end of volume two divided primates into smaller groups. Among the tailless ones he listed a third family, the upright ones, or humans. In parentheses were two genera: one he had coined meaning ape-man, and the genus *Homo*.

There were no fossils under the new genus. In *The History of Creation* in 1868, Haeckel put human origins into stages. The twenty-first was “Ape-men, or speechless primitive men.”

He placed this stage between the manlike apes and true humans. The creature walked erect but did not produce articulated speech. A later English translation preserves his view that such beings very probably had existed.

A two-part scientific name meaning speechless ape-man also circulates under Haeckel’s name. An American science library uses it in its description of the painting. An American popular science monthly that wrote about the painting in 1894 used a name with one extra word. **In 2023, a researcher went through Haeckel’s 1868 and 1877 books word by word and reported that the combination was not actually there.**

In a lecture in 1898, Haeckel called Dubois’s discovery in Java the long-sought missing link. He said he had hypothesized the genus back in 1866. **A genus in an 1866 table, a stage in an 1868 book: calling Dubois’s discovery the missing link came later, in Haeckel’s look back in 1898.**

## 4. The Left Bank of the Solo

Trinil, Java, on the left bank of the Solo River. According to the account Dubois gave in 1895, the river channel had cut more than ten meters into the ground. The fossils were in deposits of volcanic ash and gravel. The working records say that a skullcap came out here in October 1891.

A Dutch historical study gives the departure as October 29, 1887. The Dutch physician Eugène Dubois, with his wife and children, sailed for the Dutch East Indies on the *Prinses Amalia*. He meant to find early fossils of the human lineage. To get there he joined the colonial army as a medical officer. He reached Sumatra first, and a little over a year later received permission to do paleontological work in Sumatra and Java.

From about September 1, 1891, Corporal Gerardus Kriele supervised the digging on the Trinil bank. A second military assistant joined later. Dubois said the dig reached the fossil-bearing layer in August 1891. A wisdom tooth came out in September. About a month later, a skullcap came out of the same layer about a meter away.

The rainy season stopped work. Digging resumed in May 1892. In August a left thighbone was found about fifteen meters from the skullcap. In the annual report for 1892, Dubois put these pieces in an existing genus as *Anthropopithecus erectus*. The heading in his 1894 monograph gave *Pithecanthropus erectus*, a new genus and species.

In 1895, his paper in English was titled “A Transitional Form between Man and the Apes.” That year an American paleontologist writing in the *American Journal of Science* called it a missing link in the proper sense. A Batavia newspaper had already complained in 1893 that Dubois was making a connection between bones that had no proven association. **The genus meaning ape-man was twenty-five years older than the skullcap from Trinil.**

## 5. The Monkey House

On September 9, 1906, *The New York Times* reported on the scene the previous day in the monkey house of the Bronx Zoo. Its headline called it a Bushman sharing a cage with Bronx Park apes. The article mentioned a bow and arrows and a hammock at his side, and a daytime crowd of three to five hundred.

The man’s name was Ota Benga. An American named Samuel Verner brought him from the Congo to the United States in 1904. He was exhibited at the St. Louis World’s Fair. In 2020 the Wildlife Conservation Society, which runs the zoo, described his removal to the United States as forcible. In July of that year it issued a statement apologizing for and condemning his treatment.

The *Times* transcribed the sign at the monkey house on September 10, 1906. It began with “African Pygmy,” followed by his name and an age of twenty-three. It listed his height and weight, said Verner had brought him from the Kasai River in the Congo Free State, and announced that he would be exhibited every afternoon in September.

A *Times* editorial on September 11 said his people stood low in a scale of humanity. That day the paper also recorded the objection of James Gordon, a Black minister: he did not want a person from his race exhibited with monkeys.

Black people were already oppressed enough, Gordon said, and ought to be regarded as people with souls. In the same report, Verner claimed he wanted to show that Ota Benga was a person with intelligence, not that he resembled an ape. The missing link, he added, would never be found alive.

The zoo director told a reporter it was arranged purely as an ethnological exhibit. He said he believed in evolution but denied intending to use Ota Benga to show a likeness between humans and apes.

In the Wildlife Conservation Society’s 2020 account, Ota Benga was at the zoo for about a month. On September 28, 1906, Gordon arranged for him to leave for an orphanage for Black children. He was exhibited in the monkey house for a few days in the week of September 8. **That week the Bronx Zoo in New York put Ota Benga in the monkey house, hung up a sign with his age, height and weight, and let people crowd around to look. The exhibit was a human being.**

## 6. Blasted Out of Limestone

Late in 1924, in Johannesburg, South Africa, one of the anatomist Raymond Dart’s students brought him a fossil monkey skull. Dart learned that it had been blasted out of limestone at a quarry at Taung, about fifteen meters below the surface.

The fossil later called the Taung Child had been blasted out the same way. A 2026 study says the miner who freed it from the rock went unnamed. A 2025 study found that a quarry worker called De Bruyn collected fossils, noticed that two pieces were not like the usual baboon remains, and kept them.

The quarry manager showed them to a visiting geologist. The geologist took the key pieces back with him or arranged for them to be boxed and sent to Johannesburg. **Between the rock face and Johannesburg, the pieces went through the hands of an unnamed miner, a quarry worker, a manager and a geologist.**

In 1959, Dart remembered the boxes arriving on the day of a wedding, and opening them at once. A 2025 study compared his recollection with newspapers from 1925. The details do not line up. Forty days and forty nights and the use of his wife’s knitting needles to pick away the stone come mostly from Dart’s later telling and from retellings.

On February 7, 1925, Dart published the fossil in *Nature* and named it *Australopithecus africanus*. The subtitle called it a man-ape from South Africa. He had the bones of a child’s face and a natural brain cast. Sediment inside the skull had hardened into rock, preserving the shape of the brain’s surface.

A week later, a notice in *Nature* said the press had chosen its own name, Taung Man. That September Dart replied in *Nature* to the London anatomist Arthur Keith, who had tried to show that Dart was the first to call the skull a missing link. Dart said those were not his words.

## 7. More Than Twenty Years

In the same year, one man who had not seen the original and one who had said different things. On February 14, 1925, Keith wrote in *Nature* that many readers had doubts about Dart’s report. He wanted to see the fossil or an accurate cast first. A researcher in South Africa who had examined the original wrote in *Nature* that the specimen had its own distinctive features.

Beginning in 1936, that researcher found more fossils of the same kind at Sterkfontein. In 1946, a British anatomist described how after Taung many anatomists had chosen to wait for more material, preferably an organized series of specimens.

On June 29, 1946, he reconsidered the South African fossils in *Nature*. A detailed account he published in an anatomy journal in 1947 came after he had seen the specimens in South Africa himself. On March 15, 1947, Keith wrote in *Nature* that Dart had been right and he had been wrong.

The Smithsonian’s Human Origins Program says it took more than twenty years after Dart named the species in 1925 for researchers to broadly accept australopiths as part of the human family. **For those twenty-odd years while people waited on the Taung skull, Piltdown had not yet been exposed.**

## 8. Fluorine, Nitrogen and File Marks

In 1953, three researchers examined the Piltdown skull, jaw and canine again. Under a microscope, the chewing surfaces of the jaw’s teeth were unusually flat and carried fine scratches. The canine carried nearly vertical scratches too. The authors described surfaces marked as if by an abrasive.

The longer bones lie in the ground, the more fluorine they can take up from groundwater and the less nitrogen they retain. At Piltdown, fluorine was about one part in a thousand in the skull but under three parts in ten thousand in the jaw and canine. The jaw held about as much nitrogen as fresh bone.

Iron had stained the skull deep inside. The stain on the jaw was only skin-deep. **The researchers concluded that the jaw and canine were deliberate fakes.** What kind of ape had the jaw come from? The 1953 paper said chimpanzee or orangutan. London’s Natural History Museum later identified it as orangutan, and the skull as human. A 2016 study put together the finds from both Piltdown sites. It concluded that the forgery probably used bones from one orangutan and at least two humans.

The 1953 paper began by noting that Piltdown had been disputed for about forty years and that many anthropologists had been skeptical or frankly puzzled. It ended by calling the forgery exceptionally skillful. There have been several theories about who made it. The 2016 study, with participation from London’s Natural History Museum, found consistent methods at the two sites, suggesting that one person was probably responsible. The clues pointed to Dawson.

Nearly seventy years after Piltdown was exposed, a Xinhua report of September 7, 2023, used the words missing link. In that report they referred to a stretch from 950,000 to 650,000 years ago from which very few fossils survive.

**First an empty spot is set out in the chain. A skullcap, a jaw with filed teeth, and a stone preserving the cast of a brain are brought to it, one after the other. None quite fits the empty spot.**

What Dawson saw on the farm road was a collection of peculiar brown flints. What the researchers found in 1953 was that the reddish-brown color on the Piltdown bones had been put there.

The line was arranged, not walked.
