# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (11): Where Did Peking Man’s Skulls Go?

## 1. A Morning on the Han River

On the morning of May 18, 2022, by the Han River in Yunxian, Hubei, excavators saw something that looked like a skull in the dirt. They made a video call to the project leader, Lu Chengqiu. According to Lu’s later account, he set out from Wuhan and reached the site that afternoon. Half the skull was exposed, half still in the layer where it lay. You could already make out the brow ridge and the eye sockets.

This was the third skull from Xuetangliangzi. Two others, found in 1989 and 1990, had been crushed out of shape. In the line in Chinese textbooks, there are often only one or two names left between *Homo erectus* and *Homo sapiens*. On the then-common estimate of about 800,000 to 1.1 million years ago, the Yunxian skulls, like Peking Man, belonged to this stretch of time. **But the main collection of Peking Man skulls went into wooden crates in 1941 to be taken out of China. Their whereabouts have been unknown ever since.** Some skulls can be held in the hand. Others survive only as casts. Even with the original, the shape of the bone, the age of the layer and the proteins in the bone do not always tell the same story.

The period runs roughly from one million years ago to about 130,000 years ago. There are more than 800,000 years in between, enough to fit all of recorded history into it some 160 times.

Hominin fossils from these years are scattered across a great sweep of land, from Morocco’s Atlantic coast to caves in northern Spain and on to the Han River and the northeastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau. Many sites, many bones, and more than one family tree to put them on.

Eighteen specialists from different fields met to discuss the period’s deposits, environments and changes in culture. In the 1975 conference volume, the anthropologist Glynn Isaac called his closing review “Sorting Out the Muddle in the Middle.” **Researchers who study hominin fossils borrowed the phrase. These years are often called the muddle in the middle.**

## 2. On the Moroccan Coast

At the edge of Casablanca is a quarry called Thomas Quarry I. There is a cave inside. In 2026, a team led by Jean-Jacques Hublin published fossils from the cave: two adult jaws, a child’s jaw, teeth, a thighbone and vertebrae. According to Reuters reporting from the site, there were carnivore tooth marks on a thighbone. The researchers thought large carnivores had once used the cave.

The age came from Earth’s magnetic field. The north and south magnetic poles have switched many times. The cave deposits caught the most recent complete reversal. The team put the fossils at around 770,000 years ago, with an uncertainty of a few thousand years. In the team’s public account, these people stood near the point on the tree where the African and Eurasian hominin branches split.

The team did not name a new species. It told the public that the fossils were consistent with an African sister population to *Homo antecessor*, another group of hominins known from a cave in northern Spain.

To the east in Ethiopia is the Bodo skull, about 600,000 years old. In 1986, researchers compared its marks with cuts made in fresh bone by experimental stone tools. They read the marks as evidence that someone deliberately removed flesh from the head.

Farther south, a skull turned up at a lead and zinc mine in Kabwe, Zambia, in 1921. Mining later destroyed the original cave, and its deposits cannot be reconstructed. In 2020, researchers dated material from the skull itself and got about 300,000 years.

## 3. A Gap Eighteen Centimeters Wide

In South Africa’s Rising Star cave is a passage about eighteen centimeters wide at its narrowest, roughly the span of a hand. To get into it, Lee Berger put out a public call for slim people with experience excavating caves. Of more than sixty applicants, six were selected, all women. They came to be called “underground astronauts.”

In the chamber beyond the passage, they recovered more than 1,500 pieces of bone, representing at least fifteen individuals. In 2015 the team named them *Homo naledi*. Their brains were about the size of those of australopiths.

Dating published in 2017 placed *naledi* between a little over 330,000 and a little over 230,000 years ago. The paper followed that with a line saying further work was needed.

In 2023, the team proposed that *naledi* dug pits and placed its dead in them, made engravings on the cave walls and used fire. The reviewers found that the evidence had not ruled out natural processes. The following year the critics put it in their title: there was no scientific evidence that *Homo naledi* buried its dead and made rock art.

At about the same time in North Africa, there was Jebel Irhoud, Morocco. A later account says that in 1961 barite miners took a skull out of the cave wall. In 2017, Hublin’s team published new fossils and new ages. Burned flint from the same layer as the fossils dated to a little over 310,000 years ago.

Hublin explained to the public that researchers had once expected to find a single cradle of human origins. The paper’s title spoke of a pan-African origin for *Homo sapiens*.

**Around 300,000 years ago, behind that narrow gap in South Africa was a kind of hominin with a brain about the size of an australopith’s. In a mine cave in North Africa was a hominin regarded as an early part of our own branch. The age ranges overlap.**

## 4. The Pit of Bones

In the mountains of Atapuerca in northern Spain, it is about five hundred meters from a cave entrance to a pit deep inside. The last stretch is a vertical shaft about thirteen meters deep, the height of four or five stories. Human and bear bones were mixed together in the pit. Its Spanish name, Sima de los Huesos, means “Pit of Bones.”

Systematic excavation recovered at least twenty-eight hominin individuals from about 430,000 years ago. Their skulls and teeth have a number of Neanderthal features.

In 2014, researchers read mitochondrial DNA from a thighbone in the pit. This is the small portion of DNA passed down through the mother’s line, and it was closer to Denisovan mitochondrial DNA. In 2016 the same group read the much larger set of DNA in the nucleus, half from each parent. That gave a different answer: closer to Neanderthals.

Why the two answers? One possibility the authors raised was that the little piece passed down through the maternal line in Neanderthals was later replaced altogether. **Bones from one pit, two family trees, the same research team.**

Another cave in the same mountains, Gran Dolina, yielded older hominin fossils in 1994. In 1997 the team named them *Homo antecessor*, calling them a possible ancestor of Neanderthals and living humans in the title of the paper. Later dating put *antecessor* at about 950,000 to 770,000 years ago.

In 2020, researchers read proteins from *antecessor* tooth enamel. On the protein tree it fell next to the group containing later *Homo sapiens*, Neanderthals and Denisovans as a closely related sister branch. It was not in the place proposed in 1997. **The possible ancestor of 1997 became a neighboring sister branch on the protein tree of 2020.** The lead author cautioned that this assumes the protein tree does in fact tell us about kinship.

## 5. Two Trees

Which tree should the Yunxian skulls hang on? In 2025, a team compared the shape of a reconstruction of Skull 2, feature by feature, with a large set of other skulls. They drew a family tree. On it the Yunxian skull fell near the base of the *Homo longi* branch, the one that includes the Harbin skull. That branch and the one leading to *Homo sapiens* split a little over 1.3 million years ago.

The authors wrote that it might preserve transitional features from around the origin of the branch. Common ancient-genome estimates put the split between our branch and the branch of Neanderthals and Denisovans at a little over 500,000 to a little over 700,000 years ago, much later. An American paleoanthropologist commenting on the tree said it did not fit the relationships established with DNA. The team said testing it would take more fossils from around a million years ago, especially ancient DNA from the *Homo longi* branch.

In 2026, another paper redated Yunxian using quartz pebbles. It put the skulls at about 1.77 million years old and called them the oldest *Homo erectus* skulls from East Asia found in their original deposits. The 2025 tree used the old framework of around a million years. If the new date holds, Yunxian falls outside the period discussed here. **One paper calls the Yunxian skulls *Homo erectus*; another puts Skull 2 on the *Homo longi* branch. The split drawn from shape is hundreds of thousands of years older than the one estimated from DNA.**

## 6. Casts

Winter 1929, Zhoukoudian. Pei Wenzhong dug out the first complete Peking Man skullcap. His telegram had one line that mattered most: he had just found a skull, remarkably complete, and very human in appearance. Later accounts describe a cold day, the light fading and candles lit in the cave. Even the exact day has been given differently in commemorations.

By 1941, Zhoukoudian had yielded several skulls as well as jaws, teeth and limb bones. As war intensified, Peking Union Medical College packed the main collection in wooden crates. The plan was to put it under U.S. military escort and ship it out of China through Qinhuangdao. According to later reporting on the archives, the specimens were wrapped in cotton and gauze. No verified trace of the crates has turned up since.

Buried at a military camp, captured by the Japanese army, lost at sea: there is no complete chain of evidence for any of the accounts. In 2012, Berger and colleagues revisited a statement by a U.S. Marine, calling it a credible report in their paper’s title. **To study that collection today is to use the plaster casts Franz Weidenreich made in Beijing eight or nine decades ago, and his detailed descriptions.**

A few teeth had been taken to Sweden earlier and are kept in Uppsala. Zhoukoudian yielded more bones after 1949. From the material at Zhoukoudian and elsewhere, Weidenreich argued for regional continuity between Asian *Homo erectus* and later populations in Asia.

In 2026, Qiaomei Fu and colleagues read proteins from the enamel of six *Homo erectus* teeth from Zhoukoudian, Hexian in Anhui, and Sunjiadong in Henan. Think of a protein as a string of letters. All six specimens shared changes at two positions. One was not seen in the known Denisovan material; the other was present in some of it. Considering their ages and shapes alongside known genetic variants, the authors rejected the idea that these specimens were early Denisovans. They also proposed that an older ancestry component in Denisovan genomes likely came from a population related to these *Homo erectus* individuals.

## 7. The Denisovan Map

In the account pieced together later, a monk from Xiahe, Gansu, found a robust jaw in Baishiya Karst Cave in 1980. He gave it to a local reincarnate lama. It passed through several hands before reaching researchers at Lanzhou University. The cave mouth is about 3,280 meters above sea level.

The jaw had been removed from its original deposit. Researchers could date only a mineral crust on its surface that looked like limescale. It was at least 160,000 years old. After analyzing ancient proteins in the jaw, the authors said it was closely related to the Denisovan population from Denisova Cave.

Researchers later read Denisovan mitochondrial DNA from soil in the cave. Some dated to around 100,000 years ago and some was more recent. The latest layer might date to about 45,000 years ago.

The Harbin skull is at least about 140,000 years old. In 2025, proteins from the skull and mitochondrial DNA from hardened dental plaque both pointed to a Denisovan attribution. Researchers compared the chemistry of the skull with fossils and deposits in the Harbin region. The most they could say was that it came from somewhere in that area.

There was the story of the bridge worker who hid it in a well in 1933 and told his family on his deathbed. In 2025 another version appeared. The researcher who took possession of the skull from the worker’s descendant told a reporter he doubted the story. The descendant who turned it over might have found it himself. **Neither version has a record from the time.**

New dating in 2026 put the two archaeological layers at Xujiayao, taken together, at about 230,000 to 140,000 years ago. That year a paper reported ancient proteins from two Xujiayao fossils that support a Denisovan attribution. The team compared bone shapes as well and suggested that the fossils from Jinniushan, Dali and Hualongdong might belong to the same branch. This was a reading of their shape, not a recovery of the same proteins at those three sites.

The Dali skull was found in Shaanxi in 1978, usually credited to the local geologist Liu Shuntang. Jinniushan is in Yingkou, Liaoning, and Hualongdong is in Dongzhi, Anhui. This grouping does not quite match the one proposed in 2024, which put Xujiayao and other material in *Homo juluensis* and assigned Harbin, Dali and Jinniushan to the *Homo longi* side.

In 2026, at Bat Cave in Heqing, Yunnan, researchers used ancient proteins to single out human fossils from more than 60,000 bone fragments. Including teeth, Chinese reports put the count at five specimens confirmed as Denisovan.

One was part of the radius, the forearm bone, near the elbow. It was reported as the first confirmed Denisovan radius in the world and dates to about 170,000 to 130,000 years ago. **A map that began with a little finger bone in Siberia now reaches the Tibetan Plateau, Heilongjiang and Yunnan. Two Xujiayao fossils take it to the border of Shanxi and Hebei.**

## 8. Sorting It Out

Two things sit outside the evidence. The originals of that Peking Man collection have been missing since 1941. And there is no record from the time the Harbin skull came out of the ground. To talk about them today, one side has casts from eight or nine decades ago. The other has accounts told decades later and a comparison of the skull’s chemistry with the local deposits.

There are also two places where the trees do not match. The split inferred from the shape of the Yunxian skulls does not match the split estimated from DNA from other hominins. And the bones in the Pit of Bones point one way in the small piece of DNA inherited through the mother’s line, and another in the DNA from the nucleus.

**The missing Peking Man fossils, the Harbin skull without a clear findspot, the trees that do not match: they cannot all be sorted into one picture.**

Hold a single line up against the hominins of this period and things look muddled. Rising Star, Jebel Irhoud and Kabwe all fall around 300,000 years ago. In this stretch, the line in the Chinese textbook has room for only one or two names.

The line was arranged, not walked.
