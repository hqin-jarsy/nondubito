# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (23): Two People Found the Skull. The Record Kept One Name.

## 1. One Swing of a Pick

June 17, 1921, the Broken Hill mine in Northern Rhodesia, now Kabwe, Zambia. According to the later recollection of a Swiss miner named Tom Zwiglaar, at about ten that morning he and an African miner were sorting lead-rich ore by hand on a slope in the mine.

One swing of the pick, and “there was the skull looking at me.” He said he took it out carefully. The anthropologist Aleš Hrdlička recorded the gist of his recollection on a visit to the mine and published it in 1930, nearly nine years after the event. By the time of the interview, he cautioned, some details had already become hard to remember.

Hrdlička’s account records how Zwiglaar referred to the miner alongside him: “I had a colored boy (young man) with me.” The account does not give the man’s name.

In 2020, London’s Natural History Museum described an unnamed African miner digging out the skull with his Swiss colleague, Zwiglaar. A more recent history of science study says the African miner was never named in the known records.

Several early accounts tell the story differently. The British researcher who first described the skull credited someone else entirely. **In the museum’s account, two people dug out the skull together. The record keeps the name of only one of them.**

The skull ended up in London. Bones and remains of this kind are scattered across continents. A set of fossils from Zhoukoudian, Beijing, went missing in the winter of 1941. A skull in Brazil went through a fire. A collection from the Indonesian island of Flores was lent out for several months; after its return, a paper by the excavation team recorded that a jaw had broken.

By Lake Mungo in southeastern Australia and by the Columbia River in the northwestern United States, remains have been returned to the earth. At a university museum in Philadelphia, nineteen people whose skulls were held there were laid to rest in a cemetery. Skulls and other remains from several German institutions were returned to Namibia in batches.

In 2020 the Kabwe skull was dated directly. The best estimate was around 299,000 years, with an uncertainty of about 25,000 years either way. **Make a year of those 299,000 years and the time since the skull was dug out in 1921 takes up only around three hours.** Some of these bones have gone back into the ground. Some were loaned and returned. Some were lost; some burned. Some have been requested for decades and still sit in cabinets. All of this fits into that short stretch.

## 2. It Was Believed

A 1947 U.S. military order to investigate said the Peking Man fossils had been handed by Peking Union Medical College to the U.S. Marines in early December 1941, to be shipped with their baggage. The order said this was believed to have happened.

Each time a specimen goes from one pair of hands to another there should be a piece of paper saying who gave it, who received it and on what day. This is the chain of custody. Where did it break for Peking Man?

In 2021 three researchers reread records from the U.S. State Department and elsewhere and reached the opposite view. They found no evidence that the American side actually took possession of the fossils. What they could establish was that the bones disappeared in December 1941.

Earlier, in 2005, another researcher examined records from a May 1943 investigation by the Japanese military police. The available records, the researcher concluded, did not establish that the Japanese army or Japanese researchers ever got the fossils directly. **Who took them? The 1947 order said “believed.” The 2021 paper said “no evidence.”**

The human fossils from the Upper Cave at Zhoukoudian went missing as well. A Chinese paper from 1961 listed three complete or nearly complete skulls, skull fragments, jaws, isolated teeth, vertebrae and limb bones. They were lost with the Peking Man fossils during the war in the Pacific, the paper said.

## 3. Eighty Percent Visible

On September 2, 2018, a fire broke out in the palace housing Brazil’s National Museum in Rio de Janeiro. The museum held a skull called Luzia. It describes her as one of the oldest human skeletons found in Brazil. The old label was headed “Human skull, female individual.”

The museum estimates she was around twenty-five when she died and stood about a meter and a half tall. Science reporting said the skull had been in a metal box inside a cabinet before the fire. Afterward it was found in pieces.

The archaeologist Cláudia Carvalho coordinated the rescue. The team worked through the rubble, and by the museum’s announcement had recovered at least 1,500 items. **Carvalho said publicly that eighty percent of the skull that had been there before was now visible.** The rest still had to be dealt with, she said, before cleaning, stabilization and reconstruction.

On July 2, 2025, Brazil’s federal heritage authority announced a new temporary space for visitors at the National Museum. The same day a university organization released exhibition material that listed fragments of Luzia’s skull. **According to that list, the skull went on display in pieces, nearly seven years after the fire.**

## 4. What Were the Dead Called?

What were their names? Where did they die? How were they taken? For the skulls of Herero and Nama people held by German institutions, the institutions’ provenance records say many of the old labels and documents were lost. These questions often cannot be answered.

On September 30, 2011, Charité, the medical school in Berlin, handed twenty skulls to a Namibian delegation. Its research identified Herero and Nama people, men and women, and a boy of around four.

How did the skulls reach Germany? The same provenance account says that during the colonial war in German South West Africa, under a system of concentration camps and detention, skulls and other remains were sent to Germany for what was then called anthropology, anatomy and racial research. Some came from graves that had been dug open.

On March 4, 2014, the University of Freiburg returned fourteen skulls. The old labels said “Herero” and included a colonial-era term of classification. In some cases the researchers could say no more than that the skulls might have come from what is now Namibia. Charité returned another set that year.

According to Germany’s Foreign Office, the remains of twenty-seven people were handed to a Namibian delegation at a ceremony in Berlin on August 29, 2018, and taken to Windhoek two days later. The official material that year says some remains had been taken unlawfully in the colonial period.

A German foundation that traces lost cultural property counts eighty-two people in the three rounds in 2011, 2014 and 2018. **On May 28, 2021, German foreign minister Heiko Maas announced that Germany would officially call the events of 1904 to 1908 what they were from today’s perspective: a genocide.**

The two governments initialed a joint declaration that month. The German Foreign Office’s page on bilateral relations, dated March 30, 2026, still says the declaration was initialed in May 2021 and bilateral negotiations are ongoing. In August 2025 the Foreign Office also confirmed that a legal challenge to the declaration had been brought in Namibia.

## 5. No Plans

In the records of London’s Natural History Museum there is an entry for PA E 686. It is recorded as *Homo heidelbergensis*, described as the skull of an adult male. The donor is recorded as the Zambia Broken Hill Development Company. It is the Kabwe skull. **The donor field records how it got into the museum.**

Zambia wants it back. A 2026 legal study traced correspondence between the two governments to 1972 and 1973. The British Foreign Office cited a law governing museum collections and rejected the request. In 1974, Zambia’s National Monuments Commission formally asked how the skull had been acquired and whether it could be returned. The museum replied that the mining company had donated the finds from Broken Hill.

Later letters did not change the British position. According to British reporting based on archival records, a government memo in 1982 worried that a return would open the floodgates. Zambia asked UNESCO for help. In 2018 the matter came before a UNESCO intergovernmental committee on returning cultural property.

Starting April 27, 2023, representatives of the two countries spent two days in talks at the Natural History Museum in London. According to the record of questions and answers in Zambia’s National Assembly, the British government had no plans to change the law or instruct the museum to return the skull. The museum was independent and the decision lay with its trustees.

Zambia maintained its request and reserved the right to try other routes. The legal study says that in the committee’s 2024 meeting Zambia made its case in terms of an ancestor. Beginning in 2025, the museum suspended ordinary loans and viewing of its anthropology collections as it moved its stores. Requests from descendant communities or for repatriation were exempt. **In 2026, PA E 686 was still listed in the museum’s database.**

## 6. Nineteen People

According to *The Philadelphia Inquirer*, on January 22, 2024, a small group took the remains of nineteen people to Eden Cemetery and placed them in two mausoleums. On February 3, the University of Pennsylvania’s museum held a public interfaith memorial service.

In the university’s account, the nineteen were Black Philadelphians and a community advisory group had proposed the burial plan. Their skulls had belonged to Samuel Morton’s nineteenth-century collection. It was moved from the Academy of Natural Sciences to the Penn Museum in 1966. Morton used measurements of skulls to rank races.

**In April 2021, the Penn Museum released a committee’s recommendations and formally apologized for the unethical possession of human remains.** Its statement repudiated scientific racism and said it would prioritize identifying the communities the remains came from and moving toward return or other arrangements.

After the burial, people disagreed. The biological anthropologist Michael Blakey told the media that what became of the remains was entirely a matter for descendants. Critics argued that there was more to do in identifying descendants and consulting communities, and that Penn had acted too quickly.

Christopher Woods of the Penn Museum described the burial as an act of repair, allowing these people to rest after a long delay. Media reports said a skull originally in the group had been taken out because the person might have had Indigenous American ancestry.

## 7. No Marker on the Ground

In November 2017, remains traveled roughly eight hundred kilometers from the National Museum of Australia back to the Willandra Lakes region. They were those of Mungo Man, whom the geologist Jim Bowler spotted by the dry bed of Lake Mungo in February 1974.

On November 15, 2017, with coordination by the New South Wales environment and heritage department, custody of Mungo Man and more than a hundred other Willandra remains passed to a repatriation committee of traditional owners from the local Aboriginal communities. According to the national museum’s annual report, the Australian National University had formally returned the remains in 2015, and the museum had held them temporarily since then.

Where to bury them, and how? In project documents from 2021 and 2022, the state heritage department proposed reburying 108 Aboriginal Ancestors, including Mungo Man and Mungo Lady, at twenty-six locations in Mungo National Park and on several western lease properties. **There would be no markers above ground. The precise locations would not be made public for reasons of cultural sensitivity.**

According to investigative reporting, on May 24, 2022, **the two sets of roughly 40,000-year-old remains from Lake Mungo, Mungo Man and Mungo Lady, were buried at an undisclosed location in Willandra.** Public accounts differ as to who authorized the burial that day. The state heritage department told the media that the state had neither authorized nor carried out nor endorsed the removal and burial that day. It began an external review.

Later reporting said an independent review found that what happened that day was consistent with the relevant decisions. The full review was not made public. A member of the Aboriginal advisory group that supported the burial described tears of joy and of sadness.

A Mutthi Mutthi representative who opposed the approach described the plan as burying them at secret sites. Another representative told the media that Mungo Man’s burial should be decided by all the traditional owners concerned. In March 2025, media reports based on federal court proceedings said that burial of the other 106 sets of remains had begun, still at unmarked and undisclosed sites.

The remains of Kennewick Man, roughly eight thousand to a little over nine thousand years old, were found in 1996 by the Columbia River in Washington State. The find was on federal land at a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers dam project. Five tribes call him the Ancient One. Beginning in 1998 the Burke Museum held the remains under an arrangement with the Corps.

Ancient-DNA research in 2015 found that he was closest to living Indigenous Americans. A federal law that went into effect on December 16, 2016, required the Corps to transfer the remains to Washington State within ninety days on condition that the state then hand them to the five tribes.

On February 17, 2017, the Corps transferred the remains at the Burke Museum. The state returned them the same day. The museum confirmed that a private burial followed, also at an undisclosed location. Local histories and news reports put it the next day, with more than two hundred tribal members present.

## 8. A Hundred Voices

After burial, can you still ask questions? The University of Queensland anthropologist Michael Westaway told the Australian Broadcasting Corporation that reburying the remaining Willandra ancestors would silence a hundred voices.

His point was that if the remains could never be accessed again, new methods could never be used to learn about those people. Bowler had made a similar point in an earlier debate about Mungo Man: new techniques might emerge in the future.

Ivan Johnson of the Willandra Aboriginal Advisory Group argued for returning the ancestors to the land and letting them rest. When Mungo Man came back, Ngiyampaa elder Roy Kennedy told the media that research institutions had kept him long enough.

Kennewick Man’s fate was disputed too. Researchers at the Smithsonian and elsewhere wanted to keep the remains for future methods that could answer questions they could not yet answer. Tribal representatives argued that the remains should go back into the ground within a reasonable time.

In 2021, a set of five ethics guidelines for ancient-DNA research appeared in *Nature*. The authors said they could be applied globally and noted that there was still no consensus. Later that year, other scholars criticized the guidelines in the same journal. They had not drawn adequately on the ethical frameworks of Indigenous and long-marginalized communities. Who could authorize research, how to work in partnership and what to do about unequal power were left too broad.

These things happened close together between 2022 and 2025. The two people from Lake Mungo were reported buried, and some people objected. At the talks in London, according to Zambia’s record, the British side had no plans. Nineteen people from Philadelphia went into a tomb, and some people said it was too quick. In Namibia, people sued to oppose the joint declaration.

**A collection of fossils vanished. Fragments of a skull were recovered after a fire. There was a plan to rebury 108 sets of remains from Willandra. The two from Lake Mungo have been reported buried, and burial of the others was reported to have begun. The person from the Columbia River went back into the earth too. Some of the questions future methods might ask of these people stop at those decisions.**

PA E 686 is still listed in London. Of the two people sorting ore together that day, the record still has just one name.

The line was arranged, not walked.
