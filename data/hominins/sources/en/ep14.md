# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (14): A Genome First, Then a Face

## 1. One Little Finger Bone

About thirty milligrams of bone powder, roughly the weight of a grain of rice. It came from the bone at the very tip of a little finger. According to the Australian Museum’s account, the growth plate had not fused, so its owner had not reached adulthood. The bone came from Denisova Cave in the Altai Mountains of southern Siberia in 2008. Anatoly Derevianko and Mikhail Shunkov directed the excavation.

The Altai lies around the borders of Russia, Kazakhstan, Mongolia and China. In later accounts, Derevianko divided the bone in two and sent the halves to two ancient-DNA laboratories to see whether either could recover DNA. One half went to a Max Planck Institute laboratory in Germany.

In the story *National Geographic* told in 2013, Johannes Krause and the others in the lab first thought the bone belonged to *Homo sapiens*. It was only late in 2009 that he sat down to work on it in earnest. **Bone powder weighing no more than a grain of rice revealed a kind of human no one had known about.**

In March 2010, a paper led by Krause reported the complete mitochondrial DNA from the finger bone, the small portion passed down through the mother’s line. It differed from that of living humans at more than 380 positions on average. The authors estimated that it had diverged from Neanderthal and modern human mitochondrial DNA around a million years ago. They made clear that the date depended on several assumptions and was uncertain.

In December 2010 the DNA from the nucleus was published too, and the population was named the Denisovans. UNESCO’s file on the cave describes it as the first time a new human population was distinguished by genetic analysis rather than by the shape of its remains.

More than a decade passed between the discovery of the finger bone and the identification of a skull as Denisovan. Researchers had a genome from this population, teeth and eventually a jaw, but no face.

## 2. The People Who Got Mixed In

A bone that comes out of the ground passes through the hands of the excavator, the people who sort it and the people in the lab. Any of those hands may leave DNA from people alive today. How much of the DNA we read actually comes from the owner of the bone?

In May 2010 a team published “A Draft Sequence of the Neandertal Genome.” It began by screening twenty-one fragments of Neanderthal bone from Vindija Cave in Croatia. There was little to tell from their shape. The team drilled into each piece from the surface, taking fifty to a hundred milligrams of bone powder. It ended up using mainly three bones, from three individuals.

How did they work out how much modern DNA had got in? They compared mitochondrial DNA from 311 living people around the world, looking for positions where almost all living humans had the same letter and Neanderthals had a different one. A fragment that looked like ours at those positions counted as contamination. They also made separate estimates from the Y chromosome and from nuclear DNA. The three methods were independent. Modern human DNA made up less than five parts in a thousand of the mitochondrial DNA in each of the three bones, and about three parts in a thousand overall. The nuclear estimates were below one percent too.

## 3. Fifty Letters

Take a book three billion letters long. Cut it into strips of fifty letters each. Lose a good many of the strips. Reading ancient DNA starts with strips like those.

One copy of a person’s genome has about three billion bases, strung together one after another. There are four kinds of base, usually written as four letters. After death the string breaks into pieces. Water and microbes in the soil break it down further.

Fragments used in the Neanderthal draft were generally only about fifty bases long. In Neanderthal samples from two sites, one in Germany and the other in the Caucasus, the most common lengths were about sixty bases at one site and forty-five at the other.

The pieces break, and their ends also degrade. Near an end, one of the four bases can lose a small part of its structure. The sequencing process then reads it as a different base. One letter turns into another.

**In those two samples, at the first position of a fragment, some thirty to thirty-five percent of the bases of that particular kind were read as a different kind. Modern human DNA contamination was only two to four percent.** Old fragments are short and often have letters changed at the ends. That sounds like a ready-made way to tell which is which.

But the study of the two samples also noted that length alone was not enough. At another site, the lengths of contaminating fragments could overlap those of genuine ancient DNA. Another team scored fragments by damage to pick ancient DNA out of samples with modern DNA mixed in. They warned that seeing an ancient damage pattern does not prove there is no modern contamination, or even show that the amount is low.

## 4. One Point Nine Times

How many times does a genome have to be read before it has been read? The first Denisovan nuclear genome appeared in December 2010. Put the fragments that can be matched back into place, and each position was read an average of about 1.9 times. That is 1.9-fold coverage. Some positions were read several times. Others were not read at all.

There is another way to count coverage: how much of the genome was read at least once. **The three main bones used for the Neanderthal draft each covered only around half of the genome.** Among samples from other sites, some covered only a part in a thousand.

In 2012 the Max Planck Institute released a Denisovan genome with much higher coverage. Svante Pääbo said they wanted to make it freely available to everyone right away. He received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work of this kind in 2022.

Put the strips back where? To place every strip of fifty letters, you first need to find it in the original book. For ancient DNA, the book used as a guide is a reference sequence from living humans.

## 5. An Advertisement in Buffalo

On Sunday, March 23, 1997, an advertisement appeared in *The Buffalo News* in New York State. It called for twenty volunteers to take part in the Human Genome Project.

The researchers invited ten men and ten women in the order they called. A 2025 history of the project’s ethics cites a story that ran in the paper on the same day. Its opening asked for two Buffalo-area residents, one man and one woman, to donate DNA to the world’s largest science project.

The volunteers received genetic counseling at Roswell Park Cancer Institute, signed informed consent forms and gave fifty milliliters of blood each. DNA from one anonymous man was made into a collection of samples that could be drawn on repeatedly, called a library. The paper said his identity was protected by a double-blind donor selection procedure.

Much of the later human reference sequence came from that library. The 2010 Neanderthal paper said roughly two-thirds of the reference came from this donor, whose ancestry was about half European and half African. **In the primary assembly of the widely used GRCh38 reference, about seventy percent came from the anonymous man from Buffalo, by the count of the consortium that maintains it.**

When the fragments go back into the book they are compared with it letter by letter. **A letter that matches the reference is more likely to line up and stay in.** A fragment carrying it is more likely to align or get a higher score. Some fragments that differ from the reference are rejected, some put in the wrong place, and some dropped by later filters. Researchers call this reference bias. A 2019 methods paper noted that many fragments of ancient DNA are shorter than fifty bases, which can make the problem worse.

The book changes too. In 2022 a consortium published a complete reference sequence that read each chromosome from one end to the other, except for the Y chromosome. It came from a line of cells grown in the lab and added almost two hundred million bases to the old reference. In 2023 another consortium released a first reference that included genomes from forty-seven people from different populations.

## 6. Soil Without Bones

If a cave yields no human bones, can we tell whether people came there? In 2017 a team recovered DNA directly from cave sediments. The paper explained that minerals and organic matter in the soil can hold on to DNA. The researchers used human and other mammalian mitochondrial DNA as bait to retrieve corresponding fragments, then screened large numbers of soil samples in batches with a partly automated method.

**Neanderthal DNA turned up in the soil of eight archaeological layers in four Eurasian caves.** A layer near the bottom of Denisova Cave yielded Denisovan DNA. The abstract ended by saying that this opened up the possibility of detecting hominin populations at places without skeletal remains.

For a study published in 2020, a team collected thirty-five soil samples at Baishiya Karst Cave on the northeastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau. A public account from the Chinese Academy of Sciences says they fished out 242 mitochondrial DNA sequences of humans and other mammals. Among them was Denisovan DNA.

DNA survives longer in the cold. Denisova Cave is in the Siberian mountains; Baishiya is on a high plateau. There is far less readable DNA in bones and soil from warm, humid places.

## 7. Tooth Enamel

Can a two-million-year-old tooth tell you whether its owner was male or female? The shiny, hard shell on a tooth is enamel. A 2020 paper on the teeth of *Homo antecessor* called it the hardest tissue in the mammalian skeleton. It also compared proteins from different materials. Proteins in bones and the inner parts of teeth do not last as long as proteins in enamel. A 2024 methods paper notes records of ancient proteins surviving around two million years in temperate and subtropical regions.

One protein in enamel occurs in both sexes. The Y chromosome also carries a copy of its gene, making a slightly different version of the protein. **To identify a male, look for the protein made from the Y-chromosome copy. Females have no such copy, so the inference depends on how strong the signals are.**

Those differences identified the owner of a molar fragment from the Spanish *Homo antecessor* fossils as male. In 2025, researchers studied four teeth of *Paranthropus robustus* from Swartkrans, South Africa, around two million years old. Two carried the Y-linked version, indicating males. The strengths of the signals in the other two led the team to classify them as female.

That same year, researchers read amino acids at more than four thousand positions in proteins from a jaw from Penghu, Taiwan. Amino acids are the individual units strung together to make proteins. Two of the positions had variants distinctive to Denisovans.

The paper’s title described a male Denisovan jaw from Pleistocene Taiwan. Its possible dates fell into two windows: between 10,000 and 70,000 years ago, or between 130,000 and 190,000 years ago.

In 2026, a group analyzed enamel from twenty-three *Homo naledi* specimens representing at least twenty individuals. The paper’s title said enamel protein analysis of twenty *naledi* individuals had found no male markers. In the abstract the researchers said they had used several methods to check for technical biases and found no convincing evidence in any sample to identify a male with confidence. The signals from some specimens were not strong enough to establish that they were female either.

**Proteins tell us far less than DNA.** Organic material makes up under two percent of tooth enamel by weight, and there are few kinds of protein. A simulation study in 2026 compared twelve proteins able to survive in million-year-old fossils. It reported that protein data can sometimes lead researchers to infer the wrong shape for a family tree.

## 8. A Face

There is a nearly complete skull from Harbin in northeastern China. It still has a face. The team that read it was led by the Chinese ancient-DNA researcher Qiaomei Fu. In the team’s Chinese-language account, they first sampled the teeth and the temporal bone by the ear, looking for DNA. They did not get usable ancient human DNA from those samples.

For proteins they took two samples from the temporal bone, one about sixty-four milligrams and the other about thirty-five. They read nearly a hundred proteins from the skull itself. Three amino acid variants were characteristic of changes that arose in the Denisovan branch. On the protein tree, the Harbin skull grouped with the owner of the finger bone from Denisova Cave.

They eventually read mitochondrial DNA from calculus on a tooth, using about 0.3 milligrams. **It took about thirty milligrams of powder from the Denisova Cave finger bone to read its mitochondrial DNA. The Harbin mitochondrial DNA took one hundredth of that amount.**

Fu told *National Geographic* that fifteen years later they had given the Denisovans a face. The overseas edition of *People’s Daily* used “giving the Denisovans a face” in its headline and quoted the same turn of phrase from a journal review. The protein paper itself said the evidence suggested that the Harbin individual belonged to the Denisovan population.

**First the Denisovans had a genome. Fifteen years later there was a face identified as theirs.**

**The fragments that failed to align to the reference and were dropped, the DNA the team could not recover from the Harbin skull: these are part of the remainder.**

To read a person often means grinding away a little of their bone. In 2021, researchers proposed five guidelines for ancient-DNA research in *Nature*: follow the rules where the remains come from and where the work is done, make a detailed plan before starting, minimize damage, make the data public after publication, and talk with other interested parties from the start.

The fifth guideline says researchers must accept a no if the parties concerned do not jointly support the work. Permission to study remains, the guidelines say, does not transfer ownership. The guidelines single out a part of the temporal bone that often yields much more DNA, and where destructive sampling is particularly controversial. In the same year *Nature* published a letter criticizing the global guidelines. Its title said ancient-DNA researchers were setting their own rules.

Most of that reference sequence came from fifty milliliters of blood given in Buffalo in 1997. The man signed a consent form. His name was not made public.

The line was arranged, not walked.
