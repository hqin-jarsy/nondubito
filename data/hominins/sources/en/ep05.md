# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (5): Who Was Knapping Stone More Than Three Million Years Ago?

## 1. A Wrong Turn

You often hear that what sets humans apart from other animals is that we make tools. There is an assumption in that sentence: whoever made the tools was human. Go looking in the ground and what you find first is stone.

In May 2015, *Nature* published a report on stone tools from a site called Lomekwi 3, on the western shore of Lake Turkana in Kenya. The tools were dated to about 3.3 million years ago. The lead author was the French archaeologist Sonia Harmand. On the record then available, the tools were some 700,000 years older than the kind named after Olduvai Gorge. If that date holds up, **they are also about half a million years older than the earliest fossils so far assigned to the genus *Homo*.**

Most of the stones and bones in this story come from a span of about 1.3 million years, from roughly 3.3 to two million years ago. Most are from East Africa: Afar in Ethiopia, the shores of Lake Turkana and the area around Lake Victoria in Kenya. The farthest site is in Shaanxi, China. More than one kind of hominin lived during this time. Hominins are all the groups on our side after the split from chimpanzees.

There are two accounts of the discovery. In a 2015 interview with an American magazine, the team had meant to go somewhere else in 2011. The vehicle took a wrong turn into a dry riverbed. They stopped and spread out to take a look. Harmand told the reporter that the area struck them as interesting right away.

The paper itself says nothing about a wrong turn. The author-contributions statement has a line crediting Sammy Lokorodi with the discovery of Lomekwi 3.

These are not small stones. One large rock interpreted as an anvil weighed fifteen kilograms, about as much as fifteen one-liter bottles of water. Two stones interpreted as hammers weighed more than three kilograms and more than one and a half kilograms respectively.

## 2. One Afternoon

A series of photographs accompanying the paper follows one stone core through the afternoon it came out of the ground. A core is the parent stone left after flakes have been knocked off it. At 12:14 p.m. on July 16, 2011, it was still entirely buried in sediment. At 2:11 a little showed. Around half past five, most of it was exposed.

At 5:36 they took it out whole and turned it over. At 5:47 they photographed the impression it had left in the sediment. Those photographs later became part of the dispute over whether the stone was buried in place. Had it lain in that 3.3-million-year-old layer the whole time, or had it slid down from younger deposits and mixed in?

The paper recorded 149 stone artifacts, including cores, flakes, hammerstones and anvils. Some had been excavated from the deposits. Others had been picked up on the slope and at the surface. The paper marked the two separately.

A French specialist made replicas of these stones. From the comparison, the authors proposed two ways of striking them. In one, a person held the core with both hands and struck it against a stationary rock on the ground. In the other, the core sat on an anvil and was struck from above with a hammerstone. **What remains is the point of impact, the flake that came off, and the scar on the core.**

From where the blows fell and in what order, the authors read a developing understanding of how stone breaks. The editor’s note in the same issue of *Nature* said the tools might represent a tool culture that predated *Homo*.

Who struck the blows? The hominins known from around that time on the west side of Lake Turkana include the flat-faced species, the one named from the badly distorted skull found on that shore in 1999. Researchers often mention it as a candidate. But the paper did not match any tool to any skeleton.

In 2016, a Spanish archaeologist and a colleague published “3.3-Million-Year-Old Stone Tools and Butchery Traces? More Evidence Needed.” They argued that the photographs of the deposits and what was known about movement on the slope did not rule out younger stones getting mixed in; the evidence remained open to dispute. Harmand’s side replied in 2019. The words after the colon in its title were aimed at the critics: “Interpretation Without Accurate Evidence Is Fantasy.”

## 3. Scratches on Bone

There is another kind of evidence besides stone tools: marks on bones. In 2010, a team published two animal bones from Dikika, Ethiopia, in *Nature*: a rib and a fragment of long bone from medium-to-large hoofed animals. Dikika is also where Selam, the skeleton of a child of about three, was found.

The paper’s title was “Evidence for stone-tool-assisted consumption of animal tissues before 3.39 million years ago.” The finds in the paper were not tools, and no tools had been found beside the two bones. The number 3.39 million was not measured on the bones either. It came from the geological framework of the area.

There were several long, thin scratches, running roughly parallel, and a few deeper dents. The authors read the first as cuts from a sharp stone edge and the second as traces of a blow that broke bone.

**No one disputes that there are marks. The dispute is over what made them.** Another team tried trampling bones in sharp gravel and got grooved, linear marks that way too. In 2017 another team compared modern crocodile bite marks and found that some fossil marks read as cuts were within the range of shapes crocodile teeth can produce.

A blade sliding over bone, bone trampled against sharp grit, a crocodile tooth dragging across the surface: **three different events can leave a long, narrow mark behind.**

In 2015 the Dikika researchers published a study of how the animal fossils across the site came to be preserved. It gave those two bones a wider context, but there is still no conclusion everyone accepts.

Bouri, another site in Afar, yielded animal bones about 2.5 million years old, published in 1999. Their marks were read as cuts and blows. As at Dikika, no knapped stone tools were found that could be paired with them. **The marks are there. The blade is not.**

## 4. Who Was Nearby?

Who was around where the tools and scratches turned up? **Over this million years and more, Afar and Turkana yielded at least four kinds of hominin fossils:** early *Homo*, the species whose name means “surprise,” *Paranthropus*, and an unnamed australopith. They come from the same broad region and span of time, not from the same pit.

According to project accounts and reports published with the paper in 2015, the Ethiopian researcher Chalachew Seyoum spotted a lower jaw exposed on the ground at Ledi-Geraru, in Afar, in 2013. It was published in *Science* in 2015, dated to around 2.8 million years ago and assigned to early *Homo*.

*Homo* is the genus that includes us. On present evidence, this jaw is among its oldest known fossils. In 2025, teeth from the same research area were published. Some were assigned to *Homo* and others to *Australopithecus*, all dating to between about 2.8 and 2.6 million years ago. The authors did not think the australopith teeth came from Lucy’s species, but did not name a new species for them either. The teeth had not all come out of the same fossil pit.

At Bouri, where the marked bones turned up, the same 1999 issue of *Science* reported an australopith from about 2.5 million years ago. Its species name comes from an Afar word meaning “surprise”: *Australopithecus garhi*.

At Mille–Logya, another site in Afar, a *Paranthropus* jaw was reported in 2026. Its layer puts it between roughly 2.9 and 2.5 million years ago. *Paranthropus* is a different hominin genus. The skulls often have high bony crests, large cheek teeth and considerable chewing power.

A skull from the west shore of Lake Turkana, published in 1986, was about 2.5 million years old. It became known as the “Black Skull,” stained underground by manganese-bearing minerals. It is usually assigned to *Paranthropus aethiopicus*: Ethiopia is in the species name, but the skull was found in Kenya.

Most of these four kinds of fossils are separated from the stones and marks by either time or distance. At Bouri the finds are close in both, but still cannot be paired. Nowhere can the tools be handed directly to one of the four.

## 5. Oldowan

The name Oldowan comes from Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania. Mary Leakey’s influential 1971 monograph organized these tools systematically into types, though Louis Leakey had used the name in earlier work.

Here is what they look like: a core with a ring of scars where pieces have been struck away, and the sharp flakes that came off it. The raw material is often river cobbles.

In 1997, *Nature* published tools from Gona, Ethiopia. The title said “2.5 million years ago.” Later the geological chronology was refined, and a 2003 follow-up put “2.6 million” in its title. No hominin bone from the oldest tool-bearing deposits at Gona is tied directly to the tools.

In 2019, another site at Ledi-Geraru yielded tools published at about 2.6 million years old. The title called them the earliest known Oldowan tools at the time. Overall, the authors found them closer to later Oldowan material than to the Lomekwi 3 tools. The 2.8-million-year-old *Homo* jaw comes from Ledi-Geraru too, but from a different place and about 200,000 years earlier. It does not pair up with them.

At Nyayanga, near Kenya’s Lake Victoria, a 2023 paper put the finds in a window between roughly three million and 2.6 million years ago. *Paranthropus* molars turned up at the site with Oldowan tools. There were hippo bones as well, some bearing marks that the authors read as butchery.

The researchers also interpreted wear on the stone surfaces as signs of plant processing. The title referred to “food acquisition strategies.” The lead author told the public that having *Paranthropus* and Oldowan tools together posed a detective question: who actually did it?

## 6. Three Hundred Thousand Years

In 2025, researchers reported stone tools from three layers at Namorotukunan in Kenya’s Turkana Basin. The earliest layer was around 2.75 million years old and the latest about 2.44 million years old, a span of roughly 300,000 years. The abstract spoke of toolmaking practices continuing over time and of systematic selection of rock types.

**In the researchers’ reading, similar ways of striking stone turn up repeatedly in deposits spanning some 300,000 years at this one site in Kenya.** The authors also said that whether changes in behavior coincided with the emergence of *Homo* is hard to tell from the available deposits. How this early technology connects to older tool industries remained uncertain. These three layers had no hominin bones that could be paired with the tools.

The farthest site is in China. At Shangchen, in Lantian, Shaanxi, a team reported ninety-six stone artifacts in *Nature* in 2018. They came from a thick sequence of loess and ancient soils on the Loess Plateau. The lowest layer bearing artifacts was about 2.12 million years old, the highest about 1.26 million. The stone came from the nearby foothills of the Qinling Mountains.

The Shangchen dates came from matching the deposits’ magnetic barcode. They are dates for the layers containing the artifacts, not for the stones themselves. Headlines in Chinese often called this the earliest evidence of human activity in East Asia. The paper’s title said “hominin occupation.” **At Shangchen there are stones and no hominin bones.**

## 7. Man the Tool-Maker

In 1949, Kenneth Oakley, a paleoanthropologist at Britain’s Natural History Museum, published a small book called *Man the Tool-Maker*. It was revised and reissued several times. Man and tools stood side by side in the title.

In 1960, in her first year working in the field at Gombe, Tanzania, Jane Goodall saw chimpanzees put grass stems into holes in a termite mound, draw them out, and eat the termites that had latched on. She also saw a chimpanzee snap off a twig, strip its leaves and use it to fish for termites. In her account years later the chimpanzee was David Greybeard.

First change the shape of the twig, then use it. Goodall later recalled telling Louis Leakey about it. The reply most often passed on has him saying that we must redefine tools, redefine man or accept chimpanzees as human. The accounts do not agree on whether it was a telegram or a letter, on the exact words, or on how they were capitalized.

That same year, Louis Leakey’s son Jonathan found a group of fossils at Olduvai Gorge: a jaw, teeth, fragments of skull and bones from a hand. In 1964, Louis Leakey and two colleagues used them to name a new species in *Nature*: *Homo habilis*. The Latin means able or skillful; the English nickname is “handy man.”

The naming paper also changed the physical criteria for *Homo*, so that these smaller-brained individuals from Olduvai could be included. The species and the Oldowan tools became closely linked.

But the specimen had not come out of the ground holding a tool. The people who named it and those who studied it later inferred that *Homo habilis* was the maker from place, age and the shape of the hand taken together. **The name had already found the tools an owner.**

## 8. The Tools Are Unsigned

Hominins are not the only ones that can leave impact marks on stones. In the Taï Forest in Côte d’Ivoire, chimpanzees today use stone hammers and anvils to crack nuts.

An excavation published in 2007 found hammerstones and anvils there dating to about 4,300 years ago, with impact damage and traces of nuts. They matched a tradition of chimpanzees cracking nuts in that very forest. Chimpanzees can leave a stone-tool site that can be dug up and dated.

A study published in 2016 watched wild capuchin monkeys in Brazil. They picked up stones and brought them down hard on rocks embedded in the ground. The pieces that broke off had sharp edges and signs of blows. The paper described material that could be recognized archaeologically as flakes and cores.

But in the researchers’ observations, **the monkeys never picked up the sharp flakes to cut anything.** What they did again and again was strike stones. The look of a flake alone cannot tell us what the one striking the stone was after.

Lomekwi 3 has tools and hominins nearby, but no pairing. Dikika and Bouri have marks on bones, no stone tools and no pairing. Ledi-Geraru has the *Homo* jaw, with tools from another site some 200,000 years later. At Nyayanga, *Paranthropus* teeth and stone tools are found together. The paper’s own author calls that a detective question. At Namorotukunan, similar ways of working stone appear in layers spanning 300,000 years. Shangchen has ninety-six stone artifacts. Neither site has hominin bones.

On present evidence, these are among the oldest stone tools and marks on bones. All ask the person looking at them to read something about intent. Was the blow deliberate, or an accident? Did a blade make the marks, or did something step on the bone? The resulting claims, “cutting meat,” “breaking bone,” “understanding how stone fractures,” are in the papers of the people who read those marks. **The chisel marks are there. We cannot pin down who made them or what they meant to do.**

Giving a set of tools a maker is drawing a line between stones and bones. The name *Homo habilis* drew that line for Oldowan tools back in 1964, putting stone knapping at the front of the *Homo* line. If the stones at Lomekwi 3 and their dating hold up, they came half a million years before the oldest known *Homo* jaw.

The line was arranged, not walked.
