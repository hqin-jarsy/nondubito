# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (22): Pottery First, Rice Later

## 1. A Line in an Ice Core

At the center of the Greenland ice sheet, drills bore down from the surface and bring up cores of ice. The International Commission on Stratigraphy published an image with the tones reversed: clear ice looks black, and cloudy bands containing fine dust look white. The alternating bands reveal roughly annual layers. You can count them like tree rings.

At a depth of 1,492.45 meters, an isotope measurement in the ice changes abruptly over about three years. Over the next several decades, the dust in the ice drops to roughly a tenth of its former level.

On May 8, 2008, the executive committee of the International Union of Geological Sciences approved that depth as the lower boundary of the Holocene, the latest epoch in the geological timescale and the one that continues today. The paper puts the boundary at 11,700 years before the year 2000, or 11,700 b2k. Counting the layers gives a maximum counting error of ninety-nine years. The boundary marks the first signs of climatic warming.

**The Holocene’s golden spike is in an ice core.** Mike Walker, who chaired the group that defined the boundary, told the BBC years later that someone had made fun of it as a golden spike in a refrigerator.

From that boundary to about six thousand years ago is a span of a little over five thousand years, slightly longer than the time from the earliest known writing to today. Traces of those years are scattered across the New Guinea highlands, a rock shelter in Mexico, two mounds in Turkey, and a cave in Germany. Pottery fragments from a cave in Jiangxi, China, go back farther, to around twenty thousand years ago.

## 2. A Revolution

“The first revolution that transformed human economy gave man control over his own food-supply.” That is how the chapter on the Neolithic Revolution opens in *Man Makes Himself*, a book for the general reader published in 1936 by the Australian-born archaeologist Gordon Childe.

He had used the term a year earlier. In a 1935 presidential address to an archaeological society, he said this revolution in human life might be called the Neolithic Revolution. The book describes planting and tending edible grasses, roots and trees, selecting and improving them, then taming certain animals and making them dependent on humans. Childe said the two steps were closely related.

He also wrote that **the steps happened gradually and their effects accumulated. What changed was people’s outlook, or, more precisely, the outlook of a few communities.**

A Chinese high-school history textbook gives it one sentence. Once agriculture and animal husbandry appeared, humans changed from food gatherers into food producers.

In 2021, the National Museum of China staged an exhibition on archaeological sites around Shangshan in Zhejiang. In a Chinese translation of one expert’s remarks on the exhibition page, people before the Holocene lived entirely as hunters and gatherers in small groups of fifteen to twenty. The remarks call settled agriculture a settled civilization.

A scholarly article on the same museum’s website says there is no consensus about where to draw the boundary between the Paleolithic and Neolithic. From around 30,000 to 9,000 years ago, many people lived by fishing, hunting and gathering a wide range of foods. Pottery appeared in southern China during that period as well.

## 3. Pottery in a Cave

Straight sides at the mouth and a round base. Made mainly by layering coils of clay and fired at low temperature in an open fire. That is how the School of Archaeology and Museology at Peking University describes the early pottery from Xianrendong Cave in Jiangxi, China. At first there may have been just the one kind. Mountains rise around the Dayuan Basin, where the cave lies. The wide mouth is under a rock overhang, with the Dayuan River flowing past.

An archaeological survey found the site in 1962. The Jiangxi provincial cultural heritage committee led excavations that year and again in 1964. In 2012, Wu Xiaohong, Zhang Chi and colleagues published the ages of the sherds in *Science* under the title “Early Pottery at 20,000 Years Ago in Xianrendong Cave, China.” The first pottery came from layers twenty thousand to nineteen thousand years old.

**The paper says the pots were made by mobile hunter-gatherers, and that pottery came before agriculture by ten thousand years or more.** The authors also said these vessels may have been used for cooking.

Another researcher, Yaroslav Kuzmin, reviewed the dating and found the evidence for twenty thousand years insufficient. In his view, early pottery in southern China could be securely placed around eighteen thousand years ago.

The Japanese islands also have pottery from the Ice Age. The name Jōmon comes from the impressions of cord on the pots. An archaeologist who studies the Jōmon describes people who lived by hunting, fishing and gathering. She notes that abundant and predictable wild resources also enabled them to live in large, relatively permanent villages.

**In his book on the Jōmon, the Japanese archaeologist Tatsuo Kobayashi connects the early changes with the first settled villages, where people stayed in one place all year. He allows for both settled and relatively settled living.** On whether Ice Age potters had settled down, a 2019 study went only as far as saying the evidence suggested it.

## 4. Eleven Places

When does a place start to count as farming? In 2014, a group wrote in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* that at least eleven regions in the Old and New Worlds were independent centers of origin. Several more had been proposed.

**In their account, domestication began independently in at least eleven places.** The two main windows they identified were twelve to nine thousand years ago and seven to four thousand years ago.

Start with rice on the lower Yangtze. Tiny silica particles called phytoliths form in rice cells. Their shape differs in wild and domesticated rice. A 2014 release from the Chinese Academy of Sciences described a sequence stretching from Shangshan to Hemudu, from twelve thousand to seven thousand years ago. Phytoliths of the domesticated type appeared in Shangshan cultural layers, suggesting that early domesticated rice may already have been present eleven thousand years ago.

In 2021 a Chinese scholar pointed out in the journal *Kaogu* that different analyses of samples from essentially the same source at Shangshan had given different results. How well phytoliths trace the progress of rice domestication still needed investigation.

Another study in 2024 laid out three steps. People began gathering and using wild rice around 24,000 years ago and cultivating it before domestication around 13,000 years ago. Around 11,000 years ago, the phytolith indicator reached the threshold set by the authors.

The Chinese Academy of Sciences announcement for that study noted that previous archaeology had put full domestication at around seven thousand years ago. It is the same rice. Change what counts as domesticated and the date shifts by thousands of years.

Then there are the two millets of northern China. Based on finds from Cishan, a 2009 release from the Chinese Academy of Sciences says broomcorn millet predominated early at the site, from ten thousand to 8,700 years ago. Foxtail millet occurs in small quantities from around 8,700 to 7,500 years ago.

At Kuk in the New Guinea highlands, what survives is not seeds but old cultivation surfaces, raised beds, ditches and tiny plant residues. A team’s 2003 chronology found plant use and some cultivation by about ten thousand years ago. People began building mounds for planting around seven thousand years ago, and bananas were being cultivated intensively by then at the latest.

At a rock shelter in the central Balsas River valley in southwestern Mexico, starch and phytoliths on stone tools show that maize was present by about 8,700 years ago. **Related research describes those early cultivators as small groups that moved with the seasons and made a living in several ways.**

## 5. Stone Pillars on a Hill

Flint flakes covered the limestone ground like snow in winter sunlight, as Klaus Schmidt put it. The German archaeologist came to Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey in 1994. In his 2006 recollection, retold by the German Archaeological Institute, he had a colleague, a local boy as a guide and a taxi driver with him. In a pile of stones that farmers had cleared from their fields he recognized the top of a T-shaped pillar.

A joint survey by teams from Istanbul and Chicago recorded the place in 1963. One member described several rounded mounds of red earth, with flint scattered across their slopes. The big structures underneath passed unnoticed.

Excavators later uncovered large circular and oval buildings about twenty meters across, with pillars up to about five and a half meters tall. Three charcoal samples close to bedrock in one of them dated from 9664 to 9311 BCE at 95.4 percent probability. The paper notes that this falls along a flat part of the calibration curve, making the range wider.

The German Archaeological Institute first interpreted the site mainly as a place of ritual, a mountain sanctuary. Later work found signs of domestic activity and long-term occupation. The institute now calls it a settlement with a strong ritual component. A 2019 study analyzed more than seven thousand stone tools used there to process food.

**Few plant remains have survived there. Those reported are mostly of wild types, including wild einkorn and wild barley.** Signs of domestication in plant form are sparse and ambiguous at several sites nearby. At nearby Çayönü, clearly domesticated wheat appears about 10,600 to 10,200 years ago. At Nevalı Çori there is both wild einkorn and a type that does not shed its grain when ripe.

Farther into central Turkey, Çatalhöyük means “fork mound.” The east mound was occupied from about 7400 BCE to about 6200 BCE, accumulating eighteen layers. The houses ran together back to back. There were no streets, and people mostly came and went over the roofs.

The project estimates a population of 3,500 to 8,000 at its peak. In the early days, wetlands nearby supplied fish and waterbirds. There were fields on drier ground. **The site was once taken for the world’s oldest settled community. Its guide now says it came thousands of years after the earliest known settlements in western Asia and the domestication of plants, sheep and goats.**

## 6. Two Thousand Years of Neighbors

Near Hagen, Germany, is a cave called the Blätterhöhle. In 1983, a local caving team discovered a small crack leading into it. In 2004, commissioned by the city to study groundwater, they went back in. They widened the narrow passage, cleared sediment and brought out many human bones. Eventually there was a passage about fifteen meters deep that people could crawl through.

In 2013, a team published an analysis in *Science* of bones and teeth from twenty-nine people in the cave. The title spoke of two thousand years of parallel societies in Stone Age Central Europe. The layers had been disturbed by living things, so the team dated specimens individually. The later group fell between roughly 4000 and 2900 BCE; another group was several thousand years earlier.

Among the later samples, isotopes in the bones separated two groups. One ate much as farmers did. The other ate freshwater fish and gathered foods, and carried maternal lineages typical of earlier European hunter-gatherers. **The authors concluded that after farming reached Central Europe, people who lived by fishing, hunting and gathering carried on for more than two thousand years.** They suggested the communities may have had cultural contact and listed several possible relationships: hostile, reciprocal, seldom in contact, or regularly cooperating.

## 7. Fifteen Centimeters of Lake Mud

Where in the geological record do we start counting the traces people have left on Earth? In 2023, the Anthropocene Working Group formally proposed fixing the lower boundary of a new geological epoch in the mud of Crawford Lake in Ontario, Canada. The mud builds up in annual layers. The group chose the layer for 1952, in which the concentration of plutonium rose sharply.

In March 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy, under the International Commission on Stratigraphy, finished voting. There were four votes in favor, twelve against and three abstentions. Three members did not vote. **The proposal did not reach the sixty-percent threshold and did not pass.**

In a March 6 release, the working group said the result had been announced the previous day without authorization from the subcommission’s chair. It said questions remained about the validity of the vote and stood squarely behind its proposal. On March 20 the union responded that the two people overseeing the vote had acted with full integrity and that the procedure followed the commission’s rules.

The response also said the working group had analyzed just fifteen centimeters of lake mud. The chairs of the subcommissions had voted to confirm the result. The response listed three kinds of objection to a boundary in the 1950s. Human influence on the Earth began much earlier. An epoch shorter than a human lifetime would be out of scale with the geological timescale. And people’s effects arrived in different places at different times.

In a joint statement on March 26, 2024, the union and the commission said “Anthropocene” would **remain in use** even without being a formal unit of geological time, as a term for human effects on the Earth system.

## 8. Wheat That Holds On to Its Grain

Did wheat suddenly stop shedding its grain? When wild emmer ripens, the head breaks apart into spikelets. A review of the earliest crops in southwestern Asia describes each spikelet as an arrowlike device that can drive itself into the soil. Domesticated wheat has a tough stalk through the head. The grains stay on until people harvest, thresh and sow them. The same review calls those kinds human-dependent.

In 2006, two researchers wrote in *Science* that domesticated wheat that held on to its grain appeared around 9,500 years ago. At 7,500 years ago, types that shed grain were still common in cultivated fields. Their abstract said non-shattering domesticated types had emerged slowly.

Maize went farther. A review of evolutionary genetics says that after domestication the kernels no longer dispersed on their own but stayed firmly attached to the cob. A Canadian government biosafety document says maize cannot sustain reproduction without human cultivation.

What is domestication? In 2015, the American archaeologist Melinda Zeder noted a surprising lack of agreement about its definition. She offered a working definition: a mutual relationship over many generations in which one party significantly influences the reproduction and care of another in return for more predictable resources, with gains for the domesticated party as well.

One line of work in evolutionary biology asks how units that once lived separately join up at a higher level, as cells do in a body. The strict version can be put as three questions. Do the earlier units lose the ability to reproduce apart from the whole? Has a new unit emerged that reproduces as a whole? Is competition among the parts kept down over time? All three need a yes. Put agriculture to the first question and the answer comes in two halves.

**For crops it may be yes. Non-shattering wheat is a human-dependent type, and maize cannot sustain reproduction without cultivation.** Some archaeobotanists have also suggested that domestication can lock people into increasing dependence on the species they manage.

For people the first answer is no. However deep the dependence, people did not become unable to have descendants without crops. The people who fished and gathered around that German cave kept living without fields for more than two thousand years after farmers arrived.

The three questions were each framed for one answer. Agriculture gives the first one two: perhaps yes for crops, no for people. **An answer in two halves has no box in the three-question test.**

**People firing pots in a cave in Jiangxi, hunters and fishers living in villages in Japan, people raising stone pillars and eating wild wheat on a Turkish hill, people who left their bones in a German cave and whose way of eating persisted alongside farming in the region for two thousand years. Put them in boxes marked “gatherers” and “producers” and neither box holds the whole of their lives.**

The line in the ice core marks the first signs of a warming climate. The line in the lake mud did not pass.

The line was arranged, not walked.
