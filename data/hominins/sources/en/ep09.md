# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (9): Were There No Handaxes in East Asia?

## 1. A Million and a Half Years

A handaxe is usually struck on both faces. Some are about as long as a hand, broad and round at one end and pointed at the other, like an almond. Handaxes, picks and other large bifacial tools are one characteristic of the archaeological tradition called the Acheulean. It takes its name from a findspot in France.

According to a later account of the site, an archaeological team working west of Lake Turkana in Kenya found a place called Kokiselei 4 in 1994. In 2011, *Nature* published stone tools from the site, dated to about 1.76 million years ago. **The paper said the Acheulean may have originated in Africa.**

Two years later, researchers at Konso in Ethiopia published their findings. Their earliest layer, about 1.75 million years old, could not be distinguished in age from the Kenyan site. In 2026, researchers redated the layers at ‘Ubeidiya in Israel, where the tools have long been assigned to the early Acheulean. The dating model favored about 1.93 to 2.14 million years ago, but also gave a younger range of about 1.19 to 1.77 million years ago.

Accounts differ on how late the Acheulean lasted. One review called it the longest-lasting technology in prehistory. **Recorded history is about five thousand years long. A million and a half years is about three hundred times that long.**

Over that span, handaxes appear in Africa, western Asia, Europe and the Indian subcontinent, among other places. A site in southeastern India was dated to about 1.5 million years ago.

## 2. How Long Does It Take to Learn to Make a Handaxe?

At Konso, scattered artifacts were seen at the surface before excavation found tools still in the oldest layer. Eighteen of forty-seven large flakes had been shaped into tools, nearly all from local basalt. The largest pick was over twenty centimeters long, longer than an unsharpened pencil. A pick is longer and narrower than a handaxe, with a more pronounced point.

To see how a stone becomes a handaxe, researchers got people today to learn. Some experiments let people who had never struck stone practice for up to ninety hours and tracked how their skills developed. Others used brain imaging to see what planning of movements and spatial judgments making a handaxe demanded beyond striking off a simpler flake. The Konso paper also cited an estimate that stoneworkers today need upward of five years before their skills become consistent.

Boxgrove in England has a different kind of trace. There, people used soft hammers of antler and bone to tap the edges of flint handaxes, putting on the finishing touches. The traces date to about 480,000 years ago.

One site study there had sixty-two roughouts and 358 finished tools. **The finished tools mostly look neat. The roughouts show them halfway there.**

## 3. Did It Change?

Did the handaxe change over a million and a half years? The researchers at Konso compared handaxes from layers dating from roughly 1.75 million to 850,000 years ago. In their reading, later tools had more flake scars and finer finishing. In the 850,000-year-old layer they highlighted “three-dimensional symmetry”: the tools were balanced from the front and from the side.

Another group looked at differences among sites. They thought symmetry and refinement often depended on local conditions, and that the long record looked more like a variable equilibrium than steady standardization. Several studies suggest that the grain of the stone, the shape of the blank and the stage at which a piece was discarded can make handaxes from the same place and period look quite different.

A paper in 2016 asked in its title whether the Acheulean handaxe was more like bird song than a Beatles tune. The author considered whether some of the stability in its form arose from inborn learning tendencies. A bird sings the song of its own kind without every detail necessarily having to be passed down by imitation.

The next year, researchers proposed starting with the assumption that a person could work out the form of early tools independently, unless the archaeology showed it was beyond what people could arrive at on their own. **Look at the same handaxes and you can ask why they did not change for a million and a half years, or how much they really changed.** Which question comes first depends on whether you expect change or think stability needs no explanation to start with.

## 4. Map Four

In 1948, Harvard Paleolithic archaeologist Hallam Movius published a ninety-two-page paper in the *Transactions of the American Philosophical Society* titled “The Lower Palaeolithic Cultures of Southern and Eastern Asia.” Ten years earlier he had been on a field survey in Burma and elsewhere.

Map Four split the known early stone tools into two broad regions by type. Africa, Europe, western Asia and the Indian subcontinent belonged to the handaxe tradition. East and Southeast Asia he assigned to the “chopper-chopping-tool tradition,” tools made by striking an edge along one side of a cobble. **What later came to be called the Movius Line ran roughly along the transition between the two.**

Later researchers have pointed to page 411, where Movius called the broad region of East and Southeast Asia an area of cultural retardation. He thought it highly unlikely that it had played a vital and dynamic role in early human evolution.

Later researchers read those lines differently. Some warn against claiming to know what went through the mind of a writer in another era. Others see a clear ranking in the words.

There is no “Movius Line” in the 1948 paper. An archaeological dictionary traces the term to the American anthropologist Carleton Coon’s 1965 book *The Living Races of Man*, where he proposed naming the line for its discoverer.

## 5. Bamboo, Stone and Population

For more than seventy years, people have offered different reasons for the difference across the line. In 1989, an American paleoanthropologist proposed the bamboo hypothesis in a magazine for the public. Bamboo is abundant in East and Southeast Asia. People could have done a lot of work with bamboo and wooden tools; simple stone tools would not mean simple work.

In 2010 another researcher published a systematic critique in a peer-reviewed journal, questioning both the evidence and the reasoning. Experiments with modern bamboo, the experimenters themselves said, had neither proved nor disproved the hypothesis.

Another explanation looks at stone. Small cobbles, coarse rock or rock that breaks too easily make large bifaces hard to produce. Others look at population. In small, scattered groups, complex skills are easier to lose by chance as they pass from one generation to the next. Another possibility is that the first groups headed east before large Acheulean bifaces became widespread.

Yet another explanation looks at research itself. East Asia has been surveyed unevenly, and older collections are classified by different standards. **Squeeze a range of flake tools into a single “chopper” label and East Asia starts to look more uniform than the West.** A 2016 paper was titled “Life without the Movius Line.”

## 6. Bose

The first stone artifacts from the Bose Basin in Guangxi, China, were found in 1973 by geologists surveying for oil. Over the next decade or so, researchers from Guangxi and the Chinese Academy of Sciences continued the surveys. By the late 1980s, more than twenty sites were known.

A team led by Hou Yamei and Rick Potts of the Smithsonian later came to Bose. The Smithsonian archive dates one handaxe they found to 1996. In 2000 they published their findings in *Science*. The title spoke of Middle Pleistocene Acheulean-like stone technology in the Bose Basin of southern China.

The date came from tektites in the same layer as the tools. A meteorite impact melts material from the ground and throws it into the air. It cools into little pieces of glass, which fall back to the ground. **Argon isotope measurements on the glass gave an age of about 803,000 years, give or take 3,000.**

Later, at a site at Nanbanshan, one layer yielded 176 stone artifacts, including two handaxes, along with 155 tektites. The glass was fresh, with sharp rather than rounded edges. The researchers discussed whether it had been buried soon after falling. The age of the glass can be carried over to tools in the same layer if water had not moved the glass in between.

A Chinese paper in 2022 said redeposition could not be ruled out for some other layers: the material might have been carried by water or another process and piled up again.

Chinese researchers themselves have disagreed on whether the Bose tools were handaxes. A 1995 review in *Acta Anthropologica Sinica* looked at twenty-eight supposed Bose handaxes and concluded they were not true handaxes. In 2002 Xie Guangmao revisited the definition in the same journal and made the case that Bose did have true handaxes.

A 2001 headline in the *Bulletin of the Chinese Academy of Sciences* said the Bose handaxes challenged the Movius Line. News reports at the time spoke of rewriting human evolution. In 2012, researchers measured the shapes of Bose and western Acheulean handaxes. Their title described “morphological convergence,” not a difference in cognition.

## 7. A Ratchet

The line later got caught up in another question. Does human culture build up layer by layer? If only humans build it this way, the number of layers becomes another way to put things in a line.

Researchers call this a ratchet, a mechanism that turns only one way and does not slide back. In 2009, three researchers wrote that once an improvement appears, it must pass to others faithfully enough for the next improvement to build on it.

They also proposed a “zone of latent solutions.” These are behaviors individuals can figure out on their own, without learning by watching anyone else. It is the starting assumption raised earlier.

In a 2012 experiment, capuchins, chimpanzees and children got the same box, with three levels they could unlock in order. The reward improved with each level: for the animals, from carrot to apple to grape; for the children, increasingly appealing stickers. Children often reached the last level. Chimpanzees and capuchins rarely or never did. **The researchers recorded twenty-three clear instances of direct teaching, all among the children.**

In 2017, another group had homing pigeons fly home in pairs. At intervals they replaced an experienced bird with a newcomer. Across replacements, the routes became more efficient. In 2018, two researchers listed four core criteria for cumulative culture and said these might also be met by nonhuman species. **The debate about whether cumulative culture is present is partly about what the term means.**

A stricter question asks whether culture building up layer by layer amounts to a change in the level of evolution. In 1995, John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry called changes in the way information was stored and transmitted major transitions. Human language and society were on their list. A later line of work asked whether units that once reproduced on their own had joined into a new reproducing whole. By that measure, **a person can leave the group they were born into and still have children.** In 2021, other researchers proposed that the emergence of culturally organized human groups might itself be an evolutionary transition. In 2023, another researcher set out four reasons to doubt it. Which measure to use is still in dispute.

## 8. A Line on the Map

Other handaxes later turned up on the far side of the line. At the Danjiangkou Reservoir in Hubei, China, a 2014 comparison found no significantly greater variation in handaxe shape than in western Acheulean examples. Li Chaorong, one of the researchers, told a reporter that the Chinese handaxes were homegrown. A 2017 study dated a handaxe-bearing layer at one site in the Luonan Basin, Shaanxi, to around 900,000 years ago.

At Jeongok-ri in Korea, Greg Bowen, serving with the U.S. Air Force in 1978, picked up several stone artifacts along the Hantan River. He had taken an archaeology class at an American community college and thought scholars should see them. **One was later identified as an Acheulean-style handaxe.** According to a Korean newspaper account in 2022, he was out with the woman who became his wife.

Map Four showed where tools were known in 1948. To put places with handaxes on one side and those without on the other took a chisel stroke across a map. Once the line was drawn, Bose, Danjiangkou, Luonan and Jeongok-ri on its far side became part of the remainder that would not fit.

The 1948 paper called that side an area of cultural retardation. Most things put in the line up to now have been individual fossils. This time it was a vast stretch of land on a map.

The line was arranged, not walked.
