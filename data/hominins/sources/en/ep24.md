# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (24): The Margaret Mead Quote We Cannot Trace

## 1. Bones in the Attic

A surgeon was alone in an attic, looking through boxes of bones dug up at a monastery. They took him back to a lecture he remembered hearing. As he recalled it, the American anthropologist Margaret Mead had held up a human femur, or thighbone, for the audience to see.

In his recollection the bone had broken and healed. Mead said that, in her view, the earliest evidence of true civilization was a healed thighbone like this. She compared it with bones marked by arrows or clubs. For a break to heal, she said, someone had to hunt for the injured person, bring food and look after them.

The surgeon was Paul Brand. His recollection appeared in the chapter on bones in a book about the body that he wrote with Philip Yancey, published in 1980. Mead had died in 1978. The book came out after her death, and this is the earliest known page on which the story appears.

There is no date or place for the lecture. The account does not say what site the bone came from, how old it was, or where it was kept. In 1993, the two retold the story in another book. The opening now put it early in Brand’s career. There was more detail: Mead held the bone above her head and pointed to a thickened place where it had broken.

The later versions got neater. In 2012 a doctor put it in a book as a question and answer. A student now asked the question, and the site had an age: fifteen thousand years. Versions online often end by saying that civilization begins when somebody helps another person through a difficult time.

In the headline of a *Forbes* article in 2020, the bone became a fifteen-thousand-year-old human bone. A Chinese newspaper retold the story that year as a famous pronouncement by Mead.

In 2021 a researcher who traces quotations checked a scan of the 1980 edition. Whether the story is accurate, the researcher wrote, depends on Brand’s memory and credibility. In 2023 a fact-checking site called it unverified. It had found no evidence that could independently confirm the anecdote. **Neither inquiry found a passage Mead herself had written or a recording of her saying it.**

The fifteen thousand years in the later versions can go on a rough timeline. Hominins are all the members on our side after the split from the chimpanzee branch. Write their roughly seven-million-year record as a book of seven hundred pages and each page covers about ten thousand years.

Fifteen thousand years ago falls in the middle of the second-to-last page. The earliest known cities in Mesopotamia began to appear about six thousand years ago. You come to them about two-fifths of the way down the final page.

## 2. It Depends on the Definition

When does a culture become a civilization? An interviewer asked Mead this in a collection published in 1968. The section was titled “Margaret Mead on what is a culture? what is civilization?” Mead began by saying it was “a matter of definition.”

Looking back, she said, we call societies civilizations when they have large cities, an elaborate division of labor and some means of keeping records. She explained that records might use a system of signs, not necessarily writing like our own. Cities need a division of labor to keep a dense population going.

She said that civilization here was a technical description, distinct from the everyday judgment involved in calling someone uncivilized. There is no thighbone in the conversation and nothing about an earliest sign of civilization.

**The most widely shared Mead quotation can be traced back only to somebody else’s account, published after her death. The one we can trace is about cities, the division of labor and records.**

## 3. Eight Fractures

If a wild chimpanzee breaks a bone in a fall, can it heal? In 1997 a researcher compared injuries in the skeletons of several African great apes. One set came from wild chimpanzees at Gombe, Tanzania: fourteen skulls and thirteen skeletons other than the skulls, some from adults and some from younger animals.

The paper said the clearest injuries outside the skull were healed fractures. **About thirty percent of the Gombe skeletons below the skull had an injury of that kind in his count, the highest proportion among the ape groups he compared.** He mentioned thighbones as well. Healed fractures of the femur with serious disability were most common and most severe in female gorillas.

In 1989, the same researcher had examined the Gombe skeletons on their own. There were eleven individuals of suitable age and completeness for analysis. Ten had some kind of bone pathology. The abstract also recorded bites and fractures associated with fighting between individuals.

The chimpanzee with the most fractures was named Hugo. There were eight in his bones. The 1997 paper read the pattern of injuries across the groups as suggesting that African great apes living at liberty risk falls from a height and attacks from one another, especially among males. **What the bones record is that they broke and healed.**

## 4. Fire, Bows and Arrows, Pottery

Fire, bows and arrows, pottery, domesticated animals, iron, a phonetic alphabet. In 1877 the American scholar Lewis Henry Morgan published a book that used these things as markers to divide the human past into seven stages. Its title was *Ancient Society, or Researches in the Lines of Human Progress from Savagery through Barbarism to Civilization*.

The first three stages were savagery and the next three barbarism, each divided into lower, middle and upper. The last stage was civilization. Lower savagery ran from the infancy of humankind to the use of fire and the acquisition of fish for food. The middle stage ran to the bow and arrow, the upper to pottery.

Lower barbarism began with pottery. The middle stage began in the Eastern Hemisphere with the domestication of animals, and in the Western Hemisphere with irrigated cultivation and building in adobe and stone. The upper stage ran from smelting iron to a phonetic alphabet and writing. Civilization ran from then to the present. Morgan added that hieroglyphics inscribed in stone could be accepted as an equivalent.

Before listing the stages, Morgan said they were provisional. It was hard to find criteria that worked without exception on every continent. In taking pottery as a boundary he wrote “probably” and said that the line was necessarily somewhat arbitrary.

The table made room for living people too. Under middle savagery he put Australians and most Polynesians as they were when first encountered by the people writing the record. **In 1877, Morgan put Australian peoples who were alive in his own time in the box called “Middle Status of Savagery.”** Fish and fire were at one end of the box, the bow and arrow at the other.

## 5. A Book’s Subtitle

“In the light of the researches of Lewis H. Morgan.” That was the subtitle of a German book published in 1884 in Hottingen, Zürich, Switzerland. It was Friedrich Engels’s *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State*.

The first edition’s preface said the chapters that followed were, to a degree, the fulfillment of a last wish. Karl Marx had intended to set out Morgan’s findings alongside the conclusions of his materialist study of history, which he had partly undertaken with Engels.

**Literally, the first chapter’s title is “Prehistoric Stages of Culture.”** The German word for prehistory puts “before” and “history” together. The first sentence says Morgan was the first to bring specialist knowledge to the task of giving humanity’s prehistory a definite order. His grouping would probably hold unless a much larger body of material forced a change.

Engels kept Morgan’s three broad periods, savagery, barbarism and civilization. He divided the first two into lower, middle and upper stages according to how far people had come in producing what they needed to live. For the forms of the family, he explained, Morgan had sketched the formal changes, but Engels had largely to supply the economic content and causes himself.

## 6. “Before”

Around six thousand years ago, Uruk in southern Mesopotamia began to grow into a city. In most other parts of the world at the same time there were villages, pastoralists and gatherers. In a cave in Germany, bones of people buried around six to five thousand years ago have isotope readings that fall into two groups. One had eaten a diet that came from farming; the other relied on freshwater fish and gathering.

The first unit of the People’s Education Press history textbook for the first semester of seventh grade was titled “Prehistory: Human Activity within China.” A newer version changed it to “Prehistory: Early Humans and the Origins of Civilization within China.” Its introduction describes cultivation, keeping animals and making pottery beginning about ten thousand years ago, and tribal alliances appearing around five or six thousand years ago.

A 2025 introduction from the provincial government in Liaoning, China, calls the Niuheliang site, from around 5,500 to 5,000 years ago, the dawn of Chinese civilization. In a publicly available set of teaching materials, the “Learning Focus” box from a Chinese high-school history textbook says that with the emergence of classes and states, humans entered civilized society.

Different people draw the line in different places. Mead names large cities, divisions of labor and records. Morgan names a phonetic alphabet and writing. The Chinese high-school text names classes and the state. The long stretch on this side of the line, for both Engels and the Chinese textbook, is prehistory. It comes before something.

Some places never had cities. The people living there did not live before anybody else. **Only when “prehistory” is treated as a timetable for every place does “before” put people who were alive at the same time in a sequence. The city builders go out in front. All the others are placed in “before.”**

To say that a people lived before is to say they had not yet reached the line, as though it were a place every group would reach sooner or later. The “not yet” in an ordinary sentence here grows into a whole span of time.

In measuring bones the question has often been how much they resemble people alive today. In measuring living people the question becomes whether they count as civilized. The seven-stage table in 1877 asked the second question.

## 7. Measuring from the Top of Your Own Head

A person stands in a line without a tape measure and uses their own height as the scale. The person to the left is half a head shorter, the person to the right two fingers taller. Every reading in the line tells you how much taller or shorter somebody is than the one taking the measure. None gives the person’s own height.

Several measures for ancient people also start at zero in people alive today. In reading ancient DNA, researchers often align short fragments to a human reference genome, like fitting slips of paper back into a book taken as the reference.

According to the international consortium that maintains the reference, about seventy percent of the primary assembly of the widely used human reference genome GRCh38 comes from one person. He was a man who answered a newspaper advertisement in Buffalo, New York, in 1997. His name was not made public. **Some DNA from the people being read is already in the people reading it.** In people outside Africa, about one to a little over two percent of the genome comes from Neanderthals.

The American paleoanthropologist Richard Klein made a list of things such as ornaments and symbols to measure ancient people’s behavior. Two archaeologists criticized the idea that these things arrived together in a sudden revolution, calling it a deeply Eurocentric bias.

Some readings later stopped being possible. In 1996 the shallows of the Columbia River in Washington State yielded the bones of a man who lived eight or nine thousand years ago. He is commonly known as Kennewick Man. A U.S. federal law that took effect late in 2016 provided for the state of Washington to transfer the remains to five tribes. After the transfer in February 2017, the remains were buried again; the location is not public.

In 2021 researchers proposed five guidelines for ancient-DNA research. One said researchers must accept a negative answer if the parties concerned do not jointly support the work. **A law, a burial, a guideline: each was a decision made on a particular day. Earlier findings remain on record. Some remains can no longer be sampled.**

## 8. Civilization

In our own history, did we ever shift to a different level the way cells come together in a body? There are three candidates here: culture that accumulates layer by layer, language, and agriculture.

In a stricter line of work in biology on the formation of new individuals, the original units must lose the ability to reproduce independently, the new whole must be able to reproduce, and competition among its parts must be kept in check over time. Take the first question alone. People do not lose the ability to have children when they change groups or languages. People who do not farm have children too. It is different for the crops. Wheat that holds on to its grain relies on people to sow it, and maize has difficulty sustaining reproduction without cultivation.

Another line of work asks how information is stored and passed down. In 1995 two biologists used this criterion to include language among the major evolutionary transitions. In 2021 researchers proposed that human societies organized by culture might meet criteria for an evolutionary transition. In 2023 someone else set out four reasons to doubt it. The two approaches are not asking the same question.

**Around six to five thousand years ago, Uruk was building a city, the two groups in the German cave ate differently, and many other places had villages, pastoralists and gatherers. “Before” puts them in a sequence. The fact that they were alive at the same time does not fit in that word.**

This series on human evolution stops here, beside the earliest known cities around six thousand years ago. What comes next is the word “civilization” itself. Who drew the line, when, and did they use cities, writing or states? Each time, which people living at the same time were standing on the other side? That belongs to the series on ancient civilizations.

The line was arranged, and in the direction it faces there is something called civilization.
