# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (7): Neighbors on the Mudflat

## 1. Mud

In 2021, on the east shore of Kenya’s Lake Turkana, a team was clearing hominin bones from a layer of sediment. According to a later account from a participating American university, heavy rains washed away soil every wet season, and the team went out again to survey what had been exposed. Richard Loki, a member of the team, noticed several huge bird prints and then recognized a hominin footprint.

In July 2022, the team exposed more of the surface. The ground was older than a volcanic ash layer about ten meters above it, dated to about 1.52 million years ago. The paper put the footprints at around 1.5 million years ago.

The mudflat belongs to the span from about two million to 1.2 million years ago. Eight hundred thousand years is ten thousand lives of eighty years laid end to end. Most of the fossils from that time come from lakesides and gorges in Kenya and Tanzania and caves in South Africa. A few sites are farther away, in Georgia, Spain and China’s Yunnan Province.

The surface was once wet mud by a lake or on a river delta. The top few centimeters were firmer than what lay beneath, and feet sank four to more than eight centimeters. There were no drying cracks, traces of roots or obvious signs of erosion in the mud. **Soon after the prints were made, fine sand and silt covered them.**

On the same mud surface, the team recorded sixty-one bird prints, thirty from bovids (the family that includes cattle and antelopes), and three from members of the horse family. The widest bird print was about twenty-seven centimeters across.

Among the hominin prints was a continuous trail left by one individual. Three isolated prints pointed in a different direction.

## 2. Hours to Days

The continuous trail and the isolated prints do not look alike. In the trail the big toe angles farther out, and its angle varies from step to step. Subtract the smallest angle from the largest for each foot and the spread is about sixteen degrees for the left and nineteen for the right. In the comparison with modern human prints used in the paper, the reference range for that spread was about 1.4 to 10.6 degrees. In two of the isolated prints, once the depth of the impression was taken into account, the imprint left by the arch was closer in shape to that of modern human footprints.

No skeleton found beside the prints could be matched to them. When Kevin Hatala and colleagues published the findings in *Science* in 2024, they offered their attribution as a hypothesis: they proposed that *Paranthropus boisei* had made the trail and *Homo erectus* the isolated prints.

*Paranthropus boisei* belongs to a genus whose skulls often have high bony crests and very large cheek teeth. *Homo erectus* is a species in our genus and includes Peking Man. Why those two? Their reason was timing. On the evidence they used, the last reliable local records for *Homo habilis* and another early species, *Homo rudolfensis*, were around 1.75 and 1.78 million years ago respectively. *Homo erectus* and *Paranthropus boisei* were still there a little over 1.4 million years ago.

The authors also raised another possibility. Even allowing for how firm or soft the mud was, the shape of the feet and ways of walking in one species may simply have varied more than they do in living humans.

How much time passed between the two kinds of prints? There are no cracks, no roots, no signs of erosion, and no jumble of animal prints trampled over and over. The mud was exposed for a short time. **The paper’s estimate was hours, or at most days.** Hatala told the Associated Press that the different hominins may well have seen one another.

In 2009, other footprints from about 1.51 to 1.53 million years ago were reported at Ileret, farther north along the east shore. The big toe lay in line with the other toes and the foot had an arch. The authors at the time thought those prints were consistent with *Homo erectus*. When Hatala’s team looked again at the Ileret material, they distinguished two ways of walking.

## 3. Eight Walkers

There is another footprint surface along the eastern shore, about 1.43 million years old. It was not a new discovery. An American geologist and colleagues had published prints from there in 1981. According to an institutional account in 2026, the surface was later covered with sand for protection. Hatala’s team reopened it in 2016 and 2023 and recorded more of it.

According to the 2026 paper, **there are twenty-one hominin footprints representing eight individuals.** Hippos and antelopes left prints on the same surface.

Who were the eight? The paper offered two possibilities. The shape within each print most closely resembled that of the trail attributed to *Paranthropus boisei*, and the way of walking was unlike ours. But estimates of height from the prints were higher than the estimates from known skeletons of that species. That raised the possibility, the authors wrote, that the walkers were *Homo erectus*.

Either possibility has a consequence. If the walkers were *Paranthropus boisei*, that species varied more in height than its known skeletons suggest. If they were *Homo erectus*, the feet and walks of that species were more varied than usually thought.

Public releases from the Smithsonian and the Max Planck Institute called them eight members of *Paranthropus boisei*, and gave estimates for some individuals of up to around 1.8 meters and seventy-five kilograms.

## 4. Nutcracker Man

*Paranthropus boisei* went by another name at first. On July 17, 1959, Mary Leakey found a skull in Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania. According to a later account from the Leakey Foundation, it was in about four hundred pieces when it came out of the ground.

Her husband named it *Zinjanthropus boisei* in *Nature* that year. The first name draws on an old Arabic geographical name for East Africa; the second honors a supporter of their work. According to the same account, the team called it “Zinj” and also “Dear Boy.” The press saw those large molars and jaws and called it “Nutcracker Man.”

In 2011 a group measured carbon in East African *Paranthropus boisei* tooth enamel. The researchers estimated that sources based on grasses and sedges accounted for on average about seventy-seven percent of the diets represented by the samples, give or take seven percentage points. The carbon in teeth can be traced back to food sources, but it cannot by itself tell us whether the animal ate the plants directly or ate animals that fed on them.

The great chewing apparatus might be an adaptation for processing large quantities of food, the authors suggested. **The name says nutcracker. The carbon in the teeth points toward foods based on grasses and sedges.**

The species is recorded from about 2.3 million years ago to a little over 1.3 million years ago. An older museum account puts the genus as a whole between about 2.7 million and one million years ago. **The genus lived for well over a million years.** In 2025, when an international news agency reported new *Paranthropus boisei* fossils, it called the species an evolutionary dead end.

## 5. A Hand

The new fossils in that report came from the east shore of the lake and were recovered between 2019 and 2021. Public accounts give a count of forty-two pieces, including parts of the skull and teeth, hands and feet. The deposits put them a little before 1.52 million years ago.

What matters is that **the skull, teeth, hands and feet are from one skeleton.** There is no longer any need to guess whose hand it was from the size or findspot alone. The field director said in an institutional account that clearing the sediment around the bones took a great deal of time.

In October 2025, a team published the hand in *Nature*. They found its relatively long thumb and short fingers close in overall proportion to ours and far from a gorilla’s. The wrist bones and the base of the thumb were different. The authors thought a fine pinch, pad to pad, was probably less effective than it is in our hands.

Some features of the forearm and hand resembled those associated with a gorilla’s strong grasp. The place where a tendon attaches to the radius was large. The authors read those features as suited to a forceful grip and to handling food. They might also have helped with climbing. The shape did not rule out making or using tools.

In the university’s release, the lead author said the hand could make a precision grip much like ours and had a power grip more like a gorilla’s.

## 6. A Bone the Size of a Matchbox

One Sunday morning in August 1984, Kamoya Kimeu was walking a gentle slope south of a seasonal river at Nariokotome on the west shore of Lake Turkana. **He saw a piece of skull the size of a matchbox.** Kimeu had spent years finding fossils on the Leakey family’s field teams. Excavation uncovered a skull, teeth, ribs and the better part of a skeleton on the slope.

It is known as Turkana Boy. It was published in *Nature* in 1985, dates to about 1.6 to 1.5 million years ago and is usually assigned to *Homo erectus*. By the Smithsonian’s count, the skeleton is about forty percent complete. A research institute in the lake region puts it more vividly for the public: nearly the whole body is there, except for the feet.

The boy’s age at death has changed. Early estimates matched his teeth to the development of modern human teeth and gave an age around eleven or twelve. Later researchers studying growth lines in the enamel and roots concluded that his teeth developed faster than ours. He was probably closer to eight or nine.

An early reconstruction put him at about 1.6 meters when he died. Projecting from the course of modern human growth at puberty gave an adult height around 1.85 meters. As the age estimate changed and researchers came to think that early *Homo* grew differently, the projected adult height came down and its range widened. **Same skeleton, different growth chart, different age.**

## 7. Half a Million Years

*Homo habilis* was named in 1964 from Olduvai Gorge fossils. The name means “handy man.” In 2007, a *Nature* paper reported an upper jaw from Ileret on the east shore of the lake and assigned it to *habilis*. It was around 1.44 million years old, and later reviews called it the youngest known member of the species. **That is about 300,000 years later than the last *habilis* record used by Hatala’s team.**

On the *Homo erectus* side, a bone from the back of a skull in East Africa has been assigned to the species and dated to about 1.9 million years ago. Where it originally came from and from which layer were once in question. **Using the East African dates on either end, later reviews put the overlap of the two species in East Africa at close to half a million years.**

The lead author of the paper told Reuters that *Homo habilis* was a sister species of *Homo erectus*. Coauthor Meave Leakey told the public that their coexistence made it less likely that *erectus* had evolved from *habilis*.

The Smithsonian website now says scientists once thought *Homo habilis* was the ancestor of *Homo erectus*. The boundaries of the *habilis* box are disputed too. In 2012, an American paleoanthropologist suggested calling two groups by the specimen numbers of their representative skulls in some analyses, without rushing to give them species names.

South Africa was a tangle of branches around two million years ago too. In 2015, at the Drimolen field school, Richard Curtis, a student from Australia’s La Trobe University, began cleaning a specimen too fragmented to recognize. Pieced together in the field, it turned out to be the skull of a young hominin. The authors dated it to about 2.04 million years ago and found it close to *Homo erectus*. Its nickname is “Simon,” after the late field technician Simon Mokobane.

The same cave yielded a skull of *Paranthropus robustus*, a South African species of the genus. In their acknowledgments the authors credit Casey, the local landowner, with finding its first tooth. The skull took his name as its nickname. In 2020, researchers wrote that *Homo*, *Paranthropus* and *Australopithecus* coexisted around two million years ago.

That australopith came from Malapa, another South African cave. On August 15, 2008, nine-year-old Matthew Berger found a right collarbone there. It was Malapa’s first hominin fossil.

His father remembered him calling out, “Dad, I found a fossil!” The species was named *Australopithecus sediba* in 2010. Published dating in 2011, using flowstones and paleomagnetism, put the fossils at 1,977,000 years old, give or take two thousand years.

His father, paleoanthropologist Lee Berger, has proposed several relationships to *Homo*, including the possibility that *sediba* was a direct ancestor. Other researchers questioned what can be inferred from the face of a juvenile. The Drimolen team thought their young skull, which resembled *Homo erectus*, slightly predated *sediba*. On their dating, it was harder to make the case for a direct ancestor.

## 8. The Same Mudflat

Outside Africa, hominins left tools at Dmanisi, Georgia, from about 1.85 million years ago. At the Sima del Elefante in Atapuerca, Spain, there is a piece of a face from around 1.4 to 1.1 million years ago nicknamed “Pink.” The paper called it reasonable to assign it to a form of *Homo* close to *erectus*. In Yuanmou, Yunnan, the geologist Qian Fang picked up two upper incisors on May 1, 1965. When Xinhua announced them to China in February 1972, it called them two fossil ape-man teeth, grayish white and heavily mineralized.

On a lakeside mudflat 1.5 million years ago, **two kinds of footprints were left in the same mud, days apart at most.** According to the reviews, *Homo habilis* and *Homo erectus* lived in East Africa together for around half a million years. Three genera lived in southern Africa around two million years ago. *Paranthropus* lasted well over a million years, and a news story called *Paranthropus boisei* a dead end.

Put the names in a line, *habilis* first, *erectus* next, *Paranthropus* off to the side, and you have a picture people made much later. All that remained on the mudflat were footprints.

The line was arranged, not walked.
