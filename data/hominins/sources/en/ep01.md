# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (1): The Crushed Skull in the Desert

## 1. Hope of Life

July 19, 2001. In the Djurab Desert of northern Chad, a joint French and Chadian team was looking for fossils at a site numbered 266. This is the southern edge of the Sahara. Most of what can be picked up off the ground there is fragments of animal bone.

That day they found a skull. The first to spot it was reportedly Ahounta Djimdoumalbaye, a Chadian member of the team. The expedition was led by the French paleontologist Michel Brunet. Three other team members were at the site, including Alain Beauvilain, who took the photographs that day.

The skull was almost complete, but it had been flattened and broken into several pieces. It twisted to one side like a cardboard box somebody had stepped on.

The place is desert now. Back then it lay beside a large body of water. The team collected fish, crocodiles, turtles and animals that lived half in the water, like hippos, from the same site as the skull. There were animals from woodland and grassland too.

The skull soon had a name: Toumaï. Reports explain it as a word in the local Dazaga language meaning “hope of life,” a name given to children born near the start of the dry season. The story goes that Chad’s president at the time suggested it.

In 2002, Brunet’s team published the find in *Nature* and gave it a new scientific name: *Sahelanthropus tchadensis*. One phrase from the paper would be quoted again and again: this was the “oldest known” member of the human lineage.

To make sense of that, it helps to start with a rough map of time. The familiar example in stories of human origins is Lucy, who lived about 3.2 million years ago. Toumaï lived nearly four million years before her, around seven million years ago.

How long is seven million years? **Squeeze it into one day. Toumaï is at midnight. Lucy does not turn up until around one in the afternoon, and Homo sapiens around eleven at night. All of recorded history fits into the last minute.**

Then there is the word “hominin.” Our closest living relatives are chimpanzees and bonobos. Go far enough back and the two sides were once a single population of apes, which split into two branches. Everything on our side after the split, whatever it looked like, is a hominin. **To ask whether a fossil is a hominin is to ask which side of the fork it belongs on.**

The paper put Toumaï on our side of the fork and judged it the oldest find on that side at the time. In the year and a bit before that paper, two sets of bones, one from Kenya and one from Ethiopia, had been published with much the same claim. Three sets of bones, three “firsts.”

## 2. East Side Story

To see why Toumaï came as a surprise, go back to 1983. The French paleoanthropologist Yves Coppens proposed an account of where humans came from and borrowed the name of a Broadway musical. He called his model the “East Side Story.”

The East African Rift is a long wound in the ground running from the southern end of the Red Sea down to Mozambique. Plateaus and cliffs flank it, and a string of lakes lies along the valley floor.

Coppens told it this way. The ground split. The land rose on both sides of the rift, blocking moisture coming in from the west. Forest remained west of the rift. To the east it grew drier, the trees thinned out, and more open ground appeared.

A population of apes that had once ranged across the area was separated by the rift. In this account, those to the west stayed in the forest and eventually gave rise to chimpanzees and gorillas. Those to the east moved into open country, began walking on two legs and eventually gave rise to hominins. Some Chinese middle-school biology slides tell a similar story: as the forests shrank, apes came down to live on the ground.

The appeal of the story was that it joined up pieces that did not seem related: what the earth’s crust did, how rain and forests changed on either side, how the apes were separated, and how they ended up walking upright. And the oldest hominin fossils then known did all come from East Africa.

Coppens told the story to a wider audience in *Scientific American* in 1994. The subtitle put the secret of our divergence from the great apes, and of the emergence of human beings, in the African rift.

**A crack in the ground divided the apes into two families. The family to the east came down from the trees.** It was one of the most widely told accounts of those years. It was easy to tell: maps, climate and bones all seemed to be on its side.

In 1995, Brunet’s team found an australopith jaw in Chad and nicknamed it Abel. Chad lies more than 2,000 kilometers west of the rift. The jaw was not especially early. The story could still stand, with one exception out on the edge.

## 3. Two Sets of Bones at the Millennium

From October 2000, in the Tugen Hills of central Kenya, a team led by the French researcher Brigitte Senut and the British researcher Martin Pickford found several pieces of thighbone, part of a jaw and a few teeth. The people who found them included Ezra Kiptalam Cheboi, a local member of the team.

Because the bones came out of the ground at the turn of the millennium, the papers called the find “Millennium Man.” When the team published it early in 2001, they named it *Orrorin tugenensis*. In the local Tugen language, the first part means “original man.” It lived around six million years ago. At the time there were lakes and rivers in the Tugen Hills, as well as open woodland and dense forest.

The thighbone did most of the work of placing it on our side. At the top of the thighbone is a ball. Below it is a neck that angles away. In an animal walking on two legs, the weight of the body bears on that neck with every step. How long it grows and how thick its walls get depend on how it is loaded.

That was why Senut and Pickford thought it regularly walked on two legs. They read its curved finger bones as signs that it still climbed trees a good deal. They also argued that those pieces of thighbone looked more like ours than those of the much later australopiths.

Later studies agreed that it could walk on two legs but found that its thighbone resembled those of australopiths. One researcher described it as “unexpectedly intermediate” between apes and later hominins.

At nearly the same time, in Ethiopia, Yohannes Haile-Selassie published fossils from the Middle Awash valley dating from 5.8 to 5.2 million years ago. The name he gave them, *kadabba*, means something like “the ancestor at the base of the family” in the local Afar language.

In one paragraph of the abstract, two phrases sit close together. One calls the find the “earliest definitive evidence” of hominins. The other says the material “probably” represents a hominin group.

The material came from several places: teeth, part of a jaw, bits of arm bone and a toe bone. The argument about how it walked rests largely on that one bone.

At the end of a step, a person rolls up onto the toes, which bend upward. The joint at the base of the toe has a distinctive shape. Haile-Selassie read the joint surface of this toe bone as consistent with that motion. **The trouble is that the toe and the teeth used to identify the species did not come from the same individual or even the same site. They are also separated in time by several hundred thousand years.**

## 4. The Crushed Skull

Back to Toumaï. The brain was small, about the size of a chimpanzee’s. The brow ridge was very heavy. But the face was shorter than a chimpanzee’s and did not project as far.

What mattered most to Brunet’s team were the canines. A chimpanzee’s upper canine is long and sharp. Every time the mouth closes, it rubs against a lower tooth, sharpening it like a blade over a lifetime. Toumaï’s canine was small, worn at the tip and showed no sign of this honing.

There is another feature underneath the skull. A large opening there, the foramen magnum, is where the brainstem meets the spinal cord. In humans the opening is farther forward than in apes that generally walk on four limbs. That helps the head balance over the spine.

Questions came as soon as the paper appeared. In the autumn of 2002, *Nature* printed a letter signed by four researchers. Its title was a question: “Sahelanthropus or ‘Sahelpithecus’?” One name ends in “human,” the other in “ape.” Two of the signatories, Senut and Pickford, had discovered Millennium Man. To them the features taken together could not rule out an ape. They thought a female gorilla was a reasonable comparison.

The skull was crushed. To tell where the opening had been, somebody first had to put the skull back together. In 2005, a team used X-ray scans to move its pieces back into place on a computer. The authors wrote that it “might” have walked upright.

The age was hard to pin down too. At many East African fossil sites, volcanic ash forms layers. Its minerals can be dated directly. A layer above a fossil and another below it can bracket its age. **There was no volcanic ash that could be dated in the deposits where Toumaï was found.**

Brunet’s team first compared the animals from the site with animal populations from dated sites elsewhere, putting Toumaï between six and seven million years ago. In 2008 researchers tried another way. Cosmic rays in the atmosphere continually make atoms of beryllium-10. Rain carries them into lakes, where they settle into the mud. Beryllium-10 decays. Comparing it with a stable form of beryllium in the mud gives an estimate of how long the sediment has been there. The researchers combined 28 samples from Toumaï’s site and another nearby deposit they judged to be of the same age. They came up with about seven million years. To give the skull that age, they still had to establish that it had originally been buried in that deposit.

Beauvilain later raised a question. The title of a paper he published in 2008 asked whether Toumaï had been dug up, embedded in sandstone or picked up from the surface. A year later he and a colleague used the photographs from the day to argue that the skull had been lying on the surface. They even suggested it could have been moved or reburied in recent times. Brunet’s team stood by the date, pointing to agreement between the animal fossils and a large set of sediment samples.

By both of the team’s methods, Toumaï came out at around seven million years old, older than the two candidates to the east and 2,500 kilometers west of the rift. According to French-language accounts, Coppens said in 2003 that the East Side Story no longer existed. He later acknowledged that the old, clean division into east and west no longer held.

## 5. The Thighbone

Toumaï’s specimen number begins “TM 266.” TM stands for Toros-Menalla; the digits identify the site. **That string of characters ties a claim to the thing it was made about.**

In 2001, the team also recovered part of a thighbone at the same site, among bones from many other animals. Its number also starts “TM 266.” That only tells us where it came from. There is no skeleton with the bones joined up to settle whether it belonged to the same species as Toumaï.

Both ends had broken off, leaving only the shaft. The ball and neck of the thighbone, which had been so useful in thinking about gait earlier, were gone.

According to later accounts, it was taken to Poitiers, France, and stored with the other animal bones. In 2004, Aude Bergeret, who was sorting those bones, picked it out as a possible primate bone. It had not been published with the skull, and the two sides disagreed over who had the right to study it. In 2018, a *Nature* news headline said its discovery was still a secret.

The question of who gets to handle a fossil came up more than once in the story of these bones. Seven months before Millennium Man was found, the police in Kenya had held Pickford for several days over a dispute about a permit, as *Science* reported at the time.

Living bone grows in response to use. Its wall grows thicker where it regularly takes pressure. Where a muscle pulls hard, its attachment can leave a rough mark. A shaft keeps a record of how its owner used it, though the writing is faint.

In 2020, Roberto Macchiarelli, Bergeret and their colleagues published an analysis that found no support in the shaft for regular walking on two legs. In 2022, researchers from Brunet’s team published a study of the same shaft and two forearm bones. They came to the opposite conclusion: the animal walked on two legs on the ground and could climb cautiously in trees.

In 2025, Marine Cazenave and her colleagues, including some authors of the 2020 paper, published a reply. They argued that the features cited occur in plenty of other apes, and even other mammals, and do not support habitual bipedalism. Early in 2026, another team agreed that the shaft’s overall form most resembled a chimpanzee’s. But the researchers pointed to three pieces of evidence, including the twist in the shaft and a small bump, for an early form of habitual walking on two legs. That did not mean it could only get around on two legs.

**One piece of bone, four papers.** Finding a shape consistent with walking on two legs is one thing. Deciding an animal usually walked that way is another. Different researchers give those features different weight.

## 6. The Chimpanzee Side

Say “humans and apes split” and a lot of people picture a row of figures walking from left to right. On the far left is a bent ape on all fours. One by one the figures straighten, until the one at the right is standing tall. In plenty of versions the ape at the front looks like a chimpanzee.

The chimpanzee does not come at the front of that line. Chimpanzees and humans are on opposite sides of the same split. Both branches have been going for the same length of time, and both are alive now.

What fossils did the chimpanzee side leave?

In 2005, two American researchers published a paper whose title was just a few words: “First fossil chimpanzee.” The finds were three teeth from the Baringo area of Kenya. The layers above and below put them somewhere between a little over half a million and 280,000 years ago. The Tugen Hills, where Millennium Man turned up, are in Baringo too.

The chimpanzee side has left a far thinner fossil record. There are thousands of specimens on our side. **On the chimpanzee side, the finds that come up most often are those three teeth.** And their attribution has been questioned. In the nearly two million years that take in Toumaï, Millennium Man and Kadabba, no fossil has yet been identified with confidence as belonging to the chimpanzee branch.

Why so few? One common explanation is that chimpanzees mostly live in warm, wet forests. The soil there tends to be acidic, bones decay quickly, and there is not much lake mud or river sand to bury a body fast. Another part of the answer is that the people looking for fossils have mostly been looking on our side.

When did the two sides split? That is where the molecular clock comes in. Imagine two people copying the same book. They hand their copies down, and each generation makes a few mistakes in copying its version. Count how many letters now differ. If you know how many mistakes creep in per generation and how many years a generation lasts, you can work back to when the two versions parted company.

Early estimates often put the split between four and six million years ago. By that reckoning, seven-million-year-old Toumaï came before the split. Either the skull did not belong on our side, or the clock was off.

A 2012 study measured generation times in wild chimpanzees and gorillas, and used a newly measured, slower mutation rate for humans. It put the split at least seven or eight million years ago. **Fewer copying mistakes per generation than previously thought, and more years between generations.** With a slower clock, it took longer for the same number of differences to pile up.

The stretch of time that holds the three “firsts” is shaky at both ends. Estimates for the split are still shifting. Which fossils belong on the human side is still in dispute.

## 7. A Hundred and Twenty-Five Pieces

November 1994. At Aramis, in Ethiopia’s Middle Awash valley, Haile-Selassie saw a few fragments of hand bone on the ground. That was the beginning of a skeleton. Its owner lived around 4.4 million years ago, more recently than any of the three sets of bones above.

Eventually the skeleton was pieced together from about 125 fragments. Later accounts describe how soft and brittle the bones were. Some would fall apart at a touch. The team worked away the earth with bamboo slivers, wooden picks and even porcupine quills, stabilizing the bone as they went. They cut many of the pieces out with the soil around them and took them back to the laboratory to clean slowly.

The species name means “root” in Afar: *Ardipithecus ramidus*. The skeleton’s nickname is Ardi. Fifteen years later, in October 2009, *Science* published eleven papers about her together.

The team identified Ardi as female. They estimated that she stood about 1.2 meters, weighed about 50 kilos and had a brain about the size of a chimpanzee’s. Her foot was unusual. The big toe stuck out to the side and could grasp a branch like a thumb. The middle of the foot was stiff, so she could brace herself and push off on the ground. One foot doing two jobs.

That was the animal the team reconstructed: she walked on two feet on the ground, and climbed with her hands and grasping feet in the trees. **In the team’s reconstruction, she resembled neither living chimpanzees nor the australopiths that came later.**

The overview paper said this weakened the practice of using living chimpanzees as a template for the earliest hominins. In other words, the animals alive before humans and chimpanzees split need not have looked like present-day chimpanzees.

Ardi dates to 4.4 million years ago, but the point reaches further back. To draw the animals that lived near the fork in the two or three million years before her, you could no longer just reach for a picture of a chimpanzee. That is the period those three sets of bones belong to.

Not everybody agrees. Researchers have argued that some of the features claimed as unique to hominins may also have occurred in earlier apes. A 2025 study of Ardi’s ankle bone found it closer to the form used by chimpanzees and gorillas to climb. And there are differing accounts of whether Ardi’s habitat was woodland or savanna.

What happened in those fifteen years? More digging. Lifting bones with the earth still around them. Slowly cleaning and stabilizing the fragments, reconstructing them on computers and in plaster, then dividing the work among groups studying hands, feet, pelvis, teeth and habitat. Some reports said the team deliberately sat on the find, but no internal documents have been produced to support the allegation.

## 8. Three Spikes

To mark the boundary between periods, geologists select a point in a rock layer somewhere in the world and put a marker there. It is commonly called a golden spike. The rock does not choose where it goes. An international commission votes on it.

“Where does the human side begin?” is a boundary question too. **In the year and a half from early 2001 to the summer of 2002, three sets of bones from Kenya, Ethiopia and Chad were published in succession in the name of being first.** The Millennium Man paper called it the “first Miocene hominid”; the Kadabba abstract said “earliest definitive”; the Toumaï paper said “oldest known.”

Three spikes in three places, their dates spread across well over a million years. No commission voted on them.

Two paleoanthropologists put the question more directly in *Nature* in 2011. What puts these fossils on our side? The evidence is the handful of features we have already seen: small canines without a honing edge, the opening nearer the middle of the skull base, and features of the thigh and foot associated with walking on two legs. All are traits people picked out of later hominins.

The paper pointed out that similar skeletal forms do not always mean a closer evolutionary history. Near the split there are fewer features to tell the two sides apart. A similar shape can develop independently in different apes. Smaller canines might mean less fighting among males. They might just have to do with size or sex.

There is another jaw, found in Greece in 1944. A 2017 study proposed it as a hominin candidate along with a tooth from Bulgaria, dating the finds to about 7.2 million years ago, older than Toumaï. A key difficulty, the paper said, is how widespread similar forms can be. In the headlines it became “the oldest human ancestor.”

“Oldest” is a statement about the record: of the fossils that people have found and published, which is oldest? It does not tell us who the first creature on our side of the split actually was.

If an older fossil can be placed on our side, the known record moves back. When the two branches really parted is a separate question. The closer to the fork, the fewer the features we have to go by. **Drawing a line is the chisel stroke. The space it marks out is the construct. What remains and cannot be placed cleanly is the remainder.** From the three spikes at the start of the century to the thighbone studied in 2026, the record we can make out has changed several times. The parts that cannot be placed have not gone away.

One last small thing. The names of three of these four finds speak of beginnings: “original man,” “the ancestor at the base of the family,” and “root.” Toumaï is the exception. It is “hope of life,” the name of a child born near the start of the dry season.

“Original,” “at the base,” “root”: the people who gave them their names put those words there.

The line was arranged, not walked.
