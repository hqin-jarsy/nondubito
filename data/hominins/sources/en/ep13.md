# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (13): Was the “Hobbit” a Sick Human?

## 1. Six Meters Down

Saturday, September 6, 2003. In Liang Bua cave on the Indonesian island of Flores, a locally hired worker named Benyamin Tarus was digging in a square two meters on each side. About six meters below the cave floor, the top of a skull showed through the soil.

The researchers on site came over to help clear it. The skull was so small that one member of the team took it for a child’s at first. Thomas Sutikna arrived two days later. When the jaw and the permanent teeth showed, he knew it was an adult. This is how the Smithsonian’s Human Origins Program later told the story.

**On the estimate given in the 2004 paper, this adult stood about a meter tall, about the height of a four-year-old today.**

Different teams put the brain’s volume between 380 and 430 milliliters. Bones and teeth from several more individuals later turned up in Liang Bua.

Where is Flores? The Indonesian islands stretch southeast from the Asian mainland: Java, Bali, then several islands farther east, Flores. Across the sea to its north is Sulawesi. Farther north again, beyond most of the Philippines, lies Luzon.

Between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi, biogeographers draw the Wallace Line. When sea levels were low during ice ages, the islands west of the line were joined to mainland Asia. You could reach them on foot. Those to the east remained separated by deep water. Flores and Sulawesi both lie east of the line. Luzon, too, was never joined to the mainland. **To reach these islands you had to cross water.**

Possible traces of hominins on the islands go back around 1.5 million years and come up to around 50,000 years ago. There are stone tools, butchered animals, hand stencils and bones. After the Liang Bua skeleton was published, one question stayed in dispute for years. Was it a new species, or was it a human who had been ill?

## 2. A New Species

Peter Brown, the Australian paleoanthropologist, later told a *Nature* reporter that within a second of seeing the skull and jaw he knew they were important. In 2004, two papers appeared in the same volume of *Nature*. The one led by Brown gave the Liang Bua skeleton a new species name: *Homo floresiensis*.

The other, led by Michael Morwood, reported on the stone tools, layers and dates at Liang Bua. It said the species survived until at least 18,000 years ago.

The *Nature* website introduced the find as Flores Man, tiny humans and hobbits. It compared the braincase to a grapefruit. Hobbits are small people in an English fantasy novel. That nickname stuck in the English-language press.

How could an adult be so small? Brown and colleagues thought people isolated on the island had grown smaller over generations. Researchers who study island animals call it island dwarfism. Brown’s team considered whether the people who reached the island had been *Homo erectus* and shrank there.

The island’s stegodonts offered a comparison. These were distant relatives of elephants. Brown described a typical stegodont to the public as about the size of an Indian elephant. One kind from Java is estimated at a little over 1,000 to 1,700 kilograms. An earlier species from Flores weighed only about 300.

The stegodont at Liang Bua was another, later kind, not the 300-kilogram one. It was identified as a small local form. Its molars were on average about thirty percent shorter and narrower than in the form it came from. **To see stegodonts getting smaller on the island there are molars to measure. Whether the Flores people got smaller that way too is an inference.**

## 3. A Patient

How could an adult with a 400-milliliter brain have lived as recently as 18,000 years ago? **Three things keep coming up behind the papers that treated the skeleton as a patient: a tiny brain, a very late date, and *Homo sapiens* alive at the same time.**

In 2006, the Indonesian paleoanthropologist Teuku Jacob and colleagues published a paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. Maciej Henneberg was among its authors. The title assigned the owner of the skeleton directly to our species, calling it a “pygmoid Australomelanesian *Homo sapiens*.”

What they saw was a skull whose two halves did not match, along with signs of unusual growth. In their reading, the skeleton came from a short-statured human population and the individual also had a developmental disorder, including microcephaly: an unusually small brain. Jacob put it more bluntly to the *Los Angeles Times*: they were all modern humans.

Other diagnoses followed. In 2007, three researchers suggested a genetic disorder involving growth hormone. They supposed the skeleton had come from a highly inbred local population. In 2008 another group linked it to an endemic thyroid disorder.

In 2014, Henneberg and three colleagues proposed Down syndrome. They cited asymmetry of the face, relatively short thighbones, the shape of the feet and skull, and the small brain. Their reconstruction put the cranial capacity at around 430 milliliters and the height at between 1.2 and just under 1.4 meters.

Their title spoke of the invalid species *Homo floresiensis*. The four diagnoses differed. **What the four papers agreed on was that the skeleton did not need a species of its own.**

## 4. The Bones That Went to Yogyakarta

Most of the Liang Bua bones came back from Yogyakarta on February 23, 2005. They had gone there late in 2004. Not long after the papers came out, Soejono, one of the Indonesian project leaders, agreed to let Jacob examine the specimens at Gadjah Mada University in Yogyakarta.

The two sides told different stories about how the bones left. *Nature* reported at the time that Soejono had signed the arrangement. The Australian team said it only realized after the fact that the specimens were to be taken away. Morwood recalled thinking they would never see the bones again. Jacob said he had been invited and had a right to study them.

The bones spent several months in Yogyakarta. Two more leg bones reportedly did not come back with the first batch. According to reporting at the time and to the excavation team, **the pelvis came back with new cracks. The jaw had broken and been glued back together, at what critics said was a different angle.** A tooth was missing. There were scratches, residue and small losses on the skull’s surface. The *Los Angeles Times* also said something that looked like molding material clung to the skull.

Jacob told the newspaper they had damaged no bones and had in fact improved some of them. He attributed some breakage to transport. The Australian team thought some damage had come from handling in the laboratory or from molding. Brown said it was infuriating.

In 2005, the president of the Indonesian Academy of Sciences told reporters that excavation there should stop for a time. In 2006, *Nature* still described a temporary ban. **Excavation at Liang Bua resumed in 2007.**

## 5. Three Wrist Bones

Was the skull really asymmetric? Brown criticized the use of mirrored photographs to show asymmetry. Depending on the angle of the camera, he said, you could get whatever result you wanted. Most researchers who challenged the diagnoses looked instead at other evidence: the shape of the brain, the wrist, the feet and the teeth.

The brain came first. A brain does not fossilize, but the inside of a skull records grooves and folds on its surface. In 2005, a group used CT scans to make a virtual cast of that surface. They compared it with endocasts from great apes, *Homo erectus*, *Homo sapiens*, a short-statured living human and a person with microcephaly.

**They concluded that the skeleton was neither that of a person with microcephaly nor that of a member of a pygmy population.** Its endocast was closer in shape to *Homo erectus*. In 2007, they expanded the comparison to nine people with microcephaly and ten without it. The classification again put the Liang Bua endocast on the side without microcephaly. The paper also acknowledged that microcephaly varies genetically and clinically.

Then the wrist. A handful of small bones fit together there like pebbles held in a fist. In 2007, another group examined three wrist bones from the skeleton. **In their analysis, the shapes resembled those in African great apes and older hominins. Living humans and Neanderthals share a different set of shapes, which arise in the embryo.**

That reduced the likelihood, the authors wrote, that a disorder had reshaped a modern human wrist into this form. A study published in 2013 showed the same wrist structure in at least one more individual from Liang Bua.

Then the foot. A 2009 study found the foot remarkably long compared with the upper and lower leg. The big toe was fully in line with the others, as it is in us, but other proportions were closer to African great apes. The authors raised a possibility: perhaps the ancestor was not *Homo erectus* but another branch that retained more ancestral traits, whose journey to Southeast Asia remains unrecorded. In 2015, another group looked at teeth from several Liang Bua individuals and concluded that they could not simply be placed within the range of *Homo sapiens*.

## 6. Earlier, Smaller

On October 11, 2013, a few bone fragments came out of a layer about 700,000 years old at Mata Menge, in the So’a Basin on Flores. In a later interview, the provisional bag label was recalled as something like “crocodile bone fragments?” **Two years later, the lab put them together and recognized part of a hominin upper arm bone, from the end near the elbow.**

A team published it in 2024. The reconstructed upper arm bone was about twenty-one to twenty-two centimeters long. It was estimated to be around nine to sixteen percent shorter and thinner than the Liang Bua one. The paper called it the smallest adult upper arm bone in the published hominin record and took as its subject the early evolution of small body size in *Homo floresiensis*.

A jaw and six teeth from the same layer had been published in 2016. They belonged to at least three individuals. The jaw and one molar were smaller than their counterparts at Liang Bua. Their form, the authors said, favored the idea that these people descended from *Homo erectus* and had become smaller after reaching Flores. Adding the upper arm bone, the 2024 paper concluded that the lineage most likely came from early Asian *Homo erectus*.

Wolo Sege, also in the So’a Basin, goes back farther. A layer left by a volcanic flow covered some stone tools and dated to around one million years ago. In a 2010 paper led by Adam Brumm, the team concluded that hominins were already on Flores a million years ago. The same paper said surviving deposits in the basin might not be old enough to record the first arrival.

In 2016, Sutikna and colleagues revised the age of the Liang Bua skeleton from about 18,000 years to about 100,000 to 60,000 years ago. Tools attributable to the species dated to about 190,000 to 50,000 years ago. Whether the species survived after 50,000 years ago, the paper went on to say, was still an open question.

**At the late end, the date for the bones moved back by at least forty thousand years. At the small end, there were already such bones in a layer seven hundred thousand years old.**

## 7. A Rhino and Some Flakes

Were the Liang Bua people the only hominins on these islands? At Kalinga on Luzon, the bones of a rhinoceros lay buried in the sediment of an old river channel. Almost the whole skeleton was there, but the bones were scattered across an area about three meters long and two meters wide. There were marks from cutting and heavy blows.

There were fifty-seven stone artifacts around them. In 2018, a paper dated enamel from one of the rhino’s teeth to about 709,000 years ago. Its title called this the oldest known hominin activity in the Philippines. **No hominin bones were found there. The paper gave no species name to whoever did it.**

At Callao Cave on Luzon, a long, narrow bone from the middle of the foot, a metatarsal, was found in 2007. Dating the bone itself gave an age of at least about 67,000 years. The 2010 paper said it definitely belonged to *Homo*, but did not assign it to a species.

Twelve more pieces later came from the same layer: teeth, finger and toe bones, and part of a juvenile thighbone shaft. Together with the metatarsal, they represented at least three individuals. The paper described a mix of older and newer features in the teeth, hands and feet that differed from other species of *Homo*.

In 2019 the researchers named it *Homo luzonensis*. One of its teeth was dated directly to at least about 50,000 years ago.

At Calio on Sulawesi, a river had left sandstone behind. Seven struck flakes were embedded in it. The deepest was more than half a meter below the ground. A layer of conglomerate above it was so hard that one author called it harder than concrete.

The layer above the flakes contained the upper jaw of an extinct pig. In 2025, a paper used dates from its teeth to put the minimum age of the deepest flake at about 1.04 million years, possibly as much as 1.48 million. The authors did not rule out a younger date altogether but considered it unlikely.

Who struck those flakes? No hominin bones turned up. Brumm told the public that their identity was still a mystery. The paper ended by saying that the first crossing to Sulawesi remained an open question. **There were hominins on at least three islands. Flores and Luzon each have a species with a name, but the people behind the rhino at Kalinga and the flakes at Calio do not.**

## 8. A Hand Stencil

Put a hand on a rock wall, spray or apply pigment over and around it, then take it away. The outline left behind is a hand stencil. In 2015, according to later reporting, the researcher Adhi Agus Oktaviana was surveying a cave on Muna, southeast of Sulawesi, when he noticed a very faint old stencil behind newer art.

It is poorly preserved. The surviving pigment is about fourteen centimeters long and ten wide, with an outline of the palm and some fingers still visible. A layer of calcite later formed over the stencil.

Oktaviana led a paper published in January 2026. The calcite dated to about 72,000 years ago, putting the underlying pigment at least 67,800 years back. In the authors’ reading, the outline of at least one fingertip had been narrowed. Another author told Reuters that the fingertips had been carefully reshaped. Most headlines called it the world’s oldest rock art.

Whose hand was it? **The paper said there was no obvious way at present to tell what species of hominin made it.** The authors thought *Homo sapiens* was more likely, judging by the narrowed fingertips and the known timing of our species in the region. But earlier hominins had lived on Sulawesi, and no fossil shows directly whether the two overlapped. Other researchers told reporters that the image alone could not rule out an earlier kind of hominin.

Back at Liang Bua, there are two accounts of where the species came from. One team looks at the Mata Menge teeth and jaw and favors *Homo erectus* growing smaller on the island. Another looks at the foot and sees a possible ancestor in a line that kept more ancestral traits. When the species disappeared, the authors say, is unresolved. On the diagnosis side, four papers proposed four conditions. **The wrist bones, teeth and older upper arm bone found later made the question about more than one skeleton. Those diagnoses are hard to square with all these bones. Accepting a distinct species still does not tell us what branch it came from or when it disappeared.**

In a familiar picture of evolution, figures walk from left to right. The farther right you go, the straighter and taller they get. The adult from Liang Bua, a little over a meter tall, with a brain of about 400 milliliters, lived 100,000 to 60,000 years ago. There is no place for that adult in the line.

The line was arranged, not walked.
