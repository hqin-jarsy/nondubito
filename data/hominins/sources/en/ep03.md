# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (3): Who Else Was Around in Lucy’s Time?

## 1. Beyond Lucy

Ask people about early humans and many can name just one: Lucy. In popular books and on display panels she often stands in a box on a straight line, with an earlier box before hers and a later one after it, box following box.

Lucy lived about 3.2 million years ago. Go back to about 4.2 million years ago, then forward to about three million years ago. Most hominin fossils from that span of 1.2 million years come from East Africa: Afar in northeastern Ethiopia, both shores of Lake Turkana in northern Kenya, and Laetoli in northern Tanzania. Hominins are all the members on our side after the split from chimpanzees.

Two points on the map are farther away. Some 2,500 kilometers west of the East African Rift, about a three-hour flight, a jaw turned up in the Chad desert. Much farther south, an almost complete skeleton was found embedded in a cave in South Africa.

Most of the groups in that stretch of time are assigned to the genus *Australopithecus*. A genus is one rank above a species, and can contain several species. **Was one line changing gradually, or were several branches alive at the same time?** The fossils from that period fall right on the dividing line between those accounts.

## 2. The Lake

In 1995, Meave Leakey and colleagues published fossils from two sites near Lake Turkana in *Nature*. Nine came from one site and twelve from the other, mostly jaws and teeth, plus a few other bones. They date to about 4.2 to 3.9 million years ago.

The team named a new species, *Australopithecus anamensis*. *Anam* is the Turkana word for “lake.” There were twenty-one separate pieces, no skeleton and no complete skull. For a long time the lakeside species was known largely as a set of jaws and teeth.

A little later came *Australopithecus afarensis*, Lucy’s species. One followed the other in time, and both were found in East Africa.

In 2006, six researchers published a long paper. The title began with a question: was *Australopithecus anamensis* the ancestor of *Australopithecus afarensis*? It ended with their answer: a case of gradual change in the hominin fossil record. Comparing jaws and teeth from the two sets, they found traits that changed in the same direction over time. They found nothing that had to be explained by two long-lasting parallel lines.

Their reading went like this: one line, slowly changing with each generation. Its earlier stretch was *anamensis* and its later stretch was *afarensis*. **The boundary between the two species names was drawn across a continuous line.**

In 2010, the Ethiopian paleoanthropologist Yohannes Haile-Selassie and colleagues published new fossils from Woranso-Mille in Afar dating to about 3.8 to 3.6 million years ago. They wrote that the finds gave strong support to the ancestor–descendant account, with *anamensis* first and *afarensis* descended from it.

## 3. Miro Dora

On February 10, 2016, at the Miro Dora site in the Woranso-Mille research area, Ali Bereino, a local Afar man, spotted an upper jaw. The survey team picked up the other fragments piece by piece nearby. News accounts later reported that the researchers had to clear animal droppings off the ground as they searched.

Haile-Selassie later recalled seeing the rest of the skull: he could hardly believe his eyes.

In 2019, the team published the cranium in *Nature*, calling it nearly complete. Most of the face and braincase were there. It was about 3.8 million years old. The form of the canine, the upper jaw and the temporal bone near the ear led the authors to assign it to *anamensis*. A species known without a whole face now had one.

A geological study published alongside it reconstructed the surroundings. It was mostly dry scrub, with grassland, wetlands and riverside woods that expanded and shrank. Lakes and river deltas in the rift valley were nearby; sediment accumulated quickly.

The face raised a question. A forehead bone from another place in Afar, BEL-VP-1/1, could push the record of *afarensis* back to about 3.9 million years ago.

If the forehead bone belongs to *afarensis* and the Miro Dora cranium to *anamensis*, then there was *afarensis* 3.9 million years ago and still *anamensis* 3.8 million years ago. The paper put their overlap at no less than 100,000 years. That contradicted the widely accepted account of a single lineage changing gradually, the authors wrote. **Most of those hundred thousand years hang on one forehead bone.**

In their reading, *anamensis* still came first and *afarensis* came later. What changed was how: a branch split off and both lived for a while, rather than the first gradually turning into the second along one line. The 2010 and 2019 papers had the same lead author. In between, there had been a skull.

## 4. Lucy

November 24, 1974, Hadar, Ethiopia. Donald Johanson and Tom Gray drove out to survey in a Land Rover. According to the institute Johanson later founded, it was a hot morning. On their way back to camp they took a different route. Johanson recognized a short piece of forearm first, then saw fragments of skull, thighbone, ribs, pelvis and jaw nearby.

Over the next two weeks or so the team sifted, collected and fitted pieces together. They recovered hundreds of fragments. Forty-seven pieces could be assigned to the skeleton of one small individual. The institute estimates that they make up about forty percent of her bones.

Her number is AL 288-1. She lived about 3.2 million years ago, stood a little over a meter tall and weighed less than thirty kilograms.

As the camp celebrated, the Beatles’ “Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds” played over and over. The skeleton became Lucy. The institute’s account makes a point of saying that nobody remembers who first called her that or when.

In Ethiopia she has another name, Dinkinesh, meaning “you are marvelous.” It is no clearer who first called her that.

The species was formally named four years later. In 1978, Johanson and two colleagues published the name, taking it from Afar. **But Lucy was not the specimen they chose as the type.**

The type specimen is the one singled out when a species is named. The name is attached to that specimen; when there is a dispute, it is what researchers go back to compare. The type of *afarensis* is an adult lower jaw from Laetoli, Tanzania, numbered LH 4, found by the expedition Mary Leakey led. Later accounts of the naming paper say the jaw was picked partly for its distinctive, diagnostic features. It had also been fully described and illustrated.

The name came from Afar in Ethiopia; the type came from Tanzania. Mary Leakey had been the fourth author but removed her name at the proof stage. In 1980, she and two colleagues wrote in *Science* to challenge the name and the choice of type.

## 5. The “First Family” and a Child

Lucy was not the only way *afarensis* could look. In 1975 and 1976, large numbers of bones of that species came from a single site at Hadar numbered 333. Johanson later called it the “First Family.”

Early counts put it at no fewer than thirteen individuals. A 2020 count reported nearly 260 fossils, representing at least seventeen individuals of different ages and sizes. The numbers changed because there was more material and the minimum count was estimated afresh.

There was once a vivid story about how they came to be buried together: a sudden flood had swept them all to their deaths. A geologist revisited the deposits in 2008 and read the burial as the work of low-energy seasonal floods. She found no trace of a flood large enough to kill the group. She also estimated that the channel was around forty meters wide and three to five meters deep. Most of the bones were in the upper deposits as the channel gradually filled.

In 2000, the skeleton of a young child turned up at Dikika, in Afar, and was published in 2006. From her teeth, the researchers estimated she was about three when she died, around 3.3 million years ago. There was a nearly complete skull and a good deal of the trunk and limbs. Even the rarely preserved hyoid bone and both shoulder blades were there.

The researchers judged from the size of the teeth that she was a girl. According to science news coverage at the time, the skeleton was embedded in hard sandstone and took about five years to pick free with dental tools. Her name is Selam, meaning “peace.” The press preferred “Lucy’s child,” though **this child lived more than a hundred thousand years before Lucy.**

The teeth also keep a record of what *afarensis* ate. Trees and shrubs leave a different carbon signal in tooth enamel from grasses and sedges. In 2013, a team measured twenty *afarensis* tooth samples from Hadar and Dikika.

A substantial share of their diet came from grasses, sedges, succulents or animals that fed on them. The results differed widely among individuals even within a very narrow span of deposits, and there was no steady increase over time.

## 6. A Close Relative

In 2012, Haile-Selassie and colleagues reported a foot in *Nature*. It came from Burtele in the Woranso-Mille area and was about 3.4 million years old. The paper did not assign it a species. The title said the foot showed that there was more than one way of being adapted to walking on two legs.

The striking part was the big toe. In Lucy’s species, the authors noted, the big toe was roughly in line with the other four. On the Burtele foot it could stick well out to the side, rather like a thumb, as news accounts frequently put it. Other bones from the same foot suggested it also bore weight when the creature walked on two legs on the ground.

Whose foot it was remained an open question. In 2015, the same team used jaws and teeth from Burtele and neighboring sites to name a new species, *Australopithecus deyiremeda*. In Afar the species name means “close relative.” It dates to about 3.5 to 3.3 million years ago, squarely within the time when *afarensis* was alive. The foot was not included then.

Some researchers had reservations. There were few specimens and no associated skeletons. And nobody was sure how much variation there already was within *afarensis*. Individuals of the same species can be bigger or smaller, with different faces and teeth. Whether a few jaws count as two species depends on how much variation you allow within one.

In 2025, the team published newly found jaws and teeth and assigned the foot to *deyiremeda* as well. They had not yet seen evidence of another hominin at Burtele, they said. With the evidence in hand, this was the most parsimonious assignment: it required the fewest extra assumptions.

The same paper measured carbon in the enamel of *deyiremeda*. The values were low and varied little, like those of *anamensis* and of Ardi’s species, which lived about 4.4 million years ago. Their diets drew mainly on sources such as trees and shrubs. Grasses, sedges and succulents, or foods that got their carbon from those plants, made up a substantial share of the *afarensis* diet, with large differences among individuals.

In the reading offered by these papers, **Afar around 3.4 million years ago had at least two kinds of feet and two patterns of eating.** There were at least two kinds of biped, and they did not walk in the same way.

## 7. One Face, One Jaw

In 1999, Justus Erus, a member of Meave Leakey’s expedition, found a cranium on the western shore of Lake Turkana. Leakey and colleagues published it in 2001. It was about 3.5 million years old.

They saw not only a new species but a new genus. They called it *Kenyanthropus* to recognize Kenya’s contribution to the fossil study of human evolution. The species name means “flat-faced”: *Kenyanthropus platyops*. Among its reported features were a flatter area below the nose, forward-set roots of the cheekbones, and small molars.

But the skull was badly fractured and crushed in the ground. **Was the face flat in life, or only after it had been crushed and put back together?**

In 2003, an American paleoanthropologist wrote a paper in *Science* whose title asked, “Early Hominid Diversity or Distortion?” He argued that the crushing and fragmentation made the reconstruction far too uncertain to establish a distinct genus and species. The face might fall within the range of *afarensis* in the area.

In 2010, three researchers, including Meave Leakey, reexamined the upper jaw digitally. They corrected as much deformation as they could and compared it with other early hominins, chimpanzees, gorillas and living humans. They found that a set of traits still distinguished it from *afarensis*.

There are no bones of the body that can reliably be assigned to the flat-faced species, so its height and weight cannot be estimated. Its head is about the size of those of *afarensis* and other australopiths.

Some 2,500 kilometers west, a French–Chadian team found a lower jaw in Chad’s Bahr el Ghazal region in 1995. It was named *Australopithecus bahrelghazali* in 1996, taking the place name for the species and “Abel” as the nickname of the jaw. In 2008, dating gave the surrounding sediment an age of about 3.58 million years, which the authors took to point to a contemporary of *afarensis*.

Other researchers pointed out that the jaw lay beside a gully rather than solidly embedded in a layer that could be followed continuously. Whether it is a species of its own is disputed too. One side emphasizes the distinctive front of the jaw and the premolars. The other finds those differences within the range of *afarensis*.

## 8. More Than One

The story of the South African skeleton starts in a storeroom. In 1994, Ron Clarke went through boxes of fossils collected earlier from the Sterkfontein caves and recognized four small bones from a left foot. He called the find “Little Foot.” In July 1997 he sent his assistants Stephen Motsumi and Nkwane Molefe deep into the cave to look for exposed broken bone on the rock face. They found ends that matched the fragments in hand.

The skeleton was cemented into the loose stone and earth of the cave by lime, hard as concrete. Freeing it bit by bit took nearly twenty years. More than ninety percent of it had survived when it went on public display in 2017. One team dated the burial of quartz grains in the cemented rubble enclosing the skeleton to around 3.67 million years ago. Another dated flowstone above and below the bones to around 2.2 million years ago. They took the flowstones to bracket the skeleton, and inferred an age for it around then. Flowstone is limestone deposited in layers by water running along a cave wall or floor. The first team argued that it formed after the bones were already buried.

**The two readings lie over 1.4 million years apart.** On the first, Little Foot was alive in the time of *afarensis*. On the other, it came well after. The dispute is about more than which clock to use. Did the flowstone form before the bones were buried or afterwards? The species is unsettled, too. At first Clarke said it probably belonged to an australopith already known in South Africa, or another early hominin. He later adopted a species name proposed by someone else decades earlier. In 2025 a team published an argument against using it.

Put the dates together. Around 3.8 million years ago, one forehead bone sits between *anamensis* and *afarensis*. Near 3.5 million years ago, there were *afarensis*, *deyiremeda* and the flat-faced species in East Africa, that jaw in Chad to the west, and perhaps Little Foot to the south. If both fossils have been correctly assigned and dated, the forehead bone means *anamensis* and *afarensis* overlapped for a while. To say the whole of the first line turned into the second, at least one of the bones needs another explanation. How many other branches lived nearby is a separate question.

Draw a boundary between two species on the evidence of two bones, and that is a chisel stroke. Bring the difference inside the range of one species, and that is a stroke too. **After the work is done, a few bones can still make sense on either side.**

“Lucy was a human ancestor” is a familiar line in popular books. There is only one Lucy in it, and only one lineage. Lucy was an individual of *afarensis*. On the dates commonly used, that species left a record spanning about a million years in East Africa. In some of that span, the ground holds other faces, other feet and other ways of eating.

**Lucy’s name came from a song played during a celebration in camp.** To draw the lakeside species, Lucy’s species and the later groups in a row, box by box, took people who came much later, working from dates and degrees of resemblance.

The line was arranged, not walked.
