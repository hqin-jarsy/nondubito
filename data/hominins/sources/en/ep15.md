# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (15): Her Mother Was a Neanderthal

## 1. Two Thousand Bone Fragments

Denisova Cave in Siberia’s Altai Mountains has a great many broken bones. Plenty are so fragmentary that you cannot tell what animal they came from. A team picked out more than two thousand pieces like those and examined their collagen one at a time. The protein varies a little between animals, like a fingerprint.

In 2018, two of the people involved told *The Guardian* that a graduate student named Samantha Brown volunteered for the job. She got through about seven hundred pieces without finding a single human bone, then kept going. Fragment number 1,227 had the fingerprint of a hominin, a member of our side after the split from the chimpanzee branch. A later account in an archaeology magazine described it as a little over two centimeters long and weighing less than two grams.

In 2018, Viviane Slon and colleagues read the nuclear genome from the fragment. The paper was titled “The genome of the offspring of a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father.” Slon later told the public that it all began with a piece of bone so small no one could see it was human. **Its owner was female, at least thirteen when she died. Her mother was a Neanderthal and her father was a Denisovan.**

Around the time she lived, there was more than one kind of hominin on Earth. Neanderthals ranged from Europe to the Altai; Denisovans were in the Altai and on the Tibetan Plateau. The Solo River in Java and the island of Flores had their own hominins. Luzon has yielded hominin fossils too, but their dates are only minimum ages. *Homo sapiens* was in Africa and left bones on the eastern shores of the Mediterranean. When our species reached northern Australia remains disputed.

The period runs roughly from 100,000 to 50,000 years ago. At a rough estimate of twenty-five years per generation, that is about two thousand generations. **How many kinds of hominin were alive in those two thousand generations? Different people count them differently.**

## 2. A Cave Entrance You Could Not Walk Through

In 1933 René Neuville came to make a test excavation at Qafzeh Cave near the eastern Mediterranean, in what is now northern Israel. Sediment and cactus almost blocked the entrance. You could not walk in upright. Formal excavation started the following year, and human bones later came out of the cave.

At nearby Skhul Cave, Theodore McCown directed excavations in 1931 and found a child’s bones. The next year he and Hallam Movius found the bones of three more people and cleared two others a few days later. Beside one lay a wild boar’s lower jaw.

A common estimate had put the bones from the two caves at around 40,000 years old. In 1988 and 1989, two new dating studies using different assumptions about uranium uptake placed Qafzeh and Skhul between about 80,000 and a little over 110,000 years ago. The authors of the 1989 paper wrote that this suggested the two sites were close in age.

Skhul was dated again in 2005. If the burials had taken place within a relatively short time, the authors said, the best estimate was between 100,000 and 135,000 years ago. **The bones once thought to be about 40,000 years old were now placed around 100,000 years ago.**

There was an earlier find still. An upper jaw with teeth came out of Misliya Cave in 2002 and was published in 2018. It was dated to between 177,000 and 194,000 years ago. This suggested that members of the *Homo sapiens* branch had left Africa earlier than previously thought, the authors wrote.

Two researchers disagreed in the same journal that year. The uranium-series data in question supported only a minimum age, they said, and that minimum was no more than about 60,000 to 70,000 years. The original team replied that dropping those data would barely affect its estimate of around 185,000 years. **The same jaw: one side reads over 180,000 years; the other says those particular data can only establish a minimum age of about 60,000 to 70,000 years.**

## 3. Termites and Charcoal

Can stone tools buried in soil move farther down over tens of thousands of years? That question was argued over for years at Madjedbebe, a rock shelter on Mirarr land on the western edge of the Arnhem Land plateau in northern Australia.

It was excavated in 1973 and again in 1989. In 2012 and 2015, a new excavation opened twenty squares a meter on each side around the old trench. The deepest reached more than three meters. Finds larger than two centimeters, and features such as hearths and burials, were recorded in three dimensions where they lay.

The lowest layer with a dense concentration of finds included grinding stones, ground ocher and stone axes with ground edges. In *Nature* in 2017, Chris Clarkson and colleagues put the start of occupation there at about 65,000 years ago. The abstract begins by saying that when people reached Australia remains an unresolved question.

In 2018, Clarkson and colleagues answered earlier criticisms one by one. They said they saw no evidence of large-scale disturbance. Later that year, another team wrote that an age of more than 50,000 years was unlikely to hold. In 2021, some of them returned to the problem of tropical soil. Termites and other disturbances, in their view, could move buried objects downward.

Charcoal could have helped with dating. But charcoal from the lowest hearth did not survive chemical cleaning in the lab. Sixteen of forty samples failed at that stage, with more failures farther down. **The question was whether things in the soil today are still where people left them tens of thousands of years ago.**

Farther north, a cave on the Indonesian island of Muna has a hand stencil at least 67,800 years old. The authors of the 2026 paper said they had no way to know directly which kind of hominin made it. They nevertheless favored *Homo sapiens*, partly because the date fitted when our species was in the region.

## 4. One Cave, Two Groups

Stand inside Denisova Cave and you can see about four and a half meters of deposits, one layer on the next. Outside flows the Anui River. Two papers appeared together in 2019. One dated the cave sediments. The other brought bones, sediments and several kinds of dates into a model. The layers built up from around 300,000 years ago to around 20,000 years ago.

On that model, the earliest Denisovan fossils could date back to about 200,000 years ago. The youngest date to somewhere between a little over 70,000 and a little over 50,000 years ago. Denisovans may have left bones in the cave across a span well over 100,000 years.

The Neanderthal fossils from the cave all fall between 140,000 and 80,000 years ago. In 2020, a team studying Altai stone tools wrote that the appearance of tools from successive layers of Denisova Cave could not distinguish Neanderthal and Denisovan toolmaking traditions. **Two groups left bones in the same cave. The tools alone do not tell us which sets belonged to whom.**

Chagyrskaya Cave, also in the Altai, yielded dozens of Neanderthal remains and many stone tools dating to about 59,000 to 49,000 years ago. These tools look like a kind found in eastern Europe. The authors estimated that the tradition came from three or four thousand kilometers away and proposed that Neanderthals had moved into the Altai from the west at least twice.

Farther south, at Baishiya Karst Cave on the Tibetan Plateau, a layer from about 60,000 years ago also yielded Denisovan DNA in the soil. The top layers, dating as late as 20,000 to 30,000 years ago, were disturbed. The DNA in them cannot be given a precise date. An English-language release from the Chinese Academy of Sciences said it was uncertain whether these later Denisovans ever met *Homo sapiens*.

## 5. A Bone Bed by the Solo River

According to a later reexamination of the archives, it was the afternoon of August 27, 1931, and the geologist ter Haar was surveying in Java. He went to a bathing place by the Solo River. Afterward he saw bone exposed in a gravel layer beside a narrow path. The next day he had local workers dig. They soon brought up a water buffalo skull with its horns still attached.

Formal excavation began on September 13, 1931. By 1933, it had yielded twelve skullcaps and two shinbones. The bed lay about twenty meters above the Solo River; two skullcaps had been found upside down.

A short notice in *Nature* in 1932 described the find as another new kind of human in Java and recorded the proposed name, Solo Man. These fossils are now generally assigned to *Homo erectus*.

In 2020, a team put several dates for the bone bed and animal fossils into a model. It dated the bed to between about 117,000 and 108,000 years ago, and called this, to the team’s knowledge, the first robust chronology. The bed was deposited by floodwaters. The dates belong to the bed, not to any particular skull.

**Around 110,000 years ago, there was *Homo erectus* by the Solo River in Java. Several skullcaps lay in a bone bed deposited by floodwaters.** From roughly the same stretch of time, the Altai caves hold Neanderthal bones, and caves by the eastern Mediterranean hold bones of *Homo sapiens*.

## 6. A Postmark

How long did *Homo luzonensis* survive? London’s Natural History Museum says we do not know. The dated Callao Cave fossils give minimum ages: one foot bone was dated directly to at least about 67,000 years ago, one tooth to at least about 50,000.

A minimum age is like a postmark. The letter had already been written by that day. The mark does not tell you when it was written. In 2023 the museum described a revised estimate. Uranium still enters a fossil after burial. Take that into account and the fossils could be older than the original estimates, perhaps at least 130,000 years old.

The little hominin from Liang Bua cave on Flores stood a little over a meter tall. Its bones date to about 100,000 to 60,000 years ago. In 2016 the team said it was still an open question whether *Homo floresiensis* lived past 50,000 years ago. In 2023 another group argued specifically against treating the oldest or youngest fossil known as the fixed date when a population appeared or disappeared.

In 2019 a commentary in *Nature* took the two minimum ages at Callao to suggest that *Homo luzonensis* lived at the same time as *Homo sapiens*, Neanderthals, Denisovans and *Homo floresiensis*. **Counting *Homo luzonensis* itself, that commentary made five groups.**

The Smithsonian’s Human Origins page gives a different number: as recently as 70,000 years ago, at least four kinds of humans were on Earth. Its list names *Homo luzonensis*, *Homo floresiensis*, Neanderthals and *Homo sapiens*. The *Homo erectus* fossils from the Solo River do not make the list. The bone bed is around 110,000 years old. Its dates cannot tell us whether they were still there 70,000 years ago.

The Natural History Museum of Denmark lists five in a question-and-answer page and counts Denisovans as an unnamed species. The page begins by saying researchers disagree on the number of distinct species. **The Smithsonian counts at least four. The Danish museum counts five. The difference is the Denisovans, who still have no agreed scientific name.**

## 7. Her Father

Was she a first-generation child of two groups or a descendant of a meeting generations earlier? Each parent gives a copy of the DNA in the nucleus. The researchers looked at positions where they could tell the two groups apart. At each they drew two fragments of her DNA at random to compare. A little over forty percent of the time, one looked Neanderthal and the other Denisovan.

Suppose one copy at every such position came from each parent, one from each side. If you drew two fragments at random, the chance of drawing one from each side would be one in two. That is the expected proportion for a first-generation child. The forty-odd percent observed came closest to it. If the groups had mixed generations earlier, there would be far fewer positions with a copy from each side, and the proportion would be much lower.

The team concluded that she was a first-generation child of the two groups. **Her father was Denisovan. His side of the family also carried traces of earlier Neanderthal ancestry.**

Several methods are needed to estimate when she lived. Radiocarbon could only say the bone was more than 50,000 years old. Uranium-series dating gave a minimum age of about 67,500 years. On the 2019 model, the bone dates to around 79,000 to 118,000 years ago.

After 2018, the media often called her “Denny.” A *Guardian* headline called her a mystery girl. In the papers she is Denisova 11. The researchers estimated from the thickness of her bone wall that she was at least thirteen when she died.

The paper ended by saying that finding a first-generation child suggested that mixing was common when the groups met. And the Altai was not the only place they met.

In 2016, a team read traces of another encounter in the genome of an Altai Neanderthal. In their reading, about 100,000 years ago a group that had split early from other *Homo sapiens* in Africa left DNA in the ancestors of the Altai Neanderthals. The authors said this may have happened in the Near East. From around that period, the caves at Skhul and Qafzeh hold bones of *Homo sapiens*.

## 8. Telling It Backward from Today

In 2012, *National Geographic* wrote that Neanderthals had long been seen as quintessential losers. In 2019 a Chinese financial news outlet ran an article asking why, out of more than a dozen kinds of humans once on Earth, only *Homo sapiens* was left.

The Chinese Academy of Sciences website republished a popular article that told a similar story about our own species. Environment and competition, it said, might explain why the attempts of early *Homo sapiens* to settle Eurasia permanently failed until a larger migration succeeded around 65,000 years ago. It also said the idea needed further research.

Those are all stories told backward from today. Nothing in the bones or stones from the period between 110,000 and 50,000 years ago tells us which group would end up being written about as the last one left.

**The dates for the small bone fragment, the Solo River bone bed and the Skhul remains overlap around 110,000 years ago. Overlapping ranges do not put the finds in the same year. But the stretch when these groups might have been alive at the same time does not fit the sequence implied by “the last one left.”**

The groups all acquired names. The one by the Solo River was reported as another new kind of human. Some were also given places in the line: Neanderthals as the quintessential losers, *Homo sapiens* as the last group left.

The fragment has a number now, and a nickname. Her parents came from different groups of hominins. When she was born, both were alive.

The line was arranged, not walked.
