# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (12): Did Cooking Make Us Human?

## 1. A Burned Bone

The Smithsonian’s website says there were hearths at least 790,000 years ago. It adds that some researchers think cooking may go back more than 1.5 million years. London’s Natural History Museum says scientists do not know when people first managed to make fire at will.

Suppose you carry a branch still burning from woods struck by lightning. That is using fire. Keep it alight all night and build a hearth around it, and you are controlling fire. Let it go out and start it up again yourself, and you are making fire. **A burned bone tells you that it was exposed to fire. It does not tell you which kind.**

Some of the earliest evidence of fire use, from East Africa about 1.5 million years ago, is disputed. On stone tools from France about 50,000 years old, researchers identified wear marks they interpreted as traces of making fire. The case for making fire at Barnham in England some 400,000 years ago rests on a heated patch of ground, flint fractured by heat, and two pieces of pyrite taken together. On a one-meter ruler for 1.5 million years, 50,000 years take up a little more than three centimeters at our end. **In the other ninety-odd centimeters are bits of burned earth and bone, with gaps in between.**

## 2. Cooking Made Us Human

Around 1.9 million years ago, the teeth of *Homo erectus* became smaller, but bodies, especially female bodies, grew larger. The Harvard researcher Richard Wrangham and his colleagues read this as less chewing but more energy taken in. **Where did the extra energy come from?** Their answer was food cooked over a fire.

In 1999 he and his colleagues published a paper titled “The Raw and the Stolen.” In 2009 came the book *Catching Fire: How Cooking Made Us Human*. The paper said fire was already controlled when *Homo erectus* emerged, and the authors called it the best explanation for the evidence available.

Wrangham told Harvard’s news service that cooking was a defining feature of the human diet and of human life. That story needed archaeology to follow it up. In 2011, the archaeologist Wil Roebroeks and a colleague reviewed the European record. They found that reliable and widespread evidence of habitual, controlled fire use in Europe appeared much later, only a few hundred thousand years ago. More than a million years separated the evidence from the 1.9-million-year date.

Wrangham responded in a paper in 2017. He acknowledged that part of the archaeological record did not square with an early timeline for cooking. He still argued for testing the idea against archaeology, physiology, energy and comparative biology together. **The story begins with teeth and body size. Archaeology works with burned traces in the ground. The two are looking for different kinds of evidence.**

## 3. Burned Earth

When soil has been burned red, was it a natural fire or one people set? That question has been asked at two sites in Kenya. In 1981 a paper on Chesowanja spoke of “traces of fire” in its title. In 1994, a paper on Koobi Fora, about 1.5 million years old, linked the site to “controlled use of fire.”

In 2017, another team revisited Koobi Fora and worked in a different excavation area. They recorded where they found heated tools, pieces of soil and bone, down to pieces smaller than two centimeters, and examined the soil under a microscope. They wanted to tell whether the traces were left by people or by nature. At the start of the paper they also described the other view: evidence of fire at *Homo erectus* sites was very sparse and no case had convincingly established that people had made it.

In South Africa’s Wonderwerk Cave there is a layer about a million years old. In 2012, researchers cut intact blocks of sediment into thin slices and looked at them under a microscope. There were ashed plants, scattered wood ash and fragments of bone. On the readings shown for the bones, they had been heated to between 400 and 550 degrees Celsius, substantially hotter than the top cooking temperature of a home oven.

The authors called it unambiguous evidence of burning inside the cave. When they said “earliest,” they added “to our knowledge.” The burned material was in a layer where people had been. **There were no fire-lighting tools in the paper.**

## 4. One Site, Two Terms

Gesher Benot Ya’aqov sits by the Jordan River in Israel and dates to about 790,000 years ago. A 2004 paper reported burned seeds, wood and flint. The seeds and wood included at least three kinds of edible plant: wild olive, wild barley and wild grape. Small burned flints clustered in several places, possibly where there had been hearths. The authors said the finds suggested human control of fire.

Four years later, another title described hominins making fire repeatedly at Gesher Benot Ya’aqov. The evidence was still the recurrence of burned flints in layer after layer and where they lay, not surviving tools for starting a fire. **Same site: the 2004 title said control of fire; the 2008 title said fire-making.**

Traces of fire at Israel’s Qesem Cave extend from around 420,000 to around 200,000 years ago. The title of a 2007 paper used the term “habitual use of fire.”

In 2014, researchers identified a hearth of about four square meters at the center of the cave, used from around 300,000 years ago. It was a little larger than a double bed. Two phases of use were stacked in it, each made up of several brief fires. There was a great deal of flint beside the hearth, along with animal bones bearing butchery marks. Does a hearth this size mean more people were gathering around it? The authors said it might; it could reflect that.

## 5. Two Pieces of Pyrite

At Barnham in Suffolk, England, there is a disused clay pit. The site was known early in the twentieth century. Since 2013 it has hosted a field school for several weeks a year. About 400,000 years ago, the same patch of ground was heated repeatedly. Measurements put the temperature above 700 degrees Celsius.

There were flint handaxes broken by heat. The middle of one handaxe had shattered into twenty-five pieces scattered over about thirty-five centimeters; they fitted back together. There were also two pieces of pyrite. Strike pyrite against flint and you can get a spark. Pyrite is rare in local deposits. The researchers said this suggested it had been brought there deliberately.

The team spent four years ruling out a wildfire. The paper appeared online in December 2025 and in a regular issue of *Nature* in 2026, under the title “Earliest evidence of making fire.”

London’s Natural History Museum did the arithmetic in its release. The previously claimed earliest evidence for fire-making had come from northern France, about 50,000 years ago. Barnham took it back another 350,000 years.

The French evidence came from a 2018 study. Several dozen Neanderthal tools worked on both faces had marks from repeated blows and forceful rubbing against hard minerals. In experiments, researchers struck tools of this kind at an angle with pyrite, making polished patches and scratches like those on the archaeological specimens. The authors called these among the first direct evidence from tools. They acknowledged that the tools and traces of fire being found together might still count as indirect evidence.

One Barnham researcher told the public that the pyrite showed people were doing more than keeping fire going: they were making it. Roebroeks told a reporter that the analysis was excellent but called its “earliest fire-making” claim a stretch. He regarded the evidence as indirect. No one reported the fine striking marks seen in the French study on either the pyrite or the tools at Barnham.

**The two pieces of pyrite do not say who brought them or why. It is the people reading them who say they were brought on purpose.**

## 6. The Coldest Times

Two caves in southwestern France, Pech de l’Azé IV and Roc de Marsal, were used by Neanderthals. Their deposits span tens of thousands of years, from a relatively mild period into one when it was clearly colder.

Some of the hearths in the lower layers at Roc de Marsal are clear. They are about half a meter to a meter across. Some ash layers are one or two centimeters thick; others are full of small bits of burned bone. The bones range from dark brown with light charring to gray-white and blue where they were burned through.

Higher up, things change. About thirty percent of the stone tools in layer nine at Roc de Marsal had been heated. In the coldest layers above it, that falls to one or two percent. **At these two caves, traces of fire were scarcest in the coldest periods.**

In 2011, Dennis Sandgathe and colleagues wrote that western European Neanderthals were not habitual fire users. They suggested that at least some might not have been able to make fire on demand and had to rely on natural fires. They noted that the traces were scarcest just when it was coldest. That did not fit the simple story that people used fire chiefly to keep warm.

The same paper listed other possibilities. Burial processes might have destroyed traces. Fires might have been in parts of the caves not excavated. The occupations that left different layers might have involved different activities, or people might have used the caves in different seasons. Roc de Marsal did not yet have evidence on the seasons of occupation.

Roebroeks and a colleague replied that of eighty-nine later Aurignacian sites in southern France and northern Spain, only ten had preserved hearths. There were almost no specialized fire-lighting tools either, but nobody concluded from that alone that the people of the time seldom used fire. To them, no preserved hearth did not mean no fire.

Sandgathe and colleagues argued that at those two caves it was not just hearths that were missing. Burned bones and burned stones became rarer together, and preservation was good. The absence therefore counted for more. In 2017 they put the inability to make fire on demand as a hypothesis that needed more data.

## 7. Ash at Zhoukoudian

In 1929, as field director at Zhoukoudian, Pei Wenzhong began paying attention to blackened animal bones that looked partly burned through. At the end of 1930, Pierre Teilhard de Chardin took suspected burned material to Paris for comparison and analysis. Free carbon was detected in blackened antler and bone. In 1931 Davidson Black wrote that Peking Man knew how to use fire beyond reasonable doubt.

Early records list burned bone, stone, hackberry seeds, charcoal and “ash.” At a place in the cave called Pigeon Hall, three piles of ash were reported. Some bits of limestone were described as having been burned into lime. Dark red, brown and white were mixed in the deposits.

In 1996 and 1997, the Israeli researcher Steve Weiner and colleagues went back to Zhoukoudian. They sampled an old exposed section of the cave where Peking Man was found and published the results in 1998. When wood burns, tiny silica particles can survive in the ash. They did not find these in the layer called ash, and other signs of wood ash were insufficient. The supposed hearth below it was made of thin layers of fine mud. The team read them as sediment that had settled slowly in still water.

By their count, about twelve percent of the large animals’ bones and about two and a half percent of the small animals’ bones had been burned. **The abstract said there was no direct evidence for burning in place.** The team thought that burned bones alongside tools suggested fire use, but not that people had started a fire in the layers.

In 2014, another team found the silica particles and carbon in new samples from other layers. Three strongly reddened areas with possible ash came from renewed excavations in layer four in 2017. The authors called them clear evidence of fire use in place. **Neither the layers nor the samples examined in these studies were exactly the same as in Weiner’s study.**

The Chinese researcher Zhang Senshui summed up the older view, as later scholars recalled it. Peking Man used and controlled fire and could keep it going. They might have brought natural fire into the cave rather than starting it themselves. The ash and charcoal had not accumulated continuously, so it was not certain they could keep a fire alight all the time.

Chinese teaching materials give the shorter version. A 2023 teaching reference on the People’s Education Press website says Yuanmou Man and Peking Man had learned to use fire. Online slides cited as coming from the 2019 textbook say they could use fire and keep it alive for long periods.

## 8. Fire Is Three Things

Koobi Fora and Gesher are more than 700,000 years apart, yet both had “control” in a paper title. Gesher and the much later Barnham both had “making fire” in theirs. Wonderwerk’s title said the fire was in place inside the cave. Chesowanja spoke of traces of fire. Burned things are found at all of them, but the authors used different thresholds.

London’s Natural History Museum treats the things separately. Early humans probably began by getting hold of natural fire and keeping it alight for as long as possible. When they first learned to make it at will is something scientists do not know, the museum says. The Barnham paper lays out three steps slightly differently: getting natural fire, keeping fire, making fire.

What archaeology turns up is burned bone, red soil, flint cracked by heat, and now and then a couple of stones uncommon in the area. It is the people looking at those things who try to put them in the box marked “learned to use fire.”

**The intermittent traces in those ninety-odd centimeters on the ruler do not line up with a single threshold called “learned to use fire.”**

In teaching slides for China’s People’s Education Press textbook, fire use has its own box in the chain from ape to human. It comes after toolmaking and before the brain. The ground still holds one patch of burned material here and another there. What each can tell us has to be read one place at a time.

The line was arranged, not walked.
