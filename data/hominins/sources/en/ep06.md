# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (6): The Picture of Figures Walking in a Line

## 1. Four Names

A line of figures moves from left to right, one behind another. The one at the far left looks like an ape; at the far right is *Homo sapiens*. Many people have seen this picture or a version based on it. The same line can be made with just a few names.

A familiar sentence in Chinese popular science says that humans evolved through four stages: *Australopithecus*, *Homo habilis*, *Homo erectus* and *Homo sapiens*. A local science story on People’s Daily Online used that wording in 2020. China National Radio’s website listed the same four names as “four important stages in human evolution” in 2025. It is hard to pin the sentence down to any particular edition of a textbook.

Four names spread across more than four million years. The record of *Australopithecus* begins around 4.2 million years ago; the *Homo sapiens* box comes right up to today. At a rough modern estimate of twenty-five years per generation, four million years is 160,000 generations, or 40,000 generations to a box.

All four names are names of hominins, the groups on our side after the split from the chimpanzee branch. **A stage name gives you a box. The box does not tell you what went into it.**

## 2. A Lesson in the Textbook

In the People’s Education Press biology textbook for the second semester of seventh grade, the first lesson is called “Human Origins and Development.” What we can see now of the way the 2012 edition was taught comes mostly from slides and lesson plans teachers uploaded, not from the textbook pages. The next two examples come from those slides.

The story goes in a chain. Forest apes, forests disappearing on a large scale, some apes coming down to live on the ground, upright walking, hands freed, sticks and stones put to use, tools, fire, a growing brain, language. One slide says they developed “generation by generation in the direction of upright walking.” The last link has language emerging from life in groups.

Another set of slides fits the picture on one line: forest apes, a changing environment, fewer forests, life on the ground, modern humans. A separate branch stays in the forest. These lessons present “upright walking” as an important sign of the divide between humans and apes.

In 2023, a Chinese teaching-aid website offered two multiple-choice questions. What directly forced some forest apes to live on the ground? The answer was that forests disappeared on a large scale. What marked an important divide between humans and apes? Upright walking. These are exercises from a teaching-aid site, not the questions printed at the end of the textbook lesson.

In the 2024 revision, as reproduced on a third-party digital-textbook site, the lesson has the same name. It opens with Darwin in the nineteenth century.

**This is the order of the chain: upright walking, then tools, then brains, then language.** Every link follows the previous one. Every link is nearer the end.

## 3. “From Ape to Man”

Some slides for the lesson have used a sequence of figures labeled “From Ape to Man.” In Chinese the wording is also associated with an essay by Friedrich Engels.

Bibliographies put the writing of the essay around June 1876. It was intended as the introduction to a larger project and did not appear in Engels’s lifetime. It was printed in 1896 in the 1895–96 annual volume of *Die Neue Zeit*, the German Social Democratic Party’s theoretical journal.

Literally, the German title means “The Role of Labor in the Humanization of the Ape.” The line that became most famous came with a qualification: “in a sense,” labor “created man himself.” The essay was written five years after Darwin’s *The Descent of Man* came out. When Engels discusses the early apes, it is Darwin he cites by name.

According to an account on a scholarly institution’s website, Lu Yiyuan’s translation appeared in Shanghai in 1928 under the title *The Marxist Account of the Origins of the Human Race*. It is thought to have been the first Chinese translation.

In 1930 the Taidong Book Company in Shanghai published Cheng Song’s translation with the title *From Ape to Man*. One common Chinese title is *The Part Played by Labor in the Transition from Ape to Man*.

## 4. Three Generations of One Family

On the Chinese history textbook side, a report in *Hubei Daily* in August 2024 quoted the introduction to the revised national textbook for the first semester of seventh grade. China has a million years of human history, it said, with Yuanmou Man, Lantian Man, Yunxian Man and Peking Man among its representatives. The first lesson was called “Human Activity in Ancient Times.”

The timeline in the report put Yuanmou Man at about 1.7 million years ago, Yunxian Man at about one million, and Peking Man at about 700,000 to 200,000 years ago. Yunxian Man was new to the textbook. The report quoted the book as saying that the find was made in Yunyang, Hubei, and that archaeologists had excavated three fossil skulls. Yunxian Man was also marked on the new map of major early human sites in China.

The first point on the timeline is disputed in its own right. In one review of the literature, early paleomagnetic dating gave an age of about 1.7 million years. Later work suggested about 700,000 years. Further paleomagnetic work supported the estimate of about 1.7 million.

Three names on an axis easily read as one bloodline: grandfather, father, grandson. **From the first to the last is a million to a million and a half years,** or forty to sixty thousand generations at the same rough estimate. Yuanmou, Yunxian and Peking Man were found in Yunnan, Hubei and Beijing respectively. There are hundreds of thousands of years between neighboring points, and many groups may have lived in between without leaving fossils. **Those few fossils cannot tell us whether it was one bloodline.**

## 5. “Ape-Man”

The museum at Zhoukoudian has been renamed several times. Fangshan District’s official account says that the building completed in 1953 was the Chinese Ape-Man Exhibition Hall. After rebuilding and expansion in 1972 it became the Peking Ape-Man Exhibition Hall. A later history of the site dates the change to “Zhoukoudian Site Museum” to 1994. The new museum opened in 2014. Its rough exterior, the official account says, takes its cue from stone tools.

**A name changed. The old name did not disappear.** “Peking Ape-Man” still appears on the museum’s official site as a historical name, including in a feature asking whether these people were hunters. The site puts their time at about 700,000 to 200,000 years ago.

The site describes Pigeon Hall as a living place of Peking Man and says that stoneworking, the use of fire and habitation all took place there. In the account of the Upper Cave people, ornaments and bone needles from the site are connected with a love of beauty and with changes in technology.

## 6. A Foldout

In 1965 the Time-Life volume *Early Man* contained a foldout by Rudolph Zallinger. Its original title was “The Road to Homo Sapiens.” It became better known as the “March of Progress.” With the page folded, the original book showed just six figures; opened up, it showed fifteen. The smaller line was already there in the book.

The caption, according to a transcription, began by asking what stages man had passed through on the long march from his apelike ancestors to *Homo sapiens*. **It was stages the picture asked about.** Histories of the book note that the 1965 text also called some of the groups side branches or evolutionary dead ends.

It was imitated again and again: a rock album in 1972, another band’s album in 1985, a movie poster in 1992, and the cover of *The Economist* in 2005.

It is unclear whether the left-to-right figures in Chinese slides and popular books were copied or redrawn from the 1965 picture. The “From Ape to Man” sequence in the lesson slides puts “upright walking,” “use of fire” and “the emergence of language” in order of progress.

## 7. A Tangle of Branches

On March 17, 2010, the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History opened its Hall of Human Origins. It covered about 1,400 square meters and used more than seventy-five casts of skulls. An interactive human family tree was one of the main exhibits.

The museum’s public site says people once thought there was only a single line of human species. We now know otherwise, it goes on: we are one member of a large and diverse family tree.

At the opening, curator Rick Potts said that the physical and behavioral characteristics of humans did not all happen at once. The exhibition traces them separately over about six million years. In a museum retrospective at the hall’s fifteenth anniversary in 2025, educator Briana Pobiner said that they did not just put facts on a wall: they showed how people actually know those things.

The Human Evolution gallery at London’s Natural History Museum opened in its current form in December 2015. Its webpage invites visitors on a journey of seven million years, from the earliest hominins to the last surviving human species, ourselves.

Two life-size figures in the gallery took a pair of brothers who specialize in such reconstructions about six months to make. The Neanderthal figure represents someone in their twenties, 1.55 meters tall. The *Homo sapiens* figure, from around 30,000 years ago, is in their fifties, 1.75 meters tall. Chris Stringer, a paleoanthropologist at the museum, said the models drew on the best scientific evidence they could assemble.

Over the span of a little more than a decade, the hall in Washington drew a tree, the gallery in London opened with a journey ending in “us,” and the revised history textbook in China, as reported, laid out a timeline. **Three ways of drawing it on display in three places at once.**

## 8. Easy to Teach

A study published in 2011 recruited 108 students at Vanderbilt University. There were two groups of fifty-four, divided by how much biology they had studied. One group had averaged three and a half semesters of related courses, the other less than half a semester. The researchers gave them three kinds of diagrams: a textbook-style line, a ladder-shaped branching diagram, and a tree-shaped branching diagram. The subjects were hominin and horse evolution.

The line elicited the most answers interpreting the relation as one thing turning into another. Across the questions, this response averaged nineteen percent for the textbook format, eight percent for the ladder and three percent for the tree. In the abstract, lead author Laura Novick and her colleagues noted that students with more biology did better but still generally showed the same effects of diagram format.

Another study published in 2011 asked college faculty and students in New England whether evolution was the process by which a monkey, such as a chimpanzee, gradually became a human. Six percent of faculty and twenty-six percent of students marked it as true. The authors also reported that only two to eight percent of the students surveyed sent responses. Novick’s paper reviewed an earlier survey of textbooks: about two-thirds of the high-school books used evolutionary diagrams with a strong linear component. More than eighty percent of the introductory books for college biology majors had diagrams of that kind, and more than eighty percent also had branching diagrams.

**A line is easy to draw, easy to remember and easy to turn into a multiple-choice question.** But with a tangle of branches, it is harder to ask who belongs in the next box. **Several kinds of hominins were alive at the same time. Their dates alone cannot tell us which became which.**

The four “stages” do not even carry names of the same rank. *Australopithecus* is a genus; the other three names usually each point to a species. Lay out their dates and there is more than one kind in every box. In the *Australopithecus* box, around 3.5 million years ago in East Africa, the people who named the fossils identified *afarensis*, *deyiremeda* and the flat-faced species put in a genus of its own. The *habilis* box also has *Homo rudolfensis* and *Paranthropus*. *Paranthropus* was still around in the earlier part of the *erectus* box. And when *sapiens* was alive there were at least the Neanderthals as well. **Some of their dates overlap. One box did not finish before the next began.**

To put those boxes in a row, one after the next, is to use dates and degrees of resemblance. A lesson, a picture, a multiple-choice question: there is room for a line and not for a tangle of branches.

The line was arranged, not walked.
