# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (2): The Skull That Got a Million Years Younger

## 1. A Number

Archaeology news often has a line like this: “Tests put it at about so many thousand years old.” Few people ask where the number came from or whether it might change. One such change in the study of human evolution took seven or eight years of argument. It happened to a skull.

1972, northern Kenya. A long, narrow lake reaches the Ethiopian border. Today it is Lake Turkana; then it was Lake Rudolf. Richard Leakey’s team was looking for fossils in the deposits along its eastern shore.

According to a 1977 account in *Time*, Bernard Ngeneo, a Kenyan member of the team, saw several small bits of bone sticking out of the sand in a steep gully, where erosion had exposed them.

A different popular account, from 1974, says the team sifted the soil right there. They had thirty pieces by the end of the first day. After days of sifting, they had dozens.

The same *Time* account says Meave Leakey and Bernard Wood took six weeks to put the pieces together. The reconstructed skull had a brain capacity of about 800 milliliters, considerably more than the australopiths known at the time.

Its number is KNM-ER 1470. KNM is the collection prefix of the National Museums of Kenya. ER means East Rudolf, the eastern side of the lake. The skull was entered in the collection as number 1470. The 1974 article said the find was becoming widely known as Skull 1470, after its museum accession number.

In 1973, Leakey published the fossils in *Nature*. Four specimens collected the year before were “provisionally assigned” to the genus *Homo*, the same genus as living humans. The skull, the abstract said, was “probably” 2.9 million years old.

**That age was not measured in the bone.** The skull lay below a volcanic ash layer, and the age of the skull was inferred from a date for the ash. In the usual ordering of the time, large-brained *Homo* came after the australopiths. This number placed it alongside them, perhaps even earlier. From there on, the argument turned on the ash.

## 2. The Clock in the Ash

Why can you date volcanic ash? A volcanic eruption throws out pumice and ash full of tiny crystals. Some of these crystals contain potassium.

Some of the potassium-40 in a crystal decays into argon-40. Argon is a gas. It can escape from hot magma. After a crystal cools, the argon formed inside it is trapped and builds up.

**It is like an hourglass turned over at the moment of eruption.** Measure how much potassium is in the crystal and how much argon has built up, and you can work out how long ago the hourglass was turned. This is potassium–argon dating. A lab might measure pieces of pumice picked from an ash layer, and the feldspar crystals in those pieces.

Mud and sand pile up layer by layer beside East Africa’s lakes and rivers. From time to time a nearby volcano scatters ash over them. The sediments hold all kinds of animal bones. They also hold fossils such as Skull 1470.

The hourglass is in the volcanic crystals, not in bones millions of years old. Ash above a bone tells you how old the bone has to be at a minimum. Ash below it sets the other limit. With a layer on each side, you can bracket an age range. Without ash it gets harder. There was no datable volcanic ash in the deposits around the skull from the Chad desert, so the team had to find another way to put it at around seven million years old.

The ash layer east of the lake came to be called the KBS Tuff. Tuff is rock made from compacted volcanic ash. KBS comes from a person’s name. In 1969, a young geologist on the team had found stone tools in the layer. The site was named using her initials, and the ash layer took the site’s name.

In 1970, two British researchers used a modified potassium–argon method to date the ash. They wanted to know how old the stone tools were. They got about 2.61 million years, with an uncertainty of about 260,000 years. With the skull beneath the ash, this reading put it earlier than 2.61 million years. The layer was what Leakey’s 2.9 million years rested on.

## 3. Pig Teeth and Compass Needles

There are other ways of checking the age of an ash layer. African pigs changed quickly over those few million years. Pigs from different periods have molars of different shapes and crown heights. Arrange the teeth from East African sites by form and you have a sequence to compare against. If two sites yield similar teeth, the deposits are probably of much the same age.

This is biochronology. By itself it gives you an ordering: what lived at roughly the same time, and what came before or after. In principle it does not depend on the clock in volcanic crystals, so you can set one kind of evidence against the other.

In 1977, Tim White and a colleague published “Suid Evolution and Correlation of African Hominid Localities” in *Science*. Hominins are the groups on our side after the split from the chimpanzee branch. According to later accounts of the dispute, the order the pig teeth gave did not fit 2.61 million years. It pointed to a younger date.

But the evidence from pig teeth did not speak with one voice. In 1978, a paleontologist who had studied African fossil pigs for years published a paper in the same journal, making clear in the title that it offered an alternative classification. Run the same teeth through different classifications and you may get a different order of first appearances. Could the teeth settle the question? That too was in dispute.

A point raised later was that the comparison was not truly independent if pig teeth had first been dated against one set of geological dates, and then were brought back to judge those very dates.

A compass offers another way. Every so often Earth’s magnetic field flips and north and south switch places. The interval can be tens of thousands of years, or several million years or more. As sediments and volcanic rocks form, tiny magnetic particles can align with the field, keeping a record of the direction a compass needle pointed at the time.

In a series of deposits the normal and reversed layers alternate, **like a barcode.** Match it against the worldwide barcode whose dates are already known, and you can place the layers within it.

But the numbers on much of that worldwide barcode depend in turn on clocks in volcanic ash or on other ways of dating. The layers east of the lake were tested for magnetism in the 1970s. Several methods could check one another on the same rocks, but they also depended on one another.

## 4. The Same Layer of Ash

In 1975, Garniss Curtis’s team at Berkeley dated pumice from the ash again by the conventional potassium–argon method. They got two numbers: about 1.60 million years for samples from two sites, and about 1.82 million years from another.

They said what everyone had taken for one layer was really two. Both dates were well short of 2.61 million years.

In 1976, another group turned to a different clock. Now and then a uranium atom splits on its own, leaving a minute scar in a crystal. The more scars have accumulated, the longer it has been. This is fission-track dating. Later accounts give the result as about 2.44 million years, close to the original number again.

According to histories of the dispute and recollections written years later, the Berkeley team and the two authors of the 1970 paper both held to their results for a time. Leakey and colleagues eventually asked Ian McDougall, an Australian geologist who had not been involved, to date the layer independently.

On March 20, 1980, *Nature* published two papers in the same issue. One was by a member of the 1976 team, again using fission tracks. After improving the etching and counting, the researchers measured zircon from five pieces of pumice and got about 1.87 million years.

The other was from McDougall’s team. They used potassium–argon dating on feldspar from thirteen pieces of pumice and got about 1.89 million years. The papers had one author in common. They used different clocks, and the dates they obtained were only 20,000 years apart.

The fission-track paper noted that the ash contained two sets of mineral fragments of different ages. The authors said this mattered to the dispute.

**Older grains had mixed in with the newly fallen ash, like a few old coins dropped into a jar of freshly minted ones.** Later, single-grain dating made it possible to pick up these intruders by measuring one grain at a time and treating the odd ones separately.

## 5. A Million Years

In 1981, McDougall switched to argon–argon dating and tested feldspar from three pieces of pumice. It is a refinement of potassium–argon dating. Rather than measure potassium and argon separately, it measures the ratio of two forms of argon. It can also release the argon in batches as the crystal is heated in steps.

The argon from each step gave nearly the same date, forming a flat line on the graph. McDougall took that as good evidence that the samples had not been disturbed after they crystallized. They dated to about 1.88 million years. The ash layer was now well dated, he wrote.

Later measurements generally got finer. In 2023, a group came up with 1,879,100 years and gave two uncertainties: 600 years internal to the laboratory measurement, or 2,400 years if external uncertainties such as conversion constants were included. **Early studies reported uncertainty in the hundreds of thousands of years. The new paper’s preferred estimate was precise to a few thousand.**

**The skull had not moved. The age of the ash above it moved from 2.61 million years to around 1.88 million, a shift of more than 700,000 years. The skull went from “probably” 2.9 million years old to around 1.9 million.**

With its age revised, its species was looked at afresh too. Researchers compared its face, braincase and teeth with other specimens. One approach took it as the representative skull of *Homo rudolfensis*, named for the lake’s old name. There has never been just one way to divide the specimens.

Today, the Smithsonian’s page on the skull puts its age in a few words: about 1.9 million years. The two kinds of uncertainty in the paper, the changing methods and decades of argument all fit inside that one rounded number.

## 6. The Year 1950

The age on the volcanic clock has moved as people measured the ash again and again. With another kind of clock, part of the date depends on what people decided rather than on what they measured.

Carbon-14 dates much more recent things. In the late 1940s, the American chemist Willard Libby reasoned that living things constantly take in carbon. Plants take it from the air and animals from food. A tiny fraction is radioactive carbon-14. At death the exchange stops, and the carbon-14 in an organism can only diminish.

Carbon-14 decays at a fixed rate. The time it takes for half of it to decay is its half-life. Measure what is left and you can work out how long the organism has been dead.

Libby first tested objects of known age. In 1949 he and his colleagues measured wood from the time of two early Egyptian pharaohs. Historical chronologies placed it around 2600 BCE. The measurements averaged around 2800 BCE. Within the uncertainty, they agreed. Libby won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the method in 1960.

Radiocarbon ages are often given as a number of years “before present.” **The “present” is not today. It is 1950.** If it followed the calendar, every published radiocarbon age would have to change every year. The starting point had to stay put. Whether a paper was written in 1977, 2000 or 2026, “3,000 years before present” is counted back from 1950.

In 1977, two specialists laid out this system in a journal devoted to radiocarbon dates, but they did not invent the 1950 starting point. A reason often given for choosing that year is that it was close to the birth of the method and before large-scale nuclear testing.

Nuclear tests really did change the air. Mid-twentieth-century atmospheric tests produced a great deal of extra carbon-14. In the decades that followed, newly grown wood and newly born animals took up higher proportions of it, which cannot be read the old way. That was a physical change in the air. Setting the “present” at 1950 was a convention for writing dates. The two often get told as one story.

Another convention is more surprising still. Libby used a half-life for carbon-14 of 5,568 years. A more accurate value measured later was 5,730 years. Conventional radiocarbon ages today are still calculated using 5,568.

**People know the newer number is more accurate and keep using the old one.** Laboratories around the world worked out vast numbers of ages on the old basis. Changing it would put the numbers out of step with one another. The old number only governs the scale marked in “radiocarbon years.” Turning those into calendar years is a separate step.

## 7. The Same Olive Branch

That step is called calibration. Libby’s method assumed that the amount of carbon-14 in the air had stayed the same. Later researchers measured it in stretches of wood whose individual growth rings they could count. The assumption did not hold. There had been more carbon-14 in the air at some times and less at others.

A calibration curve turns radiocarbon years into calendar years. It draws on tree rings, sediments that accumulate a layer a year on lake beds, and stalagmites that grow layer by layer in caves. It is not a smooth slope. It rises and falls like a bumpy road in the mountains. Sometimes a single radiocarbon age maps onto two or three plausible stretches on a calendar.

An internationally used curve has been revised every few years since 1998; the latest version was published in 2020. The number of measurements went from just over 7,000 in the previous version to more than 12,900, and the curve reaches back about 55,000 years.

On the Greek island of Santorini, an olive branch found buried by an eruption has been used to date that eruption. Researchers divided the branch into four sections using its growth rings and measured a radiocarbon age for each. The 2020 calibration paper used it as a demonstration: take the same four numbers and run them through two curves.

Using the same model of the olive growth rings and the same probability range of about two-thirds, the 2013 curve gave 1619–1608 BCE. The 2020 curve gave 1612–1592 BCE. The range moved a little later and grew wider.

**The olive branch had not moved. Neither had the measurements. Change the curve, and the date moves.**

Carbon-14 can date charcoal, seeds, and organic material preserved in bone, but it has a limit. The longer an organism has been dead, the less carbon-14 remains. At about 50,000 years there is so little left that it is hard to distinguish it from the trace of fresh carbon that can get in when a sample is handled. Older dates need other clocks.

## 8. The Skull Did Not Move

There are several more clocks. Sunlight clears a kind of stored radiation energy from grains of quartz. Once they are buried, the energy starts building up again. Measure how much there is and you can estimate when the grains last saw light. This is optically stimulated luminescence.

Stalagmites grow layer by layer, taking up a little uranium from water. The uranium slowly decays into thorium. Measure their proportions and you can tell when the layer formed. This is uranium-series dating.

Usually these clocks do not measure the bone itself either. A bone hundreds of thousands or millions of years old generally borrows its age from something beside it: ash above or below, sand grains in the same layer, or a stalagmite that grew over it. Were the grains buried along with the bone? The ash or stalagmite over it dates a different, later event. Before you can borrow an age, work out what happened first and what came after.

A cave on the Indonesian island of Flores yielded a kind of hominin only about a meter tall. The media called it the “hobbit.” When it was published in 2004, the account most widely repeated was that these creatures had survived until about 18,000 years ago.

In 2016, researchers excavated a larger area of the cave and identified the deposits afresh. They concluded that the earlier, younger dates came from sediments in a different layer from the bones. The skeletal remains were dated instead to about 100,000 to 60,000 years ago. **The bones were the same bones. What changed was the layer they belonged to.**

Three things go into a date. One is what gets measured: argon in ash, carbon in an olive branch. One is a judgment built into how those measurements are interpreted: whether a crystal stopped leaking gas after it cooled, how carbon-14 in the air varied, which layer a bone belongs to. And one is a convention: “present” is 1950; the half-life stays at 5,568 years; uncertainty is reported at a particular level.

A number can be measured again. A judgment can be made afresh. A convention is set by people and then followed. **What falls inside the line counts as the age. What cannot be placed cleanly is left in the uncertainty, in “probably” and “provisionally.”** The ash has been measured and measured again for more than half a century. That part is still there.

The skull never moved. It shifted once on the chart that puts the finds in order. What moved it was the ash above it and the different ways people measured that ash.

The line was arranged, not walked.
