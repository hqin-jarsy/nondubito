# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (10): One Skull, Three Names

## 1. The Jaw Came First

In 2005, at Dmanisi in southern Georgia, excavators took a skull out of the ground. The jaw that fit it had been dug up five years earlier. By the time the skull appeared, the jaw already had a species name of its own.

Dmanisi lies between the Black Sea and the Caspian. Hominins came here more than once from about 1.85 to 1.78 million years ago and left stone tools. The hominin bones are generally assigned to the later date of about 1.77 million years ago. More than one kind of hominin lived in East Africa then. Peking Man, familiar from Chinese textbooks, came more than a million years later.

Things got buried quickly here. Animals were covered by sediment soon after they died, some with their joints still intact. Over more than a decade, excavators recovered five skulls and several jaws. These were eventually grouped into five sets, with a jaw matched to four of the skulls.

The excavators say the hominin fossils came from an area of about forty square meters, smaller than an ordinary classroom. **Five skulls in less than a classroom’s worth of space. One species or several? Counting from the day the jaw got its name, that argument has gone on for more than twenty years.**

The papers usually credit the excavation team rather than say who first touched which object. Skull 5 was an exception. The acknowledgments in the paper called Nioradze the “first *H. sapiens* to encounter D4500.” D4500 is the skull’s number. The species of the person who found it is perfectly clear. What to call the one she found is still unsettled.

## 2. An Odd Face

To give an animal a formal name, the rule is to pick a physical specimen, usually one, and attach the name to it. It is called a type specimen. In 2002, a paper took the Dmanisi jaw found in 2000 as its type and gave it a new species name: *Homo georgicus*.

Yet that same year, when the excavation team published Skull 3, they put it only provisionally with *Homo erectus*. Some of the authors were on both papers.

Three years later Skull 5 came out of the ground and fit the jaw. Put them together and you get an odd face. The space for the brain is only 546 milliliters, the smallest of the five. Yet the face is large and juts out, about fifteen centimeters across the cheekbones, as long as a phone.

In 2013, David Lordkipanidze led the team that published the skull in *Science*. The five skulls differ quite a bit in size and shape when set side by side. Do they differ enough to be different species?

Their answer was no. They compared the variation across the five skulls with differences among early *Homo* fossils assigned to separate species in Africa and Asia. The differences at Dmanisi looked to them more like variation within a single, highly variable population. Individuals of one species, after all, can be taller or shorter and need not have the same face.

On that reading, early *Homo* can be treated as one evolving lineage under the name *Homo erectus*. The jaw does not need a species of its own. The authors accepted that their sample was too small to say how fast the lineage had changed. **The jaw is still the same jaw. Whether it counts as a species of its own depends on where the boundary is drawn.**

## 3. One Classroom, Four Kinds of Hominin

Dividing fossils into species comes down to drawing boxes around sets of bones. The next year, four researchers dissented in the same journal. From the same skulls, jaws and teeth they drew more than one box. To them Skull 5, together with its jaw, was evidence that *Homo georgicus* really was a separate species.

The original authors replied at once. They compared them with modern chimpanzees. On their comparison, individuals in one chimpanzee population can differ as much as the skulls do. The hypothesis that the five came from one group had not been ruled out. They also answered, point by point, the proposed signs of separate species, such as the number of premolar roots or how flat the canines were.

They did another bit of counting. On the other team’s reading, a patch smaller than a classroom would have to hold at least four kinds of hominin in perhaps just two hundred years. **From one pile of bones, one side saw individuals of the same species looking different; the other saw several species.** As late as 2025, researchers were still reconsidering how many species the skulls might represent.

## 4. One Tooth Left

The owner of Skull 4 died with just one tooth left. The others had not fallen out after death. When a tooth comes out, the bone in its socket is gradually absorbed and the surface levels out. Much of the jaw had already smoothed over. The teeth must have gone years before death. **The bone records that its owner lived for years after losing the teeth.**

How did the person eat without teeth? Researchers considered two possibilities. The person could have sought out soft food, such as plants, animal brains or marrow. Or companions could have brought food and softened it first. Neither hypothesis could then be ruled out, the researchers wrote.

The public museum page says it more firmly. The Smithsonian calls the specimen the “Old Man” and says members of his social group must have cared for him. **The paper had two possibilities side by side. The webpage has only one.**

In 2020, other researchers described a bonobo that had lost many teeth and survived anyway. Its story shows that making it after losing teeth does not necessarily mean somebody looked after you.

## 5. The Anchor

Back to the jaw. Why attach a species name to a physical object? There is an internationally used code for naming animals. A species name has to be tied to something physical, usually one specimen. The specimen need not be the most typical member of that species. It is more like a ship’s anchor. Later researchers can redraw the species boundary, but the name goes with the anchor.

An anchor need not be an adult either. For *Homo habilis*, it is the juvenile from Olduvai Gorge nicknamed “Johnny’s Child.” There is a jaw, part of a braincase, and hand bones. The jaw was distorted as it fossilized; the braincase was fragmented. In 2015 a team scanned the bones, took the pieces apart on a computer, used the better-preserved side to fill in the other and put them back together.

The reconstructed jaw had a long, narrow dental arch. Compared with other early *Homo* fossils, it led the team to suspect there was more than one species in early *Homo*. If there were two, which would keep the name *habilis* depends on where this juvenile falls. **Both papers were about early *Homo*. Dmanisi gave one species, Olduvai more than one. The papers came out two years apart.**

The code has another rule: priority. When one species has acquired several names, the one published first is usually used. This is how *Homo erectus* got its name. In the 1890s the Dutch researcher Eugène Dubois named the fossils from Java. The genus name meant “ape-man,” and the species name meant “upright.”

In 1927, a Canadian anatomist used a tooth from Zhoukoudian to name *Sinanthropus pekinensis*, the Chinese ape-man of Peking. Later researchers moved both groups of fossils into *Homo* and judged them to belong to the same species, though they disagreed about exactly where its boundaries lay. The name meaning “upright” came more than three decades before the one meaning “of Peking,” so the species became *Homo erectus*. Peking Man stayed as a common name. **The code does not say whether two piles of bones belong to one species. After people decide that, it sorts the names by priority.**

## 6. Bodo

Priority can run into history. In 1907 a jaw turned up in a sandpit near Heidelberg, Germany. A researcher named *Homo heidelbergensis* from it the following year. Many complete skulls were later put under that name, but it stayed anchored to the jaw.

In 1921 a skull from Kabwe in what is now Zambia was named *Homo rhodesiensis* after the name of the place at the time. If the two specimens are one species, the 1908 name takes precedence. If they are not, each can keep its own.

In the autumn of 1976, Alemayehu Asfaw, Paul Whitehead and teammates found a skull at Bodo in Ethiopia. It is robust, with much of the face and front of the braincase preserved. Its age is generally given as about 600,000 years.

In 2021, Mirjana Roksandic and three colleagues proposed anchoring a new species, *Homo bodoensis*, to this skull. One of the coauthors was the Chinese paleoanthropologist Wu Xiujie. In their scheme, the new name covered many African fossils from the same period. Many western European fossils with Neanderthal features went with the Neanderthals, while some fossils in Asia might belong to other lineages.

They did not want either old name. *Homo heidelbergensis* had become a catch-all. Different researchers had used it for too many fossils that differed too much. As for *Homo rhodesiensis*, they said the name was associated with Cecil Rhodes and British mining colonialism; Rhodesia took its name from his. They made clear this was not the main reason for dropping it, but it could not go unmentioned.

They added an unusual qualification. *Homo bodoensis* was not a true species in the strict biological sense, they wrote. Mounting evidence indicated that populations migrated and exchanged genes during that period. **The people proposing a species were saying it was not a species in the strict biological sense.**

The challenge came quickly. In 2022 two paleoanthropologists, including Chris Stringer, cited the code’s provisions for setting an old name aside. They said none covered the reasons given for *bodoensis*. They also noted that the 1921 description did not mention Rhodes personally; it used a place name.

**The question was whether the history carried by a name is a reason to replace it.** The proposing team was frank about it: their proposal intentionally broke some rules of the code. In their view, the rules themselves needed to change.

## 7. A Finger Bone, a Skull

The Denisovan story went the other way around. There was DNA first, and still no formal name. In 2008, a bone from the tip of a young person’s little finger turned up in Denisova Cave in southern Siberia. In 2010 researchers read mitochondrial DNA from it. Mitochondrial DNA is a small set of genetic material separate from the DNA in the cell nucleus and passed down through the mother’s line.

The paper’s title contained no species name, only an “unknown hominin.” Qiaomei Fu was one of the authors. Later the same year another team read a draft of the nuclear DNA and gave this ancient population a name: Denisovans.

It was a name for the group, not a scientific name under the code. There were only a finger bone and teeth in hand, and no specimen whose shape alone could clearly identify a species. The Australian Museum says there is still no agreed taxonomic name for the Denisovans.

Some researchers are in no hurry. An American paleoanthropologist said a name like this leaves room to move. There is no need to put an answer in the name while the relationship between genes, appearance and species is still unclear.

The Harbin skull was the opposite: a large skull, nearly complete, with no clear record of where it had been buried. According to the people who later handed it over, a worker found it while building a bridge in 1933 and hid it in a disused well. Decades later he told his family where it was. The worker’s identity cannot be verified. A news story in 2025 called it a deathbed confession. The whole chain is an account passed down orally much later. There are no contemporaneous records or photographs.

In 2021 researchers dated the skull to at least 146,000 years ago. They also took it as an anchor for a new species name, *Homo longi*. They used the word “propose.”

In June 2025, Fu’s team published two papers on the same day. One read ancient proteins remaining in the skull, which survive longer than DNA. The other recovered mitochondrial DNA from hardened plaque on a tooth. Both papers pointed to the same reading: the Harbin skull belongs on the Denisovan branch.

**On one side, a little finger bone with DNA and no formal name. On the other, a big skull with a formal name and no clear account of where it came from.**

## 8. Three Names

The same skull now has three names. “Harbin Man” is the common name after the area where it is thought to have been found. “*Homo longi*” is the formal scientific name, anchored to the skull. “Denisovan” is a name for a population. The three names are not on the same level. A Chinese Academy of Sciences headline chose the last one: the nearly complete archaic human skull from Harbin was Denisovan.

Then comes the trouble. If the Denisovans and the Harbin skull belong to one species, what name should it have? *Homo longi* has an anchor and was named early. In 2025 a group used the shapes of fossils to draw a family tree with the Harbin skull and several other Asian fossils on one branch, and tried putting the Denisovans there too. According to a popular science report, Stringer thought that if they really were one species, *Homo longi* had priority.

But there is another name, *Homo juluensis*. A group of human fossils came from the Xujiayao site on the border of Shanxi and Hebei between 1976 and 1979. A Chinese paper lists twenty-one pieces and gives a cranial capacity of more than 1,700 milliliters.

In 2024, a paleoanthropologist at the University of Hawai‘i and Wu Xiujie proposed a new species name for the Xujiayao fossils and some others. They took *julu* from the Mandarin for “large cranium.” An introduction to a special issue in 2025 gave their explanation: the site name was too hard to pronounce, while *julu* was easy to remember. The introduction also conceded that the way they formed the name might not entirely fit the code.

The two groups relied on different evidence. **Wu and colleagues used the shape of teeth and jaws to assign fossils from Denisova Cave and Xiahe, in Gansu, to *Homo juluensis*. Fu’s team used proteins and DNA to place the anchor of *Homo longi* among the Denisovans.**

The 2025 paper by Wu and colleagues said the fossils were at least a new form, possibly a new species. The special issue introduction said there was then no comparable DNA or protein evidence from Xujiayao or from Xuchang in Henan. Resemblances of shape could not be set aside lightly, and sampling was the next step. In 2026, ancient protein evidence from two Xujiayao fossils became available for comparison. That comes up in the next essay. Other researchers would rather not rush to attach species names, and discuss groups of ancient people by time and region.

Every time somebody redraws the boundary, someone goes back to the actual object to measure it, scan it or take a sample. **The jaw that served as the anchor for *Homo georgicus*, and the little finger bone that yielded DNA but has no formal species name, fall between boxes. They do not fit neatly into any of them. They are the remainder.**

The Chinese textbook puts the names in a row: *Homo habilis*, *Homo erectus*, *Homo sapiens*. The anchor of *habilis* is a young person’s bones from Olduvai; that of *erectus*, a skullcap from Java. Names can be merged or replaced. The anchor is still the same object.

The line was arranged, not walked.
