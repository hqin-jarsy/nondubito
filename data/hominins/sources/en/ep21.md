# The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Human Evolution (21): “Eve” Was Not the Only Woman

## 1. In Search of Adam and Eve

The cover of *Newsweek* for January 11, 1988, said “The Search for Adam and Eve.” Its subtitle described scientists exploring a controversial theory of human origins. **The story opened by saying that scientists were calling her “Eve,” but reluctantly.**

The woman came from a paper published the year before. In 1987 Rebecca Cann, Mark Stoneking and Allan Wilson compared mitochondrial DNA from 147 people in five geographical populations. In *Nature* they wrote that all these mitochondrial DNAs stemmed from one woman.

There are two ways to count ancestors. On a family tree you have two parents, four grandparents and eight great-grandparents. Each generation back doubles the number of slots. Ten generations back, there are over a thousand; thirty generations back, over a billion. Far fewer people were alive thirty generations ago, so the same person has to occupy many places in one tree. **Follow only your mother’s mother back, and there is one slot per generation. That is the kind of counting the paper did.**

Ancestors can be near or very far away. A family-tree model puts some a few thousand years back. Farther back are two skeletons, one about ten thousand and one about nine thousand years old. Farther still are the woman called Eve, around 200,000 years ago, Peking Man, hundreds of thousands of years ago, and Millennium Man, about six million years ago. It is people who came later who call them ancestors, counting backward from where they stand.

## 2. The Other Women Alive Then

What did the one woman in the paper mean? In January 1987, Stoneking explained the work to *The Washington Post*: they had found a way to estimate when the most recent common ancestor along the maternal line had lived.

The paper placed her around 200,000 years ago, probably in Africa. The authors assumed that mitochondrial DNA accumulated differences at a rate of two to four percent per million years and estimated the date from there. **At that rate, the common ancestor along the maternal line lived between 140,000 and 290,000 years ago.**

As later news archives and academic discussions transcribe it, Wilson used the *Newsweek* story to reject the idea that Eve was the only woman of her time. That was not what they were saying. The same passage records his point: quite a number of men and women lived alongside her.

**Stoneking later made clear in a review that this “mitochondrial Eve” was not the only woman on Earth at the time. She was one of many. The other women’s mitochondrial DNA did not make it all the way to people living today.** A maternal line stops in any generation in which a woman has no daughter.

## 3. The Horse Tamers and the Pyramid Builders

Rice growers by the Yangtze. The first people to tame horses on the Ukrainian steppe. Hunters after giant ground sloths in forests across the Americas. The people who built Egypt’s Great Pyramid. Three authors ended a September 2004 paper in *Nature* with these groups. Their point was how quickly the ancestors of different people today come to overlap when we trace them back.

The paper counted family trees, following every line back on both parents’ sides. It asked two questions. How far back do we have to go to find the most recent person who is an ancestor of everybody alive today? That person is the most recent common ancestor. And is there a point farther back, beyond which everybody alive today has exactly the same list of ancestors?

From that second point backward, in the paper’s wording, people today have exactly the same set of genealogical ancestors. **Go back a few thousand years in this model and every person alive then is either an ancestor of everyone alive now or an ancestor of no one alive now.**

A simplified version of the model put the most recent common ancestor at about 2,300 years ago and the point of identical ancestry around 5,000 years ago. **The authors called estimates like these extremely tentative.** They went through the things that could throw off the numbers: how much migration took place between regions, whether any populations were completely isolated, and how populations rose and fell over time.

One author carried the thought forward in a Yale release: within two thousand years, it is likely that everybody on Earth will descend from most of us alive now.

## 4. Family Trees and DNA

People move. How does that go into a model? The paper ran another version with geography. It divided the world into continents, countries and towns, adding ports, migration routes and dates when the ports opened. In one set of assumptions, five percent of people in each generation left the town where they were born, and five in ten thousand left their birth country. Another set raised migration at every level.

The two sets gave quite different average results. In the first, the most recent common ancestor came about 3,400 years ago and the point of identical ancestry around 7,400 years ago. With more migration, the results were around 2,000 and 4,200 years ago respectively.

Ancestors in a family tree and ancestors in DNA are counted differently. **A family tree counts who had which children. DNA counts whose pieces actually made it through to today.** Half of a person’s autosomal DNA comes from each parent. The farther back an ancestor is, the less DNA they generally pass down to that person. Many pass down none at all.

**At the point of identical ancestry, the paper said, the great majority of a person’s genealogical ancestors then living would have passed little or no DNA to that person.** Mitochondrial Eve belongs on the DNA side, and even there follows just one line: mother to mother. The few thousand years on the family-tree side and the two hundred thousand on the mitochondrial side are not counting the same thing.

## 5. “Direct Ancestor”

“Sinanthropus is a direct ancestor of *Homo sapiens*.” Later historical scholarship quotes those words from a 1943 monograph by the German anatomist Franz Weidenreich. He had studied the Zhoukoudian fossils in Beijing since the 1930s. The genus named in the quote meant Peking Man at the time. In the same passage he said those fossils were closer to certain groups living today.

In 1998, the Chinese paleoanthropologist Wu Xinzhi argued in *Acta Anthropologica Sinica* for a regional model based on continuity in the form of Chinese fossils. **In his account, continuity was the principal process and interbreeding a secondary one. It was an East Asian version of the multiregional evolution hypothesis.**

The Chinese Academy of Sciences later described the proposal as continuous evolution with some interbreeding. It also noted that it had not won broad acceptance among paleoanthropologists internationally and that researchers in China held different views too.

## 6. Twelve Thousand Y Chromosomes

“African Origin of Modern Humans in East Asia: A Tale of 12,000 Y Chromosomes” was the title of a 2001 paper by Yuehai Ke and colleagues in *Science*. The Y chromosome passes from father to son. Sometimes a letter changes in the copying. The change stays as a marker, carried on by sons and their sons.

They tested three Y-chromosome markers in more than twelve thousand men from 163 populations. The abstract concluded that the data did not support even a minimal genetic contribution from local archaic humans.

Ke and colleagues also published a paper in Chinese on Y-chromosome evidence for an African origin of modern Chinese people. They analyzed samples from nearly ten thousand Chinese men. The abstract said that without exception the samples had one of three mutations. On that basis, the authors said the Y-chromosome evidence did not support independent origins.

In 2005, in *Acta Anthropologica Sinica*, Wu responded to several molecular studies, including Y-chromosome papers published from 2000 to 2002. The conclusion that archaic people in China had been completely replaced by modern humans from Africa, he said, did not follow from a partial analysis of the Y chromosome. Human molecular evolution was far more complex than had been thought. The three markers pass down the paternal line; they can speak to that line. They cannot rule out archaic segments surviving on other chromosomes.

## 7. A Skull in the River

On July 28, 1996, a race was being held on the river by Columbia Park in Kennewick, Washington. According to a later compilation of local newspaper reports, twenty-one-year-old Will Thomas and nineteen-year-old Dave Deacy were wading in the shallows. Thomas’s foot hit something round. He thought it was a rock, but what he picked up was a human skull with teeth.

The same compilation says the two hid the skull in bushes for a time and went back to watch the race. Later, they put it in a container and turned it over to police. According to Seattle’s Burke Museum, part of the skull was found on the riverbed about three meters from shore. More searching recovered a nearly complete skeleton. He is generally known as Kennewick Man. The tribes who claim him call him the Ancient One.

On October 22, 1996, a religious organization called the Asatru Folk Assembly filed suit. The court record says they claimed the remains were actually of European ancestry. They asked that transfer be stopped and scientific testing allowed. If European ancestry were established, they wanted custody.

A federal district court ruling from 1997 preserves a line from the complaint: the ultimate aim was reburial according to native European beliefs.

On February 4, 2004, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled that the record then available did not establish that the remains were Native American human remains as defined by a federal repatriation law. That law did not apply, and scientific study could go ahead under another law governing archaeological resources. The court was deciding on the basis of the legal definition and the administrative record at the time.

The ruling summarized an early examination of the bones. The man was about forty-five to fifty when he died. The bones were well preserved and had several injuries, one of them a stone point embedded in his body.

In 2015, a team published his genome in *Nature*. The radiocarbon age cited in the paper was about 8,300 to 9,200 years ago. The abstract said he was more closely related to living Indigenous Americans than to any other population worldwide.

The Burke Museum says that on February 17, 2017, the remains were returned to the tribes who claimed him as an ancestor: the Colville, Yakama, Umatilla, Nez Perce and Wanapum.

## 8. Claiming an Ancestor

In April 2010, a headline in Kenya’s *EastAfrican* said Baringo residents wanted their “ancestor” back. According to the report, residents of Kapsomin village wanted the fossils brought back from Nairobi. What they were asking for was Millennium Man, the set of fossils some six million years old.

The previous July, Kenya’s *Standard* had reported that the mystery of Millennium Man’s disappearance was deepening. A villager said they had been told the fossils were taken away for research and had not seen them again. The report used such words as treasure and fossils.

The 2001 naming paper has a table of who collected each fossil and when. The first find in 2000 was a finger bone, collected by Evelyne Kiptalam on October 13. Another collector, Kiptalam Cheboi, found two jaws twelve days later. Two parts of a thighbone were collected by the paper’s two authors on November 4 and 5.

The Flame Emperor and the Yellow Emperor are legendary rulers of early China. The first unit of the People’s Education Press history textbook for the first semester of seventh grade in schools for blind students says the legends reflect a picture of our ancestors’ lives five or six thousand years ago.

The editors of *The Road of Huaxia*, a book edited by the National Museum of China and published in 2021, said the ancestors of the Chinese, like those of other populations on Earth, had come down from antiquity one generation at a time.

In December 1903, workers digging a drainage trench in Gough’s Cave in Cheddar, England, came upon a nearly complete human skeleton. The bones were sealed beneath a layer of cave calcite, and the workers inadvertently moved the skull. Radiocarbon dating later placed the man about ten thousand years ago. He became known as Cheddar Man.

A British documentary in 2018 was called *The First Brit: Secrets of the 10,000 Year Old Man*. Researchers drilled a two-millimeter hole in the skull and took a few milligrams of powder to read DNA. Channel 4, University College London and the Natural History Museum released a reconstruction with blue eyes, dark curly hair and “dark to black” skin pigmentation.

**The museum’s question-and-answer page said the most likely skin color fell into the darkest two of five categories, not among the lightest ones.**

A museum researcher told the public that reconstructing a face takes both art and science. A University College London researcher later said on a podcast that we do not know all the genes that affect skin pigmentation. Some of the ones with large effects, he said, are known.

**One word, “ancestor,” and two ways of counting that do not line up. The part that will not line up is the remainder.** Adam and Eve were the two names on the *Newsweek* cover. Start with one person and a family tree doubles its width at each generation. Follow mother to mother and choose just one person each time. What you get is a line.

The line was arranged, not walked.
