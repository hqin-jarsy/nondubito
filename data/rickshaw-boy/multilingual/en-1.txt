Xiangzi wanted a rickshaw.
At first, that was all. It was the whole of his plan.
Not to get rich, not to marry, not to buy land back in the country, not to become somebody. He wanted a rickshaw of his own, so that pulling it would no longer mean handing part of his earnings to its owner.
Lao She makes this wish very concrete. With his own rickshaw, he would pay no daily rental; he would not have to live at a yard owner's mercy; he could pull when he wanted to and rest when he did not.
He had worked out the figures. A new rickshaw cost around a hundred dollars. He saved day by day, for three years.
## How small the wish was
Pause here and consider the size of this wish.
A rickshaw cost roughly a hundred dollars at the time.
It was a sum a puller could save in three years by working and denying himself. It was not a dream. It was a commodity.
And what could this commodity bring him?
One less payment to make.
Most pullers rented their rickshaws. Each day they first paid the yard its share; only the remainder was theirs. Owning one meant no longer making that payment.
That was all.
He would still have to pull. Still run beneath the blazing sun and into the winter wind. Still bargain with fares and be looked down on. Nothing else about his life would change.
He wanted to keep the product of his labor, rather than hand over half of it every day.
It was about the smallest demand a person could make.
## And he was the best placed to do it
The beginning of the novel makes another point clear: among the pullers, Xiangzi was best equipped for the task.
He was young, in his twenties.
He was extraordinarily strong. Lao She spends a long passage on his body: tall, broad-shouldered, long-legged, able to run, with strength to spare. He describes him as being like a tree.
He did not drink, smoke, gamble, or visit brothels. These were four common drains on a puller's money, and he avoided all four.
He could endure hardship and was willing to do so. He pulled faster and longer than other men and never shirked.
He was honest, too. He did not snatch fares or take other men's work, or play tricks on people.
He possessed all the virtues available to a laborer at the bottom of that society.
What the novel would do next was render them ineffective, one by one.
## The first time
After three years, he had saved enough.
He bought his rickshaw.
The account of that day is among the brightest passages in the book. He remembered the date and decided to make it his birthday. He ran his hands over the rickshaw. It was his, bought with the sweat of his body.
Pulling it along the streets, he felt even the ground beneath his feet had changed.
Six months later, it was gone.
There was fighting outside the city. Someone offered a high fare for a journey to Tsinghua. The other pullers were afraid to go. Xiangzi thought of the money and went.
On the way, soldiers seized both him and the rickshaw.
They took the rickshaw away.
He was marched along and made to work for the troops for days. Eventually he escaped in the confusion, taking three camels with him. They gave him the nickname he would carry afterward.
He sold the three camels for thirty-five dollars.
Three years of savings had come back to him as thirty-five dollars.
## The second time
He began saving again.
This time he drove himself harder. Working as Mr. Cao's regular puller gave him a relatively settled life, and the money accumulated little by little. He kept his savings in an earthenware money jar, unwilling to entrust them to someone else.
This time, the man who took them was a policeman.
Detective Sun arrived saying that Mr. Cao was a political subversive and that arrests were to be made. Xiangzi would not escape either, he said, but for old acquaintance’s sake he would give him a way out.
He demanded all Xiangzi's money.
Xiangzi said it was the money for his rickshaw.
Sun said: if you do not hand it over, you come with me.
Xiangzi handed over the money from the jar.
In that scene, he cannot resist. The immediate threat turns his rickshaw savings into the price of getting away.
It is the money he has been putting aside to buy another rickshaw.
## The third time
The third time, hope again took the shape of a rickshaw, but someone else supplied the money.
Huniu bought it for him.
Huniu was the daughter of the yard owner, Fourth Master Liu, and more than ten years older than Xiangzi. She maneuvered him into a relationship and then pressured him into marriage. He married her against his wishes.
After their marriage, she used her private savings to buy a secondhand rickshaw from Er Qiangzi.
Xiangzi longed to have a rickshaw again, but he disliked this particular one.
Its history was bound up with a daughter sold and a wife dead; its black-and-white colors also struck him as unlucky. Yet the money was not his, and he felt unable to decide.
Later Huniu died in obstructed childbirth.
To pay for her funeral, he sold the rickshaw.
That was the third collapse of hope. The first two losses had been his own rickshaw and the savings to buy another.
## The shape of those three losses
Set the three events beside one another and look at what they share.
The first: soldiers.
The second: the police.
The third: a funeral.
What they share is this: their outcomes cannot simply be charged to his lack of diligence or thrift.
He was willing to work and to save. He did take a risk for the high fare to Tsinghua when other men refused. But that does not make the soldiers' seizure his wrongdoing, nor does avoiding such a risk guarantee a secure life.
He treated diligence and thrift as ways to hold on to a life.
The cruelest thing in the novel is that doing those things right did not protect what they produced.
He avoided drink and gambling, so he saved money. The money was taken.
He was strong and could endure hardship, so he pulled more than other men. The extra was taken.
He was honest and did not play tricks. That very honesty let Detective Sun approach him directly.
Within that arrangement, each virtue merely increased what could be taken from him.
## What was that arrangement?
We must now be clear about what was taking these things away.
It was not one particular villain.
The soldiers were not singling Xiangzi out: they seized rickshaws they encountered on the road. Sun's extortion was not personal either; it was how he lived. Today Xiangzi, tomorrow someone else. Huniu's death in childbirth was a calamity.
Taken together, the three events form something very clear.
In that environment, anything a laborer at the bottom managed to accumulate lay exposed.
He had no protection he could rely on.
He distrusted putting his money elsewhere and had few dependable avenues. Police protection was hardly available when the extortionist was a policeman. He lacked the money and knowledge to sustain a lawsuit. Even meeting someone willing to help did not give him a reliable system of safeguards.
From the first day he saved it, his money was liable to be taken by someone with a little power.
He did not understand that.
He thought that once he had saved enough, the rickshaw would be his.
## Something that must also be said
But this essay cannot speak only of what was taken from him. There is also something Xiangzi himself did.
After losing his first rickshaw, he took three camels.
They were someone else's camels. He took them while escaping and sold them for thirty-five dollars.
It was not robbery, nor much of a crime in this reading: it was something a man fleeing for his life did in a time of disorder.
It matters because it was the first time Xiangzi took something that was not his.
He felt a little unease, and it soon passed.
It is the first thread Lao She lays down.
For the novel will finally ask what this man becomes, and that change does not begin on one neatly marked day.
## What he thought
Between the three losses, Xiangzi's thinking was also changing.
After the first, his answer was: start again.
He gritted his teeth and planned to save from the beginning. If he worked hard enough, surely one day it would be possible. His spirit was still intact.
After the second, his answer was: there is no justice in this world.
Other thoughts began to enter. Men who did not work properly seemed to do well; what was he exhausting himself for? Yet he still did not give up.
After selling the rickshaw for the funeral, his spirit sank another level.
The change was not completed at the moment of that loss. Xiaofuzi would still give him the thought of beginning a new life.
But repeated disappointment was pushing him toward an idea: even selling his strength could not secure a life of his own.
## That judgment
Early in the book, Lao She offers a judgment that bears on the whole novel.
Its sense is this: Xiangzi thinks of his own rickshaw, his own life, everything resting on his own ability; yet this world will not allow a person to live that way.
There is another, harsher judgment after his collapse.
He had been respectable, ambitious, full of dreams, self-interested, individual, robust, magnificent Xiangzi.
Now he was a human failure.
Notice the word “individual.”
Lao She means something by it. Xiangzi's defining aspiration is to accomplish something alone.
He places his deepest trust in his own strength and dislikes staking his life on other people's help. He believes that being good enough will let him earn a life by himself.
The novel says that this road leads nowhere.

There were two women in Xiangzi's life.
One was Huniu: more than ten years older, unattractive, fierce, the yard owner's daughter. She maneuvered him into a relationship and pressured him to marry. She gave him a rickshaw and took possession of his days.
The other was Xiaofuzi, Er Qiangzi's daughter. Her father sold her; after she returned, she supported her two younger brothers by selling sex.
Both lived within even narrower limits than Xiangzi.
The next essay is about them.
Their role in this novel is not simply to provide a love story.
They are two instances of the ways a person can be disposed of.
