While Sun Shaoping is working as a day laborer in Huangyuan, Tian Xiaoxia comes to see him.
He is sleeping under other people’s eaves. Carrying stones has torn his back raw; at night he must soak his shirt with hot water to separate it from the flesh.
She finds him.
Her first response is not to tell him to go home or offer to arrange an easier job.
She brings books.
## How does she see him differently?
To understand Xiaoxia’s place in this reading, consider how others see Shaoping.
In Shuangshui, he is “Sun Yuhou’s second boy.”
A farmer’s son, literate, secondary-school educated, briefly a village teacher, reasonably presentable, capable of hard work. A complete file.
At the Dongguan labor market, he is a casual laborer.
A young man waiting beside the road to be chosen: strong, willing, inexpensive. Another complete file.
At Daya Bay Coal Mine, he is a new worker.
Someone who goes underground. This file records occupation, shift, and pay grade.
None of these files contains Shaoping himself.
They record what he can do, what he costs, and where he fits.
No individual malice is required. This is a normal way of becoming known in a society: through one’s position.
Shaoping also accepts it. In Huangyuan, telling people who he is seems useless; at the mine, he conscientiously performs the worker’s role.
Xiaoxia, in the contrast this essay draws, does not let that file determine the person she meets.
## The books she brings
They meet while still at secondary school. She is a cadre’s daughter, widely read and better acquainted with the world; he eats the lowest-grade school meals.
She lends him reading material.
Reference News, novels, newspapers. They discuss international affairs, literature, and things that have no immediate bearing on their daily lives.
Pause over that.
The books offer no immediate solution to his material situation.
They do not buy wheat buns, change his household registration, or secure a job. In a narrowly practical account, reading them is wasted time for a rural boy who could be doing more work.
Those supposedly useless things are what Xiaoxia shares.
Later, when he is nearly exhausted by carrying stone in Huangyuan, she still brings books. They agree to read and exchange their thoughts.
She does not make intellectual conversation something he must postpone until his living conditions improve.
## She helps him, but sees more than a position
A detail easily passed over matters greatly to this portrait.
Xiaoxia’s father is Tian Fujun, a powerful local official who becomes prefectural Party secretary.
She does use her father’s name to help Shaoping gain admission to the mine’s workforce. She does it without telling her father; it is her intervention, not a placement personally arranged by Tian Fujun.
Their relationship therefore includes very practical assistance.
She also says that after a year or two she will ask her father to have him transferred out.
She worries about his circumstances and wants to find him an easier route. She is not someone who never intervenes.
Where, then, does the difference lie?
Not in an absence of practical help.
It lies in seeing more than a man who needs a different position.
Shaoping has convictions of his own, but he also accepts help. Independence cannot mean that he has never benefited from anyone’s connections.
The books and conversations concern things that do not immediately change his job. The assistance she gives does not exhaust the relationship.
The second essay described Shaoan calculating Runye’s future and acting on the result.
Xiaoxia also has more resources than Shaoping, and she uses them. The contrast cannot rest on her never having arranged an opportunity.
The question is whether helping someone becomes authority over their entire future. The books, conversations, and promised meeting let us see a Shaoping whom a better job alone could not define.
## The promise on Guta Mountain
On Guta Mountain in Huangyuan, beneath a wild pear tree, they make a promise.
Two years later, on that day, they will meet there again.
Notice its content.
It settles no practical arrangement.
Not marriage in two years, not his transfer to the city, not a plan for what their circumstances must become.
Simply a meeting beneath the same tree.
By the usual practical calculation, it is a useless promise.
It solves none of the questions of registration, work, family, or the distance between their circumstances.
That is part of its weight.
The practical matters they could discuss offer few easy answers.
This promise says something else: in two years, we will still want to see one another.
## Her death
Xiaoxia dies in a flood.
She is reporting from the front line of the disaster. A young girl is swept away, and she enters the water to save her.
The child survives. Xiaoxia is carried off.
Her body is not found.
The date of their promised meeting is approaching.
Shaoping is at the mine, waiting for that day, until the news reaches him.
He goes to Guta Mountain and stays for a long time beneath the pear tree.
He keeps the appointment.
## Why does the novel make her die?
Readers have fiercely objected to this decision. Accounts of the novel’s reception include pleas that she should not die and reports of the difficulty Lu Yao himself felt in writing it.
Her death can also be read as an expression of a structural impasse, rather than only a technical decision about the plot.
Consider the possibilities if she lives.
They would have to confront their unequal circumstances.
She is a senior official’s daughter and a provincial newspaper journalist; he is a miner of rural origin. The essay tests several imaginable routes for them.
First, Shaoping could gain a more prestigious urban job through her family. Accepting help need not destroy his independence: he has already accepted some. But making advancement the final explanation of every earlier struggle would narrow what his journey means.
Second, Xiaoxia could abandon her career and follow him to the mining community. That would turn their story into another woman’s sacrifice for love, a pattern the novel already explores through Xiulian.
Third, they could continue without resolving the difference. That postpones the question rather than answering it.
In the essay’s deliberately severe account, each of these routes threatens something essential to one or both of them.
That is how it interprets the decision to end her life.
On this reading, the severity is not merely the writer’s. It belongs to a social structure that makes a shared life, without the sacrifice of either person’s direction, so difficult to imagine.
Her death gives narrative form to the difficulty of joining a miner’s life to that of an official’s daughter without sacrificing either person’s direction.
The novel does not state this as a proposition. It speaks through the flood.
## What remains with him?
Here is where the reading arrives.
Shaoping changes after Xiaoxia’s death.
Not in the most predictable direction.
He grieves, but does not cease living. He continues underground, continues working, continues his days.
Nor does “moving on” simply become pursuing a higher position. Later, he refuses opportunities to leave the mine.
What, then, has she left him?
The experience of her regard becomes a ground for his later choices.
Before her, he struggles against his rank. As the first essay argued, part of his desire to leave is a desire to escape the farmer’s place. He reaches upward.
Her way of seeing him makes another possibility knowable: his rank does not determine who he is.
They talk about books and international events without treating their conversation as a benefactor’s uplift of a rural youth.
Their appointment beneath the tree makes no stipulation about social identity.
Within this reading, she represents a relationship in which his position never has to be his definition.
Once someone has been treated that way, they know such treatment is possible.
## Something that must not disappear
We must not make Xiaoxia perfect merely to turn her into a device that illuminates the male protagonist.
She has projects of her own.
She wants to be a journalist who goes to the scene. Entering the flood to save the child is not an act for Shaoping. It follows her own judgment.
The novel shows her reporting, writing, arguing, and confronting superiors over her work. That vocation is hers, not an accessory to his life.
He is not there when she dies and does not know what is happening.
Her leap into the water is not about him.
That independence is part of who she is: someone with an imperative of her own that does not wait upon a lover’s permission.
They recognize each other while each has such a direction.
## Next
Shaoping later suffers an accident at the mine.
He is struck by falling rock while helping a fellow worker. He survives with a deep scar on his face.
Someone comes to him afterward.
Jin Xiu, his closest friend Jin Bo’s younger sister, is a medical student with a promising future.
She tells him of her affection. Crucially, her letter offers to work at his mine’s hospital after graduation, not simply to take him away into a different class position.
Separately, Lanxiang and Wu Zhongping propose a transfer to the provincial capital. Jin Xiu’s offer moves toward his life at the mine; theirs opens a route away.
Shaoping does not accept Jin Xiu’s proposal.
He returns to Daya Bay Coal Mine.
He returns to the community that includes his late mentor Wang Shicai’s household, Huiying and Mingming.
That is where the novel ends.
The last essay examines that ending and the question:
Is fidelity to oneself ordinary or extraordinary?
