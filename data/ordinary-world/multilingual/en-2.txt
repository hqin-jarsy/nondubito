Tian Runye sends word asking Sun Shaoan to come to the county town.
He goes. They meet and walk together for a long time.
Then she says what she has come to say.
Her meaning is clear: she wants to be with him. She does not mind that he is a farmer or what others will say. She can find a way.
Shaoan refuses.
The form of his refusal is one of the novel’s most consequential gestures.
## He does not say he does not love her
First, let us describe the act accurately.
Shaoan does not say he has no feelings for her.
They grew up together. Their childhood closeness is known throughout the village. The novel never leaves his affection for her in doubt.
He says something else.
In substance, he says: Runye is a teacher, employed by the state and entitled to commodity grain; he is a farmer scratching a living from the soil. They are not a suitable match. He cannot hold her back. She will regret it.
Each difficulty he names is real.
The urban–rural divide was material: different household registrations, grain supplies, occupations, and consequences for the registration of future children. A teacher with state grain entitlements marrying a farmer would provoke county-wide comment. Her uncle Tian Fujun is an official; the marriage would involve an entire family.
His arithmetic is not mistaken.
But it is her account he is adding up.
## The account is not his
Here is the central issue.
Rejecting someone’s affection is not itself the problem. That is his right; anyone may refuse a relationship.
What he does here is calculate Runye’s future for her, reach a conclusion, and act on it.
The conclusion is: “She will regret being with me.”
But that conclusion concerns her life.
She has already spoken. She has considered the risks. As a teacher, she knows what registration means and what resistance her family will offer. She has thought, and then come to him.
That afternoon, she offers a decision of her own.
He does not receive it. He withdraws it for her.
The two acts look alike from outside: in either case, he has refused her.
The difference lies in the reason he gives.
“I do not want to be with you” would state his decision. That would be enough.
What he says is: “You will regret it.”
The sentence claims to know her future mind better than she does, and on that ground settles the matter for her.
## Another reason he does not state
Looking further, we find something more concrete.
There is another immediate reason Shaoan cannot marry Runye: he supports a household.
His father is aging, his younger siblings are at school, and the family is desperately poor. Marrying her seems to require going to town, or at least acquiring a position commensurate with hers. To him, that would mean abandoning the household that depends on him.
He understands that burden. But he does not put it to Runye in those terms.
He says: “You will regret it.”
One reason has been substituted for another.
“I cannot leave” concerns his circumstances and his choice. He is entitled to make that choice.
“This is for your good” is the reason he presents instead.
It sounds more honorable and is harder to answer: nobody can prove in advance that they will never regret a decision.
One of the book’s most admired people makes a defining decision in this way.
To be clear, he is not a villain or a schemer.
He probably sincerely believes that life with him would make Runye suffer. He probably believes he is considering her welfare. People often sincerely mean well when deciding for others.
The question is not the sincerity of the motive. It is whose decision this is.
## What follows
Runye is devastated for a long time.
Later she is steered into marrying Li Xiangqian, a driver from a reasonably comfortable official’s family: an unassuming man who genuinely likes her.
She marries him.
For years afterward she does not share his bed. She withdraws into a room, neither quarreling nor seeking divorce.
Xiangqian cannot bear it and begins drinking heavily. After drinking, he crashes his vehicle and loses both legs.
That is the subject of the third essay.
For now, remember that the afternoon’s decision is followed by years like these.
## The woman he marries
After refusing Runye, Shaoan goes to Shanxi.
He meets a prospective bride and settles the marriage within days. She is He Xiulian, from a poor household, attractive and direct.
The match is made possible by one condition: her family does not require a bride price.
For a rural household, marrying a son can be its greatest expense, leaving debts for years. Shaoan’s family cannot pay it.
Xiulian says she does not need it. It is the man she wants.
She follows him to Shuangshui, into a household that cannot even provide a decent cave dwelling for them.
## Spending herself
Xiulian is easy to read past. We should not.
What does she do after arriving?
She works.
She works alongside Shaoan from before dawn until after dark. After they establish a separate household in a dilapidated cave dwelling, she keeps working. When he needs capital for a brickworks, she goes to her own family to borrow it.
The first venture makes money. He expands and hires a supposed technical expert, but the man cannot deliver what he promises. A failed firing ruins the bricks. The business collapses into debt.
It is Shaoan’s darkest period. He hides at home, dreads the creditors who come to the door, and loses his ability to face the world.
Xiulian sustains him.
She does not reproach him. Money is external to life, she tells him; as long as they are still here, they can begin again. She stays near him, insisting that he eat and go outside.
The brickworks eventually starts again and grows.
Shaoan becomes the village’s successful entrepreneur. His household joins those earning over ten thousand yuan a year; he receives county praise and appears in newspapers. He rebuilds the village school.
Around this time, Xiulian begins to cough.
At first it is taken for irritation from brick dust. Then she coughs up blood.
The hospital diagnosis is lung cancer.
## The shape of this life
Put the sequence together.
She enters the household when it has nothing, asking no bride price.
She gives years to the heaviest work, including the smoke, heat, and dust around the brick kilns.
She borrows from her own family and helps carry them through their worst days.
Just as the household finally prospers, she falls ill.
The novel describes Shaoan’s response with restraint. He sits there, unable to speak.
He has worked to improve this family’s life. When that improvement finally arrives, the person who endured its hardest years beside him receives a terminal diagnosis.
The promised ease scarcely reaches her.
## Not merely fate
The easiest reading is that fate has played a cruel trick: goodness receives no reward.
But this essay does not read the sequence simply as fate.
Xiulian has lung cancer. The narrative has also placed her amid years of exhausting labor, including work at the kilns.
Lu Yao supplies no medical analysis establishing a cause. He repeatedly puts work before us: smoke, ash, earth, heat, morning to night.
Reading her illness beside that labor makes the bodily cost of this life visible.
Why such work? The household needs to survive, Shaoan wants to build the business, and the routes visible to her are painfully narrow.
The essay reads this not as a decree from heaven but as an exchange: she spends her body for the household’s chance to rise.
That chance arrives when she can no longer make use of it.
## Did she choose it?
Here is the difficult question.
Xiulian acts willingly.
She chooses the marriage, the work, the borrowing from her family, and the long days beside the kilns. She is not presented as being physically forced into them or constantly protesting them.
But what does “willingly” mean here?
A poor young woman from rural Shanxi, with little schooling, sees marriage as the available way forward. She selects Shaoan from the few possibilities she can see.
Once she enters the household, work dominates the contribution available to her.
She puts all her strength into it because that strength is what she has to offer and what she can exchange for a different life.
I would not reduce her position simply to exploitation. The sharper point here is that she has so little else to put on the table.
The coldness of the situation lies in how closely “I choose this” can coincide with “I see no other way.”
## Something Xiulian wants
We cannot stop there, or we turn her into nothing but a victim.
The novel gives her a small wish, more than once.
She wants a home of her own.
Not the abstraction “family,” but a cave dwelling for the two of them. At first they are crowded in with her in-laws. She presses for a separate household, a controversial demand that creates serious tension between Shaoan and his father.
She persists.
They move into the poor cave dwelling. Later, when the brickworks makes money, they build a new home.
She does not live in it for long.
What is most moving about her is not only how she works. It is the specificity of her desires: a room of her own; Shaoan coming home for supper; new clothes for the children; plans for later.
Do these wishes increase the household’s productive capacity? Not in that ledger’s terms.
A new cave dwelling does not make the brickworks more profitable. The “later” she imagines is cut short.
These wishes are precisely what keep her from being only a pair of working hands.
## Next
Shaoan makes a decision for Runye that afternoon.
She later marries Xiangqian and keeps her distance for years. He drinks, crashes, and loses both legs.
Then she returns.
She goes to the man she did not love, now disabled, cares for him, and later has a child with him.
This is among the novel’s most difficult and disputed episodes.
Some call it an awakening; others, the final wearing down of her will; still others, an easy ending supplied by the author.
The next essay examines it directly.
The reading I propose differs from all three.
