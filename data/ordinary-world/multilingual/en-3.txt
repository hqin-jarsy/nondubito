When news of Li Xiangqian’s crash reaches Tian Runye, she is working in the county town.
He has driven after drinking and overturned his vehicle. His life is saved, but not his legs.
Judging by the preceding years, one might expect the news to have little bearing on her life.
They have been married for years and have scarcely spoken.
She does not share his room or meals or return to his family for New Year. Within this marriage, she has withdrawn herself and endured one day after another.
Yet she goes to the hospital.
She stays, cares for him, feeds him, washes him, and wheels him into the sun.
Later they have a child.
## Three familiar readings
Few passages in the novel provoke more disagreement. The question is always the same: what are we looking at?
The first answer is that she finally comes to her senses.
This familiar reading follows the narrative’s affirmative tone: she sees Xiangqian’s goodness, abandons her impractical attachment to Shaoan, and accepts a wife’s responsibilities. It is a story of growth.
The second is that she has been worn down.
This critical reading says the social order has finally domesticated her. She once had a will of her own and resisted for years, but ultimately returns to her assigned place. That is surrender, not growth.
The third is that Lu Yao gives her an easy ending.
Here the crash is an authorial solution to a problem the author cannot otherwise solve. Xiangqian’s disability supplies her reason to return and gives the writer an ending he can deliver. The objection concerns narrative convenience.
All three have something to say. I think they also overlook the same thing.
## What Xiangqian has been doing
To understand the episode, look at him before and after the marriage.
He has liked Runye for a long time, sincerely.
He is not a pampered libertine. He is a driver, awkward and unassuming, from a somewhat better-off family rather than some immensely powerful household. Around her he becomes too nervous to finish a sentence.
What does he do? He asks others to intervene.
His mother pursues the match. Runye’s aunt Xu Aiyun and her aunt’s father, Xu Guoqiang, help press it upon her. Tian Fujun initially does not know the pressure being applied in his name.
Her own willingness is not what directs that process.
That is the starting point. He relies on the most normal, respectable, apparently reliable route available in his surroundings: approaching the elders, rather than allowing Runye’s answer to govern the proposal.
Her aunt advocates the match, while Xu Guoqiang invokes her uncle’s political future. Around her come the familiar arguments: it is a good match, she is of marriageable age, his circumstances are so favorable.
Her future is arranged.
After marriage, Xiangqian tries every way to please her. He buys things, treats her kindly, endures her distance, and waits year after year.
Those gifts are also part of what bears down on her.
Every kindness is counted as another addition to what she owes.
The novel presents her as trapped. Divorce carries formidable social consequences for a teacher and her connected family; accepting the marriage is equally impossible to her, because she loves someone else and never wanted this arrangement.
Between those pressures, she can only hold still.
His kindness makes holding still harder and harder.
An abusive husband could be hated. With a husband everyone calls exceptionally kind, even hatred seems forbidden. She can only remain indebted.
## The drinking
Xiangqian eventually drinks heavily.
In this reading, his immediate grievance is that years of giving have brought no return.
His reasoning is familiar: I have treated you so well, waited so long, done everything, and still you will not look at me.
The decisive element is this:
He treats his own expenditure as a reason she ought to respond.
It is not his private notion. His mother, neighbors, and members of her own family share it: after all he has done for you, what more can you want?
Within that accounting, Runye owes a debt that grows daily.
He drinks, drives, and crashes.
The crash is an accident, but the essay reads the drinking behind it as the collapse of someone under that accumulating account, not as an event without a history.
## What changes after the crash?
Here is the reading I propose.
After losing his legs, Xiangqian appears to Runye, for the first time, no longer as someone making a claim on her.
In the hospital he lies helpless. The familiar routine of buying, pleasing, and accumulating claims through giving has been interrupted.
In this scene, he seems to have nothing left to offer as an installment toward the response he hopes to receive.
That is when Runye returns.
On her first hospital visit she sees someone utterly broken, no longer asking anything of her. She has not encountered him in this way before.
On this reading, she does not return because she has suddenly fallen in love, or simply surrendered to her fate.
Before this moment, she has experienced their relationship as a continual demand, an account she could never repay.
In her changed experience of him, the crash wipes that account clean.
When the account falls away, the person can at last be approached.
## Why read it this way?
Compare the three earlier interpretations.
“She wakes up” supposes that she finally discovers his goodness.
But she has always seen it. The novel does not make her mistake him for an evil man. What she cannot bear is not simply mistreatment, but the obligation attached to all that goodness.
“She is worn down” supposes that she surrenders.
Then why not earlier? She has held out through years of misery, until his drinking ends in a crash. His disability alone does not explain a sudden reversal in someone who has resisted for so long.
“The author takes a shortcut” calls the accident convenient.
It is indeed a designed event. Lu Yao needs something to open the impasse and chooses a crash.
But within this reading it is not arbitrary. It interrupts the specific form of giving that has kept the impasse in place.
Had he made Xiangqian richer, more powerful, or more accomplished, the account would only have grown heavier.
He moves in the opposite direction.
## It is still an ugly situation
This interpretation must not become a defense of everything that happens, as though we had finally reached a consoling resolution.
There is no such resolution.
The thing Runye wanted has not been given to her.
She wanted Shaoan. That possibility ended in the afternoon examined in the second essay, when someone else judged her future for her.
Her subsequent life unfolds after that decision: an arranged marriage, years of deadlock, a man’s drinking and disabling injury, and then sustained care.
She did not freely select that sequence in advance.
Going back is, in this account, the first act along that imposed course she can distinctly make her own. Yet the opening comes through another person’s terrible injury.
That is not a good situation. It is a situation she can finally enter.
Colder still, the care she provides looks entirely respectable to the people around her.
Neighbors praise her, her family relaxes, Xiangqian’s mother is grateful. She has finally become a “good wife.”
An act the essay reads as her own decision appears to outsiders as her eventual submission.
Their praise makes no room for the difference.
## Two women beside one another
Put Runye beside Xiulian.
Xiulian has her strength to offer. She invests it in the household’s rise; the improvement arrives when illness prevents her from enjoying it.
Runye has had a decision disposed of on her behalf. Much of her subsequent life takes shape within that disposition.
Both are called willing.
Xiulian willingly marries, works, and borrows from her family. Runye ultimately says yes to marrying Xiangqian, remains in the marriage, and goes back to care for him.
Yet “willing” does not name one simple, identical condition in these lives.
It says that each chooses among the few possibilities visible to her.
Who has laid out those possibilities?
For Xiulian, the narrow prospects of a poor young woman in rural Shanxi make marriage the visible route ahead.
For Runye, the costs of divorce, the position of a woman teacher, and pressure from an official’s family constrain the alternatives.
They have not made the available cards themselves.
They choose a card from those laid before them, then must live that choice as their own.
## Something each retains
But this essay does not end where that account might lead you to expect.
Xiulian asks for a cave dwelling of her own.
Her usefulness as labor does not require that room. The household’s work allocation has no need to grant it. She wants it because she wants it.
On the day Runye returns, she herself walks into the hospital.
No new command from her uncle sends her there. Xiangqian’s family does not dare demand it. Shaoan does not direct it. She goes herself.
She does not go to secure a reputation for respectability. That reputation is added afterward by other people.
In the ward she encounters a man whom she no longer experiences as buying a response or claiming one from her.
It may be the first moment in this relationship that does not feel like an outstanding account.
