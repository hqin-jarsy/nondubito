# Ragtime: Why Does It Have to Be That Car?

*Adapted from E. L. Doctorow's novel, Ragtime has a book by Terrence McNally, music by Stephen Flaherty and lyrics by Lynn Ahrens. It premiered in Toronto in 1996 and opened on Broadway in 1998. This essay follows the story and songs of the original Broadway version, without treating a later revival's designs as fixed features of the musical or drawing on the film. It discusses a death in Act One and the violence that follows but leaves the outcome of the final siege undisclosed.*

Coalhouse Walker Jr. has a car of his own. For a Black pianist at the start of the twentieth century, it's something he's earned through his work. It's also part of the pleasure of dressing well to go see the woman he loves. He hasn't taken anything from anybody, and he isn't driving out there to challenge anyone. But a group of white volunteer firefighters see him in that car and decide he needs to be taught a lesson. Who said someone like you could have something like that?

The car is later wrecked. Coalhouse insists that it be restored and that those responsible be held to account. What looks like a loss that could be settled with money sets a conflict in motion that gets harder and harder to stop. Is it worth it, people might ask, for a car? As Ragtime goes on, we begin to hear what he's asking. The car doesn't matter more than a life. Why, when it's his turn, do the rules everyone already understands suddenly stop counting?

Coalhouse isn't alone in the story. There is also Tateh, a Jewish immigrant who crosses the ocean with his daughter, and a wealthy white family in New Rochelle, New York. The three families seem to lead separate lives. It's only when someone needs to come in through another family's door, or someone drives past the wrong group of men, that we see the invisible lines were there all along.

## One Harbor, Three Lives That Haven't Met

The opening is in no hurry to make friends of its characters. Wealthy whites, Black people in Harlem and newly arrived immigrants enter with different ideas of what this country might be. There are a lot of people, some of them famous, and at first it can be hard to know where to look. One crossing is worth holding on to: a father leaves America for the Arctic, while another arrives with his daughter, desperate to make a life in America.

In “Journey On,” Father and Tateh look at each other from different ships. Both are going a long way. Father is leaving a home he trusts will be waiting when he returns. Tateh carries as much of home as he can and goes looking for a place that might or might not make room for them.

Someone else hasn't boarded a ship at all. Mother stays home and takes charge of the household in her husband's absence. He expects to come back to everything as it was. She already has a faint sense that this time may change her. She's always had enough to eat and wear, but she's also been given the job of keeping things as they were. Other people get to go and have their adventures; she makes sure there's still the same home to come back to.

The song lets three people think at once. We can't blend their thoughts into one stirring sentiment about setting out. Some can take a chance because they have somewhere to go back to. Others are taking one because they have nowhere left to go. Mother's hesitation is different from either father's. She hasn't left home, but the change may already have begun there. The music puts them together without making their situations the same.

## A Child Is Alive in the Garden

Change begins with a terrible discovery. In her own garden, Mother finds a Black baby who's been buried and is still alive. She learns that a young woman named Sarah is the baby's mother. When the police bring Sarah in, it would be easy for things to go the usual way. Sarah would be taken away, the child put in someone else's care, and the household would slowly return to normal.

Mother decides to take them both in. She doesn't wait for her absent husband's permission to change what the household is going to have to take on. This isn't a declaration against discrimination. It means a room, meals, care and the quarrel that's bound to come when her husband returns. She uses her own judgment, and she has to put in the daily work that follows from it.

Still, Sarah deserves more than to be the person Mother needs in order to wake up to things. In “Your Daddy's Son,” Sarah tells the baby about her fear and despair and what she feels for the baby's father. The harm she did to the child is still there. The song doesn't make the act innocent. It lets us see that the woman who did it can't be summed up in a headline.

In talking about the song, Ahrens has singled out the need to let audiences understand Sarah's fear and panic. The stage needs to leave Sarah a voice of her own. Otherwise she can easily become something that happens in other people's stories, first making a white woman brave and then making a Black man angry, without anyone leaving her time to live through her own grief.

So when it comes to whether Sarah will go downstairs and see Coalhouse, we shouldn't treat it as another step the leading man has to get through. He left her and has come back to find her, without yet knowing he has a child. He's sorry, he's charming and he has music to say what he wants to say. They still need Sarah to agree to see him. Giving her a room doesn't give Mother the right to decide whether Sarah should forgive him.

## What the Piano Does to the Parlor

Coalhouse calls on Sunday after Sunday and ends up at the piano. The music gradually changes the room and how the family feels about the visitor. When Father comes home, he finds his wife more independent, a Black couple accepted by his family and unfamiliar sounds coming from his own piano.

“New Music” introduces more than a style. What unsettles Father is that other people have grown close in his absence without waiting for his approval. He had taken for granted that everything in this household would be measured by him. Now it's clear that his family can have parts of their lives he didn't arrange.

The lightness of this part of the musical matters. Later we'll see a great deal of Coalhouse as he seeks redress and takes revenge. Here we meet someone who can bring pleasure into a room. He cares whether Sarah comes downstairs, can show what he knows how to do at the piano and is excited about the future. Skip these days and all that's destroyed later is a political principle. Leave them in and we can see that the conflict breaks into a life just beginning again.

By the time Coalhouse and Sarah sing “Wheels of a Dream,” they're already imagining their child's future. They aren't asking for very much: to make a family, to go where they want and to give their child a life that doesn't have to inherit their limitations. The wheels stand for a freedom they could actually use. When the car is wrecked, that hope is publicly humiliated. The life you thought was yours can still be taken away on somebody else's whim.

So telling him to let it go isn't costless advice. If today he swallows the loss of a car for the sake of his family, what will he have to swallow tomorrow, and for whose sake? If the person harmed is always the first one called on to be reasonable, while the people who broke the rules don't have to answer, keeping the peace begins to look a lot like teaching people to put up with being bullied.

## When Every Door Turns Him Away

Coalhouse doesn't turn to violence straight away. He tries to use the law to get his car restored and the men who wrecked it held to account. Only after he is turned away again and again does the advice to go through proper channels lose its force. It isn't that he never thought of those doors. He's stood in front of them and found they won't open for him.

Sarah believes there must be someone higher up who can put things right. She tries to reach a visiting candidate for vice president to ask for help, is mistaken for a threat and is fatally beaten. She wasn't approaching someone in power to attack him. She thought she'd finally found somebody who could hear her out. Now a dispute about a car is the fact of a person who can never come home again.

At this point we may find ourselves saying that after that, anything Coalhouse does is understandable. If “understandable” ends the discussion, we'll leave out the next people to encounter violence. Coalhouse shoots and kills firefighters involved in wrecking his car and burns their firehouse. His followers later set fires elsewhere in the city. Coalhouse then takes over the financier J. P. Morgan's library, threatening to blow up the collection and putting his own life and his companions' lives on the line. He's no longer just a man asking to be made whole.

None of that makes the original injustice less important. And the injustice doesn't make everything he goes on to do right. Once an action keeps putting other people's lives at stake, asking who gets to decide how much risk they should take isn't a demand from the sidelines for a docile victim. It's a way of being answerable to another set of people.

Mother's Younger Brother joins Coalhouse as the violence escalates. He sympathizes with him and finally feels he has something to give himself to. What he brings is a talent for making explosives. Helping someone and finding a place for your own passion in their struggle can happen in the same person. His commitment needn't be written off. But enthusiasm can't tell him what the consequences will be. Finding a purpose in life isn't a reason to stop asking what the person in front of him needs.

## The Book Was for His Daughter First

Meanwhile, Tateh's story begins to look like another kind of answer. He sells silhouettes on the street, does hard factory work, takes his daughter out of danger and eventually finds his way into the new motion picture business. One man succeeds and another heads toward ruin. The musical seems to offer two very different American dreams.

But to treat the two paths as alternative answers to the same question is to miss what most divides them. Tateh isn't rewarded for being more patient than Coalhouse. A better attitude on Coalhouse's part wouldn't have made the men who wrecked the car admit what they'd done. An unexpected opening for one person can't tell us that everyone shut out went about it the wrong way.

“Gliding” is worth stopping for. On the train away from the clash at the mill, Tateh takes out a little book of silhouettes that seem to move when you flip the pages. He uses it to calm his frightened daughter. The conductor sees it and wants to buy it for his own child. Only then does a new way of making a living appear. Before anyone buys it, the book has already done something. The child in front of it is a little less afraid.

If nobody had bought the book, making it wouldn't have been a waste. Tateh saw what his daughter needed and used something he knew how to do to help her. The market finding a use for it and giving them a turn of fortune was a lucky thing that happened afterward. The money the book later made didn't give value to the time he spent comforting her on the train.

This bit of tenderness also helps us see the scale of the show. Great events sweep people up, newspapers chase the famous, a city worships success, and it's easy for an audience to watch only who ends up finding a place. The child on the train doesn't need proof that her father will succeed. She needs someone who sees that she's afraid and has it in him to sit with her amid the upheaval.

## Being Heard Takes More Than a Louder Voice

Later, Mother and Tateh meet again and their children play together. By “Back to Before,” Mother knows she can't be the woman who waited for her husband to decide what life would be like. She's met Sarah, has cared for a child and has made decisions of her own. If things going back to the way they were means acting as if none of this happened, she can no longer go back.

Not being able to go back leaves plenty of questions. Who will care for the child left behind? Who has the means to live differently? Who might die just for asking for something to change? Whether the families can draw closer doesn't depend only on whether they're kind. It depends on whether they're prepared to change how they actually arrange their time, property and responsibilities.

It's worth listening to “Make Them Hear You,” near the end, with those questions in mind. A story people have ignored needs to be told. But a louder voice isn't enough. What people hear may be the story of somebody worth remembering, or it may be one more piece of evidence for what they already think. After they've heard it, what are they willing to change? Or do they just want to say how heartbreaking that history is?

The people in Ragtime can of course be described as a white family, a Black musician and a Jewish immigrant. Those descriptions help explain the barriers each faces, but they don't tell us how any of them hoped to live. Sarah isn't simply someone who died, Tateh isn't a record of professional success, and Coalhouse is more than both the wrong he suffered and the violence he went on to commit.

It's hard to tell him to let the car go because behind the car there was an ordinary day. A man finishes his work, straightens his clothes and goes to see the person he hopes to build a life with again. Sometimes that's where we have to start asking whether a society is fair. We shouldn't wait for him to become a hero, or for his life to become a tragedy, before we agree to hear what he has to say.
