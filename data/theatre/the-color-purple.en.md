# The Color Purple: What Kind of Life Does She Want After Surviving?

*The Color Purple draws on Alice Walker’s novel and the 1985 film. Marsha Norman wrote the book, and Brenda Russell, Allee Willis, and Stephen Bray wrote the music and lyrics. The musical opened on Broadway in 2005. This essay takes the 2015 revival and its subsequent touring productions as its main reference, without bringing in scenes or new choices from the 2023 musical film. It discusses sexual assault, domestic violence, the sisters’ separation, the hidden letters, and Celie’s later independence, but does not describe the final reunion. The story spans several decades in the American South in the first half of the twentieth century.*

For years, Celie doesn’t know why her younger sister has stopped writing. Nettie promised she would write, and she hadn’t stopped loving Celie when they parted. But the days pass and no letters come. Celie can think of fewer and fewer explanations, and the one she fears most begins to look like a fact: her sister may be dead.

Only later does she learn that the letters kept coming. Her husband had been hiding them. When you hold on to someone else’s letters, you get to decide for them whether they still have family out there, whether they still have anything worth waiting for. Celie thought she was up against the cruelty of fate. All along, someone who lived under the same roof had been deliberately making her world smaller.

This is, in part, a story about escaping pain. But tell it only as the story of a woman who suffers terribly and finally grows strong, and you leave out some of what matters most. Celie didn’t have to become strong before her life mattered. She shouldn’t have to spend the rest of it proving to her past that it failed to break her. Once she is out of harm’s way, what she likes, whom she wants, and how she cares to spend a day are hers to decide, too.

## Is there a place in the household that she can choose?

Celie is still a child when the musical opens. An older member of her household has already sexually abused her, and she has been forcibly separated from the children she bore. The sisters’ hand-clapping game and the shared life of the church belong to this same world. Other people’s days go on as usual, while Celie’s body has already been set on a course she did not choose. Later, the man known as Mister wants to marry Nettie, but the family gives him Celie instead. She isn’t entering a new home founded on love. There is housework, farm work, and care to be done, and someone has to do it. For the men deciding the marriage, the first question about a woman’s suitability is what she can do for the household.

Celie has wishes of her own. She wants Nettie to get an education and tries to keep a way forward open for her. But she does so with almost no room to choose. The fact that she wants to protect her sister does not make the marriage a bargain she entered freely. People still find ways to act for themselves in hard circumstances. That does not make those circumstances acceptable.

“Big Dog” brings out the kind of male authority Mister knows. Being the head of a household means you should be feared and obeyed; it doesn’t require you to get to know the people in it. Harpo grows up watching his father and comes to think that if he wants his wife Sofia to obey, he must learn the same methods. Each generation need not invent oppression anew. Call it the way a household is run, and it can be passed down.

The voices around them are more complicated. The church, the neighbors, and the women passing news from one to another make up a public life, but they do not automatically offer Celie shelter. A community can keep people from feeling alone. It can also come to see familiar harm as a private matter best left unexamined. Being surrounded by people is no guarantee that there’s anywhere to say what is happening to you.

## Why Sofia’s refusal hurts Celie at first

Sofia is very different from Celie. Marriage, in her view, gives her husband no right to discipline her. “Hell No!” brings refusal into the musical with force and humor, and Celie sees that some women do not consider being hit something they have to put up with in a marriage. But the show doesn’t give us a stirring remark that changes someone on the spot. When Harpo asks how to make his wife obey, Celie actually tells him to hit her. A woman who has lived with violence advises someone to do to another woman what was done to her.

It is painful to watch precisely because Celie’s own pain hasn’t gone away. She is drawn to the sight of another woman fighting back, and she may also feel the unfairness of it: why can she, when I can’t? Leave this moment out to protect the heroine’s goodness, and the support women can give one another starts to look too easy. Having been hurt doesn’t teach you, all by itself, how to stop hurting others. Envy may come out first as resentment. Sofia confronts Celie, bringing into the open what had gone unspoken between them. She doesn’t have to put up with the hurt to make allowances for Celie, and she doesn’t have to write Celie off as an enemy forever. Any real support between them begins with being able to say, “What I just did hurt you.” Similar experiences don’t guarantee they already understand each other.

Later, Sofia is subjected to violence and imprisoned for standing up to white authority. Being able to say no at home does not make her safe outside it. The musical is unsparing about the distance between a person’s strength and the world she has to live in: however brave Sofia is, she cannot decide by force of will whether others will use their power to punish her. See her only as someone Celie needs to learn from, and the days she actually lost disappear from the story once again.

## Shug doesn’t arrive with the answers

At first, the singer Shug Avery seems to belong to another world. People in town talk about her, Mister is infatuated with her, and her name suggests a life onstage and on the move, beyond the reach of everyday rules. When she finally turns up, she is ill and tired and needs someone to look after her.

Celie takes care of Shug, and a relationship slowly grows. This is why Shug is more than a liberator who appears out of nowhere. She can look at Celie in a way no one has before; Celie already knows how to care for someone, pay attention to them, and grow fond of them. Neither is an empty vessel waiting for the other to give her a life. In “Too Beautiful for Words,” a woman who has repeatedly been put down gets to see her body through different eyes. Until now, much of what Celie has known of her body has been work, pregnancy, violation, and endurance. Now someone finds her lovable and helps her feel that she can want someone, too. Her desire doesn’t have to appear only as a consequence of somebody else’s.

“What About Love?” makes room for love between two women rather than a general feeling of sisterhood. Adaptations have given the relationship different weight. When we talk about the stage work, we shouldn’t strip the love of sex and desire and call it encouragement just to make the story easier for everyone to accept. Part of finding a life of her own is finding out whom she loves.

Even so, someone else’s praise is not the last word. If Celie can only stand up to Mister calling her ugly because Shug tells her she’s beautiful, her sense of herself is still in somebody else’s hands. For now, those hands are gentler. Being loved can help someone begin to change. It doesn’t make the person who loves her responsible for keeping her whole sense of worth intact.

## Hiding the letters takes away more than news

When Shug finds Nettie’s letters, Celie can finally look back on their long separation differently. Nettie hadn’t given up on her. It wasn’t that Celie wasn’t worth remembering. All the explanations she had gone over in her loneliness suddenly have to be reconsidered.

Knowing the truth doesn’t give Celie back the years she lost. Nettie kept writing, but Celie didn’t receive the letters when she needed them. The letters can bring her the news she missed. They cannot undo the evenings she struggled through alone. Mister did more than tell a lie. He put himself in charge of a bond between two sisters that was never his to govern.

When the letters come to light, Celie starts to get angry. It isn’t that she has finally woken up from some unreasonable misery. At last she has a particular act to point to. Before, she might have asked only why God had arranged things this way for her. Now she can ask: what gave you the right to do this to me? Once she can say who is responsible, what happened to her need no longer feel like something she simply had to endure without appeal.

Nettie’s letters open a window into a world far away. Nettie has seen things and had experiences of her own; she doesn’t exist merely as the person Celie is waiting for. The stage has to compress years of correspondence. The audience needn’t reduce Nettie herself to a piece of good news that finally made it through. The sisters can reconnect because they have each kept living a life of their own, and neither has thought of the other as someone who belongs only to the past.

Later, at the dinner table, Celie announces she is leaving. There is force in how she says it, and there are circumstances that have been falling into place for some time. Someone is willing to go with her. She has somewhere to stay for a while. What she has learned gives her reason to believe she need not stay here forever. If we take only the brave refusal out of that scene, it’s easy to mistake people who still cannot leave for people who haven’t learned to love themselves. Leaving takes resolve. It also takes a way out that is actually open now.

## Why sewing pants is different from doing the housework

While staying with Shug in Memphis, Celie finds she has a knack for making pants. After inheriting the house where she lived as a child, she turns that skill into a business. “Miss Celie’s Pants” brings work back into the story, but this time it isn’t the endless list of things to be done in Mister’s house, jobs no one ever needs to explain to her. Celie worked just as hard before. She didn’t learn to be useful by leaving. What’s different is that now she can decide what to make, how to organize the work, and how to use what she earns to support herself. The skill itself hasn’t changed; her relationship to her work and to what she owns has. Praise her only for finally making good with her own two hands, and you miss that she always had those hands. She just had little chance to use them for herself.

The inheritance deserves mention too. Having a home gives her somewhere to start from when she sets up her business. These are practical supports for independence. The story doesn’t prove that anyone who has been oppressed can get out once they see things clearly. Celie has talent, but she also gets help and has resources to draw on. Acknowledging that doesn’t diminish her efforts. It keeps “becoming yourself” from turning into one more way to blame people who haven’t managed to get out.

Pants change how a body can move, too. In a story where a woman’s body is so often used to bear children, do work, and meet other people’s demands, clothing can be comfortable and made the way she wants it. That is a pleasure that need not wait for a great victory to matter. Her days begin to have room for choosing a fabric, thinking up a cut, or making something for a friend. Such small things matter as much as the moment when she sings “I’m Here.” We needn’t wait for a scene that brings an audience to its feet to recognize that a woman has got her life back. Before the loudest few minutes onstage come many ordinary days that draw much less attention. Those are also the days people actually have to live when they leave the theater.

## When someone she loves leaves again, Celie doesn’t have to disappear

Later, Shug falls in love with someone else, and Celie faces loss again. If all the story wanted was to replace a bad man with a good woman, we probably wouldn’t have this moment. Instead we see that love which once made someone freer can change, too. It can’t promise that nobody’s heart will break again. “I’m Here” doesn’t announce that Celie will never need anyone. She has loved, she still misses people, and she remembers her sister and her children. What she is beginning to understand is that the end of a relationship doesn’t cancel out her or the days she has left to live. She can grieve and still have a home, a job, a body, and things she wants to do next. None of those has to wait for someone to come back before it matters again.

Speaking about the adaptation, Marsha Norman has described Celie’s movement toward freedom as the heart of the story. That helps explain why the musical doesn’t stop at suffering. It also points to a risk: if a production is too eager to get to the celebration, a song can make years of harm seem too easily put behind them. The critic Andrew C. McGibbon questioned whether Sofia seemed too fully recovered in the 2015 production. That is worth keeping in mind. We can celebrate that she goes on living without assuming the hurt has stopped.

Mister’s later repentance deserves to be seen on the same terms. It’s worth asking whether he can stop doing harm and try to make amends. Celie becoming his wife again shouldn’t be the proof that his change has finally been accepted. One person can change, and the other can still decide not to entrust the rest of her life to that relationship again.

The Color Purple doesn’t leave us wanting to believe that suffering forges us into better people, or that love will always come if we just endure long enough. None of that suffering had to happen. What is worth holding on to is that it’s no longer all Celie has. She can make clothes, love someone, think of her sister, or decide, on an ordinary day, how she’s going to take care of herself.
