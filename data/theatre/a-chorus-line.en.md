# A Chorus Line: What I Want Is Not to Become a Star

*The stage work, not the 1985 film. Conceived by Michael Bennett, with music by Marvin Hamlisch, lyrics by Edward Kleban, and book by James Kirkwood and Nicholas Dante. Personal disclosures and an injury are discussed; the successful applicants are not named.*

Cassie auditions for the chorus. Director Zach thinks she should not.

They have worked together and been lovers. Knowing her ability, he sees returning to an ensemble as defeat. Her answer is simple: she needs work and still wants to dance. A place in a line does not make dancing unworthy.

Backstage stories usually discover someone in a crowd and bring her to center stage. This musical asks whether love of work still matters without stardom, even when someone no longer wishes to pursue it.

Bennett originally directed and choreographed, with co-choreographer Bob Avian. The work moved from the Public Theater to Broadway in 1975. Its audition narrows candidates to eight chorus jobs, four men and four women.

The suspense exceeds who gets hired. After knowing them, can we accept a list’s distinctions as easily as before?

## Each person in the line has come a long way

The audition initially tests competence: learn movements, keep time, avoid mistakes. “I Hope I Get It” places another activity inside the moving body—calculating chances, worrying about age, needing another job.

Zach then asks for lives beyond names and résumés: childhood, motives, ordinarily private matters. Bodies comparable by precision acquire different histories.

“At the Ballet” gives Sheila, Bebe and Maggie experiences of growing up that made ballet a more hospitable place than home. It was not only a later skill for earning money; it opened another world to a child.

Art need not compensate for every injury. A particular class or achievable movement could interrupt others’ evaluations. A body called awkward, ugly or surplus elsewhere could do something powerful here.

Rejection therefore threatens more than wages. It touches a longstanding way of knowing oneself. The director need not hire everyone, but judging a skill may be heard as judging an entire person. Upright posture conceals anxiety.

Original performer Baayork Lee describes the audience seeing dance, learning names, then hearing stories and hoping for favorites. We are not naturally impartial: like Zach, we choose whose story moves us, who is appealing, who deserves a place.

## Disclosure can be opportunity and price

The stories move us, but their listener controls employment. How much can applicants refuse? Can they be sure silence will not count as noncooperation?

This is not voluntary disclosure among friends. Zach may sincerely care, and telling may bring understanding, without making power equal. Someone needing work can hear “show me the real you” as a demand for more than professional ability.

Paul’s monologue makes this especially clear. Sexuality, performing and memories of parents cannot become convenient personality scores. We hear a life through shame, fear and the wish for acceptance, not merely an applicant’s fit.

Respecting that hearing does not validate private disclosure as humane recruitment. Workplace coldness should not be repaired by requiring everyone’s most painful history. Someone refusing family or sexuality questions deserves equally serious professional attention.

The musical itself developed through dancers’ experiences. Lee joined early workshops, and Connie draws on her life. That brings formerly background performers forward, while remaining shaped theatre rather than unprocessed testimony in every line.

Precisely because listening is powerful, respect not purchased through disclosure matters. Stories may deepen recognition, not become its precondition. Otherwise articulate and willing people gain opportunities while the silent remain outside the light.

## Praise can close a road too

Cassie faces the opposite rejection: being too good to return. Praise prescribes one-way upward mobility. A former leading performer must remain one; someone thought destined for fame must validate the prediction. Present needs and wishes disappear behind it.

“The Music and the Mirror” lets her wish occur in dance rather than adding another résumé credential. She is not asking belief in eventual promotion. She asks for this work, now, to be allowed a place at the center of her life.

Zach also has professional grounds. A chorus needs coordination, and conspicuous performance may spoil the design. Recognizing her wish does not exempt her from the job; she must learn to join the group too.

“Your current performance does not fit this choreography” can be discussed and adjusted. “Someone like you should not want this job” dictates a life. She must answer the first and can reject the second.

Ensemble work does not inherently erase people. Someone can love placing movement inside a shared rhythm. If only solos, billing and applause realize a self, apparently progressive ambition marginalizes people who genuinely want collaborative work.

## What if the body cannot continue?

Paul’s injury changes the scale from competition to vulnerability. What if dancing stops? Being hired today cannot answer that. “I want nothing else” sounds passionate amid opportunities and frightening when bodily capacity ends. Work matters beyond income, so losing it can threaten a way of being oneself.

“What I Did for Love” grows here. In the stage work Diana leads it about dedication to dance, not primarily Cassie and Zach’s romance. The position gives it both farewell and refusal to repudiate the past.

Practice, acquired ability and moments completed together do not require an acceptance letter to validate them. This does not ease unemployment or injury; it prevents an outcome consuming the whole experience retrospectively.

Nor does love absolve an industry. Unstable pay, bodily wear and inadequate protection cannot all become necessary dream costs because performers are willing. Willingness to give does not license unlimited extraction. Admiration for endurance can become an attractive excuse for harshness.

A respectful environment must enable serious dancing and life after dancing ends. Past glory cannot replace future conditions. A person’s life is not completed at the boundary of professional capacity.

## Looking at the coordinated line again

When “One” brings matching clothes and formation, these are no longer unfamiliar bodies. The work has let people speak separately before returning them to shared movement.

Uniformity can suggest industry pressure and the pleasure of accomplished work. Coordination is not a crime or beautiful ensemble dancing a fraud. Skill and cooperation are real. The question is whether admiration remembers who makes the whole.

Casting remains: limited jobs and artistic selection cannot disappear. Its authority can be bounded. A director decides fit for this production, not whose wish is ridiculous, whose past useless or whose future unworthy of attention.

Cassie’s return need not mean accepting insignificance. She knows where she wants to spend time now. The next time we see matching dancers, we might remember that doing the same movements does not mean living the same life.
