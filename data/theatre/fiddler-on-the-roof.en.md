# Fiddler on the Roof: How Can a Father Love a Daughter Who No Longer Obeys?

*Book by Joseph Stein; music by Jerry Bock; lyrics by Sheldon Harnick, after Sholem Aleichem. First produced in 1964, originally directed and choreographed by Jerome Robbins. The three marriages and threats to the village are discussed, not everyone’s final destination.*

Tevye has arranged his eldest daughter’s marriage to Lazar Wolf, a prosperous butcher. The poor milkman believes he has finally secured something good: she will not struggle to live.

Tzeitel refuses. She loves Motel, a poor, timid tailor who can scarcely speak to his prospective father-in-law. They have already promised themselves to one another.

Modern audiences readily support her. The musical nevertheless lets us insist on her choice while hearing her father out. His concern for livelihood and his love are real. Does wanting her good entitle him to decide her life?

Early-twentieth-century Anatevka, a Jewish village under Russian rule, is home to Tevye, Golde and their five daughters. Three marriages repeatedly change what he thought unchangeable.

This is more than a lesson in enlightened parenting. The world that supports him is itself threatened. How can he face a loved person’s changes while struggling to keep that world?

## More than authority is at stake

“Tradition” gives fathers, mothers, sons and daughters positions, duties and permissible futures. Restrictive to us, it first means Tevye knows how life can continue.

Poverty is not his only identity. Faith permits arguments with God; customs organize the Sabbath; neighbors place individual life in a familiar world. Outside society may reject them, but here a meal, wedding or blessing has recognizable meaning.

Reducing tradition to backward opinion misses why a daughter’s resistance feels larger than lost authority. If this rule can change today, what can be relied on tomorrow?

Anxiety cannot require her obedience. Stability must be considered from the person paying for it too. The same arrangement that lets a father sleep may remove a daughter’s hope.

Tzeitel offers no grand theory against family. She says her own life partner cannot be chosen around her absence.

Tevye agrees, then invents a frightening dream to persuade Golde. The comedy retains his awkwardness: he changes without admitting why. The wish must wear an old world’s recognizable clothes.

Change is incomplete. An exception for this child does not yet become every child’s right. Welcome flexibility cannot replace a choice genuinely belonging to her.

## From permission to blessing

Hodel and the student Perchik ask not permission to marry but a blessing.

Permission gives the father a veto. Blessing acknowledges a decision already made while inviting him into its happiness. She does not call him unimportant; she offers him another place.

Parents may hear that as demotion: once needed to decide, now wanted only to approve. Tevye must learn that losing final authority does not mean becoming unnecessary.

When Perchik is imprisoned for political activity, Hodel chooses Siberia. “Far from the Home I Love” gives the choice real cost. Freedom removes neither attachment nor difficulty; she accepts a path as her own.

She has not triumphed over family. Leaving hurts because she loves it. A free choice need not prove itself by severing ties; that would merely replace one mandatory standard with another.

The daughters’ questions also reach Tevye’s marriage. He asks Golde, “Do You Love Me?” After many shared years, the words seem borrowed from their children’s world.

For Golde, years of work and care already answer. The song neither mocks her nor vindicates arranged marriage because affection grew within one. Feelings can develop in given circumstances while children retain the right to refuse those circumstances.

Children do not simply take something from parents. Their questions can reopen half-lived lives. Tradition changes through asking a wife how she feels, not only through outside destruction.

## With the third daughter, he stops

Chava loves Fyedka, who is not Jewish. Earlier challenges remained within Tevye’s religious community. This crossing seems intolerable. He refuses to listen, and after the marriage refuses to acknowledge her.

Two apparent lessons in openness have not produced an endlessly adaptable father. Identity, history and continuity enter the marriage with him.

For an excluded community, crossing a boundary is not merely exchanging lifestyles. He fears the next generation leaving the world he sustains. Ignoring that makes both him and minority self-preservation thin caricatures.

But Chava cannot become an instrument for preserving the community. Her entire marriage is not proof owed to her origins. Cultural continuity must accommodate members who live differently; affection cannot demand that they surrender decisive choices to guarantee transmission.

Describing only a father sacrificing for faith makes all the tragedy his, with the rejected daughter incidental. She still wants his response. Loving someone has not deleted her past; he is the one declaring that loving Fyedka and remaining his daughter cannot coexist.

“Little Bird” carries paternal grief without erasing injury. Love and harm can share a person. The musical keeps both onstage rather than allowing either to cancel the other.

## Violence at a wedding is not another generation gap

Tzeitel’s wedding is disrupted, and the Jewish community later faces expulsion. Those debating marriages lose even authority over whether they can stay home.

Placing these changes together can make daughters’ departures and exile seem one historical current. They must be distinguished. A daughter chooses her life; authorities deprive residents of that same capacity. “Times change” cannot absolve violence.

Threat makes tradition’s other side visible. Alongside power needing revision, it carries memories people can retain through persecution: food, weddings, arguments with God. Questioning one rule does not destroy all that value.

“Sunrise, Sunset” can move us with parents watching children grow. Violence afterwards requires more than wistfulness. Growth is nobody’s crime; persecution has perpetrators. Both losses may be moving without requiring the same response.

Balancing like a fiddler on a roof does not mean staying still guarantees safety. Telling someone casually to jump is not help either. Tevye must find relationship after the past ceases to decide everything, rather than simply discard the past.

A daughter can carry home’s lessons and decline to transmit some rules. A father’s blessing can come to address not perfect continuity but someone loved whose life he cannot live. The work makes this difficult. Having watched Tevye stop and refuse, we can understand what even another small step towards her would weigh.
