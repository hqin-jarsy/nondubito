# Billy Elliot: What If He Never Became a Star?

*Billy Elliot the Musical, with music by Elton John and book and lyrics by Lee Hall, was adapted from the 2000 film. It premiered in London in 2005 and opened on Broadway in 2008. The story takes place during the British miners’ strike of 1984–85. This essay discusses the stage musical and does not treat scenes from the film as stage details. It touches on Billy’s father’s change of heart, the raising of money for the audition, and conditions in the mining community, without giving away exactly how the admissions decision or the farewells unfold.*

When Billy’s father finds out that his son is doing ballet, he doesn’t think he’s discovered a hidden talent. His first thought is that the boy has gone somewhere he doesn’t belong.

Billy had gone to learn boxing. Instead, he stayed for Mrs Wilkinson’s dance class. While his family worries day after day about the strike, their income and the future of the mines, Billy starts learning movements his father doesn’t know what to make of. In the world his father knows, the questions of how a boy should grow up and what he should do with his body had answers long before anyone asked what the boy liked.

It’s easy to see this as a story about following a dream. But if that’s all we see, it’s easy to wait until the boy dances well enough before deciding he ought to be free to dance. We might ask a little sooner: if he never became anything more than someone who liked to dance, would taking that first step into the classroom still be worth somebody’s support?

## They Know What a Body Can Do

The men in the mining community have plenty of reason to know what a body can do. In the mines, in the boxing ring and on the picket line facing the police, they use their bodies to bear hardship and prove themselves. Strength can’t just be something you enjoy feeling. It has to help you protect your family, get through hard work or keep others from looking down on you.

That’s part of why Billy’s father finds it so hard to take dance seriously. He knows what strength is supposed to look like. He isn’t used to hearing from his son that it might take a form he wouldn’t recognize. In worrying about the boy’s future, he narrows it to the handful of paths the past has already approved.

But Billy isn’t setting out from a comfortable life to find the hobby that will most embarrass his family. He’s lost his mother and is looking for somewhere he can go amid the strain at home. Dance offers more than a possible profession far down the road. It gives him time now to practise, to feel what his body can do and, for a little while, to stop taking his cues from the mood at home. In “Dear Billy,” he brings the letter his mother left him into a lesson. His teacher gets to know more than a promising pupil: she gets a glimpse of what it’s like for him to live with that loss. Dancing doesn’t drive the grief away. It lets him carry it with him into something new. Learning unfamiliar steps and remembering his mother needn’t compete for the same child.

His grandmother’s song gives dance a longer history in the family. She remembers the violence in her marriage, and she remembers how much pleasure she took in dancing. Dance, then, isn’t a refinement someone had to bring to the mining town from London. It was already one way of living in this family, even if little of that possibility survived. The loudest men in a place can’t be the only witnesses to its culture.

Mrs Wilkinson sees that Billy has talent and offers him extra lessons. What she can do at first is give him a chance to go on practising. She can’t tell his father that success is a sure thing. Plenty of girls dance in her class, too. The class doesn’t need every one of them to become a professional in order to be worth having, and Billy shouldn’t have to promise future honours in return for a place there either. Stay in that classroom a little longer and the centre of the story shifts. We tend to remember the child who was picked, and not the work of a teacher who shows up day after day to help ordinary children practise. Without that daily work, Billy might never have had a chance to find out what he liked. Waiting for a prodigy and making sure local children have a class to go to are two different hopes for education. The second may produce fewer miracles, but it gives more people room to grow.

## How One Body Moves Among Others

“Solidarity” sets the dance class alongside the confrontation between the miners and the police. The children practise their steps; the adults hold their lines. In both, bodies are learning how to move in concert with other bodies. Instead of casting ballet as a world untouched by violence and the mining town as a world of brute force, the staging brings two ways of life that had seemed far apart suddenly close.

The difference isn’t simply between soft and forceful movements. It lies in what each group asks its members to do together. The miners need solidarity because one person alone can do little in the face of pit closures and unemployment. Dancers, too, need practice and coordination; a moment’s enthusiasm won’t carry them far. If freedom meant never having to accept a constraint, it would be hard to explain why Billy keeps coming back to class.

What he wants is a chance to tell which demands help him grow and which are only there to keep him looking like the boy others expect. He can accept the discipline of dance because it has something to do with what he’s discovering he wants. It isn’t that hard work earns him the moral right to be free.

He has something similar to learn with Michael. His friend tries on girls’ clothes and invites him to play along. In “Expressing Yourself,” a change of clothes doesn’t immediately call for a verdict. Liking ballet doesn’t determine whom you’re attracted to, and liking certain clothes doesn’t have to be explained at once as a fixed identity.

If all we say in Billy’s defence is that ballet can be masculine, we risk leaving Michael behind. It would mean that you deserve tolerance only if you can prove you haven’t strayed from the old standard. What’s more precious about this friendship is that the boys get to be friends first and find out about each other as they go. Neither has to make a point of being different from the other to stay safe.

Michael has feelings of his own, too. Billy needn’t feel the same way, and he needn’t make his friend pay for speaking up by humiliating him. The boys don’t have ready-made, grown-up answers, but after the fun and the embarrassment they can still leave each other room to be friends.

## When the Adults’ Fight Lands on a Child

Preparations for the first audition don’t go to plan. The conflict in the mining community comes home: Billy’s brother Tony clashes with their father, and Mrs Wilkinson is drawn into it. Tony even demands that Billy dance in front of everyone. By then, it’s no longer a matter of finding out what the boy likes. He’s being asked to use his body to show everyone whose side he’s on.

“Angry Dance” gives Billy’s anger a way out that doesn’t require him to make a case for himself. He hasn’t found words that will persuade his father, but we can already see how much force has been bottled up in his body. That force doesn’t establish in advance that he’s good enough to dance professionally. Even if we don’t know how good he is yet, we can see a child’s wish being crowded out by the grown-ups’ fight.

It won’t do just to call Billy’s father ignorant. The strike drags on, money is tight, and he has nowhere much to put his grief for his wife. In “Deep into the Ground,” the man who expects everyone to stay strong can’t always hold himself together. Billy needs freedom; his father needs someone to see that the ways he knows of coping aren’t enough for what’s happening. But the father’s hardship doesn’t make it the child’s job to keep the family as it was. Billy can go on missing his mother and find new things he loves. He doesn’t have to give his father a son who never changes. What his father needs to learn isn’t how to become an authority on the arts overnight. It’s how to listen to something his son wants even when he doesn’t understand it.

Only after seeing Billy dance again does his father begin to take another path seriously. It’s a moving moment, and it raises a question about where the bar is set. Is he finally willing to get to know his son, or is Billy so good that he’s willing to make an exception? Both can be happening at once, but they lead to different places. If only prodigies can escape the old rules, those rules still govern everyone else. An appreciation of Billy’s ability can get his father started. Going further means admitting that a way of life he doesn’t like might belong to an entirely ordinary child, too.

## Where Does the Fare Come From?

Once Billy’s father decides to back the audition, he has to think about money. Going to London, getting training and leaving the life he knows all take resources his family can scarcely spare. No amount of talk about following a dream pays the fare. His father goes as far as crossing the picket line, intending to earn money back down the mine. To him, it’s a way to give his son a chance. To Tony, it weakens the stand they’ve all been taking together. Both have reasons that come from caring about the people close to them. It isn’t that one of them loves and the other doesn’t.

That doesn’t make a family’s interests naturally more important than a common cause, any more than a common cause gets to claim a child as its own. The cruel thing is how little other support there is. For a child to audition at an arts school, his father finds himself facing a choice between his family and his fellow miners that comes close to betrayal.

The miners start to give what they can, and what had looked like one boy’s way out gradually becomes a path a whole group helps keep open. There’s something worth noticing in that change. Their solidarity is no longer only a demand that the next generation stay in line. It begins to make room for one of their own to do something they haven’t done themselves.

Some of the money also comes from a source Tony finds hard to accept. The musical doesn’t make the fundraising look wholly untroubled, as if the money would take care of itself once everyone thought things through. A real opportunity can depend on complicated sources of help and on compromises they have to talk through. Those difficulties deserve to be named; they don’t all disappear when a child sets out for London.

When Lee Hall discusses the stage adaptation, he singles out how much more room it gives the chorus and the voices of the community than the film does. That choice keeps Billy’s story from becoming one of escape from a backward mining town, as if the means to leave had come from nowhere. Part of what lets him go elsewhere is that people here find ways to give him training, money for the journey and their trust. He doesn’t have to look down on where he comes from in order to leave.

## An Audition Can’t Judge the Whole Person

At the Royal Ballet School, Billy still has to learn that wanting something doesn’t excuse how he treats other people. Nervous and frustrated, he hits another child who tries to comfort him. The audience may be on his side, but that doesn’t make it a charming little outburst.

He has to face the school’s professional standards and acknowledge the hurt he caused. Neither requires him to let the school’s decision sum him up. In “Electricity,” he tells us what dancing feels like. For a moment, the question moves away from whether he measures up and back to what dancing means to him. That feeling is hard to put into words, but the fact that he keeps wanting to return to it tells him something about himself. He needs training to see how that feeling might develop, and he needs the chance to find out how far his abilities can take him. The admissions decision will affect what he can do next. It can’t reach back and decide whether the experience was ever worth having.

Meanwhile, a good dance from Billy won’t solve the mining community’s problems. We can be glad for one child’s prospects and still worry about the way of life so many families are losing. If we let one person’s success make up for a loss shared by the whole community, we make those who stayed disappear a second time, as if their only part had been to supply the hardship an uplifting story needs.

There ought to be room for two things in Billy’s story. He should be able to go without having to stay put to prove he’s grateful. And the people who stay should still be able to make a life there, without one person’s success being taken to settle the account for everyone. Neither wish is in the other’s way. Both need more than a round of applause.

The most moving help in Billy Elliot may have little to do with spotting a future star. It might be a father who lets his child finish speaking, a teacher who gives him another try or a group of people whose own money is tight but who find a way to scrape together the fare. They don’t have to know what Billy will become to give the child in front of them a little more room to become himself.
