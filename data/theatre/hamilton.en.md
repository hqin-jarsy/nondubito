# Hamilton: Why Does a Wedding Have to Happen Twice?

*Hamilton, with book, music, and lyrics by Lin-Manuel Miranda, opened on Broadway in 2015. This essay discusses some relationships and marital turning points, but leaves the finale aside.*

There is a wedding in Hamilton that the audience has to go back and see again.

In “Helpless,” Eliza tells the story of falling in love with Hamilton. Her sister Angelica helps bring them together, courtship leads to marriage, and everything seems happily settled. Then Angelica raises a glass to the couple, and “Satisfied” takes us back through what has just happened. Only now do we learn that, before helping her sister find happiness, she too had been drawn to this young man.

Director Thomas Kail and choreographer Andy Blankenbuehler do more than give Angelica an extra song about her feelings. In the original staging, the ensemble also rewinds, taking the audience back to the earlier encounter. The people and the scene are the same, but with someone else telling the story, an apparently straightforward romance acquires a feeling that has nowhere to go. The younger sister’s happiness does not become false, and the older sister’s blessing need not be insincere. Yet something that the wedding has settled remains unsettled within one person.

This is a useful way into Hamilton. It is, of course, a musical about an American founder: a young man who comes from the Caribbean to North America, joins the War of Independence, assists Washington, and eventually becomes the country’s first Treasury secretary. Inspired by Ron Chernow’s biography, Miranda turns that life into rap, R&B, and musical-theatre songs. But if we hurry along with the protagonist’s achievements, we miss a good deal. Other people may appear in his story without living the same life he does.

We should also remember that this romantic triangle is a dramatic reconstruction. The historical Angelica married in 1777, before her sister married Hamilton. The regret she sings about cannot simply be treated as testimony to the historical woman’s inner life. What it reveals first is how this work tells a story: when one person’s success seems enough to sum up an entire scene, music can pause the progress and let someone else speak again.

## Why Is He in Such a Hurry?

To hear the others, though, we first need to understand why Hamilton is so compelling. If we begin by reducing him to a careerist who neglects his family, the later conflict becomes too easy, with little left to discover.

The musical’s Hamilton has no distinguished family position to rely on. He grows up amid the loss of loved ones, and arrives in New York with little to offer beyond intelligence, language, and energy. The opening songs quickly bring him into a group of young revolutionaries. In “My Shot,” he wants both to seize his opportunity and to show that he has something to contribute to the great events unfolding around him. For these young people, revolution is a shared cause, but also a future that has not yet taken shape.

Rap has a specific role here. Someone without standing cannot invoke a title and expect to be heard; once he starts speaking, he must make people realize that his words cannot easily be dismissed. The density of expression presses his thinking, urgency, and confidence together. It also makes it easy for an audience to catch his excitement before weighing the costs of such a life. To extract a lesson about the importance of youthful effort would be to lose the experience of a person struggling to be heard.

In a Library of Congress interview, Miranda described one way he found his way into Hamilton: by noticing how this man changed his circumstances through writing. That observation also helps explain the character’s actions. Writing is much more than an expression of feeling. An essay can attract attention, an argument can win support for a political position, and a letter can establish a relationship. Language keeps opening doors for him, strengthening his belief that, so long as he keeps writing, he can still change the situation.

There is something worth preserving in that belief. A person refuses the limits assigned by his origins, develops his abilities, and undertakes work that no one has yet invited him to do. His later mistakes do not entitle us to reinterpret all of this as pathological ambition. Hamilton genuinely wants to make things happen and has the ability to do so. Unless we understand that creative drive, criticism can become the advice of someone comfortably established, telling a person still trying to find his footing to be content a little sooner.

But the question gradually changes. At first, he asks others to give him an opportunity. Later, more and more people and concerns must wait until he finishes the work in front of him. One task is completed, and the next immediately becomes urgent. We can then ask which commitments he truly values and is willing to take responsibility for, and which are driven by an inability to face himself if he stops. The two motives may be hard to separate. That does not mean we should stop trying to distinguish them.

## Those Who Wait Have a Time of Their Own

Aaron Burr, standing beside Hamilton, often seems elusive. He too is ambitious, but reluctant to declare a position as quickly as Hamilton does. As the protagonist presses ahead, it is easy to see Burr simply as his opposite: one acts while the other hesitates; one has convictions while the other calculates.

“Wait for It” unsettles that judgment. Burr speaks here about losing people close to him, and about a life he has not yet attained but still wants to wait for. Someone who has been helping tell another man’s story gets a moment to describe his own circumstances. In NPR’s interview with Leslie Odom Jr., the original Broadway Burr, the song is described as moving away from rap toward R&B and gospel expression. That shift matters: Burr is no longer merely responding to Hamilton. For the duration of a song, we have to listen to how he understands himself.

If someone has seen important things abruptly taken away, a reluctance to act rashly is understandable. Hamilton’s pace can be his own without becoming the measure of everyone else. Burr is entitled to move more slowly and to keep some thoughts to himself. He need not imitate another person’s courage to establish his worth.

Yet understanding that reserve does not spare Burr further questions. After the war, the new country still has to decide how its government will work. Near the end of the first act, Hamilton invites him to join the writing campaign in support of the Constitution, and he remains unwilling to take a clear side. Waiting now bears on a shared undertaking. What am I still trying to judge? What reasons would be sufficient for a decision? If I always wait until the likely winner is clear before committing myself, then a choice that seems to remain in my hands may in fact be governed by the outcome outside them.

The difference between the two men is therefore more than speed. Hamilton may be propelled by the next chance to prove himself; Burr may be held in place by his unwillingness to lose any possible opportunity. Both try to command their lives, and both need to discern what they want and what they are willing to relinquish for it. The musical offers no comfortable middle position from which we can judge them without difficulty. It gives each a persuasive voice and then puts those voices to the test of action.

## What She Wants Cannot Always Wait

Back at the wedding, another person is easy to read too quickly: Eliza, who has married Hamilton. Angelica’s regret has an obvious dramatic force. By comparison, Eliza’s hopes for a shared life may seem ordinary. But ordinary hopes can still express a judgment of one’s own.

When Hamilton returns home from military service and learns that his wife is pregnant, “That Would Be Enough” briefly gives desire onstage a different direction. While he is eager for distinction, she wants him to recognize that the life they already share deserves time together. He need not arrive with good news or an honor to be welcome. In contrast to songs about securing an opportunity or persuading an opponent, this is another kind of request: stop proving yourself to the world for a moment, and respond to the person in front of you.

This does not make Eliza’s hopes inherently nobler than his work. Loving him does not authorize her to decide that he must leave politics forever. Equally, the national importance of his work does not authorize him to defer her expectations indefinitely. The life they want together requires actual discussion. If every conflict ends with his concerns declared more urgent, she remains his partner in name while having to fit her own life into whatever his schedule leaves over.

Giving her a song of her own allows the audience to reconsider this hierarchy of importance. For Hamilton, a homecoming or a break from work may be an interval in a public career. For her, it is the time of their shared life. Only by hearing her out can we notice that the small matters supposedly delaying great achievements may constitute another person’s life.

The question becomes sharper when the marriage encounters betrayal. To counter allegations of financial corruption, Hamilton publicly discloses his affair, seeking to show that the payments were unrelated to public business. For him, this is a defense that must be put into writing. But the same document exposes his wife’s private life to spectators. Even if it establishes where the money came from, it cannot thereby settle his responsibility toward his family.

“Burn,” which follows, gives the response back to Eliza. Historical accounts of destroyed letters cannot be equated directly with the theatrical scene in which she burns her husband’s letters after the scandal. Within the musical, however, the scene has a clear purpose. Hamilton can always produce another explanation; she refuses to let their intimacy continue serving as material for it. Her anger needs to be heard, not counted merely as one more loss in the protagonist’s downfall.

Eliza’s opposition gives the principle that a person is an end a concrete meaning. Hamilton’s talent, work, and historical contribution do not automatically oblige her to accommodate him. She can decide what to support. What she wants to keep private does not lose its importance simply because others want to know. What will happen to the relationship is a question the work can unfold in its own time. We do not need to prescribe the right ending for her before acknowledging her present refusal.

## The Musical Has Not Spoken for Everyone, Either

Hamilton’s casting offers another source of its appeal. The original production has many actors of color embody founders usually encountered as white portraits in history books, bringing an eighteenth-century story into contemporary voices and bodies. For some audience members and performers, that can change their distance from the past. But the opportunity to tell the story does not mean that every kind of life within that past has been represented.

Writing in The Paper, Li Hansong considers the work’s musical and theatrical inventiveness alongside its remaking of founding heroes, asking how slavery is mentioned without becoming a fully developed lived experience. This takes our earlier question a little further outward. Actors of color can portray the founders brilliantly, yet the enslaved people of the founders’ world do not thereby receive equally substantial stories. We can be moved by the production and still ask about these absences.

This does not require a musical to tell the whole of American history in its limited running time. It reminds us only that liking the protagonist does not entitle us to count those who have not reached the stage as already attended to. When creators give more voices to the people around a hero, we can go on asking whether those voices have sufficient independence, rather than treating their presence as a completed task.

That is also why I think Hamilton is worth seeing for more than the story of a young man finding opportunity and entering history. At certain moments, it interrupts our impulse to follow him and turns us back toward someone else. Song and staging often accomplish these shifts more powerfully than an added moral could.

The wedding is one such moment. The first time, it shows us how a couple comes together. The second, it makes us recognize what the same encounter leaves within someone else. We need not withdraw our good wishes for the newlyweds. But the next time someone tells us a story that ends happily, we may listen a little more closely for what the person standing beside them has not yet said.
