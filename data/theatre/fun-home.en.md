# Fun Home: How Can a Grown Daughter Come to Know Her Father?

*Music by Jeanine Tesori; book and lyrics by Lisa Kron, after Alison Bechdel’s graphic memoir. Premiered at the Public Theater in 2013 and on Broadway in 2015. Family secrets, sexuality and the father’s death are discussed; the death is a narrative premise, not a concealed twist. The finale is left aside.*

Visitors are coming. Furniture must be arranged, possessions put away, and children’s traces removed from their father’s lovingly restored house. Helen directs her three children through one task after another. The home should look beautiful; keeping it beautiful requires everyone’s careful cooperation.

Bruce is genuinely gifted with antiques and restoration. Yet the instructions raise a question: once the house satisfies him, can its inhabitants relax? Order ordinarily serves comfortable living. Here the family must first ensure they do not disturb perfection.

An adult daughter is also onstage. Alison, a cartoonist, is assembling memories of her father. Three performers portray her as a child, at nineteen and at forty-three, not handing the role along chronologically but sharing the stage. One is living through childhood, another discovering love, while the adult tries to understand what has happened.

“Fun Home” is the family’s affectionate abbreviation for its funeral business. The children really play and invent a lively commercial. Joy is not merely irony. Calling every happy moment a façade in an unhappy family would close interpretation earlier than the musical does.

## A woman passes by

In “Ring of Keys,” young Alison notices a delivery woman in a diner. Hair, clothes and keys produce an intense recognition she cannot yet fully explain.

The stranger need not instruct her. Simply working as herself makes another adulthood visible: Alison might not have to become the girl others prescribe. The song gives recognition time rather than letting an adult immediately announce its meaning.

In an NPR conversation with Bechdel and Tesori, Kron described worrying that audiences accustomed to treating this kind of woman as a joke would laugh. The artistic task was to suspend mockery and hear why a child admired her.

The song does more than declare sexuality. It changes who can judge what Alison sees. Her father or convention may call something unsuitable; she already has feelings of her own, even without complete words. Music lets us look with her before correcting her.

Self-knowledge is often imagined as inward work. Here one person sees another. The delivery woman neither legislates Alison’s future nor demands imitation. Her presence makes a possibility an actual life rather than a taboo or joke.

Such opportunities need not be formal lessons. Working openly or wearing comfortable clothes may leave someone else room for self-recognition. That does not oblige every unusual person to become an exemplary representative: the woman has her own day to get through. Being oneself can nonetheless have effects beyond one’s knowledge.

At college, nineteen-year-old Alison falls for Joan. “Changing My Major” lets first love fill the present. In her script preface, Kron insists on its joy: later grief must not turn youth into an anticipation of disaster. Alison did not know the future. Neither should later misfortune cancel pleasure already lived. Her openness changes family relationships, but needing adjustment is not a reason she should not live this way. Her life is not merely a footnote to her father’s tragedy.

## The days her mother lived

After coming out, Alison learns more about her father’s secret relationships with men. A new likeness between them makes childhood both closer and harder to understand.

Father and daughter can become a neat contrast: he could not live openly; she can. But Helen is there too. What she knew, endured and managed cannot be absorbed into that comparison. As the daughter rediscovers her father, her mother must become knowable in her own right.

“Days and Days” gives duration to Helen’s marriage. Tasks and responses previously scattered through the story accumulate. No single day accounts for the whole loss, yet the years have passed in this way.

Maintaining a family can mean mutual support or one person continually postponing herself. Outsiders see a beautiful house and an enduring marriage, not necessarily whose time sustains them or whose wishes are omitted.

Social pressure on Bruce deserves understanding, but Helen is not a refuge prepared for his difficulties. Her life cannot be reduced to the conditions of his survival. To grieve only his inability to become himself while treating her lost years as inevitable would repeat the family’s hierarchy.

His secrets also extend beyond sexuality. Relationships with underage boys raise questions of adult abuse, not merely marital fidelity. Recognizing that gay people should not be oppressed does not conflict with confronting harm to minors. Calling all these relationships forbidden love would erase younger, less powerful people, even if the musical gives them less space.

Nor should hindsight ask why Helen did not leave sooner. The conditions needed to leave cannot be supplied by a spectator’s demand for clarity. Staying did not endorse every arrangement; describing loss does not require proving every past decision correct. Hearing her first helps us learn what staying meant.

Acceptance of sexual difference is therefore necessary, not a universal solution. How Bruce treated his wife, children and others remains a separate question. Outside oppression does not make harm inside the home unreal. Holding both prevents sympathy for one person from requiring another’s silence.

## The person who knows the ending

Adult Alison tries to place irreconcilable things in one account. Her father was talented and attractive to her, and frightening. They shared something but followed different paths. Any summary catches part and misses part. She is still working out how to draw and write these people, not merely delivering a finished verdict.

“Telephone Wire” returns to a car journey. Adult Alison enters a younger self’s memory, placing the body participating in the conversation beside the consciousness looking back. In an American Theatre interview, the creators describe how knowing this meeting cannot recur gives its hesitations another weight.

We witness both the unsaid words and the later urgency to say them. Learning how to ask or listen years afterwards cannot send that ability back. Memory can rearrange a scene, not give its people another chance.

Regret may also make the adult unfair to her younger self. Nineteen-year-old Alison did not possess her father’s whole mind and was not responsible for predicting his actions. An imperfect conversation is not the cause of a later death; grief cannot simply erase that distance.

Bechdel has described conjecture and guilt concerning her father’s death. The musical has its own dramatic treatment, but sequence is not established causation. The daughter’s coming out must not be called the cause. Kron likewise resists making every earlier scene foreshadow the ending. These people were living forward, not collecting evidence for later explanations.

The irreducible remainder has a precise demand here. Not fully understanding her father does not mean Alison failed. Uncertainty about his interior life does not suspend judgment of known harm. Some actions can be described while some motives remain unknowable. Limits of knowledge and responsibility must be distinguished.

Writing may bring reconciliation, but forgiveness or closure need not be conditions of its value. Distinguishing knowledge from uncertainty, and acknowledging that pleasure did not cancel fear or fear erase closeness, can already free someone from a single final judgment.

Several ages sharing a stage embody this understanding. Childhood need not disappear into adult interpretation; first love need not surrender its joy to later sadness. The cartoonist keeps working. She may revise the words beside a drawing without first finding a sentence that ends every question.
