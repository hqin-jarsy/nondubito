# Into the Woods: The Story Continues After the Wish Comes True

*Music and lyrics by Stephen Sondheim; book by James Lapine. Broadway, 1987. This essay concerns the complete two-act stage work, including the second act’s premise, not the 2014 film or an abridgment. Final outcomes are left aside.*

A baker and his wife want a child. To break a witch’s curse, they enter the woods seeking several objects, including a white cow. Jack happens to pass with one. They offer beans, telling the boy they are magic.

The exchange will change many lives. For now it is a small practical problem: how can they get what they need? The baker dislikes deceiving a child; his wife sees a more convenient interpretation. Besides, their wish is ordinary and sympathetic. Who could object to wanting a child?

Into the Woods begins with such defensible wishes. Sondheim and Lapine place Cinderella, Little Red Riding Hood, Jack and Rapunzel in the same forest, with the baker’s household moving among them. Paths once leading separately to happy endings now intersect: a shortcut to your happiness may cross someone else’s life.

The result is funny and draws on familiar tales. Its strongest move comes afterwards. Everyone gets what they want, but the curtain does not end the story. There is a second act.

## Someone else lives by that

The cow means different things to the two sides. To the couple it is an item needed to lift a curse; to Jack it is a loved companion. Once incorporated into their plan, the child’s shared life with it becomes incidental.

The couple do not suddenly become monsters. They remain appealing, anxious and understandable. That is why their bargain resembles ordinary lapses: we need not dislike people to exploit them. Our urgency can make their loss look like a minor inconvenience.

Jack’s cow is more than a fable’s prop. A quick exchange cuts across a much longer attachment. Keeping both timescales prevents the couple’s urgent wish from disposing of his grief for a companion. Later surprises do not make their failure to listen unimportant.

The beans really are magical, adding irony. The couple did not offer a fair exchange on reliable knowledge: they chose the account most likely to secure agreement while uncertain themselves. Later luck cannot vindicate earlier carelessness. Otherwise a fortunate result can retrospectively authorize risks borne by someone who was never consulted.

Their wish itself need not be condemned. The musical does not require people to extinguish desire. A child, a better livelihood, escape from humiliation: these can begin serious lives. Trouble starts when a desirable end guarantees the means, or past suffering supposedly makes it someone else’s turn to yield.

The witch’s love for Rapunzel has a related difficulty. Fear of the dangerous world leads her to keep her daughter in a tower. The danger is real, but the safety she designs has no place for an adult decision. Protection achieved by cancelling choice becomes hard to distinguish from making a child manage her parent’s fear.

Wishes are therefore not a chart dividing good from evil. Prospective parents can disregard another child; a mother afraid of losing her daughter can deny her room to live. Affection does not itself teach us how to treat others.

## Cinderella has not decided

Cinderella’s hesitation is striking among people who urgently want things. She initially wants to attend the palace festival. Visiting that world and committing her life to a prince are different wishes; familiarity makes audiences supply the second.

“On the Steps of the Palace” gives uncertainty a whole song. What looks like an obvious escape still permits doubt. The baker’s wife cannot understand fleeing such a prospect. But she envies a life seen from outside; Cinderella must decide whether to live it.

Declining a celebrated opportunity is not necessarily ingratitude. Someone may not yet know who she would become inside it. Others can list the palace’s advantages without feeling whether this relationship is right for her.

Nor does hesitation guarantee maturity. Cinderella tries letting circumstances and her pursuer decide, as if not choosing personally would reduce responsibility. The harder a decision is to reverse, the more tempting someone else’s certainty becomes. Yet the life remains hers to live.

She is not a fully enlightened independent woman inserted to criticize fairy tales. She longs, retreats and improvises evasions. She must discover wishes through experience, not memorize a doctrine of freedom. That is more interesting than a command to be herself: decisions often begin before self-understanding is complete.

## Who answers in the second act?

The dead giant’s wife comes looking for Jack. His victory has left a bereavement in another life.

Her grief does not license violence. Bringing her back instead interrupts a narrative convenience: a protagonist’s success need not silence everyone outside the story. Her voice is now too loud to ignore.

Threats to homes and loved ones change the forest’s encounters. Others once helped accomplish tasks; now they look like culprits. In “Your Fault,” everyone finds a genuine causal fragment explaining why responsibility belongs elsewhere.

These reasons are not invariably absurd. No individual controlled everything; much was unforeseeable. But defending innocence crowds out what needs doing now. More knowledge of causes produces less capacity to act together.

Participation in one link cannot make someone responsible for all later consequences. Infinite debt would paralyze action. Yet lack of foresight cannot make later events irrelevant either. What is now known, what can still be prevented, and what needs acknowledgment or repair are harder, more useful questions than identifying the first villain.

Fear also tempts them to hand someone over: remove one person from protection and perhaps the rest survive. This tests the standing of a person as an end. Affirming it in safety costs little; refusing to treat someone as material for appeasing catastrophe costs something.

That does not require passive submission to violence. Protection and resistance need action. It requires refusing to confuse convenience with justification or collective fear with evidence that someone has lost the right to count.

## Adults must keep learning

The familiar divisions no longer work: knowledgeable parents and obedient children, rewarded goodness and punished wickedness, completed tasks and permanent safety. Even getting the desired child is not the same as knowing how to be a father.

Grief and destruction make the baker want to leave. “No More” meets that exhaustion. A simple injunction to accept responsibility would flatten him again, making fatigue a defect and fatherhood a task permitting no failure.

He need not become fearless first. Responsibility for a child does not require repairing the whole world alone. Admitting limits can begin help, consultation and shared burdens. Responsibility includes finding how to carry things together, not merely receiving more weight.

“No One Is Alone” offers consolation without transferring judgment to a group. Parents can be wrong; unanimous answers can be wrong. Reliable companionship does not remove the difficulty of deciding. It stays when a person must decide.

The characters learn more than caution about wishes. They lose the convenience of interpreting the world only through their own desires. Everyone in the forest has somewhere to go and can go astray. Being the protagonist cannot oblige others to clear a path to the ending.

The musical’s adulthood comes not from darkening fairy tales. It still allows children, love and new beginnings. It simply suggests another question after happily ever after: tomorrow morning, how will they treat the people beside them?
