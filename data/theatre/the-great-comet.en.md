# The Great Comet: She Made a Mistake. She Is More Than That.

*This essay takes the Broadway production as its reference. It discusses seduction, the thwarted elopement, and its aftermath without describing the final meeting. The musical includes a scene of self-harm; this essay does not describe how it happens. The great comet will come into this evening from very far away. Before anyone looks up, though, they have to learn how to look at someone close to them who has already made a mistake.*

Sonya discovers that Natasha is planning to run away with a man.

Natasha is already engaged, but not to him. She is waiting for Andrey to come home from far away, and she believes she has found in Anatole a love she cannot resist. Sonya tries to talk her out of it. Natasha won’t listen. The closer people are to her, the more their objections seem to stand between her and the only thing she truly wants.

*Natasha, Pierre & The Great Comet of 1812* turns a small stretch of *War and Peace* into a musical. Dave Malloy wrote the book, music, and lyrics. It premiered at Ars Nova in New York in 2012 and reached Broadway in 2016. It isn’t an abridgment of Tolstoy’s novel, and you don’t have to know every Russian surname going in. Two people are enough to start with: a young woman in a hurry to fall in love, and a man named Pierre who is less and less sure why his life should go on.

## Everyone Seems to Know Her Already

The opening introduces the characters in quick succession. A few vivid verdicts help the audience keep the relations straight: who is young, who is charming, who is good, who is lost. The labels serve as a provisional map. It gives newcomers their bearings, though it’s easy to forget that a map hasn’t told you who a person is.

Natasha arrives in Moscow to stay with her godmother, Marya D., and wait for her fiancé to return and their wedding to take place. To the people around her, her future is already clear. They have experience to pass on about whom she’ll marry, what sort of family she’ll join, and what might damage her reputation. Natasha loves Andrey too. She doesn’t arrive wanting to overturn all of it.

Visiting her fiancé’s family doesn’t reassure her. The severity of the encounter, the embarrassment, and their failure to warm to one another make marriage something more than two people in love. It also matters how she will be received. The longing in “No One Else” is not just the wait for someone to return. It’s an effort to hold on to the certainty of a love while being judged by people she barely knows.

That helps explain the force of someone’s looking at her with such interest later on. She doesn’t have to prove she’s grown up for her wish to be liked to make sense. The fact that someone takes advantage of that desire doesn’t mean vanity was all she cared about from the start.

But the pleasure of being looked at can make it easy to believe you’ve already been understood. Anatole’s desire is swift and unmistakable. Before Natasha has had time to know him, she’s given a certainty that seems to need no further explanation: he wants me, so he must see who I really am. The danger starts there. Desire isn’t wrong in itself. For a time, though, it takes the place of getting to know someone.

## The Party Can Do the Deciding for You

They first draw close at the opera. Natasha goes to see a performance, but she also enters a room where everybody is watching everybody else. People are performing onstage; out in the audience, people are noticing who came, who speaks to whom, and who draws the eye. It is on a night like this that Anatole approaches her. She has few facts about him. What she already has is a look, a voice, and the feeling of being singled out. The usual order of getting to know someone quietly goes into reverse.

Anatole isn’t acting alone. His sister Hélène invites Natasha into her social circle and to the ball. A room full of people who know exactly how to move and talk can make a decision that called for care seem like nothing to worry about.

The compliments in “Charming” give Natasha a different sense of herself. She can be beautiful and wanted; she no longer has to feel as helpless as she did visiting her elders. Taking pleasure in that isn’t the same as agreeing to everything that follows. But when everyone around you behaves as though nothing could be more natural, it gets harder to give your own hesitation the weight it deserves.

Malloy has explained that different characters and scenes led him to different kinds of music. Anatole’s appeal is bound up with the sound of the ball; Sonya’s solo goes in a plainer direction. The music isn’t ranking the characters from refined to vulgar. It lets the audience experience seduction by ear: first something sweeps us along, and only gradually do we hear where it’s taking us.

We don’t have to pretend we would have kept a clearer head in Natasha’s place. To be welcomed, to slip out of familiar constraints, to believe suddenly that another life might be possible: all of this really can make the risk feel worth taking. Understanding their appeal makes Anatole’s responsibility for what he withholds clearer than simply calling her foolish.

He is already married, but lets her make decisions about their future without telling her. This is more than a case of two people being equally reckless. One is staking her own life on the decision; the other holds back something she needs in order to judge it. They look as though they’re both pursuing love, but they aren’t taking the risk on the same terms.

## Can Sonya Say No?

The first thing Sonya loses when she discovers the plan is the chance to be heard as a friend. She still cares about Natasha, but because she won’t approve, Natasha casts her as an enemy. Closeness seems to require backing whatever the other person wants most at that moment. A warning starts to look like betrayal.

“Sonya Alone” gives someone who can easily recede into the background time to make up her own mind. She knows that intervening might damage the friendship, but she can’t take silence for respect. Natasha doesn’t know the whole story, and Sonya can’t pretend this is just a difference of opinion that leaves nothing for her to do.

Sonya also fears for Natasha’s reputation and the family’s. That concern belongs to an aristocratic society with rules of its own. It can’t simply be translated into today’s language of individual freedom, as if she had already stepped outside her time. What Sonya discovers first is the plan to run away. Being on the side that wants to stop it does not give her access to all Anatole’s secrets. The audience knows more, but we can’t lend her that hindsight and make a hard decision look unquestionably right.

From what has already happened, we can see that important information has been kept from Natasha and that she faces real harm. Stopping an immediate danger can leave her in a position to make a better-informed choice later. Using that intervention to claim the right to decide for her from then on is something else. Supporting Sonya’s refusal this time doesn’t give one friend authority over the other’s love life.

So “I’m doing this for your own good” isn’t enough to justify what Sonya does. We still need to ask what she knew, what she was trying to stop, and whether afterward she still sees Natasha as someone who can reconsider for herself. Good intentions that never let up can turn help into a kind of guardianship that no longer allows the other person to make a mistake.

Natasha may not thank her right away. When something we badly want is suddenly stopped, we aren’t always able to step back and see the whole situation. Sonya’s willingness to go unthanked doesn’t mean she’s there to sacrifice herself forever. Somebody ought to hear her fear and disappointment too. Calling her good doesn’t mean she no longer has needs of her own.

A friend’s standing up to say no can be a sign that the friendship is still alive. If all friends can do is echo what the other person says, someone can be surrounded by people and find it harder and harder to hear a genuinely different judgment.

## After Her Reputation Falls Apart

In order to run away, Natasha has already written to break off her engagement to Andrey. Sonya has tried to dissuade her and resolved not to stand by; at the last moment it is Marya D. who stops the elopement. The praise that once surrounded Natasha changes its tune. Her elders demand answers, and the future all that excitement seemed to promise never comes. She starts to face what she did. The people around her may want something more: if you did this, then this is the kind of person you are.

Those aren’t the same judgment. She concealed the plan, hurt her fiancé, and brushed aside her friend’s warning. She has to face those things. But they don’t settle whether she can go on living, be cared for, or learn something she didn’t know before.

Reputation is especially good at swallowing up the rest of a person. An act has particular people it affects and particular consequences. Once it becomes a label passed from mouth to mouth, nobody has to ask about those details. How Natasha waited, why she was drawn in, what she knew and what she didn’t all shrink into a scandal anybody can repeat.

The musical depicts self-harm in the middle of her crisis. It shouldn’t be treated as a sublime proof of what a woman will give for love. What calls for care is someone who can no longer bear the life in front of her. She needs help getting safe, not an audience measuring the depth of her feelings by the depth of her pain.

Andrey has been hurt too, and he doesn’t have to renew the engagement to prove he’s generous. He can choose not to continue the relationship. But declining to go on with someone isn’t the same as deciding there is nothing left in them worth taking seriously. The first refusal leaves both lives open. The second asks one verdict to explain everything the other person might become.

## Pierre Hasn’t Figured Out His Own Life First

Marya D. asks Pierre to help with the crisis. He isn’t a faultless adult arriving from outside the story. He has money, but that hasn’t taught him how to live. Hélène is his wife, and the marriage causes him pain. Drinking and thinking often put off anything he might actually do. He’s impulsive too, and even ends up in a duel. Treating Natasha well later doesn’t mean he had the answers all along.

“Dust and Ashes,” in the Broadway version, gives that bewilderment a fuller voice. It isn’t a shortage of things to do that leaves him empty. The things that fill up his days don’t tell him what he would want to give his life to. Other people might think he already has more than enough, and he might still have no sense of direction that connects with the life he actually lives.

Natasha’s crisis takes him out of abstract judgment. Finding Anatole, making him leave Moscow, and hearing how things came to a pass others already see as a disgrace aren’t things he can do by turning them over in his mind. Helping someone else doesn’t cure him. What he begins to discover is that there are things worth answering with an act.

That answer changes the way he understands Natasha. She is no longer merely a once-innocent girl whose reputation has to be restored, or someone he needs in order to recover a sense of purpose. To take someone seriously again doesn’t require sending them back to a past in which they had made no mistakes. He can leave her a future without undoing what has happened.

The more moving the help, the more important it is not to make it an exchange. Natasha doesn’t have to fall in love with Pierre to make his concern worthwhile. He doesn’t have to save her completely before he can say that responding to her mattered in his own life. Two people can meet, really meet, without solving everything for one another.

## Why a Show This Loud Goes Quiet

In discussing how he wrote the meeting between them, Malloy has explained why he took away the music. A musical constantly changing styles and crowded with voices lets a sentence go unaccompanied at a moment of immense feeling. There is no need to repeat that line here. Let it happen in the theater.

This doesn’t mean silence is truer than music. The songs have already brought us close to a great many people and given the ball, desire, and rumor their force. The quiet now lets the speaker answer without letting a beautiful form do it for him, and lets the listener hear without having to join at once in another rush of collective feeling.

*The Great Comet* finds its way into a vast novel through one small episode and loses none of its scale for that. The war is far off; the times will carry these people elsewhere. But on the night when the story comes to rest, the way one person meets another person’s mistake is enough to make the world look different.

Good intentions can’t erase what has happened. Nor do we have to make the person who did wrong stay there forever. Natasha is more than her worst decision. Sonya is more than the friend who has to clean up after it. And Pierre doesn’t have to become a whole, finished person before he’s capable of caring for someone. The comet doesn’t explain his life to him. But when he looks up at it, he is no longer only the man we met at the beginning, waiting for time to pass.
