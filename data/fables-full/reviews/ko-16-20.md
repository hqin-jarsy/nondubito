# Korean review: Fables 16–20

## Result and scope

Completed the Korean translations in `translations/ko/16.json` through `20.json`. Each file is a JSON array matching the corresponding Chinese `source/NN.json` `texts` array, including the title and final source note.

- 16: 116 entries.
- 17: 132 entries.
- 18: 120 entries.
- 19: 136 entries.
- 20: 146 entries.
- Total: 650 entries. No paragraphs were omitted, combined, or reordered.

The register follows the existing Korean 01–05 translations: natural non-honorific literary essay prose, short rhetorical turns, concrete images, and questions that preserve the author's rhythm. Dialogue uses forms appropriate to the characters. Abstract distinctions remain explicit without turning the essays into academic summaries.

Only these five Korean JSON files and this review were written. No Chinese source, HTML, script, manifest, index, or other language file was edited. No commit or push was made.

## Review actually performed

After drafting all five essays, I reread every Chinese–Korean paragraph pair, from each title through each source note. This was a complete bilingual self-review, not a sample or an independent second-editor review. The comparison checked completeness, negation and qualification, the direction of the arguments, conditional versus asserted facts, dialogue, metaphors, and Korean idiom. Revised passages were checked again in context. A truncated tool display was re-read in a separate smaller range so that it did not leave an unchecked gap.

The source notes were translated as supplied. The historical editions and primary texts cited in them were not independently re-researched during this translation task.

## Essay-specific semantic checks

### 16: The two pots

- Kept the distinction between good intentions and the companion's actual capacity to withstand harm.
- Preserved the asymmetric-risk argument, the limits of initial consent, and the reciprocal limits on accommodation. The essay does not become a demand that the stronger party accept unlimited responsibility.
- The suggested distances, pace changes, container, and alternative meeting places remain possibilities absent from the fable, not events inserted into it.
- The reread corrected `texts[7]` and `[112]` so standing in front to shield the clay pot cannot be read as blocking the clay pot's path.
- At `[70]`, made the clay pot the explicit subject of breaking, consistent with the surrounding account, rather than leaving Korean syntax to suggest that both pots break.
- At `[89]`, removed an ambiguous reference to “the other side”; `[99]` was smoothed into idiomatic Korean.

### 17: The golden-egg goose

- Preserved the distinction among existing output, its living source, and a future that does not yet exist as inventory.
- Retained the limited ownership claim: legal power to dispose of something is not the ability to make a destroyed process continue.
- Kept the qualifications allowing efficiency improvements and affirming that wealth itself can matter.
- At `[58]`, clarified that the conditions cannot be recovered once destroyed, rather than calling the conditions themselves “irreversible.”
- At `[107]`, clarified the source bearing an unrecoverable cost; `[120]` was smoothed without changing why the living goose is more precious.

### 18: Morning three, evening four

- `texts[14]`–`[20]` explicitly retain that the source gives no reason for the monkeys' preference. In particular, `[16]` does **not** assert that they were hungrier in the morning, more active in daytime, or unable to store the evening acorns.
- The examples at `[37]`–`[43]` and hypotheses at `[88]`–`[89]` remain illustrative or conditional. `[90]` and `[113]` retain the lack of information needed to decide which explanation applies.
- Preserved both limits: equal totals do not establish equality in every relevant condition; a change in emotion does not establish unfairness or a substantive change.
- Smoothed `[57]`; revised `[91]` to avoid wording that presupposed the monkeys had been wronged; clarified the request to state the measuring standard at `[116]`.
- The single internal newline at `[67]` is preserved.

### 19: The people touching the elephant

- Preserved real partial contact versus an overextended whole-object claim, as well as the king's control of access conditions.
- The account still rejects both automatic relativism and the idea that adding up all the metaphors automatically yields a living elephant.
- Kept the explicit separation between actual visual impairment and ignorance or obstinacy. The king's precise motives remain unspecified; the later political possibilities remain possibilities.
- At `[1]`, clarified that the people live in the city, avoiding an unintended implication about where they were born.
- At `[7]`, restored the king's first sentence as a declaration, not an added question.
- Clarified the whole-object implication at `[34]`, the part-to-whole direction at `[52]`, and the experience-support relation at `[74]`; smoothed `[54]` and made exchanging positions explicit at `[113]`.
- The single internal newline at `[24]` is preserved.

### 20: The Brahman and three tricksters

- Used `염소` consistently for the animal, following the specified Ryder story, “The Brahman’s Goat.” It is not translated as a sheep.
- Preserved three conflicting animal identifications, a single coordinated information source, the unfalsifiable shape-shifter explanation, and the identity/ritual-purity pressure.
- Kept both the manipulators' responsibility and the Brahman's responsibility to check. The conclusion does not become either “always trust yourself” or “always trust the majority.”
- Retained the political-strategy framing and its distance from a modern moralized reading.
- At `[8]`, replaced an unnecessarily insulting idiom with the direct “보이지 않소?”; at `[135]`, kept the author's statement about the world he was fleeing rather than shifting it into an intention claim.

## Unresolved source contradiction requiring the author's decision

The contradiction flagged in the assignment is retained in meaning at its exact zero-based indexes. It was **not** silently repaired by changing “directly order” into “force,” “coerce,” or “physically compel.”

- `source/20.json texts[20]`: “你怎么把一头驴扛在肩上？快把它放下，别让更多人看见。”
- Korean `[20]`: “어째서 당나귀를 어깨에 메고 가십니까? 더 많은 사람이 보기 전에 어서 내려놓으십시오.”
- `source/20.json texts[90]`: 他们没有直接命令婆罗门放下羊。
- Korean `[90]`: 그들은 브라만에게 염소를 내려놓으라고 직접 명령하지 않았다.

The first passage explicitly commands him to put the animal down; the later analysis denies a direct command. The Korean translation is complete and faithful to both statements, but this review does not certify the source argument as internally reconciled. Any correction to either passage needs the author's decision and then corresponding translation updates.
