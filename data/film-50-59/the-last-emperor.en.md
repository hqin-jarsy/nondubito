# Told Who He Was

At three years old, Puyi is carried through the gates of the Forbidden City and informed that he is emperor. Every ceremonial step is complete—edict, procession, kneeling officials—except the participation of the child whose life has been renamed.

The film repeatedly returns to this pattern. Emperor, abdicated ruler, puppet emperor, prisoner 981, citizen, gardener: institutions announce the name, prepare the documents, and arrange the room. Puyi is present at nearly every transformation, but seldom controls the official description that follows him.

That does not mean he has no agency. Under Reginald Johnston he cuts his queue, installs a telephone, investigates palace accounts, expels eunuchs, and begins using the English name Henry. These exceptions matter. They show a young man attempting to name and direct himself inside a structure built to speak first.

Manchukuo is the most serious exception. Japan supplies the throne and controls the documents, but the film's Puyi also wants to be emperor again. He calls it his country and enters the arrangement with ambition. Manipulation does not erase responsibility; responsibility does not make the manipulation unreal.

This double truth improves the film. Puyi is neither the innocent object of every historical force nor the sovereign author imagined by the ceremonies around him. He makes choices from within identities that have already trained his desire. Sometimes he resists them. Sometimes he pursues the very title through which others can use him.

The final guide's speech reduces his life to three facts: name, accession, death. Each is accurate and radically incomplete. The film has spent its length restoring what an official description cannot carry—the nurse he chased, the doors he could not open, the name he tried, the country he wanted, and the cricket no archive thought important.

# The One Who Walked Out

Doors organise *The Last Emperor*. As a child, Puyi runs after his nurse when she is expelled, but the palace gates close. Later he charges those gates on a bicycle and is stopped again. The emperor may command a court; he cannot decide where his own body goes.

The women around him receive even less ceremony for their confinement. Wanrong and Wenxiu are selected as empress and consort. Neither chooses the role or the husband. The film does not reduce their lives to rivalry for his favour. It watches what each woman does with a position decided in advance.

Wenxiu eventually leaves. She asks for divorce, walks through the gate, and accepts the social cost of becoming an ordinary woman. Her departure is not staged as betrayal. It is the film's clearest case of someone giving up rank in order to recover authorship of her life.

Wanrong remains. In Tianjin she still dances, jokes, and pushes against the role. In Manchukuo, isolation, opium, and political surveillance close around her. When her baby is taken and killed, even motherhood is administered as a matter of state. Her later removal repeats the nurse's expulsion: Puyi runs toward a closing gate and cannot keep the person inside his world.

The visual rhyme is severe. As a child he is the prisoner who cannot follow; as an adult he is also the emperor whose household has helped imprison someone else. Power and helplessness occupy the same frame.

Wenxiu's exit does not prove that everyone could simply have walked away. She pays for leaving, and different people possess different exits. It proves something narrower and more important: the roles surrounding Puyi are not natural facts. One person refuses hers, and the entire palace grammar briefly loses its claim to inevitability.

# The Cricket

At Puyi's first coronation, an elderly official gives the restless child a cricket in a small cage. He asks whether it is alive. The question cuts through the thousands of kneeling bodies: among all the symbols handed to him, the child's attention settles on the one living thing.

Decades later, in prison, Puyi is ordered to write an account of his life. At first he produces the familiar official story. The governor returns it and asks him to write again. For perhaps the first time, he must use the first person not as a royal plural or a ceremonial signature, but as responsibility.

The re-education scenes are open to argument. The prison is still an institution with the power to decide when his account is acceptable. Confession can become another authorised script. Yet the governor sometimes refuses the easier domination. He does not merely dictate the correct autobiography; he leaves Puyi to confront the distance between what was done through his office and what he is willing to call his own.

After release, Puyi buys a ticket to enter the Forbidden City as a tourist. The former emperor must pay admission to the place once described as his. He finds a boy near the throne and tells him he used to live there. The boy asks for proof.

Puyi reaches behind the throne and brings out the cricket cage. The cricket appears alive, though decades have passed. The moment is not historical evidence. It is cinema's answer to the guide's tidy summary: a life contains objects, affections, and private continuities that official records cannot certify.

The cage does not restore the throne. It does something smaller. It lets Puyi offer one memory without commanding belief, and lets a child decide what to make of it. After a lifetime of names imposed from above, his last act is not a decree. It is the sharing of a living remainder.
