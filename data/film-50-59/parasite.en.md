# Reasons Do Not Make Innocence

Ki-woo enters the Park household with a forged university certificate. He explains that he expects to enter the university eventually, so the document merely brings the future forward. The reasoning is comic, self-serving, and internally coherent.

That pattern runs through *Parasite*. Nearly every character has reasons the film allows us to understand. The Kim family needs work. Ki-jung has real ability despite her invented credentials. Moon-gwang hides her indebted husband because he has nowhere else to go. The Parks protect the comfort of their household.

Understanding those reasons does not make the actions innocent. The Kims frame a driver and exploit Moon-gwang's allergy to take her job. They deliberately convert two people into obstacles that can be removed. Their poverty explains the urgency; it does not erase the people harmed by their method.

Mr Park is not exposed through a secret criminal vice. He can be a courteous employer and still possess the privilege of drawing a “line” around his comfort without knowing where his workers sleep. His private disgust at Ki-taek's smell is not a theatrical mask falling away. It is an ordinary class boundary spoken where he assumes the employee cannot hear.

The film loses force if one class is made wholly guilty and the other wholly blameless. It also loses force if structure is used to dissolve responsibility. Both must remain. Individuals answer for forgery, framing, concealment, and violence; the architecture of scarce jobs, debt, housing, and service continues to shape what each person can see as necessary.

That is the harsher proposition: people do wrong without becoming cartoon villains. A motive may be sympathetic and an act still require judgment. The tragedy grows when each person's survival appears inside one frame while the cost imposed on another remains outside it.

# Each Person Looks into One Square

Mr Park repeatedly praises employees for not crossing the line. The line is not a written rule. It is his personal comfort made authoritative by ownership. He does not have to explain where it lies; everyone else must learn to feel it.

The rain exposes how such private squares divide one city. For the Parks, the storm cancels a camping trip and clears the air for a garden party. For the Kims, it fills their semi-basement with sewage and sends the neighbourhood to a gymnasium floor. Mrs Park's delight in the weather is sincere. So is Ki-taek's silence on the phone.

Local sincerity is not the same as a complete view. The Kims never ask what became of the employees they displaced. Moon-gwang's return forces the missing consequences into the house. Her own square is organised around her husband: years of food, secrecy, and fear. That devotion is durable enough to include blackmail and violence against another family.

Geun-sae's world is smaller still. He thanks Mr Park for shelter and food, though Mr Park does not know he exists. Gratitude travels upward without recognition returning downward.

Da-song, the child, notices what adults exclude. He smells that the four workers belong together. He recognises Morse code in the stair light. The adults treat both perceptions as childish noise because taking them seriously would require redrawing the household's picture.

During the night beneath the coffee table, each person has a locally intelligible action and nobody has the whole scene. That does not acquit earlier deceptions. It shows why responsibility does not automatically produce understanding. One may answer for what one did and still remain blind to the lives attached to it.

The house joins these squares without making them equal. The people with least security must study everyone above them. The people at the top can live well while seeing the least. The architecture is not only vertical in appearance. It distributes the obligation to know.

# There Is Only One Position

When Moon-gwang opens the hidden basement, two poor families recognise one another. Each sees a version of its own strategy: one family has entered the household through jobs and false identities; the other has survived below it through concealment.

Recognition does not create solidarity. It creates a fight over scarce positions. The jobs are occupied. The bunker can hide one dependent arrangement. Exposure threatens both. Each side reaches first for the other family's silence.

The owner is absent from the struggle. The six people below treat the number of available positions as fixed and themselves as competitors for them. Nobody imagines negotiating a new arrangement with the household whose abundance contains the bunker. Scarcity has become a fact before anyone speaks.

At the garden party, what has been hidden erupts into daylight. Mr Park reaches for his car keys and recoils from Geun-sae's smell. The gesture is involuntary, but Ki-taek sees in it the entire hierarchy of the previous night: a flooded home, borrowed clothes, a performance demanded by an employer, and one final instinctive withdrawal from the poor body on the grass.

After the killing, Ki-taek occupies the bunker. The person changes; the position remains. He uses the same lights to send Morse code upward. Ki-woo eventually deciphers the message and forms a plan to earn enough to buy the house.

The imagined reunion is tender and devastating. Ki-woo does not imagine multiplying positions or abolishing the bunker. He imagines becoming the owner of the one position above it. The film then cuts back to the semi-basement, where the plan's improbability is visible without commentary.

The scholar's stone completes the argument. Ki-woo carries it as a promise of mobility, takes it downstairs as a weapon, and is struck by it. He later returns it to a stream, but keeps the plan. He has released the object, not the social imagination attached to it: salvation still means winning the only seat.
