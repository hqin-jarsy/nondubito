# The Table as Summons

The opening of *Eat Drink Man Woman* dwells on Master Chu preparing a Sunday feast. The labour is exquisite and nearly solitary: bone, slice, steam, fry, smoke, fold. A telephone conversation about cooking briefly interrupts the kitchen sounds. By the time his three adult daughters arrive, a day of work has become a table they consume in minutes.

It is tempting to say this is simply a father's unspoken love. It is that, but the dinner also functions as a summons. Time and attendance are fixed. Absence requires explanation. The meal offers care and takes roll call in the same gesture.

That combination makes refusal difficult. A gift can be declined; a family duty cannot. When Jia-Chien criticises the seasoning, Chu hears more than culinary judgment. It is one of the only signals travelling back through a language he controls from the sending end.

The lunch boxes he prepares for the neighbour's daughter Shan-Shan reveal the difference. She can enjoy them, request changes, trade food with classmates, and answer without an inherited debt. Her mother Jin-Rong even admits sharing the lunches. The food circulates because nobody has made gratitude the price of receiving it.

Chu's cooking is not the problem by itself. The problem is that an extraordinarily expressive language has become the family's only language. It can say “I care” with precision but has no easy grammar for “I do not want this,” “I am leaving,” or “you used too much ginger.”

The daughters are not too insensitive to understand the food. They understand the account attached to it: the years of labour, the lonely father, the expectation that a proper response will organise their lives. They flee not because the meal means nothing, but because it means so much that no ordinary answer can settle the debt.

# Four Announcements

The Chu family's Sunday dinner is its only regular public forum. Because it meets once a week, under ritual pressure, it cannot hold a slow conversation. It holds conclusions.

Jia-Chien announces that she has bought an apartment and will move out. Jia-Ning announces her pregnancy and leaves on a waiting motorcycle. Jia-Jen announces her marriage after constructing a fictional romantic injury to explain years of withdrawal. Finally, Master Chu announces that he will marry Jin-Rong, stunning everyone who expected him to choose her mother.

Different personalities use the same form: decide elsewhere, make the decision irreversible, report it at the table, then leave. Announcement protects each speaker from a conversation they do not trust the family to conduct.

Chu's announcement proves he is not merely the victim of his daughters' secrecy. He invented the table's language and eventually uses it more completely than any of them. His decision is genuinely his; the form still injures people by denying them any place in the process.

Jia-Chien provides a subtler contrast. She does announce her plan to leave. What she never announces is the later decision to stay after seeing her father's vulnerability and losing Uncle Wen. She rejects the Amsterdam post without turning the sacrifice into a claim on anyone's gratitude.

This does not make staying morally superior. The opportunity is real, and the cost remains. It shows only that the table is best designed for departures. It can transmit “I have decided” but cannot easily hold the unfinished reasons through which a decision changes.

At the last Sunday meal, Jia-Chien cooks in the old house and invites everyone. Only Chu comes. The forum remains, but almost all its members have moved beyond it. What finally passes between father and daughter is not an announcement. It is a taste, a hand, and a word neither has prepared in advance.

# Taste Keeps the Accounts

Master Chu has lost his sense of taste. His skill remains intact, and Uncle Wen quietly checks the seasoning, but the chef who feeds a hotel and a family can no longer receive the food he produces.

The film never supplies a medical explanation. Instead it places the loss beside a life organised around service. Chu's mouth names dishes, gives instructions, and judges other cooks. His own appetite is almost absent from the frame.

Ginger carries the family's only permitted disagreement. Jia-Chien says he uses too much; Chu rejects the judgment. Beneath the seasoning dispute lies a larger sentence: what I taste is not what you taste, and there is more than one mouth at this table.

When Uncle Wen dies, Chu loses more than a friend. He loses the last person able to tell him what his food tastes like. Jia-Chien sees his grief and abandons the overseas post. Again, the film refuses a clean moral ledger: care arrives through sacrifice, and sacrifice may later become another unspoken debt.

Meanwhile the daughters interpret Chu's medical examination as evidence that they must arrange his old age. They repeat his own habit in reverse. He organised their futures out of love; they prepare to organise his. Nobody asks what he wants.

At the final large dinner, Chu publicly states a decision about his own life: he will marry Jin-Rong. The declaration is abrupt and hurtful, especially because he later sells the house without consulting Jia-Chien. Self-direction does not guarantee considerate action. It only makes the action his to answer for.

In the empty old house, Jia-Chien serves soup. Chu tastes it and says there is too much ginger. Taste has returned. He calls her daughter and takes her hand. The first recovered flavour is the disagreement that has crossed two generations—but this time he does not close it down. The account is not balanced. At last, it can be read from both sides.
