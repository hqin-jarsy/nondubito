# She Walked In

The first thing Songlian does in *Raise the Red Lantern* is choose. She refuses the sedan chair her new husband has sent, changes into bridal red, lifts her suitcase, and walks to the Chen compound by herself. The film will spend the next two hours showing how little freedom remains inside those walls. It begins, however, by refusing to turn her into someone who was simply carried there.

Her choice is made under pressure: her father is dead, the family is poor, and her stepmother has reduced the future to work or marriage. Songlian answers with bitterness that marrying a rich man only means becoming his concubine. She sees the bargain clearly and enters it anyway. That does not make everything that follows her fault. It means coercion and agency can occupy the same decision.

Inside the compound, the master is almost never given a face. What matters is not his personality but the system organised around his arrival. A lit lantern brings dinner, a foot massage, attention, and status. The wives compete because material life has been made to flow through one man's nightly preference. Even rebellion must use the grammar of the contest.

Songlian learns quickly. She lies about pregnancy, punishes a servant, and exploits the rules that confine her. To call her merely a victim erases the harm she causes; to call her merely calculating erases the structure that makes calculation a condition of survival. The film holds both truths in the same room.

Her opening walk therefore matters until the end. She did walk in. Yet a choice made among narrowed exits does not authorise everything waiting behind the chosen door. Responsibility begins with recognising her decision, not with pretending that the walls became hers because she crossed the threshold.

# Who Keeps the Score

The lanterns in the Chen compound look ceremonial, but they function like a scoreboard. When a courtyard is lit, everyone knows who has won that evening. The massage hammers announce it. Servants change direction. Dinner moves. A private preference becomes public rank.

No written rule says the wives must compete, yet almost every useful thing is attached to victory. The household has converted affection into an allocation system: attention, comfort, information, and influence arrive together. Refusing the contest would not place a wife above it; it would place her outside the household's flow of goods.

Each woman develops a strategy. The first wife preserves seniority. The second performs kindness while working through servants and secrets. The third turns opera and open contempt into a private territory. Songlian tries intelligence, then deception. Their characters differ, but the scoreboard makes those differences legible only as tactics.

The master's absence is essential. He does not need to supervise every quarrel. Once everyone has learned what the lantern means, the wives police one another, servants anticipate the winner, and humiliation circulates horizontally. Power becomes most efficient when those beneath it administer its measurements themselves.

This is why Songlian's false pregnancy is more than a plot twist. She does not invent a new game; she falsifies the score in the existing one. Her punishment follows the same logic. The lanterns are covered, her privileges removed, her courtyard visually erased. The household answers fraud not by questioning its measure, but by excluding the fraudulent player.

The question is therefore not which wife deserved to win. It is who designed a life in which winning one evening could decide whether a person was warm, heard, or visible. Once that question is asked, the beauty of the lanterns changes. They are still beautiful. They are also counting devices.

# The Face the Film Withholds

The master of the Chen household governs almost every scene, yet Zhang Yimou rarely lets us see his face clearly. He arrives as a voice, a silhouette, a pair of feet, or a body placed at the edge of the frame. The women receive full faces. The man who determines their rank remains visually unfinished.

This is not because he is unimportant. It is because the household does not depend on his psychological depth. A charming master and a cruel master could both operate the same lantern system. What matters is that his desire has been installed as the house's organising principle.

The withheld face also changes where the viewer looks. We cannot solve the film by diagnosing one man's temperament. We must watch the courtyards, doors, roofs, servants, rituals, and the route along which lanterns are carried. Authority has become architecture.

The women, by contrast, are scrutinised relentlessly. Songlian's smallest expression is readable. Meishan's defiance, Zhuoyun's calculation, and Yan'er's resentment are all exposed. Those with least control become the most interpretable; the person with most control owes nobody an explanation.

By the final winter, a new concubine enters while Songlian wanders the compound in an old school uniform. The household has already absorbed scandal, death, and madness. The lanterns can be lit again because the structure does not require anyone to remember what happened to the last person.

The unseen face is thus not an empty centre. It is a centre that has made its own individuality irrelevant. The women are forced to become roles in relation to him, while he is freed even from becoming a character in relation to them. The film's most powerful man is the one person it does not need to finish drawing.
