# He Was Not Made That Way

Alex enjoys violence. *A Clockwork Orange* gives him no rescuing explanation: no starving household, brutal parents, or irresistible gang hierarchy. He skips school, commands his friends, and turns assault into theatre. The film blocks the comfortable route from cruelty back to an innocent cause.

That refusal matters because the second half introduces a different violation. The state promises to remove Alex's violence through the Ludovico Technique. Drugs, restraints, and films condition his body to answer aggressive and sexual impulses with nausea. Beethoven, used on the soundtrack, is caught in the same mechanism.

The treatment “works” only by changing the meaning of the patient. Alex no longer chooses not to strike. He cannot strike. He cannot defend himself, close his eyes, or escape the music paired with the images. The government counts the disappearance of behaviour and ignores the disappearance of agency.

None of this makes the earlier victims disappear. The writer's injuries, his wife's death, the murdered woman, and Alex's betrayed friends remain. A person can be responsible for terrible acts and still be wronged by a procedure imposed on him. Moral accounting does not require one side to become innocent before the other side may be judged.

Kubrick's difficulty is deliberate. If Alex were secretly gentle, opposing the treatment would be easy. By choosing a protagonist who gives us strong reasons to want him stopped, the film asks whether a government may turn a person into a safe mechanism when punishment, persuasion, and risk all look less efficient.

Alex was not manufactured by one social defect. But the “cure” manufactures something else: a body that performs goodness because every alternative has been made physically impossible. The question is not whether society should leave him free to harm. It is whether the only alternative it can imagine is to abolish the chooser.

# They Did Not Know What They Were Cutting

The Ludovico demonstration is staged as public proof. Alex is placed before officials, struck by an actor, and confronted with a naked woman. Each time his body collapses before he can act. The minister calls the treatment a success because the unwanted behaviour has vanished.

The prison chaplain objects: goodness must be chosen. His language sounds old-fashioned beside the machinery and statistics, but he has identified the variable the experiment never measures. The technicians can count blows prevented. They cannot tell whether a person remains capable of moral action.

The procedure cuts more than violence. It removes self-defence and sexual agency. It contaminates Beethoven, one of the few things Alex loves without using it merely as an instrument. The state does not know how these capacities are connected; it treats the mind as a list of separable switches and discovers the error only after several have gone dark together.

“It is effective” therefore proves much less than the minister thinks. Effective at what? Reducing prison populations, winning an election, preventing assault, producing citizens who cannot resist? Those outcomes are not interchangeable. A technique may succeed under one description while becoming catastrophic under another.

The film also refuses the cheap answer that free choice is valuable because people generally choose well. Alex chose badly, repeatedly. Choice matters because without it, praise, blame, refusal, reform, and responsibility all lose their object. The state has not made him virtuous. It has made certain verbs unavailable to him.

The experimenters do not have to hate Alex. Their blindness is more ordinary: they measure only the result they were commissioned to produce. What disappears outside that frame is treated as collateral. The film's warning is not that technology always fails. It is that a tool may work perfectly while its operators remain unable to say what, exactly, they have cut.

# No Longer the Government's Property

After the treatment, Alex becomes useful to everyone except himself. The minister displays him as policy. The opposition writer uses him as evidence against the government. Journalists turn his suffering into a public contest over which side owns its meaning.

The writer's rage is not invented. Alex and his friends destroyed his body and contributed to his wife's death. But once he recognises Alex, private revenge enters the political campaign. Beethoven is blasted through the ceiling until Alex attempts suicide. A man opposing state control uses the conditioned body as an instrument of his own.

The minister later appears at the hospital with flowers, food, photographers, and an offer. The treatment will be reversed; Alex will publicly support the government. The same administration that removed his choices now restores them because a different image is electorally useful.

This is why the ending should not be read as a clean victory for freedom. Alex's fantasies return, but the terms of his return are negotiated by power. He has moved from experimental subject to public-relations asset. His inner life escapes the government's technique, while his social position remains something politicians believe they can purchase.

The famous final line announces that he is “cured.” The word has reversed meaning. Earlier it meant unable to choose violence; now it means able to imagine it again. Neither definition tells us what a just response to Alex would be. The film ends before responsibility, repair, or genuine change can begin.

In 1973 the film was withdrawn from UK circulation by Warner Bros at Kubrick's request amid controversy and threats, and it remained unavailable there until after his death. That history echoes the movie's problem without solving it: once an image enters other minds, its effects cannot be governed like an object kept in a vault. Alex's Beethoven, Kubrick's film, and the state's treatment all exceed the purposes assigned to them.
