Rose asks Nancy to stay.
At the hotel, she urges her to leave danger for a safe place. Later, at London Bridge, Brownlow explicitly offers arrangements for a life far away, even abroad.
The offers are sincere, not idle assurances.
Between those meetings, Rose has met Brownlow. Together they can provide resources and assistance, and want to open a way out.
Nancy refuses.
## What she says
She gives several reasons. Each leaves something difficult for her listeners to understand.
One concerns her sense of degradation.
She feels too deeply involved, beyond recovery, and fears bringing trouble upon the people offering help.
Rose will not accept the past as a reason to abandon her.
There is also fear.
Nancy fears discovery and fears bringing destruction upon people to whom she remains attached.
The proposed refuge, and later the offer of another country, could reduce the danger. They cannot instantly dissolve the old life’s grip.
The deepest attachment is to Sikes.
She intends to return to him.
Asked why, she describes how a woman without family or friends can make one man the center of her life.
She speaks of her feelings and of being entangled in the life she knows. She is not without reasons, though those reasons remain difficult to accept.
She even says she will return knowing that he might kill her.
## Why the refusal is difficult
Pause over this refusal. Few moments in the book resist explanation more strongly.
Two familiar readings seem insufficient.
The first is that she loves Sikes irrationally.
That assigns the matter to love, after which explanation can stop: love has no reasons, so obscurity is expected.
But stopping there leaves her recent conduct unexplained.
She has disclosed the associates’ scheme, reporting Fagin and Monks while Sikes sleeps under a drug she has given him.
Her attachment has not extinguished her judgment or her opposition to what they intend for the child.
The second reading is that her environment has destroyed her ability to choose anything else.
Taken to its limit, that reading makes her wholly incapable of action.
Yet she has just performed one of the novel’s most resourceful interventions.
She overhears conversations, assembles the clues, drugs Sikes, and crosses London alone to meet a stranger. She explains the scheme clearly and arranges another time and place for contact.
The act requires planning, judgment, and courage.
Her power to act has not been completely erased.
## What else can the refusal mean?
I propose another aspect: she does not want helping the child to become a bargain for a new life of her own.
Look at what Rose and Brownlow offer across the two meetings.
Money, arrangements, a safe place, a chance to begin again.
They are offered to Nancy.
They come from people in the respectable world who had not previously known her.
Why do they offer them?
Not simply because she has done a good deed. Rose is already willing to help this suffering woman before the full information is disclosed.
Later, Rose appeals to Nancy’s rescue of the child and capacity for repentance, but also explicitly says her concern is not only a reward for the information.
That compassion must not be erased.
What I want to examine is another form within the persuasion: a person performs a worthy act and is consequently judged fit for a new place.
Nancy has long lived within a version of that form.
She entered Fagin’s world as a small child. Her place there depends on her usefulness: watching, acting, obtaining information.
She is allowed to stay because she performs well.
Now the appeal to leave also includes the thought that she has saved a child and begun to repent.
This does not make Rose’s conduct equivalent to Fagin’s. It suggests that the pattern of “what you have done, and therefore what you deserve” can appear again under very different intentions.
The judges, standards, and proposed benefits have changed. The question is whether Nancy’s act must be settled by any of them.
## An act she does not put up for exchange
Helping Oliver was not conditional upon securing a reward for herself.
Notice her insistences throughout.
She does not want money.
She refuses payment because money is not why she came. Later, however, she asks Rose for a small personal keepsake and accepts her handkerchief.
She asks for secrecy: no following her, no investigating her, no public disclosure of what she has done.
At the bridge, Brownlow wants to locate Fagin. Nancy will not hand him over. After receiving a promise not to pursue him without her agreement, she continues supplying clues about Monks.
She draws that boundary.
She will not deliver those people up.
They have harmed her and also make up the life she knows. Saving the boy is not, for her, permission to surrender every former associate.
That does not make them innocent. It means her decision does not follow every step her helpers would prefer.
Consider the direction of these limits.
She will carry the disclosure this far, and no further.
She tells the scheme for the child’s sake, not in exchange for payment, public praise, or the arrest of all her old companions. Accepting a handkerchief does not put a price on the information.
She does not offer the act as a commodity.
## Why return?
“I will go back to him” can be read as another part of that refusal.
On this reading, disclosure immediately followed by a new life could too easily be narrated as an exchange.
She gives information; the good people give a future.
The account appears settled.
Her refusal interrupts that settlement.
Recall her admission that she helped bring Oliver back.
She does not erase her responsibility.
I would not read what follows only as atonement, as if a good deed canceled a bad deed and reduced the balance to zero.
After helping, she returns to the man who will kill her and to the danger of her former life.
The act has not produced a monetary return for her.
## Reasons that do not settle an account
Nancy speaks of attachment, fear, and the people with whom her life is bound up.
Those reasons can be heard without turning them into a table of gains and losses from which someone else calculates her proper choice.
What she does is not an account closed by payment.
She still goes back. Her attachment is real, and so is her judgment that the child must be helped. She has not arranged the two into an answer that satisfies other people.
Her explanations do not make the refusal easy to understand.
An act not aimed at reward can have reasons without those reasons being convertible into profit.
## That night
Noah follows her.
At the second meeting, she comes to London Bridge. He hides near the steps and listens.
He reports to Fagin.
Fagin passes the story to Sikes.
The manner of its telling is especially sinister.
He first works Sikes into a fury, building the threat before fully identifying Nancy’s part.
Sikes rushes away.
Fagin calls after him not to be too violent.
He immediately explains his concern in terms of safety: cunning rather than recklessness. Even his restraint contains a calculation for himself.
## Her death
Sikes returns and locks the door.
Nancy wakes.
Seeing his face, she asks in terror what has happened.
She does not escape.
She clings to him.
She grasps his clothes, begs for her life, and urges him to repent. There is still time: they can leave and live better lives far apart, never seeing each other again.
He still has time to turn back.
While pleading for herself, she also points toward a future in which he need not carry out the killing.
He strikes her.
He hits her twice with the butt of his pistol. Bleeding, she struggles to raise the handkerchief Rose gave her and offers a final prayer.
He kills her with a club.
## Sikes afterward
The novel follows him.
He runs.
He cannot get away.
Before pursuit catches up, he is already pursued by his own mind.
Wherever he goes, he seems to see Nancy’s eyes. Darkness, solitude, and sleep become intolerable.
He throws himself into fighting a fire, working desperately because activity briefly interrupts the images.
His dog follows. He tries to kill it because it could identify him.
The dog escapes.
Eventually he is cornered on a roof. Attempting to lower himself by a rope, he sees those eyes again, loses his balance, and is hanged by the rope caught around his neck.
The dog leaps and falls to its death.
## What does this ending explain?
Dickens gives Sikes’s flight considerable space. This essay reads it as more than retribution.
It makes something visible.
The person who finally kills Sikes is not a judge or one of his pursuers.
His own terrified mind contributes to his death.
In this reading, Nancy’s last moments are part of what gives the pursuing eyes their power.
Had she offered only curses and hatred, perhaps he could have forced the act into a story of two people injuring one another.
Instead, even as she clings to him, she says he can still turn back.
A man who believes himself betrayed hears the person before him begging to survive and still urging him away from destruction.
He cannot put that encounter into a form he can bear.
The eyes follow him.
## In closing
Bring the four essays together.
A nine-year-old asks for more gruel and causes an institutional crisis. The portion has already been calculated, and the calculation has no column for his hunger. The board need not personally hate him. It is managing expenditure.
A five-pound offer is made to remove a liability. The parish, not the person taking him, pays.
Then he reaches a place where he is an asset. Food, shelter, companions, and praise are real, and they come at a price.
Useless and useful lie close together. Both can belong to the same way of accounting for a person.
Yet one person’s action cannot be fully contained by that calculation.
She is an accomplice. She is the woman who deceived the crowd and helped take the child away. She acknowledges it herself.
She brings the information out without making money or a new life the condition, refuses to hand over every old companion, and wants her identity kept secret.
Others sincerely want to help and also speak in the language of redemption and worthiness. Her intervention does not wait to be settled by a better position.
She refuses the offered departure.
She describes her attachments, yet still leaves the others struggling to understand why she must return.
After returning, she is murdered.
Her act has earned her nothing in money.
The novel’s calculable things, the ration, the five-pound reward, the usefulness of a small body, all find their places in an account.
That act does not.
