That evening, the workhouse boys have agreed on a plan.
They draw lots. Whoever is chosen must stand up after supper and ask the master for another bowl of gruel.
The lot falls to Oliver.
The nine-year-old approaches with his empty bowl and says the sentence:
Please, sir, I want some more.
## The room’s response
Watch what follows. The response is the subject of this essay.
The master freezes. He turns pale, steadies himself against the copper, and stares at the boy.
His assistants are equally astonished.
Then come the cries. The master strikes Oliver on the head with the ladle, seizes him, and calls for Mr. Bumble.
Bumble rushes into the boardroom to report that Oliver Twist has asked for more.
The assembled gentlemen are aghast.
One gentleman in a white waistcoat declares that the boy will certainly be hanged.
They then advertise a reward of five pounds to anyone willing to take him as an apprentice.
## Why such a great disturbance?
A nine-year-old says he is still hungry.
The result is an institutional crisis.
Why?
Because the portion has already been calculated.
Dickens describes the calculation: three meals of thin gruel a day, an onion twice a week, and half a roll on Sundays.
The board sets the ration.
He also describes its reasoning with devastating composure. These enlightened gentlemen have discovered that the workhouse is a public entertainment for the poor, somewhere to eat and sleep, a tavern that requires no work.
Their answer is to make it unpleasant.
So unpleasant that the poor will prefer hunger outside to admission.
Dickens gives them two alternatives: gradual starvation inside the house, or a quicker starvation outside it.
One of the book’s fiercest sentences arrives in its second chapter.
## It does not require hatred
Here is the central point, different from the impression many readers carry of the novel.
The board need not hate Oliver.
Its members scarcely know him. No strong personal feeling about this particular boy is required.
He is not really what they are discussing.
They are discussing a budget.
The workhouse costs money, supplied by parish rates. Those funds are limited; local tradespeople and property holders pay them.
The question takes this form: how can the greatest number of poor people be dealt with at the lowest cost?
“Dealt with” has two meanings: discourage them from entering, or reduce the cost of those who do enter.
Cutting the food allowance serves both.
Within that budgetary and administrative reasoning, the plan makes sense. Dickens is satirizing, among other things, the treatment of poverty as a management problem associated with the period of the 1834 Poor Law reform.
The gentlemen can regard themselves as conscientiously doing their jobs.
## Where is the problem?
The things entered into the calculation are people.
A board member writing down so many ounces per person per week handles a quantity multiplied by a head count.
Every unit in that head count is a child capable of hunger.
The page does not distinguish the two descriptions.
Once someone becomes one unit among the counted, there is no obvious place for the question of whether this particular child is hungry.
The question has not necessarily been refuted. It has no column.
Oliver’s request is therefore received not as a request but as an entry outside the permitted form.
The calculation has no way to process it.
It becomes an incident.
## Five pounds to take him away
What follows makes the arrangement clearer.
The parish advertises five pounds for anyone who will take Oliver Twist.
Notice the direction in which the money moves.
The person taking the child does not pay. The parish pays that person.
The child is a liability.
Keep him, and the parish must continue providing gruel. Remove him, and a one-time payment closes the account. Five pounds looks cheaper than continued maintenance.
On this ledger he is a negative entry that five pounds can cancel.
The chimney sweep Gamfield comes to claim him. Dickens notes the accusation that Gamfield has beaten three or four boys to death.
The board agrees to the arrangement.
The price is settled at three pounds ten shillings. What finally stops it is not bargaining: a magistrate notices Oliver’s terror, questions him, and refuses to sign the indenture.
A child’s destination has first been put into a bargain over three pounds ten. On this occasion, someone finally stops to look at the child himself.
## The undertaker’s shop
Oliver is eventually taken by the undertaker Sowerberry.
He sleeps beneath the counter, among the coffins.
A detail of this new arrangement is especially cruel.
Sowerberry finds work for him as a funeral mute.
That is an occupation: a figure in the funeral procession whose appearance communicates mourning. Oliver seems ideal because he is small, thin, and melancholy.
His appearance, Sowerberry thinks, will be particularly effective at children’s funerals.
Notice the reasoning.
Something entirely real in Oliver, his hunger and misery, has been discovered to be an asset.
His sorrowful face can earn money.
Once suffering has a use, those profiting from it have less reason to relieve it.
## More than personal cruelty
The horror is not that there is no cruelty. It is that cruelty can accompany the ordinary fulfillment of a role.
Oliver is beaten. The master uses his ladle; Bumble uses his cane. Noah’s bullying is not the whole of the violence.
Yet in the institution’s account, its officers can still say they have fed him according to the stipulated ration.
The board is also willing to send him to Gamfield. A magistrate’s refusal, not the board’s protection, interrupts that arrangement.
People at each stage can describe themselves as performing their assigned work.
Bumble administers the procedure. The master distributes the ration. The board meets over expenditure. Sowerberry conducts his business.
By their separate procedures, they can consider themselves competent.
And a nine-year-old has scarcely known what it is to eat enough.
## His mother
Return briefly to the first chapter.
Oliver’s mother dies in the workhouse after giving birth.
She has been brought in from the street. Nobody knows who she is or where she comes from. She sees the child, kisses him, and dies.
An old workhouse attendant and a doctor are present.
The doctor has first comforted the mother. After her death, before leaving, he examines her left hand and comments on the absence of a wedding ring.
He calls it the old story.
He puts on his gloves and leaves.
Oliver enters the world through an act of classification.
Unmarried, unknown, dead: the file seems complete.
Years later, the attendant will tell of something else. The dying woman held her hand, spoke to her, and entrusted an object to her care.
The attendant stole that keepsake and later pawned it. At her own death she leaves the pawn ticket.
What the woman said has had no place in the official account of her.
## Next
Oliver eventually runs away.
He leaves the undertaker, walks for seven days toward London, and is nearly starving when help appears.
Someone gives him food.
It is a boy not much older than he is, dressed in an adult’s clothes, with a hat that threatens to fall off, with an extraordinarily assured manner: Jack Dawkins, the Artful Dodger.
He takes Oliver somewhere.
There is an old man, a group of boys, and handkerchiefs hanging from a line.
The old man is genial. He feeds Oliver, lets him sleep, and jokes with him.
The next essay examines that room and a change of position.
At the workhouse Oliver was a liability. Here he becomes something useful.
And usefulness, in this place, has a price.
