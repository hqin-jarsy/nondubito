Oliver is close to starving when he reaches the outskirts of London.
The Artful Dodger feeds him.
The boy is little older than Oliver, wearing an adult’s coat with rolled sleeves and a hat at an angle. He buys Oliver ham and bread, and gives him an alcoholic drink.
Come with me, he says. I know an old gentleman who will let you stay without charging you.
Oliver follows.
## The room
It is dirty and dark.
An old man toasts sausages at the fire. Boys smoke and drink at a table. Silk handkerchiefs hang from a line.
The old man is very friendly.
Fagin bows, shakes Oliver’s hand, and says he is pleased to meet him. He supplies food, drink, and a place to sleep.
The boys gather around and joke with him.
Oliver eats and sleeps better than he has for a long time.
## The lesson
The next morning, he watches a game.
Fagin walks about with a snuffbox, pocketbook, watch, spectacles, and handkerchief distributed about his person.
The Dodger and another boy follow him.
Their task is to remove each object without his noticing.
When they succeed, he laughs and praises them.
Oliver enjoys watching. It seems an amusing game.
Fagin later invites him to try. He takes a handkerchief from Fagin’s pocket.
Fagin pats his head and predicts that he will be as accomplished as the Dodger.
## More than deception
Pause here. It is often said that Fagin deceives Oliver.
He certainly does.
He describes the stolen goods as fine workmanship and encourages Oliver to think he can learn to make handkerchiefs. The boy does not understand what the game is preparing him for.
But lies alone do not explain the lesson’s power. Real provisions accompany the deception, drawing an uninformed child closer one step at a time.
First: food.
After seven days of hunger and walking, the Dodger is the first person in this new world to treat him kindly.
Second: somewhere to stay.
A nine-year-old with nowhere to go is given a bed.
Third: companions.
This matters most. Oliver has scarcely known acceptance. The workhouse counts him; the undertaker puts him beneath the counter; Noah bullies him.
At Fagin’s, boys joke with him, teach him to play, and treat him as one of their own.
Fourth: praise.
When he removes the handkerchief, Fagin pats his head and tells him he has a future.
For Oliver, praise is something almost unknown.
## What does Fagin want?
Fagin understands the account behind these four gifts.
He is cultivating instruments.
The novel later makes the business plain. The boys steal for him. He receives the goods and provides their maintenance.
It is a commercial arrangement.
Its crucial step is not merely teaching them to steal.
It is making other destinations harder to reach.
Watch how that happens.
Once a child participates in theft, exposure becomes a threat. Reporting to the authorities may expose his own involvement. His former home may not welcome him; elsewhere, nobody will vouch for him.
More importantly, Fagin knows about him while he knows little about Fagin.
Fagin therefore keeps the boys by more than locked doors.
He also makes them feel that outside there is nowhere to stand.
## Oliver’s first outing
Oliver first goes out with the Dodger and Charley Bates.
He does not know what they intend.
At a bookstall, he sees the Dodger put a hand into an elderly gentleman’s pocket.
For a moment he stands astonished. Then he understands.
Terrified, he runs.
The running boy is taken for the thief.
The gentleman cries for pursuit. People join in. Oliver is knocked down, struck, and taken to the magistrate.
This is one of the novel’s sharpest episodes.
Of the three boys, Oliver is the one who has not participated in the theft.
The Dodger and Bates escape, joining the cries to catch the thief.
The boy who fled in horror is captured.
The crowd is sure it has the right person.
The evidence seems simple: he ran.
## The elderly gentleman
The gentleman is Mr. Brownlow.
One opening in the case is that Brownlow himself is unsure.
He admits that seeing the boy run led him to pursue. He cannot be certain Oliver took the handkerchief, and asks the magistrate for leniency.
The magistrate will not properly listen.
Fang is bad-tempered and arbitrary. He cuts Brownlow short and sentences Oliver to three months’ hard labor.
The bookstall keeper supplies the evidence that saves him.
He arrives to explain that Oliver stood by while another boy took the handkerchief.
Someone who does not know Oliver speaks about what he has actually seen.
He almost fails to get a hearing: Fang rebukes him and initially orders him away.
He insists on speaking.
Oliver is released.
## A few days with Brownlow
Brownlow takes him home.
These are among the happiest days Oliver has known.
He falls ill and is cared for. He has a clean bed, food, people who speak kindly to him, new clothes, and books.
The significance of that interlude emerges in what follows.
Fagin’s circle finds him.
Nancy and Sikes seize him in the street.
Nancy embraces him, calling him her brother, and tells the crowd that he is a naughty runaway whom she has finally found.
To the bystanders, this looks like a welcome recovery: a sister has located her missing brother.
Nobody intervenes for Oliver.
He is dragged away.
## The second half of Fagin’s lesson
Back in the den, another part of the lesson begins.
They mock Oliver’s new clothes, make him change into his old ones, and take the new suit away.
Brownlow bought those clothes. He also entrusted Oliver with a five-pound note to pay a bookseller four pounds ten shillings and bring back the change.
The note is taken and ends up with Sikes. The clothes are put into Fagin’s keeping.
The effect reaches beyond taking his possessions: it makes Oliver appear a thief to Brownlow.
From Brownlow’s side, a trusted boy has gone out with money, books, and new clothes, then disappeared.
That is exactly what Fagin wants.
He does lock Oliver up.
He also works to close the route back.
Alongside confinement and violence, there is the effort to make the people outside believe that the child is a thief.
As we have seen, a lock is not the only thing that keeps someone inside. The fear of having no place outside can hold them there too.
Fagin locks doors and dismantles bridges.
## Two accounts beside one another
Put the first two essays together.
The workhouse counts Oliver as a liability.
A head, a portion of gruel, a negative entry to be removed for five pounds: in that account, he costs money.
With Fagin, he becomes useful.
What does Fagin see in him? A clean-looking child with an innocent face, who might pass for someone from a respectable home.
That appearance has a use.
A boy who looks like a little gentleman is valuable to thieves. He can avoid suspicion, enter respectable places, and disarm strangers.
Later, Sikes wants his small body for a different reason: it will fit through the window of a house he plans to rob.
On this side, Oliver is an excellent instrument.
The relation between the two sides is the point.
The workhouse calculates him as a liability and tries to remove him.
After removal, he lands somewhere that calculates him as an asset.
Useless and useful can lie only a street apart.
The accounting form is shared.
## Fagin is not a supernatural devil
One problem in this character must not be passed over.
Dickens writes Fagin through the anti-Jewish prejudices of his society.
Repeatedly naming him by his Jewishness, and using stock associations of appearance, greed, and degradation, the novel draws on antisemitic stereotypes. Dickens later altered some of this language after criticism.
That is a serious defect, not something the interpretation should excuse.
It does not prevent us from examining the character’s construction.
Reducing him to someone who enjoys evil explains too little.
He conducts business and manages risk, sending the children out rather than doing their work himself. His pet names and moments of attachment need not all be sheer performance.
What is most frightening is that food, shelter, companions, and praise are real.
In some immediate respects, the boys receive more there than the workhouse would give them.
And the provision has a price.
## Next
Another person belongs to that room.
Her name is Nancy.
She lives with Sikes and has been under Fagin’s influence since childhood. She says she began stealing before she was half Oliver’s age and has continued for twelve years.
She is the woman who embraces Oliver in the street, calls him her brother, and helps drag him back.
Her role in the kidnapping is crucial.
A man pulling away a screaming child might invite intervention. Her performance as a sister gives the crowd a reason not to interfere.
She knows what she is doing.
The next essay turns to her.
It begins by refusing to erase that participation.
