Oliver is captured in the street and taken back.
The woman who embraces him, weeping and calling him her brother, is Nancy.
She tells the crowd that he has run away, that the family is frantic, that she has finally found him. She performs the part well.
The onlookers believe her.
Some even rebuke Oliver for distressing his sister.
Then Sikes appears, takes hold of his collar, and drags him away.
## She knows what she is doing
We must begin firmly here. This character is too easily softened into innocence.
Nancy participates.
She embraces Oliver and calls him her brother, presenting an abduction as a family taking its child home. Her performance makes a difference.
She is crucial to the kidnapping.
A man dragging a screaming boy down the street might provoke intervention. A sister collecting a runaway brother supplies an acceptable explanation.
She provides the story that allows everyone else to stand aside.
And she knows where Oliver is being taken.
She knows Fagin’s business, the dangers facing his boys, and what it means to put a child with a newly opened route to safety back into that room.
She does it nonetheless.
## Other things she does
It is not her only involvement.
She has long worked within Fagin’s operation.
After Oliver’s arrest, she also goes to the police office for information. She initially refuses. Sikes and Fagin alternate threats, promises, and inducements until she agrees.
She changes her appearance and eventually goes, convincingly performing the anxious search for a relative. The pressure she experiences does not make her participation disappear.
She steals.
She drinks.
Her first appearance is with Bet at Fagin’s rooms, where they subsequently drink.
She is an established, useful part of his world.
## Where she comes from
Now consider her history.
Dickens gives it briefly, and its few sentences carry enormous weight.
She says she began stealing before she was half Oliver’s age and has lived that way for twelve years.
Her childhood belongs beside those of the Dodger, Bates, and Oliver. She has simply been in that world much longer.
She has grown from a child thief into someone able to act a part, keep watch, and obtain information at the police office.
At one point she puts it bluntly.
The streets have been her home.
She has been cold and hungry there since childhood, and there she remains.
Another home has scarcely been imaginable to her.
## Nancy and Sikes
The man beside her is Bill Sikes.
In the contrast this essay draws, he is the novel’s most unqualified brute.
Fagin calculates his business. Sikes lashes out, robs, and kills. His violence toward his dog makes the animal fear him, yet it continues to follow.
He also beats Nancy.
The narrative presents violence as part of the ordinary conditions of their relationship.
She stays with him.
When she is urged to leave, her attachment becomes unmistakable.
He is the man to whom she is bound.
She describes both her feeling for him and the grip of the old life, as something from which she cannot simply turn away.
## The thing she cannot bear
Yet there is a line within this person, and that is the subject here.
After years in this world, she cannot bear what is happening to Oliver.
Why?
The distinction is particular.
Oliver has not yet been made into a thief.
The Dodger and Bates already take pride in their skill. Bates will later reform, but at this moment Oliver has not reached the position they occupy.
He is different.
Dragged back, he still cries, struggles, and hopes to return to Brownlow. He has not accepted what they accept.
Nancy sees something of her own childhood in him.
She tells Fagin and Sikes, in one of the novel’s most powerful speeches, what this life has meant.
She has stolen for twelve years, since she was a very small child. The cold streets have been her home, and the man who put her there has kept her at this work by day and night.
She condemns herself for helping bring the boy back.
## Taking him to Sikes
Later, Oliver is taken on a burglary.
Sikes needs his small body to enter through a window.
Nancy has previously shielded him from a beating.
When she comes to collect him, she says she has done all she can and shows him bruises on her neck and arms.
She nevertheless takes him to Sikes. She does not take part in the housebreaking itself. While trying to comfort the boy, she is still delivering him into danger.
The burglary fails. Oliver is shot and subsequently abandoned.
The household he was forced to enter takes him in: Mrs. Maylie and Rose.
Nancy does not then know where he is.
## Listening in secret
Monks appears.
He is tied to Oliver’s hidden family history. We eventually learn that he is Oliver’s half-brother on his father’s side and wants to destroy him for the sake of an inheritance.
He bargains with Fagin: money in return for turning Oliver into a genuine criminal, someone whose future can be ruined.
Nancy overhears.
She listens on separate occasions and pieces together the scheme.
Then she acts.
She drugs Sikes with laudanum, waits for him to sleep, and goes out alone.
## The hotel meeting
She seeks Rose.
Their meeting takes place at a hotel.
A woman from the lowest reaches of London enters a young gentlewoman’s room. Nancy trembles and hesitates at the threshold.
Rose asks her to sit.
She will not.
Then she tells her story.
She explains Monks, the bargain, and the plan to ruin Oliver. She has listened and remembered, and gives the information coherently.
She also says something about herself.
She helped bring Oliver back to Fagin.
She does not make herself innocent in the telling.
## The refusal
The next part is where this character becomes most difficult.
Rose wants her to stay.
She offers help, urging her to leave the dangerous place and find a safe home.
That concern begins before Nancy has supplied the whole story. Rose has not yet met Brownlow. His later offer at the bridge belongs to another meeting.
A route away is being offered to her.
She refuses.
Why?
The next essay will consider that question.
For now, the important point is that she has not made her own rescue a condition of helping the boy.
## She does not bargain with it
Consider the account.
She helps a child who has no family claim upon her.
Oliver is neither her relative nor a close friend. Their time together has been brief, and she is one of the people who deceived and captured him.
She does not demand a benefit for herself if his rescue succeeds.
Rose thanks her and wants to help. Nancy nevertheless intends to return to Fagin and Sikes’s world, with its old dangers.
She understands the risk of what she is doing.
She trembles and repeatedly insists that Rose must not follow her, investigate her, or reveal her part in the disclosure.
She proposes a place and time for contact: London Bridge, between eleven and midnight on Sunday nights.
Discovery could cost her her life at Sikes’s hands.
She goes ahead.
## Next
At the next meeting, Rose brings Brownlow to learn more about Monks.
Nancy comes as arranged.
This time Fagin has someone follow her.
It is Noah, the former undertaker’s apprentice who bullied Oliver and has since come to London and joined Fagin.
He follows her to the bridge and hides near the steps to listen.
He reports to Fagin.
Fagin tells Sikes.
The final essay examines that night.
It also returns to the refusal: Nancy could accept help, yet goes back.
She speaks of love and of a life that holds her fast. Those words do not finish explaining the decision for us.
