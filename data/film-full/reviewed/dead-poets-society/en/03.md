# Not a Tribute, but a Retraction

## How the Investigation Begins

After Neil's death, Welton's first concern is the world outside. Parents will telephone. Newspapers may write about it. Donors will want an explanation, and next year's admissions will require an account of what happened. In the assembly hall, Nolan announces that the school will conduct a thorough investigation.

Notice his choice of words. He speaks of investigating responsibility, not understanding causes. The two sound close. In practice, they are entirely different: one asks what happened, the other asks who is to bear the blame.

Finding out what happened would draw in a great many people. It would draw in a father who would not listen to his son, a production line that sends boys into the Ivy League, and the four words hanging on the wall. None of these things can be disposed of.

Finding someone to bear the blame is much simpler. Only one new thing has entered this building that year: an English teacher, an employee who can be dismissed at any time, with no ties to the donors or to any of the parents.

**What the school wants has never been to know why Neil died. It wants a document that can close the case. And the act of standing on a desk is not a lyrical gesture. It is the withdrawal of a signature in front of those who signed.**

## The Shape of the Answer It Wants

To see what a document capable of closing the case looks like, work backward from the outcome. The school's eventual conclusion is itself a set of instructions. It tells us the shape this document must take to be usable.

When the investigation ends, the school concludes that Keating bears full responsibility for everything that has happened that term. Two expressions matter here: “full” and the unspoken “no one else bears any.”

For this conclusion to hold, it needs a set of signatures. They are not there to establish the truth. The truth has been settled before anyone signs. The signatures are there to give the document effect.

With the students' signatures, it is no longer merely the headmaster's account. It is an investigative finding supported by the testimony of those involved. It can be shown to the parents, the board, the people who call.

From the beginning, then, this investigation has been looking for witnesses, not an answer. The answer was written the moment Nolan said Keating's name. All the work remaining is to complete the formalities and make that answer presentable.

A document that can close a case needs no causes. It needs someone who can be disposed of and a stack of signatures that can be produced. Once it has both, the matter is settled. No one will bring it up when admissions come around next year.

## Called In Separately, One by One

The technical heart of this investigation lies not in what it asks, but in the form of the questioning. Ask the same question in a different form and the answer can be entirely different. The form is decided by the person asking.

This is not a meeting. Nolan does not bring the boys into a room together to tell the story in one another's presence. Nor does he let them consult. He calls them into his office one at a time, closes the door, and has each boy's parents sitting beside him.

The effect of this arrangement is very specific. There is only one sixteen- or seventeen-year-old in the room. Opposite him is the headmaster. Beside him are his own parents. He does not know what the boy before him said or what the boy after him will say. Above all, he does not know whether, if he refuses to sign, he will be the only one.

Cameron is the first to speak. He gives his account, then comes out and tries to persuade the others to do the same. By the time the later boys are called in, names are already on the document. The question has changed from whether to speak to whether to be left alone.

**Questioning them separately is the most important technical detail in the procedure.** There is no need to threaten anyone. It is enough to prevent each person from seeing what the others are doing.

## Cameron's Reasons

Cameron is usually read as an informer, cowardly and selfish. That reading is too easy. It also misses what is genuinely difficult about this scene. The difficulty is not that someone betrays the others. It is that his reasoning is complete.

His argument goes like this: the truth will come out sooner or later, so we should cooperate before we are found out. Keating is the teacher. He encouraged us. The responsibility belongs to him in the first place. If we do not talk, we will be the ones who suffer. We will lose our places at school and our futures, and none of us will be able to help the others.

Every one of those claims can stand. The affair really will come to light. The cave, the society, the roll book are all there. They cannot be hidden. Keating really is the person who took them up onto the teacher's desk and told them to tear pages out of their textbooks.

Refusing to cooperate really can lead to expulsion. An example will be supplied almost immediately. Cameron offers a complete line of reasoning, one that can persuade parents and can also persuade the person making it.

More important still is where the reasoning comes from. Tradition, Honor, Discipline, Excellence. Cameron does exactly what those four words have taught him: obey the institution, protect his place, put his future first. He is not this order's traitor. He is its best student.

**No one in this scene breaks the rules, and a document is produced that places the cause of a death on one person's shoulders.**

## Charlie Does Not Sign

In the corridor, Charlie hears out Cameron's argument, then punches him in the face and knocks him down. The others cannot hold him back. The punch solves nothing. He knows it, and so does everyone around him.

When he is called into the office, he does not sign. Nolan gives him a chance, asks him to name the others and cooperate, tells him it is for his own good. He refuses. The result is expulsion. He becomes the first student ever expelled from Welton.

The cost must be stated, not merely the beauty of the gesture. Expulsion means his enrollment is void, his university applications ruined. It means going home to face his own father. It means beginning his life again somewhere else. What he pays for this is everything.

**Someone did not sign, and he paid the price himself.** That fact makes “we were all forced” an incomplete statement. It is still true. It is simply no longer the only possibility.

On the day Charlie leaves, the film does not show him saying goodbye to anyone, nor give him a sentimental parting shot. His departure resembles so much else he has done: direct, leaving no room for compromise. He never appears in the film again.

## Todd Signs

Todd is among the last to be called in. By his turn, the names of several boys called in before him are already on the paper. The document has all but taken shape. Only the final few strokes are missing. He signs.

To write fairly about this scene, we must first lay out the circumstances in the room. He is sixteen. His parents sit beside him, watching him all the time. They do not say a word on his behalf.

His brother was an honors graduate of Welton. This has weighed on him since his first day here. Even the desk set sent to him is the same. And he has just lost the only friend who drew him out of silence.

He cries. His hand shakes as he writes his name on the paper. When he is done, he puts down the pen without looking up. He does not defend himself. Nor, like Cameron, has he prepared a line of reasoning to justify what he has done.

This is the key to the second half of the essay. **The signature records not what he believes, but only that he signed.** What remains on the paper is handwriting, not judgment.

On paper, the document says that the students confirm Keating's full responsibility. What has actually happened is that a sixteen-year-old has written his name in front of his parents as instructed.

On the page, those two things look exactly alike. Even the handwriting belongs to the same person. Yet they are not the same thing. In this office, there is no box in which to record that distinction. No one intends to record it either.

## The Last Lesson, and Those Words

The investigation ends, the conclusion is delivered, and Keating is dismissed. Unable to find an immediate replacement, the school has Nolan take over the class's English lessons himself. He takes his place at the front, where the students once took turns standing on the teacher's desk.

He tells them to open their textbooks and read Pritchard's introduction on how to score a poem. When they reach the place, the page is missing: the page Keating had them tear out. Nolan asks what happened, has a copy of the book passed over on loan, and has the reading continue, sentence by sentence.

At this point, Keating opens the door. He has come for the belongings he left at the back of the classroom. No one speaks. The students keep their heads down. Nolan tells him to hurry. He takes a few things from the cupboard, holds them against his chest, and turns to leave.

Todd stands up. His first word is not “Captain.” What he says is: sir, they made us sign it. You have to believe me. From the front of the room, Nolan shouts at him to sit down. He does not sit down. Instead, he begins climbing onto his desk.

**Those are the words he calls out before he stands on the desk. The gesture is not a general tribute. It retracts a specific document, in front of the person who obtained it.**

## Some Do Not Stand

After Todd climbs up, others follow one by one. First one, then three or four, then half the class has its feet on the desktops. Nolan shouts from the front, louder and louder. Not one person pays him any attention.

Cameron does not stand. He stays in his seat, looking up at those around him, his face uncomprehending, even a little anxious. A few others remain seated too. The film gives them no close-ups and has no one reproach them. It simply leaves them sitting there, in the same frame as those who stand.

We must distinguish what this gesture can do from what it cannot. We cannot blur that line. Keating is still dismissed and will leave once he has gathered his things. Charlie is still expelled. By now, he is no longer in the building.

Neil is still dead. The document still exists, locked in some drawer in the headmaster's office. Not one signature can be erased. Not one word of the school's conclusion will change.

What the gesture accomplishes is something else. For the first time, these boys decide for themselves where to stand in this classroom, and they do so in front of the person who can expel them. The earlier times they stood on the teacher's desk, it was because the teacher told them to.

Keating stands in the doorway and looks back around at the boys on their desks. He thanks them, then leaves, closing the door behind him. Nolan is still at the front shouting at them to sit down. Some are standing. Some are sitting. The page is still missing from the textbook.

*This essay is based on the 1989 film, with a running time of approximately 128 minutes.*
