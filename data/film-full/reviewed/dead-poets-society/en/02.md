# That Night, He Went on Stage Anyway

## How Much Could He Decide for Himself?

The first time the film places Neil and his father in the same frame is in the dormitory on the first day of school. Two boys he has just met are also in the room. His father opens the door, comes in, and says in front of them: give up that assistant editor's position on the yearbook.

Neil says it is unfair, that he has only just taken it on, that he earned it himself. His father's answer is short: don't talk back. Then he leaves. The door closes. Neil sits on the edge of the bed and says nothing more. His roommate Todd watches, not daring to put in a word.

That scene establishes the rules of this family. The next few scenes fill in their reach: the school was his father's choice, the courses his father's decision. Extra chemistry in the summer. Medical school eventually, with Harvard first.

None of this was arrived at through discussion. These are announcements, delivered quite calmly. There is no need to raise a voice or give a reason, because no one in this family has ever asked why.

At school, Neil is the liveliest of them all. He has good grades and plenty of friends. He is the one who digs the old yearbook out of the library, revives the Dead Poets Society, and leads the way through the woods to the cave at midnight.

There is a great deal beneath his uniform: enthusiasm, ideas, the ability to lead. None of it can grow beyond the uniform. In his father's presence, it all folds back inside.

**All the places in his life where he can make up his own mind amount to one small patch of ground. This essay looks at what he does on that patch. As for how he later ends his life, this series passes no judgment.**

## He Went and Got the Role Himself

One day, he sees a notice on the board. A theater company in town is staging A Midsummer Night's Dream and holding open auditions. The time and place are written underneath. He stands looking at the notice for a long time, then takes it down.

No one suggests that he go. Keating has said nothing about acting. His friends have not egged him on. His father certainly would not. He sees it himself, notes the time himself, goes to audition himself, and gets the role of Puck, one of the roles with the most lines, and one of the most vivid.

When he comes back, he is radiant. He tells his friends that for the first time in his life he knows what he wants to do. This is his. From him, those words are not a figure of speech. Until now, there really has been nothing he chose for himself.

We need to see what kind of action this is. It is not rebellion. He has smashed nothing, insulted no one, set himself against nobody. He has not thought of teaching his father a lesson. He simply stopped in front of a notice, then went at the time it specified.

**For the first time in the film, he has given himself something to do, and it has nothing to do with opposing anyone.** The conflict with his father comes later. It was not his purpose.

## He Wrote the Letter Himself

The company requires a letter of consent signed by a parent. It is routine paperwork for an underage performer. Everyone has to provide one. His father will not sign. Neil knows that better than anyone. He has just had a lesson in it on the first day of school.

So he types a letter, signs his father's name, and hands it in. To be safe, he also types one for the school. Both letters are forgeries. He types them alone in the dormitory, without consulting any of his friends beforehand.

This cannot be written up as cleverness. Forgery is forgery. He deceives the theater company, deceives the school, and later deceives Keating. That a seventeen-year-old does such a thing tells us he has no second route available. It does not make this route clean.

Nor can it be written up as weakness. For the first time, instead of asking for the conditions he needs, he sets about making them himself. Until now, everything he did had to wait for someone else's approval. Only once approval arrived did it count. This time, he does not wait.

**When someone who has never been allowed to decide for himself makes his first decision, what he produces usually looks something like this: incomplete, indefensible, ready to collapse at a touch, and something he has made all by himself in a room.**

## His Father at the Dormitory Door

Rehearsals continue for several weeks. Every day, he finds a way to get out. When he returns, he talks to his friends about his lines, what the director said, the girl playing Helena. These are the scenes in which he is most at ease in the entire film. Even the pace of his speech is different.

The night before the performance, his father appears at the dormitory door. He has heard about it somewhere. The film never tells us who let the news slip. It simply places him in the doorway, his face expressionless.

He calls Neil into the corridor. Tomorrow, he says, you will tell them you are withdrawing. His reasons are these: I have sacrificed a great deal for you. You cannot do this to me. Once you finish medical school, you can do whatever you want, and I will not say a word.

Neil tries to speak. He gets halfway through “but I” and is cut off. His father finishes speaking and leaves, with a final warning to the effect that he does not want to hear about this again. The entire exchange takes less than two minutes. Neil's share of it is less than five seconds.

In this scene, his father does not hit him, does not swear, does not say anything a father might not say. What he does is announce a decision and leave. In this house, there has never been any need for an argument, because there has never been a second opinion.

## He Sees That Man from the Stage

The next evening, the play goes ahead. Neil has not told the company he is withdrawing, nor spoken another word to his father. He arrives at the theater on time, puts on his makeup and the crown woven from branches, and waits in the wings.

The early scenes go well. His Puck runs about the stage, speaks his lines, lets his movements open out, and makes the whole room laugh. His friends are in the audience. Keating has come too. Todd is applauding hard.

Halfway through, the doors at the back of the hall open. His father comes in and stands at the rear. He does not look for a seat or take off his coat. The stage is bright and the audience dark, but Neil sees clearly: that man has arrived, and he is standing there, not leaving.

**He sees him, and then he finishes the play.** This is the most important action in the entire film. It comes with no declaration, only an actor onstage continuing to speak his lines.

There is still part of the performance to go. He continues playing Puck, right through to the closing address: if we shadows have offended you, think only that you have slept here for a while. The whole audience rises to applaud.

## By Then, Every Calculation Has Failed

To understand the weight of this passage, we have to work through every calculation available to him at that moment onstage, one item at a time. Once we do, we find that not one item pays. Not even one breaks even.

His father is already there. Whether Neil now finishes the play, leaves the stage midway, or apologizes on the spot, punishment is certain. Its severity will not change much with his choice. Finishing will not make things better. Stopping will not make things better either.

Then consider what he might gain. Completing this performance will not secure his father's consent, alter the plans to transfer him, or give him anything he can trade. When the play is over, he will still have to get into that car and go home.

In other words, **the second he sees his father, every calculation of “what good will this do me?” becomes invalid. And still he finishes the play.** Between those two facts, there is nothing that could be called a motive.

What someone does when there is no benefit to be gained and no way out tells us most clearly who that person is.

His earlier choices, made while things were going well, could admit other explanations: wanting to stand out, wanting to be liked, wanting to defy his father. None of those explanations works for the part of the performance after he sees his father.

This is what the essay wants to look at. **The last decision he makes on that little patch of ground is to finish what he himself chose, with no prospect of any return.** He finishes it. He does not leave out a single line.

## What Happens Afterward

After the performance, his friends rush backstage. The director embraces him. Someone tells him how well he acted, that he was born to do this. Keating comes over and says something too. Neil stands there, still wearing his makeup and his crown.

His father takes him away from behind the crowd, speaking to no one as they leave. There is no conversation in the car either. Back home, he announces what comes next: withdrawal from Welton tomorrow, a transfer to Braden Military Academy, then Harvard, then medical school. When those ten years are over, he says, you will thank me.

Neil tries to speak. His father presses him to say what he actually wants. His mother stands to one side. He opens his mouth and cannot get anything out. It is the last time he speaks in the film. All he says is, “Nothing.”

After that night, Neil is dead. The film does not follow him into that room to film it. Nor does it have anyone explain afterward what he was thinking, still less supply a voice-over to speak for him. The next morning, the news spreads through the whole building.

This series follows a rule: how a person ends his life is his own affair. It passes no judgment and does not rank the causes on his behalf. What we can look at is what he did before that, because those actions were his.

## Back to the Play

So this essay ends in the hall, not in that room. The film itself did not go into the room to film it; this essay will not go in either. What we are looking for is not there. It is under the theater lights.

The boy onstage with the crown of branches is playing a role he found himself, using the letter he wrote himself, in a play he made time to rehearse himself. He sees the man who has opposed it from beginning to end, then performs the final two acts and finishes the closing address.

Todd and a few friends sit in the audience. They laugh and applaud, laughing louder than anyone else. At the very back stands the man who does not applaud. Light falls on the stage. The person standing in it is doing the only thing in his life that belongs to him from beginning to end.

The curtain falls. Applause rises. Someone in the front row gets up. Neil bows at the front of the stage, twice, then lifts his head and looks into the audience. He is looking toward the very back, where a man stands without applauding.

*This essay is based on the 1989 film, with a running time of approximately 128 minutes.*
