# The Face the Film Withholds

## A Face That Never Comes Into View

Chen Zuoqian is the master of this house, the man the four mistresses compete for. In more than two hours, the film never gives us a frontal shot of his face. The actor is listed in the credits and has a substantial part, yet from beginning to end the audience cannot clearly see what this man looks like.

He appears in a few recurring ways. From behind, in a long shot, silhouetted against the light, standing outside a doorway with only half his body visible, or passing through an area outside the focus. In several scenes he is speaking directly across from Songlian, but the camera refuses to turn toward him.

This is not a matter of production constraints. The actor is there, the scene is being played, the lines spoken. Only the face is withheld. To sustain this throughout a film of more than two hours requires a specially planned camera position for every scene. The slightest lapse would reveal him.

So this man acquires a peculiar kind of presence on the screen. He has a body, a voice, a will, decisions that can fall at any moment. What he lacks is a face that can be seen clearly, remembered, studied.

**The thing four people spend their lives competing for is the thing the film refuses to give a face.** It holds to that decision through the final frame, never relaxing its grip, never making an exception for a crucial scene.

## What He Says When He Speaks

He has few lines, and his sentences are short. He does not explain or genuinely negotiate, and he need not persuade anyone. His words do not necessarily sound fierce, yet each sentence is final as soon as it falls. Even when repeated, it remains a command.

On the first night, he tells Songlian: Get your feet properly massaged, then you can serve a man well. The causal relation is perfectly clear. The massage is not a service to her. It is a stage in a process performed on her body, with its purpose elsewhere.

When he learns of the false pregnancy, he says: Don't you know where you are? How dare you behave like this in my house, the Chen house? Then he storms out, slamming the door and throwing back the order: “Cover the lanterns.” From exposure to sentence, the whole thing takes less than a minute.

He is absent when Yan'er kneels in the snow. He is absent when Meishan is taken upstairs. The steward carries these things out; the mistresses relay the messages. He need only say the word at some point. The rest will run its course without his supervision.

**No one speaking to him is ever negotiating with him.** His sentences are not contributions to a conversation. They are announcements before execution. He says them and leaves, waiting for no reply. No one expects an answer in return, either.

## Someone Who Cannot Be Persuaded

What is a face for? With a face, someone can be looked at while being addressed, implored, contradicted. For an instant it might reveal a trace of yielding, something another person can seize to pull the situation back an inch.

Songlian has many things with which to persuade someone. She is nineteen, educated, articulate, quick-witted, direct, and bold enough to show her displeasure to another person's face. Elsewhere, these abilities would be capital. Each could obtain something.

In this house, that capital never serves her once. She has no scene in which she argues her case before someone who can change the outcome. The only people she can confront are the steward, the servants, and the other three mistresses. None holds the decision in their hands.

What actually passes sentence does not show its face. It sends word through the steward, displays results through the lanterns, and takes effect through “Those are the rules.” **You cannot persuade something that will not show its face. You can only gain points or lose them.**

So the four mistresses spend all their strength on one another. Not because they are stupid; quite the opposite. They identify the few places where they can exert leverage with great precision. Beyond one another, nothing is within reach. The only people they can influence are the other three contestants.

## Meishan Sings Before Dawn

Before entering this household, Meishan was a celebrated performer of female roles in an opera troupe. Her room is arranged like a stage, with costumes, painted faces, and props. The first time Songlian visits to play mahjong, she says as she enters: You can certainly tell the third sister was an opera performer.

Meishan sings whenever she likes. Before daylight, she puts on her costume and sings in the courtyard, without asking whether anyone is asleep or caring what season it is. In Songlian's first days here, this is the sound she hears in the early morning, drifting from a distance and becoming clearer phrase by phrase.

Doctor Gao once plays a record in her room, a recording from her days onstage. It is the only appearance of her past inside this house: she was once heard by many people in a place that did not bear the Chen name. Her worth then was not for this courtyard to decide.

Singing opera serves no purpose in the Chen household. Everything everyone else does points in the same direction: competing, yielding, enduring, calculating. This one activity of hers points nowhere. It cannot earn her a single lantern.

That does not make her noble. She is just as ruthless in competition. It was she who feigned illness and called the master away on Songlian's wedding night. The film does not make her innocent. It simply gives her one thing more than the others: something she can do herself, and do very well.

## The Little Room on the Roof

There is a little room on the roof, visible from the courtyard merely by looking up. Songlian asks what it is. The answer is that people have died there: two concubines from the previous generation got into trouble, and both ended up there.

That is all the answer contains. No one elaborates, no one is angry, and no one lowers their voice to tell her alone. In the Chen household, this is common knowledge, like foot massages and choosing the dishes. It is part of the place. Once you have asked, you are considered informed.

The room remains in plain sight. It is not hidden because it does not need to be. **A rule everyone knows and no one speaks of works far better than a rule written down.** Written rules can be consulted, argued with, changed. This one has no text, and therefore no place to get a grip.

After Meishan's affair with Doctor Gao is revealed, she is found, brought back, and taken into that room. The film does not show what happens inside. It shows Songlian following them onto the roof, pressing herself to the crack in the door, then turning and running down.

Meishan does not come down. The next morning, the courtyard is as usual: meals are eaten, mahjong played, servants sweep as before. The door on the roof is closed. No one looks up at it.

## You Saw Nothing

Songlian cries out in the courtyard: “Murder!” Those words appear only once in the entire film, and she alone cries them. None of the other mistresses, the steward, or the servants responds. Not one looks surprised.

They are not afraid. Fear would produce a reaction: retreating, exchanging a glance. What the film records is no reaction. Whoever is walking keeps walking; whoever is carrying something keeps carrying it. In their daily lives, this does not constitute an event that needs attending to.

The master's reply is a string of questions: What did you see? What exactly did you see? Nonsense, what did you see? You saw nothing! You're mad. You've gone mad.

We can take the structure of this speech apart. The first sentences ask questions. The last is not a question but a ruling. The ruling is not that what she says is wrong. It is that she herself no longer counts. **The person who speaks is immediately assigned to the side that need not be heard.**

This ruling requires neither evidence nor anyone's endorsement. It is delivered by the thing that never shows its face, and everyone else continues living as before. The courtyard takes no more than a day to dispose of the matter. Afterward, no one mentions Meishan again.

## The Third Courtyard Is Haunted

Soon after Meishan's death, a rumor spreads that the third courtyard is haunted: opera singing can be heard there at night. The servants take a detour, and after dark no one will go in that direction. It is the house's first explanation, and its only one.

The rumor has a source. Songlian is using the gramophone to play recordings of Meishan's old performances, over and over. She has also lit the red lanterns in Meishan's room and her own. No one has told her to do this. No one comes to stop her.

None of these actions can obtain anything. The lanterns are lit by her, not awarded to her; hanging them does not mean she has been chosen for the night. The recording is a dead person's voice. No one in the house wants to listen. Hearing it only makes them want to leave as quickly as possible.

This house has no word for the act. It has words for competing for favor, for insolence, for madness. It has no words for “someone is doing something for the person who has died.”

So it calls it a haunting. That is the nearest explanation it can piece together. It also reveals the limits of its vocabulary: an act not performed for points can only be counted here as something unclean.

## The Fifth Person

The following year, the Chen household takes in a fifth concubine, a very young woman called Wenzhu. The display on the day she arrives is the same as it was for Songlian a year earlier. Nothing has been added, nothing taken away.

The courtyard fills with activity again. Lanterns are carried in rack by rack, lit, and hung under the eaves. The gongs and drums sound as before, the servants move as quickly as before. No stage of the procedure is missing. Not one movement has changed.

Entering, Wenzhu sees someone walking slowly along the covered corridor, in black-and-white student's clothes with her hair in two plaits. Who is she? Wenzhu asks. A servant beside her answers: The former fourth mistress. She's lost her mind.

The film now gives Wenzhu a close-up. There is no bridal joy on that face, only a trace of something she cannot yet name. This is the last face the film shows frontally, and it belongs to someone who has only just arrived at the starting point.

In the past year, two people have died in this courtyard. **The newcomer does not need to know, because this system does not run on remembering anyone.** It runs on someone standing at the gate every evening, waiting. Tomorrow, someone will do that again.

Songlian walks along the corridor. Out of one doorway, into the next, then the next. The lanterns of the fourth courtyard are dark. Those of the fifth have just been lit. Their red shines brightly.

*This essay refers to the 1991 version, approximately 125 minutes long.*
