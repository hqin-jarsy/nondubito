# Who Set the Scoreboard?

## A Procedure Performed Every Day

This is how the rules of the Chen household work. At dusk, the mistresses wait at the entrances to their respective courtyards. The steward comes to announce where the master will spend the night. Great red lanterns are hung in the chosen courtyard on the spot. That night its mistress receives a foot massage; the next day she chooses the dishes for the whole household.

The servants carry the lanterns into the courtyard, light them rack by rack, and hang them under the eaves. The process is accompanied by the urgent percussion pattern known as jijifeng in Peking opera. As soon as the gongs and drums begin, the servants quicken their steps. The courtyards all hear it at once. No one needs to ask.

The right to choose the dishes is especially ingenious. The next day, the whole family sits at one table. All four women know whose choices are being served. Over the course of a meal, the previous night's result is repeated, and repeated in front of everyone.

This system did not exist in history. Lighting the lanterns, covering them, massaging feet, choosing the menu: all of it was invented for the film. This is often cited as an indictment: folklore fabricated for foreigners, an orderly array of rituals no real old-fashioned household ever possessed.

As a matter of fact, the charge holds up. But to regard it as a fatal flaw, one must first assume that a rule needs a pedigree before people will take it seriously. **A rule does not have to be ancient to be obeyed.**

The film shows precisely why it works so well. Performed once a day, publicly, with everyone present, it converts an invisible thing, whom the master prefers tonight, into a number anyone can read at once.

## The Sound of the Bell-Hammers

The foot massage follows a specific method. First the feet are soaked in aged vinegar, then covered with a red bridal veil. An older maid taps the soles back and forth with two little bell-hammers. The clear sound repeats, stroke after stroke, audible even through the courtyard walls.

The crucial feature is that it makes a sound. Lanterns address the eyes; they cannot enter someone else's room. The bell-hammers can. The other three courtyards know who has been chosen without anyone stepping outside, and they must listen for quite some time.

At her first massage, Songlian feels awkward. The whole display seems pointless, and the tapping makes her uncomfortable all over. It is the reaction of someone fresh from university. She has not yet learned to hear anything else in the sound.

After a while, she says the massage feels good: with her feet properly massaged, she has energy for everything. Later still, she says she cannot sleep without the sound. **Something that once made her uncomfortable has become the reason she lies awake at night.**

Once she loses favor, the sound comes from another courtyard. She lies in her room listening to the bell-hammers in the second courtyard, every stroke distinct, until they stop. By now no one needs to explain what the sound means. The sound itself is the score.

## Sometimes There Are No Instructions

On several evenings, the mistresses arrange their hair, dress respectably, and stand at their courtyard entrances as usual. The steward comes and says the master has given no instructions today. They each return to their rooms. The next evening, they do it all again.

These scenes are filmed very plainly. No music, no reaction shots. People stand, then disperse, without so much as a complaint in between. Yet they reveal more about the system than anything else: the procedure continues, while its content is empty.

Songlian has complained about this. If the master has instructions, she says, why not just send word? Why all this ceremony? The steward replies with those four Chinese characters: “Those are the rules.” His voice is level. He is not reprimanding her, only stating a fact.

When a rule can run without any content at all, the people obeying it are no longer doing so to collect something in return. The act of waiting at the gate has become more solid than the result of the wait.

Notice who keeps this going. Not the master. He is not even present that evening; perhaps he is not in the house at all. The four mistresses maintain it themselves: they come out on time, take their places, wait, and disperse. No one comes to take attendance.

## The Four Women Never Attack the Rules

The film depicts the struggles among the four mistresses in dense detail. Feigning illness to call the man away. Passing a message to someone's face and reporting her behind her back. Having a maid make a cloth doll and stick pins into it as a curse. Buying off someone in a rival's rooms. The methods grow ever more intricate and ever more effective.

From beginning to end, no one ever says this: Why should a lantern decide who chooses tomorrow's dishes? The question never appears in the film, not even as a joke.

One explanation is that they lack the strength to resist. The film's evidence does not support it. They have plenty of strength. Zhuoyun's meticulous thinking, Meishan's ruthless methods, Songlian's quick mind: these are genuine abilities. Strength is not in short supply. It has only one direction: toward one another.

The rules therefore remain securely in place, never once touched. They do not even need defending, because all four women work for them. Every contest confirms them once again. The fiercer the struggle, the firmer the rules.

After her lanterns have been covered, Songlian says something devastating to Meishan: What is a person worth in this courtyard? Like a dog, a cat, a rat. Like anything except a human being. It is the clearest statement in the film.

Then she goes back to competing. **Seeing exactly what a system is and ceasing to reckon by its terms are two different things.** The film arranges no consequences for that remark. It does not let it alter anyone's next move.

## The Second Mistress Is Not a Bad Person

Zhuoyun is one of the film's most reviled characters. The audience's verdict is a bodhisattva's face and a scorpion's heart: the warmest to your face, the cruelest behind your back. The pin-stuck doll is her idea. She is also the one who reports the false pregnancy.

At first, she is kinder to Songlian than anyone else in the household. She sends gifts, speaks in confidence, offers advice, and makes the newly arrived nineteen-year-old feel that at last there is someone here she can talk to. When Songlian discovers that Zhuoyun ordered the doll to be made, the shock is colder than the covering of the lanterns.

That verdict is convenient and saves us trouble. Declare her a bad person, and the rules recede into scenery. The whole affair becomes something that might have ended well had one malicious person not ruined it.

Her position needs to be spelled out. She entered the household before Songlian, her face is no longer young, and she has borne a daughter, not a son. By this courtyard's reckoning, she has neither youth nor a son. The only capital she has left is her resourcefulness.

Nothing she does breaks these rules. She has not changed them or taken a single point beyond what they allow. She has simply used them to their furthest extent, earlier and more precisely than anyone else. **To read her as a bad person is to concede that these rules could have produced a good outcome.**

The film gives her one very brief shot. Songlian is cutting her hair when the scissors slip and cut her ear. Blood runs down. Zhuoyun cries out. She looks up at Songlian. The film does not tell us what is in that look.

## Songlian Becomes the Second Mistress

After losing favor, Songlian devises the false pregnancy. She lies in bed pretending to be weak and falsely claims to be pregnant. Delighted, the master orders lanterns to be kept burning day and night. This is the highest score the system offers.

For the days when those lanterns hang, the fourth courtyard is lit even in daylight. She stays motionless in bed, food is brought to her room, and the servants tread carefully as they come and go. She has won more than any other mistress, all of it resting on something that does not exist.

Yan'er exposes her. Washing Songlian's clothes, she discovers the telltale sign and reports it to Zhuoyun, who reports it to the master. Doctor Gao then establishes that she is not pregnant. The master storms out, slams the door, and throws back the order: “Cover the lanterns.” He has asked why she deceived him, but has given her no opportunity to change the verdict.

This sequence of actions is exactly Zhuoyun's usual method: do not show yourself, do not act directly, deliver the information where it needs to go, and let the rules descend of their own accord.

What Songlian does next is the other half of the same method. She has long known that Yan'er secretly keeps lanterns in her room and has said nothing. Once she learns that Yan'er informed on her, she reveals the secret.

The film handles this transformation by not marking it. There is no musical cue, no shot of hesitation, no one standing by to say, You've changed. Songlian does it with the practiced ease of someone who has done it many times. It is her first time.

**Every round she wins or loses is scored on a board someone else has set.** From the first time she acts to gain points, only one difference remains between her and Zhuoyun: which of them calculates more accurately.

## Yan'er Wants the Right to Compete

Yan'er, the maid of the fourth courtyard, is in her teens. She wants to become a concubine. The whole household knows, and no one takes it seriously. Songlian has caught the master flirting with her. There is no fear in Yan'er's eyes, only defiance.

In her own servants' room, she hides a collection of old lanterns and lights them secretly at night. The room is small and dark. The lanterns were used and discarded by others, and the light falls on her alone. No one will see it. No one will award her points for it.

When Songlian first discovers the room's secret, she also sees the cloth doll bearing her own name. She keeps quiet and makes no report. Yan'er kneels before her and begs once. It is the only time in the film that Yan'er bows her head to her.

This collection of lanterns is the film at its sharpest. **She does not even qualify to compete, and qualification is precisely what she wants.** For her, the lanterns are not a reward; an old lantern buys no practical advantage. They mean being counted as someone who can be given a score.

After the secret comes out, the lanterns are burned in the courtyard in front of everyone. Yan'er is made to kneel in the snow. The steward brings a message: The mistress says that if you admit you were wrong, it will all be over. That route remains open before her. She can get up at any time.

She does not admit it. She kneels until she faints, is carried away, and dies in hospital. It is not the punishment she refuses; kneeling can be endured. What she refuses is “I was wrong,” because saying those words would mean conceding that she had never been entitled even to want such a thing.

## The Black Covers

The covering of the lanterns is filmed at length. The servants bring ladders, climb them one after another, and slip black cloth covers over the red lanterns. One, two. They finish one row of the corridor and move to the next. No one speaks. Their movements are practiced. They have clearly done this before.

Once covered, the lanterns remain hanging. A little red shows through the gaps in the cloth, but light can neither enter nor escape. The courtyard darkens, darker than when there had been no lanterns at all, because this is brightness revoked.

What has been revoked is not affection. The master has never loved any of them. The film is unambiguous about this, and the four mistresses have never misunderstood it themselves. What has been revoked is the score: the right to choose the dishes, the angle at which servants bow their heads, whether others make way as she passes.

Songlian stands in the courtyard, watching from the first lantern to the last. She neither intervenes nor speaks. She knows exactly what these covers mean, because she learned to read the system on her first day here. The more accurately she reads it, the harder it is to stand there.

By the time the last lantern is covered, it is completely dark. The servants put away the ladders and file out. The courtyard gate closes behind them. Two rows of invisible red remain.

*This essay refers to the 1991 version, approximately 125 minutes long.*
