# There Is Only One Position

## When That Door Opens

The doorbell rings on the rainy night. The four are drinking in their employers' living room. Moon-gwang stands outside, soaked through, her hair stuck to her face. She says she has left something in the basement.

Chung-sook lets her in. Moon-gwang goes into the kitchen and, without a word, shifts a cabinet. A hidden door appears behind it. She presses a switch. A steep staircase leads down to a concrete room with leftover food piled inside.

This is the turning point of the film. Until now, the audience has thought it was watching a story about poor people deceiving rich people, with the tricks supplying the interest. From this moment, it becomes a different story, and that story is much uglier to watch.

Geun-sae lies in the basement. He has lived here for years on the food his wife brings each day. He lifts his head and narrows his eyes. Instead of the familiar person, he sees several strangers.

**The fight that follows is not about hatred between poor people. It happens because there is only one position.** How many such positions exist is not for those fighting over them to decide.

## Two Families Recognize Each Other

Moon-gwang understands quickly. She sees all four Kims in the house, their bearing, the practiced understanding in the glances they exchange. One sentence exposes them: you are a family.

The Kims understand too. They see the man in the basement, the words carved into the wall, the accumulated food wrappers. They know someone has already been living beneath the house, since long before they arrived.

At this moment, both sides see the same thing: the others are doing what they themselves are doing. Only one family does it in the open and the other in hiding; one has more people, the other fewer.

Their reaction is the same as well. Moon-gwang's first response is to take out her phone, record a video, and threaten exposure. The Kims' first response is to seize the phone, cover mouths, and pin people down. Not one of the six imagines a third way.

From this point on, everything said on either side follows the same form: this position was mine. Moon-gwang invokes seniority; she arrived before everyone else. The Kims invoke possession in the present; it is theirs now, already in their hands.

Both reasons hold, and neither resolves anything, because **the two reasons lay claim to the same thing, and there is only one of it.**

## The Fight

What follows is the film's only real fight. All the earlier tricks and schemes had proceeded without anyone once coming to blows. Here they do, and the struggle lasts a long time.

Four people against two, grappling back and forth between the basement and the kitchen. Someone is pushed down the stairs, someone is tied up, someone is gagged. The film makes the struggle solid and physical, without music and without giving either side an angle that justifies it.

We need to see what they are fighting for. It is not money. From beginning to end, nobody has touched a penny of the Parks' money. Nor is it a grudge. Before this night, the two groups did not know each other, not even by name.

They are fighting over entitlement: who is entitled to stay in the house, who is entitled to keep receiving that food. Four jobs and a basement add up to a limited number of positions. Now six people need them. However you count, there are not enough.

**The owner of the house is not in this fight.** He is camping, or driving home. Throughout the struggle, nobody thinks of going to speak to him. In the calculations of these six people, the number of positions is a given. It cannot be negotiated.

## The Family Upstairs Does Not Know Their Names

This asymmetry must be made clear, because it is the backbone of this essay: six people are fighting desperately downstairs, while the owner is absent and does not know it is happening.

Mr. Park knows he has a driver, a housekeeper, a tutor, and an art therapist, all recommended by people he knows. He does not know those four people are a family.

He also does not know that a man lives beneath his home, that he has lived there for years, or, still less, that the man bows to him every day.

In the whole film, he never asks Ki-taek where he lives or how many people there are in his family. Once, in conversation with his wife, he asks what the smell resembles. He compares it to the smell of people who ride the subway. That is his only attempt to locate this man's life.

The two in the basement do not register in his world at all. The former housekeeper's only fault, in his own words, was that she ate enough for two. It is the most exact misunderstanding in the film: **he gets the fact right and the whole situation wrong.**

One house, three families. The family at the top does not know there are two beneath it. The one in the middle has just discovered another below. The one at the bottom believes it has a place in this order, a place graciously granted to it.

This asymmetry is nobody's fault, nor does it require anyone to maintain it deliberately. It results from each person looking only into their own square. The square that sees the least happens to be at the very top. It also happens to be the only one where people can live very well without looking outside it.

## The Birthday Party

The next day is Da-song's birthday party. Canopies go up in the garden, tables are set on the lawn, and a houseful of guests is invited. Mr. Park arranges a scene of playing Indians, assigns Ki-taek a part, and demonstrates the movements himself.

Ki-taek has just come from the gymnasium. The previous night he slept on the floor among hundreds of people. His home is underwater. In borrowed clothes, he puts on the headdress and stands at the edge of the lawn waiting for his entrance.

The film shows the catastrophe unfolding very quickly. The man from the basement comes up. The garden falls into chaos. Guests scatter.

Here I will describe only what this sequence does structurally: everything that has been shut beneath the house appears all at once on the sunlit lawn, before people who never knew it existed.

Then comes Mr. Park's last gesture. He wants the car keys to take someone to hospital. They have fallen on the ground beneath a person's body. He reaches for them. As he gets hold of them, his other hand rises to cover his nose.

The gesture is involuntary. He says nothing, insults nobody, does not even look at the person. Inside his square, it is simply the normal response to an unpleasant smell. It has nothing to do with who is in front of him.

Two steps away, Ki-taek sees the gesture. Only the previous night he had moved out of the water and spent the night on the gymnasium floor. He alone knows where the smell on his body comes from.

## Morse Code in the Basement

Afterward, the police search the city for Ki-taek. He has not fled far, has not left the city, has not even moved to another district.

He has returned to the house, entered the kitchen, moved the cabinet, gone down the stairs, and taken up residence in the concrete room. Later the house is sold to a German family. The new owners move in without knowing someone is in the basement.

What he does there is exactly what Geun-sae did: live on the supplies left behind, come out at night to look for food, hit the switch with his head to make the lights on the stairs blink. He sends Morse code. The message is a letter to his son.

**The position has not changed. The person occupying it has.** The same room, the same switch, the same signal, the same hope that somebody above will receive it. The previous man lived here for years without discovery. This one probably will too.

The film shows this quietly. There is no grief or indignation in the music, no shot that makes an accusation. It simply lets this man continue the life the other man lived, day after day.

His son Ki-woo eventually receives the signal. From the hill he sees the light blinking in the distance, writes it down, and deciphers it. The Morse message Da-song once failed to finish translating is finally completed by someone else.

## The Letter

After deciphering the letter, Ki-woo writes a reply. That reply is the film's final voice-over and the last time anyone in it says what they intend to do.

He says he has a plan. He will earn money, a great deal of money, and buy the house. When that day comes, he will stand waiting in the garden. His father will only have to climb the stairs from the basement and step into the sunlight. He will need to do nothing else, say nothing at all.

The film gives the letter a sequence of images: Ki-woo is older, well dressed, standing in the garden of the house. The lawn is neatly trimmed. The basement door opens, his father comes up, and they embrace on the grass.

Then the film cuts back. He is still sitting in the semi-basement. The same street lies outside the window, the same old computer at his feet. Not a thing in the room has changed.

The plan does not add up. The film has given us the family's level of income and the scale of the house. At that distance, this is a project of several centuries. The audience can do the calculation. The person writing the letter probably can too.

But the film is not mocking the plan. It shows us its shape: **Ki-woo is not thinking of creating more positions. He is thinking of occupying that one himself.**

From beginning to end, nobody in this house asks why it can have only one owner. Nobody asks why someone has to live in the basement.

## The Stone

Finally, there is the stone. It accompanies the film from beginning to end, the only concrete object that runs through the entire story.

At the beginning, Ki-woo's school friend Min-hyuk brings a landscape stone, saying it is a treasured object passed down in his family that brings wealth. Ki-woo keeps it with him. On the night the semi-basement floods, everything else sinks. Only this stone floats up, and he reaches out to retrieve it.

The next day, he carries it down into the basement. He is going to deal with those two people, holding the very object supposed to bring good fortune. Instead, he is struck on the head with it himself and almost dies.

Later he takes the stone to a stream, crouches down, and puts it back in the water. He settles it upright and lets the water flow over it. When he lets go, he says nothing and does not look back.

This act is only half completed. He has put down the object, and with it the claim that holding the right stone will change one's position. What he has not put down is the plan. Back in his room, he sits down and begins to write the letter.

The window is still the same window, at pavement level. Someone walks past outside. From inside the room, only legs are visible. On the paper he writes: Father, today I made a plan.

*This essay concerns the 2019 theatrical color release, approximately 132 minutes long. A black-and-white version also exists and is not discussed here. The Chinese original consistently uses 基婷 rather than the alternative 基静 for the sister's name; this English version uses Ki-jung.*
