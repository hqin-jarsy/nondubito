# Yan Hui Was Not a Fan

A student's account of being led to where there is no path.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

For many people, Yan Hui looks like this: everything the teacher says is right; he has only a bowl of food and a gourd of water but remains cheerful; he dies young, is later honored as the “Sage Restorer,” and is enshrined beside his teacher. A number-one fan. A moral model content in poverty and joyful in the Way. A signboard used to tell poor people not to make trouble.

Few of his own words survive. One passage is the *Analects*' sole firsthand account by a student of “how I was taught.”

> Yan Yuan sighed deeply and said, “The more I look up to it, the higher it is; the more I bore into it, the harder it is. I see it ahead, and suddenly it is behind. The Master skillfully guides people step by step. He broadens me with cultural learning and draws me within bounds through ritual. Though I wish to stop, I cannot. When I have exhausted all my ability, something seems to stand there, towering. Though I wish to follow it, I have no way.”

Yan Yuan is Yan Hui. With a long sigh he says: I look upward, and it rises higher; I probe, and it grows harder; I see it in front, and suddenly it is behind. The teacher leads us step by step. He opens me outward with cultural learning and draws me inward with ritual until I cannot stop. I use up every power I have, and something seems to stand there, distinct and towering. I want to reach it. There is no path.

First see what this is not. A fan says, “The teacher is so great,” or “I will follow you forever.” This passage contains neither. It describes something happening within the speaker.

“The more I look up, the higher; the more I probe, the harder; ahead, then suddenly behind.” He is saying that what the teacher teaches cannot be seized. It is not a doctrine to memorize. The nearer you get, the more you discover that it has no fixed location. A fan needs an idol he can grasp. Yan Hui says this thing cannot be grasped.

“The Master skillfully guides people step by step.” The teacher does not pour things in. He leads. The verb means inviting you to come forward on your own.

“He broadens me with cultural learning and draws me within bounds through ritual.” Notice that the object of both verbs is “me.” What the teacher broadens through learning is me; what he brings within bounds through ritual is also me. Yan Hui does not say, “The teacher gave me a great deal of knowledge.” He describes two actions upon him, one opening outward, one gathering inward. Teaching applies its force not to the delivery of knowledge but to this person's growth.

“Though I wish to stop, I cannot.” This is the crucial line. The driving force no longer resides in the teacher. It resides in him. A student under pressure says, “I dare not stop.” He says, “I cannot stop.”

Then the final two sentences: “When I have exhausted all my ability, something seems to stand there, towering. Though I wish to follow it, I have no way.” He has spent all his strength. He sees something standing there. He wants to reach it. No path.

This is often read as complaint or modesty: the teacher is too high; I cannot reach him. Neither reading is right here. **This is a student's most exact description of his teacher: he has brought me to a place where there is no path.** The teacher gives materials, “broadens me with cultural learning”; gives training, “draws me within bounds through ritual”; gives direction, “guides people step by step.” He gives all the way to this point. But for the final step, from seeing it stand there to standing there oneself, there is no path. Only you can take that step. Nobody can lead another through it. When Yan Hui says “I have no way,” he does not mean the teacher has failed to provide a road. There is no road to provide along that final stretch. **A fan cannot say “I have no way.” A fan always has a way, because all a fan needs to do is follow.**

The passage has another special value: it comes from the student's side. What earlier essays found from the teacher's side, waiting for the question, giving one corner and leaving three, not spelling out the “one,” a student now reports from the other end: impossible to grasp, being guided, unable to stop, no path. The readings from both sides agree.

Now see what the teacher says about Yan Hui. In the same book: “Hui is no help to me. There is nothing I say that does not delight him.” Yan Hui does not help me; he accepts whatever I say. The student says, “I cannot catch up.” The teacher says, “He is too compliant.” The recipient's gain and the giver's regret stand side by side. Nobody presses them into one pretty story.

After Yan Hui dies, the teacher offers a judgment. Not “Hui was humane,” not “Hui was worthy,” but: “What a pity! I saw him advance; I never saw him stop.” Such a pity. I only saw him going forward, never stopping. **Even the words at the closing of his coffin remain in the progressive tense.** Later generations give him the completed title “Sage Restorer.” The teacher says only that he never stopped.

As for “a basket of food, a gourd of water, in a poor alley,” the teacher praises not his capacity to endure poverty, but this: “Others could not bear its hardship, yet Hui did not change his joy.” In conditions others cannot bear, his joy remains. Its source lies outside his circumstances, so circumstances cannot take it away. This does not tell the poor to know their place. It says a person's joy need not be tied to external things.

I call what the teacher does with Yan Hui cultivation: materials, training, direction, then stopping at the place where there is no path and letting him walk himself. Yan Hui reaches that place and says truthfully: there is no path. It is the most honest thing a student can say, and the highest appraisal of a teacher.

Among the people you have followed, has anyone led you to a place where “there is no path”?
