# To Whom Was That Call to “Keep the People Ignorant” Addressed?

“The people may be made to follow”: restoring the subject of “make.”

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

The most reviled sentence in the *Analects* is probably this one:

> The Master said, “The people may be made to follow it; they cannot be made to know it.”

The usual reading is: the common people may be made to obey, but must not be allowed to understand. Here is the founding father of a policy of keeping people ignorant, with evidence as solid as a mountain. The sentence has been quoted for two thousand years by two opposing camps. One cites it to prove that Confucius was an accomplice of rulers; the other takes it as a ready-made justification: the public need not know too much.

Both camps quote the same reading. And that reading does not fit the whole of this man’s practice.

First look at where it fails to fit. This man said, “In teaching there are no distinctions of class”: the door to learning stood open to everyone. He said, “By nature people are close to one another”: human beings do not begin very far apart. When an unlettered man came with a question, he did not pour in a ready-made answer. He explored the question from both ends so the questioner could think it through himself. He made the people’s trust the foundation of government: “Without the people’s trust, it cannot stand.” **A man who opens the door of education for everyone to enter will not turn around and say, “Keep the common people ignorant.”** If that really was what he said, what had he been doing all his life?

How, then, should the sentence be read?

The key is the word “make” before “know.” “Make them know” has a subject: the ruler. The ruler installs his own “knowing” in the people’s heads. What the sentence says is not “Do not let them know,” but “You cannot install your knowing in them.” The commentator Cheng Yi had already pointed this out: it is not that one does not want people to know; it is that one cannot make them know. Knowing is something a person can only do for themselves. It cannot be installed.

This is wholly consistent with his teaching. Even with Yan Hui, his best student, he would not speak before the student had struggled into a question. If even Yan Hui could not simply be told before reaching that inward pressure and difficulty of expression, how much less could the whole population? “They cannot be made to know it” states a limit of knowing, not a limit of the people. And “follow it” means letting them walk for themselves: “follow” means following their own road. Taken together, I read the sentence as addressed to rulers: **The common people can be allowed to walk their own road; you cannot install your ideas in them.** This is not keeping people ignorant. It is precisely a prohibition against rulers chiseling away at their minds.

Excavated material bears witness for this reading. The Guodian bamboo manuscripts, unearthed in the 1990s, include a text called *Honoring Virtue and Rightness*. It says: “The people may be guided along the Way, but they cannot be made to know it. The people may be guided, but they cannot be coerced.” Guidance is possible; compulsion is not. In this early context of the same saying, “making them know” corresponds to “coercion.” The reading about keeping people ignorant becomes possible only after that context is stripped away.

Here we can see how a misreading is made. Not a single word needs to change. Strip away whom the sentence addresses. Strip away the subject of “make.” Strip away the surrounding context of “no distinctions of class in teaching” and “exploring the two ends.” A bare string of words remains, and the art of keeping people ignorant is complete. Stripping context is the cheapest and most ruthless move in misreading, because it requires no forgery, only deletion.

There is another passage worth looking at alongside this one. Chapter 3 of the *Laozi* discusses how rulers should treat the people. In the received text, a sentence reads, “Make the clever dare not act.” Make intelligent people afraid to do anything. In the silk manuscripts excavated at Mawangdui, the wording is: “Make those who know dare not fail to act, and that is all.” Make knowledgeable people afraid not to act. A difference of one character reverses the direction: one silences the knowledgeable, while the other forbids them to leave the field. And the silk manuscript is earlier. These are two separate books; I am not using Laozi to prove Confucius. But the same misreading, turning “do not chisel away at the people” into “silence the people,” has left a material example in another book. The early text does not support the later reading.

Return to the sentence itself. Read this way, it places someone with power opposite him. What he said to those with power always pointed in the same direction: let the ruler first be a ruler; first hold yourself to account; do not install your own ideas in other people. This sentence belongs to that line, not to its opposite.

**The sentence in the *Analects* that makes him look most like an accomplice was originally a boundary drawn for rulers.** To read it as a policy of keeping people ignorant, one must first erase everything the speaker did throughout his life.

That “The Master said” you find most objectionable: have you checked to whom he said it?
