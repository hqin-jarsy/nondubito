# The Gatekeeper Who Defined Him

Knowing it cannot be done, and doing it nonetheless.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

By worldly standards, his life was a failure. He held office for a few years and was pushed out. He traveled among the states for more than a decade, yet not one ruler truly put him to use. On the road with his students, he went hungry, was surrounded, and was compared to a homeless dog. Hence one familiar reading: Confucius was a failed politician, good at ideas but no good at getting things done. A sharper version says that he knew nothing would come of it and went on peddling himself anyway. A hopeless pedant.

The person who gave the most exact definition of his life was a gatekeeper.

> Zilu spent the night at the Stone Gate. The gatekeeper asked, “Where have you come from?” Zilu replied, “From Confucius.” “Is that the man who knows it cannot be done and does it nonetheless?”

Zilu had stayed overnight at the Stone Gate. Where had he come from? From Confucius’s household. Was that the man who knew it could not be done but insisted on doing it anyway?

The remark was a taunt. The gatekeeper was probably a recluse. There were people in that age who had seen through the ways of the world and thought the intelligent thing to do was withdraw. To them, doing what one knew could not be done was unwise. But Zilu heard this taunt, it was recorded, and two thousand years later it had become the most concise statement of his teacher’s life.

We need to think carefully about what “knowing it cannot be done and doing it nonetheless” means. It does not mean charging ahead blindly. It does not mean being unaware that one will fail. He knew. “The phoenix does not come; the River brings forth no chart. It is over for me!” He had sighed those words. “No one understands me!” He had sighed those too. He knew this road would lead nowhere. Why, then, keep walking?

Because success was not the purpose of what he did.

There are two ways to reckon an action. One first asks whether it can succeed, and acts only if it can. This makes success or failure the end. The other first asks whether it ought to be done, and does it if it ought to be done; whether it succeeds is another matter. This makes doing what ought to be done the end. “Knowing it can be done and doing it” belongs to the first. “Knowing it cannot be done and doing it nonetheless” belongs to the second. The gatekeeper used the first reckoning to laugh at him; he lived by the second. **It was not that he could not calculate. He did not use success and failure as his measure.**

He himself described the disposition behind this:

> The Master said, “No one understands me!” Zigong asked, “Why do you say that no one understands you?” The Master said, “I do not resent Heaven or blame other people. I learn from what is below and reach toward what is above. The one who understands me must be Heaven!”

No one understands me, he said. Why does no one understand you, Zigong asked. I do not resent Heaven or blame people. I begin learning with the things below and reach toward the things above. Perhaps only Heaven understands me.

These four clauses are not parallel slogans. They form two pairs of cause and consequence. By not blaming other people, he can “learn from what is below.” Only when he does not blame others for his road’s being blocked can he continue learning from anyone, including people less accomplished than himself and people traveling in a different direction. For someone nursing resentment, the door to learning is shut. By not resenting Heaven, he can “reach toward what is above.” Only when he does not lay failure at the door of fate can he continue upward. Someone who resents his lot cannot make that ascent. He laid out method and result together before Zigong, and then said: perhaps only Heaven understands me. He was speaking of himself. He did not require Zigong to be the same.

Another incident shows how this man went about his work. A man named Gongbo Liao slandered Zilu before Jisun, a powerful political figure. Zifu Jingbo came to tell Confucius. Jisun had indeed been misled by Gongbo Liao, he said, but he himself had the power to have Gongbo Liao killed and his body exposed in the marketplace. Confucius answered:

> “Whether the Way is to prevail is a matter of fate; whether the Way is to fall into disuse is a matter of fate. What can Gongbo Liao do against fate?”

If the Way can prevail, that is fate. If it cannot, that is fate too. What can Gongbo Liao do to fate?

Someone offered to act on his behalf. He refused. Whether the Way prevailed was not something one slanderer could decide, so there was no need to protect it through killing. His end was right, and his means had to be right as well. He would not use wrongful means to reach a rightful end. This too belongs to “knowing it cannot be done and doing it nonetheless”: it does not mean acting at any cost. It means acting within the bounds of what ought to be done, and refusing to go beyond them.

In the end, it was his students who recorded the gatekeeper’s remark. They could easily have left it out. It was a sneer from someone who did not share their teacher’s path. That they preserved it shows that, to them, “knowing it cannot be done and doing it nonetheless” was precisely where their teacher was most himself, something to take pride in. **By preserving a taunt, the disciples were saying: yes, that is what he is like, and we stand by it too.**

So the phrase “failed politician” measures the wrong thing. There was much he never accomplished, but “accomplishing it” had never been his purpose in the first place. His measure was: ought this to be done? By that measure, he spent his whole life doing what ought to be done, without leaving a single thing undone.

Does the measure in your hand ask whether something can succeed, or whether it ought to be done?
