# Neither Curse Confucius nor Kneel Before Him

What we oppose, and what we do not claim to know.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

The preceding thirty-one essays have been about a person, and about how his words came to be misread. One matter has remained untouched: there are several sayings in the Analects that, however one reads them, do not fit the person we have encountered.

The man who teaches without distinctions says, “Only the wisest above and the most foolish below do not change.” The man who dismantles the claim that he is “a sage endowed by Heaven” says, “Those who know from birth rank highest.” The man who tells students to challenge him says they should “stand in awe of great men and of the words of sages.” The man who never establishes a transmissible inner doctrine is assigned, in the final book, an orthodox succession running from Yao and Shun to Zhou. The man who says that “people are close in nature” says, “Only women and petty men are difficult to deal with.”

For two thousand years there have been two attitudes toward these sayings. One accepts them all: the classics cannot be wrong; if the sage said it, it must be right; if you cannot make sense of it, the fault is yours. These sentences then become heavy hammers for holding people down. The other rejects everything: if Confucius said these things, this must be the sort of man he was. The same sentences then become evidence for bringing him down.

Both attitudes share one premise: he said these things, so they must either be right or expose him as bad.

We do not use that premise.

First, let us be clear: we do not determine who said these things. We do not call them fabrications, “later additions,” or “not Confucius's own words.” This is not caution but honesty. We do not possess the evidence needed to decide: no variant text, no earlier manuscript, only the transmitted words. Attribution requires evidence. We do not have it, so we do not make the judgment.

What, then, can we say? We can ask: **Does what this sentence establishes fit the person we have encountered in the previous thirty-one essays?**

We already know this person. He waits for you to struggle toward a question before speaking. He gives one corner and leaves three for you. Twice he refrains from spelling out the “one.” He finds his best student too obedient. He acknowledges an error before the whole class. When he cannot persuade Zai Wo, he lets Zai Wo act on his own judgment. Even with an uncultivated man, he probes the question from both ends instead of pouring an answer into him. All his life, he refuses the role of sage. He says, “When it comes to benevolence, do not yield even to your teacher.” Everything he does points in one direction: rather than putting his own contents into other people, he lets them develop judgments of their own.

Now measure those sayings against this person. What do they establish? “The wisest above and the most foolish below do not change” establishes hierarchy: a class of people is incapable of change by nature. “Those who know from birth rank highest” also establishes hierarchy: knowledge is divided into four ranks, and people are ranked with it. “Stand in awe of great men and the words of sages” establishes authority: there is a kind of speech that cannot be questioned. The Yao Yue book establishes an orthodox succession: the Way travels along a lineage, and those on the pedigree have the final say. “Only women and petty men” establishes exclusion: a class of people is marked off. Four things: hierarchy, authority, orthodox succession, exclusion. Together they turn a living teaching, open to everyone and treating people as ends, into a system with ranks, authority, an orthodox succession, and exclusions.

**What we oppose is not these sayings as such, but the operation they establish: turning cultivation into an institutional order.**

This objection does not require knowing who spoke. If a saying establishes that “a class of people is unteachable by nature,” whoever said it, it does not fit “teaching without distinctions.” If a saying establishes that “a kind of speech must not be questioned,” whoever said it, it does not fit “When it comes to benevolence, do not yield even to your teacher.” **We do not judge the speaker; we measure the words. The preceding thirty-one essays provide the measure.**

This is, in fact, the cleaner approach. Attribution presents two traps. Say a sentence is fabricated, and someone asks where your evidence is. Say it is authentic, and someone asks whether the sage was not precisely this kind of person. Both traps circle around “Who said it?” Set that question aside, and only one remains: does what the sentence establishes fit what this person spent his life doing? If it does not, we say, “We do not think it fits Confucius as we understand him.” That is all. No more.

One more distinction must be clear: not using a passage as an anchor does not mean it is wrong, still less fabricated.

There are many sayings in the Analects that we do not use as evidence for knowing this person, yet do not oppose. “The gentleman has nine things to consider”: all nine are sound, but we cannot see to whom they were addressed or when. The Xiang Dang book records how he dressed and ate; much of it is probably true, but it cannot record why. The three kinds of friends, three kinds of pleasures, and three cautions in Ji Shi do not contradict his meaning; they simply are not how he would speak. Zengzi's “Let your thoughts not go beyond your position” misunderstands him, but Zengzi signs his own name. All these fall under “not an anchor”: we do not use them as evidence, but need not oppose them. **Only where cultivation is turned into an institutional order do we explicitly register opposition.** The questions must stay separate. Whether a sentence can help us know him and whether it should be opposed are two independent lines of inquiry.

The next five essays will measure those passages one by one. Each ends with three lines: what we oppose, why, and what we do not infer. The third is always the same: we do not decide who spoke; we say only that it does not fit Confucius as we understand him. The four essays after those concern how to recognize a passage that “does not sound like him”: a named interlocutor without teaching fitted to the person; several chapters cast in the same mold; praise with or without reasons; sweeping bitterness toward the world. Those concern the use of the measure, not opposition.

When a sentence is placed before you, do you first ask “Who said it?” or “What does it establish?”
