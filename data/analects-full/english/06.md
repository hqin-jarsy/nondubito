# Even His Son Received No Private Lessons

Chen Kang questions Boyu, looking for the teaching kept behind closed doors.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

One persistent fantasy says the real thing is always transmitted in private. A master has an inner method reserved for intimate disciples. A great thinker puts his secret in letters to his son. Classroom teaching is for outsiders; behind closed doors there is another system. Everything sold over two thousand years as the “true transmission of Confucius” or his “inner method” could probably fill a room.

The *Analects* happens to contain an investigation of exactly this question. The person investigated is his son.

> Chen Kang asked Boyu, “Have you received any different teaching?” He answered, “No. Once he was standing alone, and I, Li, hurried across the courtyard. He asked, ‘Have you studied the Odes?’ I answered, ‘Not yet.’ ‘Without studying the Odes, you have no means of speaking.’ I withdrew and studied the Odes. Another day he was again standing alone, and I hurried across the courtyard. He asked, ‘Have you studied ritual?’ I answered, ‘Not yet.’ ‘Without studying ritual, you have no means of taking your stand.’ I withdrew and studied ritual. Those are the two things I heard.” Chen Kang withdrew delighted. “I asked one thing and gained three: I learned about the Odes, about ritual, and about how the exemplary person keeps his own son at a distance.”

Chen Kang asks Boyu, Kong Li, the son of Confucius: at home, have you heard anything we have not? No, says Boyu. Once he was standing alone and I hurried across the courtyard. Have you studied poetry? No. Without poetry, you cannot speak. So I went and studied the *Book of Odes*. Another day, the same thing: he stood alone, I hurried past, he asked whether I had studied ritual. No. Without ritual, you cannot stand. So I studied ritual. Those two things. That is all.

Chen Kang leaves pleased. One question, three discoveries: study poetry, study ritual, and an exemplary person does not make special arrangements for his son.

Look first at the questioner. Chen Kang is not asking casually. He comes with a suspicion: surely the teacher has one set of teachings for outsiders and another for his son. He is looking for inside information. Now look at the person questioned. Kong Li is the most likely recipient of such information. If a secret transmission existed, he ought to receive it first. A man looking for a secret questions the man most likely to possess it, and the answer is: none. Two instructions, study poetry and study ritual, the same as for every other student, both delivered casually as he crossed the courtyard.

**This is the strongest evidence in the *Analects* that there was no secret transmission, because the teacher did not simply assert it. Someone investigated.**

The teacher did say it himself, too. Once, when students suspected he was concealing something, he said:

> “Do you think I am hiding something from you? I hide nothing. There is nothing I do that I do not share with you. That is Qiu.”

Qiu is Confucius's personal name. You think I conceal something? I do not. There is nothing I do outside your sight. That is who I am.

Why were they suspicious? Easy to understand. Twice he declined to spell out the “one” that threaded his learning together. When a teacher will not say everything explicitly, students naturally suspect he is holding something back. His answer is: nothing is hidden. You can see everything I do. The piece you feel is missing has not been withheld. It cannot be given. It is not in my hands, or anyone else's.

He really did give his son no special treatment. Kong Li died before him. He was buried with an inner coffin but no outer coffin, a simple funeral. Later Yan Hui died, and Yan Hui's father wanted Confucius to sell his carriage to buy an outer coffin. Confucius replied: when my son died, he too had an inner coffin but no outer one. Talented or not, each is someone's son. He did not give Yan Hui an elaborate funeral because of his worth, nor make an exception because Kong Li was his son. Chen Kang's “keeps his son at a distance” does not mean emotional estrangement. It means opening no private passage unavailable to others. **“In teaching there are no categories” must first be practiced in one's own home.**

Here the distinction between a cultivator and a lineage of masters becomes clear. A teaching lineage has a true transmission because knowledge is an asset: whoever receives it becomes a legitimate successor, able to establish a school and transmit it to posterity. Confucius has no such asset. Everything he has is public, available to anyone willing to study poetry and ritual. The final stretch is something no one can give anyone, not even a son. He therefore has nothing to transmit secretly. Not because he does not wish to, but because there is nothing of that kind.

The recorders are honest, too. A school intent on establishing an orthodox succession would write, “The Master gave Boyu a special teaching,” demonstrating the closeness of the direct successor. They wrote an investigation finding no secret transmission. They even retained Chen Kang's delight. He is happy because he found nothing.

There is a small irony. Boyu “hurrying across the courtyard” later became a literary allusion: “instruction received in crossing the courtyard,” or “hurrying through the courtyard,” elegant ways to refer to a father's teaching. Writers used them solemnly. A scene proving the absence of special teaching became a fine expression for special paternal instruction. The name survived; what happened inside it was turned around.

All those “true transmissions of Confucius” over the next two thousand years came long after this report.

Could the thing you want your teacher to give you be something that was never in his hands?
