# Setting Names Right Is Not Quibbling over Words

Before asking what to do, ask where you stand and by what right.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

“Setting names right” later became the signboard of a doctrine of status: titles, ranks, ruler and minister, father and son, each with a named position. Put the names right, and order will be right. Opponents attacked those same words: Confucius was a pedant; his first move on taking office would be to correct forms of address rather than do anything useful.

The first person to say this to him was Zilu.

> Zilu said, “If the ruler of Wei were waiting for you to govern, what would you do first?” The Master said, “Surely it would be to set names right!” Zilu said, “Can that be so? How impractical you are! What is there to set right?”

Zilu asked: if the ruler of Wei is waiting for you to take charge, what will you begin with? Surely, he answered, by putting names right. You really are impractical, Zilu replied. What names need putting right?

The student called his teacher impractical to his face. The teacher answered:

> The Master said, “How crude you are, You! Where the exemplary person does not know, he should leave a space.”

You, Zilu's personal name: you are too crude. When an exemplary person does not understand something, he should first leave it open and refrain from speaking.

Then comes the famous passage: if names are not right, words do not follow properly; if words do not follow properly, affairs cannot be accomplished; if affairs cannot be accomplished, ritual and music do not flourish; if ritual and music do not flourish, punishments do not fit; if punishments do not fit, the people have nowhere to put their hands and feet. It ends with one sentence: “In his words, the exemplary person is simply never careless.” A person of this kind must not be casual about what he says.

To understand “setting names right,” one must know what was happening in Wei. Duke Ling had died. His crown prince, Kuai Kui, had offended him years earlier and fled abroad. Kuai Kui's son Zhe was installed as ruler. Kuai Kui returned to claim the position, and father and son fought over the state for years. A son held the title of ruler while his father was outside trying to take it back. This was Wei's “names not being right.” Name and reality did not match; who ought to be ruler was unresolved. Zilu asked what to do first. Confucius answered: first make this clear. This is not correcting forms of address. It is first setting the fundamental position right: who exactly are you, and by what right do you sit here?

Here too lies the disagreement between him and Zilu. Zilu asked about action: what task do we undertake first? He answered about one's ground: first stand in the right fundamental position. Zilu found this impractical because he wanted something he could do immediately. Confucius called him “crude” because he could not see the relation between root and detail; he fixed his attention only on the work at hand. This agrees with his persistent diagnosis of Zilu: too hasty, acting first and thinking afterward.

Look again at the chain. Names not right → words not following properly → affairs not accomplished → ritual and music not flourishing → punishments not fitting → the people with nowhere to put their hands and feet. It describes a failure transmitted through successive levels. If the fundamental position is wrong, the principles are wrong, the methods are wrong, the outward forms are wrong, the results are wrong. Finally the people suffer: they do not know what to do. **The chain ends with “the people have nowhere to put their hands and feet,” with ordinary people's suffering, not with “use punishments to keep the people under control.”** Its destination is concern for the people, not their management.

The later doctrine of status read this chain as a technique of rule: rectify status → establish ritual and music → employ punishments → the people obey. The same chain had been reversed. “If the foundation is wrong, the people are harmed” became “Set names right so punishments can be used.” The distortion is the same as in the previous essay: words spoken out of concern for the people were turned into a manual for managing them.

A further word about the chain itself. “If A, then B; if B, then C,” carried through six levels: this kind of linked deduction is unusual in his speech. His usual way is a single closing sentence, an image, a question turned back on the questioner. This chain looks more like the students taking his open-ended “Surely it would be to set names right” and unfolding a complete deduction on his behalf. The meaning has not gone astray; it is his meaning. He probably would not have spelled it out quite so fully. This is a “faithful filling-in.” What gets filled in is his meaning, but once everything is filled, there is less room for someone else to take a step in thought.

Three passages can be identified with certainty as his: “Surely it would be to set names right”; leave open what you do not understand; do not be careless in speech. Taken together, they have nothing to do with quibbling. They say: get the foundation right; do not talk recklessly about what you do not understand; stand by what you say.

A few years later, Zilu, the student who called him impractical, died in that civil conflict in Wei. Where names are not right, the price is finally paid in lives.

That task in your hands: have its names been set right? Who exactly are you, and by what right are you doing it?
