# The Chapter Next Door

“Only the wisest above and the most foolish below do not change.”

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

First, look at two things he says.

> The Master said, “People are close to one another in nature; acquired habits take them far apart.”

Human natures are close; the habits acquired afterward create distance.

> The Master said, “In teaching, there are no distinctions of kind.”

Education does not sort people into classes.

The first sentence concerns the relation between people. As human beings, their starting points are close; differences develop afterward, each in its own form. He does not say “human nature is good” or “human nature is bad,” nor that everyone is identical. He says only “close.” That leaves room for individual differences but none for “a certain class of people is inferior by nature.” The second puts the first into practice. Since people are close as human beings, the door to teaching opens to everyone, regardless of birth, wealth, cleverness or slowness, or what they have done before. Together, these two sayings form the foundation of his whole practice.

Now look at the chapter immediately following the first:

> The Master said, “Only the wisest above and the most foolish below do not change.”

Only those at the highest extreme of wisdom and the lowest extreme of foolishness cannot change.

Notice where it stands. It is right next door to “close in nature, far apart through acquired habits.” One sentence has just said, “People are close, and what comes afterward changes them”; the next says, “There are two extremes that do not change.” It does not alter a word of the preceding sentence. It establishes an exception beside it: apart from the very highest and the very lowest, people are close.

The touch is light; the effect is heavy. Once this sentence is present, “close in nature” becomes “close in nature except at the two extremes”; “teaching without distinctions” becomes “teaching without distinctions, except for the lowest fools.” **Without changing a word, an exception added next door puts a ceiling on an open structure.** Over two thousand years, most people quoting this sentence have used it this way: there is a class of people who, by nature, need not be taught, because teaching them is useless.

That use does not fit what he did throughout his life. He even meets a boy from Huxiang, a place whose people are “hard to talk to.” When his students fail to understand, he says, “I approve his coming forward, not his retreat.” If someone has cleaned himself up and come to seek improvement, let him improve; do not prosecute his past. To Ran Qiu he says, “Those whose strength is insufficient give up halfway. You are drawing the line yourself.” It is not that you cannot; you have set your own limit. Never does he announce of anyone, “This person cannot be taught.” A man who closes off no individual is followed, next door, by a sentence that closes off a class.

We must be precise about what we oppose. The sentence admits other readings. “Do not change” can describe an extremely stable condition: the wisest and the most confused are both exceedingly difficult to change from outside. That is an observation, not a law of inborn rank. We do not say that an ordained caste system is the only possible reading, or that the sentence “necessarily” imposes a ceiling. We oppose this use: making it the controlling interpretation of “close in nature,” using it to prove that “some people are unteachable by birth,” then turning it into a permit to give up on them. **Anyone who cites it to prove that “a certain class cannot be taught” has chosen the reading we oppose.**

Something else must be said: people really are different. We do not claim that everyone is homogeneous. Quite the opposite: so many earlier essays concern his teaching according to the person, which is necessary precisely because people differ. Difference is real. But difference calls for teaching fitted to the person, not ranking; different prescriptions for different people, not a verdict that some class needs no prescription. **Difference does not authorize hierarchy.** “Close in nature” guards that boundary; people use the neighboring sentence to cross it.

The recorders placed these two sentences side by side. That is itself interesting. Had the compilers wanted to establish hierarchy, they would have deleted “close in nature” and kept only “do not change.” Had they wanted to protect their teacher, they would have moved “do not change” farther away. Instead, the two remain adjacent: one opens, one closes. We can see the seam because it has not been erased.

**What we oppose:** Making “Only the wisest above and the most foolish below do not change” the controlling interpretation of “close in nature,” using it to establish that some people are unteachable by birth, and abandoning their education on that basis.

**Why:** He never declares anyone unteachable; “teaching without distinctions” permits no such exception; difference calls for teaching fitted to the person, not ranking.

**What we do not infer:** We do not determine who historically said this, call it fabricated, or claim that an ordained caste system is its only possible meaning. We say only that the reading which imposes a ceiling does not fit Confucius as we understand him.

That person you have heard described as “incapable by nature”: who passed the verdict on them?
