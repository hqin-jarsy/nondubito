# He Left No Doctrine

After the reconstruction: a way of treating people, and the corners left for the reader.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

After forty-nine essays, it is time to say what all this has been for.

Not to overturn a verdict on someone from antiquity. Overturning a verdict would mean: he was wronged; he was good after all. But not one essay in this series has pleaded his defense. He disliked Yan Hui's excessive obedience; he admitted an error in public; he failed to persuade Zai Wo; a gatekeeper summed him up in a single sentence; he wept beyond restraint; he called Zai Yu rotten wood. Zai Wo and Zai Yu are the same student. None of these things looks flattering. We wrote them down anyway. We are writing about a person, not arguing a case.

Nor is the aim to devise a new interpretive system for the *Analects*. Quite the opposite. The repeated action across these forty-nine essays is dismantling: putting names back before sentences, scenes back around words, “who asked?” back before “The Master said”; reopening the gaps the students filled; rescuing three questions from three declarations. **We are not adding things to the book. We are removing, layer by layer, what later ages added, to see the person underneath.**

What do we see?

A man who keeps no stock of ready answers. Someone comes with a question; he does not reach into an inventory. He probes the other person's question all the way to its root. A man who will not spell out the crucial thing. Twice the thread running through everything appears in conversations with his students; twice he leaves it unspoken. When students ask, “If you no longer speak, what shall we record?” he asks whether Heaven has ever spoken. A man who waits until the moment is ripe. Unless you struggle your way into a question, he does not speak. He gives one corner and leaves three; if you cannot infer them, he does not give the lesson a second time. A man who says different things to each person. The same question: to Zilu, don't; to Ran You, do. Four people ask about filial devotion: four prescriptions. Three ask about ren: three answers. A man who withdraws his own authority. When it comes to ren, do not yield even to the teacher. Yan Hui is no help to me. No one daring to disagree is a sign of a state's ruin. A man who leaves the last step to the other. “If you are at ease with it, do it”; “Practicing ren comes from oneself.” Yan Hui's “There is no way to follow” describes the student's experience at that boundary: no one else can provide the final path for him. A man who refuses to be a sage. “As for sagehood and ren, how would I dare?” I was poor as a child. A man who does not write a book. In this reading, “transmitting rather than creating” is linked to a limitation: written words no longer have that particular questioner standing before them.

What this person left was not a doctrine. A doctrine is something that can be recited: its core, its several principles, its classifications. He had no such thing. What he had was a way of treating people: taking the person before him as someone who must grow a judgment of their own; giving that person material, direction, and boundaries; then stopping short of the step the other must take alone. This way cannot be summarized in a few propositions. The moment one does so, it becomes a doctrine, precisely what he did not want. **So his leaving no doctrine is not a regret. It is something he got right.**

Doctrines become outdated. They are revised, replaced, or left behind by their age. But “treating a person as an end and leaving them room to arrive for themselves” does not become outdated. It was hard two thousand five hundred years ago. It is just as hard today.

At its close, this series does not want to offer a conclusion. It wants only to point to a few places where this way remains useful today.

In education. Until the other person wants to understand but cannot, do not force an explanation. Explain one corner and leave the other three. If the student cannot infer them, do not do the inferring for them. The hardest requirement is to let a student say, “I disagree,” and to let them act according to their own thinking.

In government. First act like a ruler; only then speak of ministers. Punishment is not a way to govern a state. Killing without first teaching is cruelty. If the people have enough, how could the ruler lack enough? And there is the saying that has been read backward: let people walk their own road; do not fill them with your ideas.

In dealing with others. What you do not desire for yourself, do not impose on others. Remember why this must be negative: what you want is not necessarily what another wants. Giving the other what you want is treating that person as a copy of yourself. Recognizing that the person before you is someone else yields only “do not impose.” It does not tell you “what you ought to give them.”

After forty-nine essays, something else needs saying: this series, too, is written down. He does not write a book because written words say the same thing to everyone; we have written forty-nine essays. Is that a contradiction? In each essay, we have tried to do the same thing: keep the name before the sentence, place “to whom was this said?” at the center, let every prescription remain a prescription rather than become a universal remedy. Readers can see how far we have succeeded. We have no better method than his. We are simply trying not to freeze his words into lifeless form all over again.

One final thing. Every judgment in these forty-nine essays may be wrong: which saying serves as an anchor, which does not, which reading we oppose. The criteria are set out in the books; each can be checked. Excavated evidence could overturn any one of them. If it does, that judgment should change. Only one thing does not change: treating people as ends. That is the measure by which everything is judged, not a judgment that some particular saying could overturn.

As for whether Confucius really was such a person, or whether this way of treating people is worth practicing today, forty-nine essays have not answered for you. **Those are the three corners left to you.**

When you open the *Analects*, do you see a doctrine or a person?
