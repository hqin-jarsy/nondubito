# He Kept Refusing to Be a Sage

“As for sagehood and humaneness, how would I dare claim them?”

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

“Supreme Sage and First Teacher,” says the tablet in the temple. He is enshrined, his statue sits upright, dynasty after dynasty adds titles. Confucius is a sage. For many people, whether they like him or not, this is common knowledge.

The trouble is that the man spent his life refusing it.

Someone praises him; he dismantles the praise. A high official, the Grand Steward, asks his disciple Zigong: your teacher must be a sage, surely? How else could he possess so many abilities? Zigong says Heaven has made him a sage and also given him many skills. When this reaches Confucius, he says: how could the Grand Steward know me? I was poor when young, so I learned many menial tasks. Does an exemplary person need many skills? No. “A sage endowed by Heaven” is dismantled with one sentence: I was poor as a child.

Someone answers for him; he revises the answer. The Lord of She asks Zilu what sort of person his teacher is. Zilu cannot answer. Confucius later tells him what to say:

> “Why did you not say: he is the kind of person who becomes so absorbed in his efforts that he forgets to eat, so joyful that he forgets his worries, and does not notice old age approaching?”

Why didn't you say: this person forgets meals when working hard, forgets worries when happy, and does not realize he is getting old?

This is the self-introduction he writes for himself. Three clauses, all in the progressive tense: working, rejoicing, forgetting. Not an achievement, not a title, not an “already.” Someone trying to erect his own statue would never write this way.

He says it more directly, too:

> “I was not born knowing. I love antiquity and diligently seek it out.”

I am not someone who knew from birth. I simply love ancient things and work hard to seek them.

> “As for sagehood and humaneness, how would I dare claim them? But working at it without growing weary and teaching others without tiring: that much may be said of me, and nothing more.”

A sage? A humane person? How could I presume? Only that I do not tire of the work or grow weary of teaching. That is all.

“Sagehood” and “humaneness” are things other people want to give him. He refuses both. He acknowledges only two things: not growing weary, not tiring. Still the progressive tense.

A student's remark follows. Gongxi Hua says, “That is precisely what we disciples cannot learn.” Exactly this is what we cannot learn from you. It is an extraordinarily apt postscript. Gongxi Hua is not saying they cannot learn sagehood and humaneness. He is saying: acknowledging only that one is still doing the work, accepting no title that says it is done, that position is what they cannot learn.

Confucius also says, “A sage is someone I have not been able to see.” He does not think he has ever encountered a sage. Naturally, he has not counted himself as one.

Someone may object by citing the siege at Kuang: “King Wen is gone; does not this culture reside here?” King Wen has died, and the cultural inheritance is here in me. Isn't that a claim to sagehood? Look at the occasion. Surrounded, possibly facing death, a person tells himself: I cannot fall; this inheritance has not been broken off in me. That is taking responsibility, not boasting. And he continues: if Heaven meant to destroy this culture, I would not have had a share in it; if Heaven does not destroy it, what can the people of Kuang do to me? He leaves both possibilities in place, rather than simply declaring Heaven's intention to be on his side.

His refusal of sagehood is not a gesture of modesty. It belongs to his whole practice. A sage is something completed: available for worship, reverence, citation, and use as an authority to press other people down. He does not want that position. He wants a place where he is still learning, still teaching, still making mistakes. In earlier essays, his acknowledgment of error, complaint that Yan Hui is too obedient, and declaration that he knows nothing all come from that position. **Once someone has been erected as a sage, every word he spoke becomes untouchable. Refusing sagehood leaves his words open to challenge.**

Some of the students recording these sayings had themselves tried to elevate him. Zigong spoke of Heaven making him a great sage. Yet they recorded exactly how the teacher dismantled their praise, even “I was poor when young and did menial work.” Manufacturers of a sage would not keep these things.

For the next two thousand years, titles accumulated: King Who Spreads Culture; Supreme Sage, King Who Spreads Culture; Great Accomplisher, Supreme Sage, King Who Spreads Culture. Every title was the kind of thing he had pushed away while alive. He spent his life refusing; others spent theirs bestowing. The temples grew larger, and the man who said “How would I dare?” grew harder to see.

A man who spent his life refusing to become a sage was enshrined as one. What do you think he would make of that temple?
