# The Step Beyond the Teacher's Reach

Zilu and Zigong heard the same lesson, then walked different roads.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

A good teacher's students ought all to turn out well. It is a natural and common thought: to judge a teacher's teaching, look at what later became of the students.

Confucius had two students. Later, one died; the other saved a state. They had heard the same lesson.

First, Zilu. He is the student most often admonished by the teacher in the *Analects*, almost always in the same direction. Once, at his most despairing, the teacher said: if the Way cannot prevail, perhaps I should take a raft out to sea. The one to come with me would probably be Zhong You, would it not? Zhong You is Zilu. Zilu was delighted. The teacher continued: You is braver even than I am, but has no sense of how to temper it. Zilu heard that he had been named. He did not hear the second half. Seeing him stand there “bold and unyielding,” strength bursting out of him, the teacher said: someone like You will probably not meet a natural death. When the question arose of whom the teacher would take to command an army, Zilu put himself forward. The teacher said he would not take someone who fought tigers barehanded, crossed rivers on foot, and died without regret. “On hearing it, should I act?” To Zilu, the teacher said: your father and elder brothers are there. Wait first.

Then there was that lesson. Zilu asked: Guan Zhong did not die for Prince Jiu and went on to serve Duke Huan, who had killed Jiu. Surely that was inhumane? The teacher answered: Duke Huan brought the feudal lords together nine times without resorting to war chariots. That was Guan Zhong's achievement. That is his ren. Humaneness lies not in whom you die for but in how many people you enable to flourish. Fidelity that insists on dying for one master is a small fidelity.

Zigong asked the same question. To him the teacher made it still clearer: Guan Zhong benefited the people of the world, and they still enjoy those benefits today. Without him, we would all wear our hair loose and fasten our robes on the left. Should he have held to the narrow fidelity of an ordinary man or woman, killing himself in a ditch where no one would know?

One lesson, two students. Look at where they went afterward.

Zilu later held office in Wei. Father and son contended for the state, and the household he served was drawn into the conflict. The city was already in turmoil. Zilu, outside it, heard the news and headed in. Someone urged him not to enter. He replied: if you eat another man's food, you cannot flee his danger. He went in. During the fighting, his cap strings were cut. He said, “An exemplary person may die, but does not let his cap fall.” Even in death, the cap must be straight. He tied the strings and died. After his death, the teacher cried, “Ah, Heaven has cut me down!” The *Book of Rites* records that he ordered the minced meat in his house thrown away: Zilu had been chopped into mincemeat.

Zigong, when Lu was in danger of attack by Qi, asked to go on a diplomatic mission. He went to Qi, Wu, Yue, and Jin, maneuvering alone among five states. In the end, Qi's army did not attack Lu. The historical account says, “With one mission, he preserved Lu.”

Zilu gave his life for the very kind of fidelity he had asked about: if you have eaten a man's food, die in his danger. Zigong followed Guan Zhong's way and benefited the people of an entire state: he did not ask about the form of action, but about how many people its results reached. **The same lesson: one heard “rightness means dying where one ought to die”; the other heard “rightness means enabling more people to live.”**

This essay is not saying that Zilu was wrong. He had his judgment, acted on it, and followed it to the end. His manner of dying, tying his cap strings, was the manner of his whole life: firm, upright, never evading. The teacher's diagnosis had been right from beginning to end: he pushed past others, was too unyielding, and would not meet a natural end. The diagnosis was right, and the prescription was given, again and again. But Zilu took the last step himself.

That is the point of this essay: **what a teacher can do ends with giving the prescription.** He saw Zilu's tendency to press ahead of others and spent a lifetime holding him back. He spoke of Guan Zhong and explained that ren did not lie in dying for one's lord. He told him to wait, with father and elder brothers still there. All this he gave. Whether to receive it, how to receive it, and what to do with it on that day in Wei belonged to Zilu. The teacher could not reach that far, nor should he. He himself said, “If you are at ease with it, do it,” and “Practicing ren comes from oneself.” Yan Hui's “There is no way to follow” expresses the same boundary: for that last step, no one else can supply a ready-made path. This is the boundary of cultivation, of helping another person form their own judgment: give everything one can, then leave the last step to the other. Leaving it to the other means that the other may go somewhere you hoped they would not.

One more student deserves a mention. Ran You, the hesitant one whom the teacher urged forward with “On hearing it, act,” later became a retainer of the Ji family and helped them increase taxes and amass wealth. The teacher said: he is no disciple of mine. You may beat the drums and attack him. Yet later, when someone asked whether Zhong You and Ran Qiu, another name for Ran You, counted as great ministers, he said no, only ministers who filled their posts. But he added: if ordered to murder a father or ruler, they would not obey. Even after expelling him, he preserved a moral boundary for him. This, too, is one outcome of receiving a teaching: taking in one half and missing the other.

So the notion that “a good teacher's students ought all to turn out well” measures something beyond the teacher's reach. What the teacher can measure is himself: did he see accurately, give appropriately, leave enough room? How the student proceeds belongs to the student. **Teaching fitted to the person is the teacher doing all that lies within his power. Receiving it belongs to the student.** If the two were one and the same, this would not be cultivation of independent judgment. It would be molding someone into a predetermined shape.

One qualification must be explicit. The later lives of Zilu and Zigong are recorded in historical works, not in the *Analects*. Reading them back alongside that lesson is a literary juxtaposition, not proof of causation. We cannot say, “Because the teacher said this, Zigong saved Lu.” The teacher himself would not keep accounts that way. What he gave was a prescription, not a guarantee.

One teacher, two students, two roads, each followed to the end. The teacher did everything he could for them. The step beyond his reach, each took for himself.

In the best teaching you have ever received, who took the last step?
