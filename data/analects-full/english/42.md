# A Funeral Against the Teacher's Wishes

The lavish burial of Yan Hui, and the students who recorded their own disobedience.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

The teacher's dignity is inviolable; students obey him absolutely. That is how many people imagine the school of Confucius. Yet the *Analects* records an occasion when, in the greatest matter of all, the students did not listen.

Yan Hui died. It was the heaviest blow of his life. He cried, “Ah! Heaven has bereft me! Heaven has bereft me!” He wept beyond his usual restraint. When the students reminded him, “You are grieving too deeply,” he answered: if not for this man, for whom should I grieve?

Then came the funeral. Yan Hui's father, Yan Lu, approached first. He wanted Confucius to sell his carriage to buy an outer coffin for Yan Hui. Confucius replied: gifted or not, each is still his father's son. When my son Li died, he had a coffin but no outer coffin. I cannot sell my carriage and go on foot to provide him with one; having held the rank of a grand officer, I may not go on foot. He refused, not because he could not bear to part with his carriage, but because a funeral should follow ritual and the family's actual circumstances. Yan Hui's death was no reason to make an exception.

Then came the students.

> Yan Yuan died. The disciples wished to give him a lavish burial. The Master said, “That must not be done.” The disciples gave him a lavish burial. The Master said, “Hui regarded me as a father, yet I have been unable to regard him as a son. It was not I who did this, but you few disciples.”

Yan Yuan is Yan Hui. The students wanted to bury him lavishly. Confucius said no. They did it anyway. He said: Yan Hui treated me as a father, but I have been unable to treat him as a son. I did not do this. You did.

The teacher said no, and the students went ahead. This is the first instance in the *Analects* of the students collectively defying the teacher and deciding on his behalf, and it happened at a funeral.

We need to see what each side wanted. The teacher wanted to follow ritual. Burying Yan Hui according to ritual meant treating him as a person entitled to the ordinary rites. Confucius had not made an exception even for his own son; he could not make one for Yan Hui. The students wanted to put their own reverence and grief into the funeral. Its grandeur gave their feelings for Yan Hui somewhere to rest. **The teacher took Yan Hui as the end; the students took their own reverence as the end.** Their reverence was real. But it overrode both the teacher's judgment and the ordinary rites due to Yan Hui himself.

“It was not I, but you few disciples.” There is pain in that sentence, and a somewhat helpless drawing of a boundary: this was not what I wanted. I said no. You did it.

Why does this matter? Because it is a prototype. Everything the students later did to the teacher's words is foreshadowed in this funeral. The teacher refused to spell out the “one”; Zengzi filled it in with “doing one's utmost and putting oneself in another's place, and nothing more.” That is a lavish burial. The teacher prescribed for particular people; the students arranged his prescriptions into “the exemplary person's three objects of awe” and “nine considerations.” That is a lavish burial. Zigong declared that Heaven had ordained the teacher a sage; Zilu arranged the display of household retainers when the teacher was seriously ill. These, too, are lavish burials: their own reverence overriding the teacher's intention. While alive, he kept dismantling them: the heavenly ordination, the sham retainers, the lavish funeral. After he died, no one remained to dismantle them. **Yan Hui's lavish burial was the first scene of everything later done “on the teacher's behalf.”**

But this passage has another side, more important than the first.

Who recorded it? The students themselves. They recorded the teacher's “That must not be done,” the fact that “the disciples gave him a lavish burial,” and his verdict: not I, but you. It is a distinctly unflattering record: they disobeyed their teacher and were repudiated to their faces. A group intent on erecting his monument and establishing its own school would have deleted this part, leaving only the splendor of “Yan Hui's lavish burial.” They did not delete it.

The passage therefore proves two things at once. The students could let their reverence override the teacher. And they were honest enough to record that fact just as it stood. **The earliest recorders revered him too much, but they did not lie.** They decided for the teacher, then faithfully wrote down that the teacher had said no.

These two sides together give the *Analects* its character. The students' hands are in it: filling gaps, arranging lists, fashioning an image. Their honesty is there too: preserving the teacher's refusal alongside their own disobedience. If we can separate these two layers today, it is because of the second.

They did not listen to their teacher. Then they honestly recorded that they had not listened.

If someone you revere says no, but you think your reverence matters more than that person's judgment, what will you do?
