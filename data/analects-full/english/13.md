# Humaneness Has No Standard Answer

Three people ask about ren, and receive three different prescriptions.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

The textbook says: the core of Confucius's thought is *ren*, humaneness; humaneness means loving people. One definition, a compact phrase. Write it on the exam and earn your marks.

The difficulty is that many people in the *Analects* ask him what humaneness is, and he gives each person a different answer. Three exchanges occur consecutively at the beginning of the book *Yan Yuan*.

> Yan Yuan asked about humaneness. The Master said, “Mastering oneself and returning to ritual is humaneness. Once one achieves this, the world turns toward humaneness. Practicing humaneness depends on oneself. Could it depend on others?” Yan Yuan said, “May I ask for the particulars?” The Master said, “Do not look at what is contrary to ritual; do not listen to what is contrary to ritual; do not say what is contrary to ritual; do not act contrary to ritual.” Yan Yuan said, “Though I, Hui, am not quick to understand, I ask to put these words into practice.”

Yan Yuan, also called Yan Hui, asks about humaneness. Restrain yourself and return to ritual: that is humaneness. Once you accomplish it, the world will turn toward humaneness. It depends on you; could it depend on someone else? Yan Yuan asks for particulars. Do not look, listen, speak, or act contrary to ritual. Though I am not clever, he says, I will practice these words.

Yan Yuan receives the most abstract answer because he already understands humaneness and needs no explanation from the beginning. He asks for particulars and receives four “do nots.” These mark boundaries. How to look and listen within them is left to Yan Yuan. Even for the highest student, the teacher does not fill in a checklist.

> Zhonggong asked about humaneness. The Master said, “When you go out, be as though meeting an honored guest. When employing the people, be as though attending a great sacrifice. What you do not wish for yourself, do not impose on others. Have no resentment in the state, and no resentment in the household.” Zhonggong said, “Though I, Yong, am not quick to understand, I ask to put these words into practice.”

Zhonggong, whose personal name is Yong, asks about humaneness. Go out as though meeting a distinguished guest. Employ people as though conducting a great sacrifice. Do not place on others what you would not want yourself. No resentment in the state, no resentment in the household. Though I am not clever, he says, I will practice these words.

Zhonggong receives a way of dealing with people: respect and reciprocity. Why? Confucius has said Zhonggong “may be made to face south,” that is, entrusted with governing. “Employ the people as though attending a great sacrifice” is tailored to a future governor.

> Sima Niu asked about humaneness. The Master said, “The humane person is hesitant in speech.” He asked, “Hesitant in speech: is that all it means to be humane?” The Master said, “When doing it is difficult, how could one not hesitate to speak?”

Sima Niu asks about humaneness. A humane person speaks slowly and cautiously. Is speaking cautiously enough to be called humane? Doing it is hard; how could speaking not be cautious?

Sima Niu receives only a short phrase: “hesitant in speech.” He talks too much and is impatient, so his prescription for humaneness treats that alone. He resists: is that all? The teacher says: things are hard to do. Words, once spoken, have to be fulfilled. How can you not be careful?

Three people, three answers: one abstract, one concrete, one extremely brief. They are not three aspects of humaneness. They are medicine for three different people. Notice that the recorders even note whether the medicine was understood. Yan Yuan and Zhonggong both say, “I ask to put these words into practice.” They receive it. Sima Niu asks, “Is that all?” Very possibly he has not understood. The teacher does not switch to an easier prescription because he fails to understand, nor explain further. The medicine may fit the condition without the patient understanding it immediately. Understanding is another layer of the matter.

As for the textbook's “humaneness means loving people,” that appears in the *Analects* too, and is another prescription. Fan Chi asks about humaneness; the Master answers, “Love people.” It is for Fan Chi. Fan Chi asks about humaneness three times and receives three different answers: “Love people”; “Put the difficulty first and the gain afterward”; “Be courteous in daily life, respectful in carrying out duties, and wholehearted with others.” The same person, three questions, three answers, because each time he arrives at a different place. **The phrase later generations treated as a definition was a prescription for one particular person at one particular moment.**

Why does humaneness have no standard answer? Because humaneness means treating people as ends, and people differ. A standard definition, saying the same thing to everyone, treats them precisely as objects to which a single formula can be applied. That is itself inhumane. Giving each a different prescription shows that he sees each as a person. **If humaneness has only one definition, that definition already violates humaneness.**

The compilers place the three passages together at the start of a book. They do not merge them or add “Humaneness is…” as a summary. They know what they are preserving.

That definition of humaneness you learned: for whom was it given?
