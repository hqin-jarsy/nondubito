# It Counts Even If Confucius Did Not Say It

The Zizhang book, where the teacher appears through his students.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

There is one book of the *Analects* in which Confucius does not speak a word. Book Nineteen, “Zizhang,” has twenty-five passages, all spoken by students: Zizhang, Zixia, Ziyou, Zengzi, Zigong. The teacher appears only in their reports: “I heard this from the Master.”

What does this book resemble? The students finding their own voices after the teacher has gone. Some argue, some pass on his words, some defend him against outsiders. Zigong's four defenses, discussed earlier, all occur here. This essay looks at two others: Zixia and Zizhang.

First, an argument.

> Zixia's disciples asked Zizhang about friendship. Zizhang said, “What does Zixia say?” They answered, “Zixia says: ‘Associate with those fit for it; reject those who are not.’” Zizhang said, “That differs from what I have heard. An exemplary person respects the worthy and makes room for the many, commends the good and has compassion for those who cannot manage it. If I am greatly worthy, whom could I not make room for? If I am unworthy, others will reject me. How could I reject them?”

Zixia's students ask Zizhang how to make friends. What does Zixia say? Befriend those you can befriend; reject those you cannot. That is not what I heard, Zizhang replies. Respect the worthy, but also make room for everyone else; appreciate goodness, but also feel for those who fall short. If I am truly worthy, whom can I not accommodate? If I am not worthy, it is others who will reject me. What standing have I to reject them?

Two students, two accounts, pointing in opposite directions. Zixia speaks of discernment: friendship requires judgment. Suitable and unsuitable cannot go undistinguished. Zizhang speaks of self-cultivation: begin with yourself. If I conduct myself well, suitable people will naturally come; if I do not, I should be the one rejected. One moves from outside inward, first discerning the other. The other moves from inside outward, first cultivating oneself.

Which is right? Both are right, and neither can be dispensed with. Discernment without self-cultivation becomes picking people apart. Self-cultivation without discernment becomes indiscriminate association. The teacher said, “A humane person may be led to go, but cannot be trapped.” Ren must be joined to wisdom. In this argument, each student holds one half of the teacher. What is interesting is the recorder: he sets the two accounts side by side without deciding who wins. The students argue in this book, and the book does not settle their dispute.

Now consider two sayings of Zixia.

> Zixia said, “The craftsmen stay in their workshops to accomplish their work; the exemplary person learns to attain his Way.”

The various craftsmen work in their workshops and complete their tasks. The exemplary person reaches his Way through learning.

Notice the syntax. The craftsmen and the exemplary person are set in parallel: one accomplishes work in a workshop; the other reaches the Way through study. The structure is the same. In a sharply hierarchical age, he compares an exemplary person to craftspeople without a trace of contempt. Only the places where they work and the things they accomplish differ. This sentence carries the teacher's sense of equality. He said, “By nature, people are close to one another.” He said, “Then I shall take up driving a chariot.” He does not place the exemplary person in a separate class above all craftsmen. Zixia learned that.

> Zixia said, “In matters of great virtue, do not cross the boundary; in lesser matters, some deviations are permissible.”

One must not breach the great commitments. In small matters, a little deviation may be allowed.

The saying leaves room for people. No one can be faultless in everything. Hold to what matters greatly; do not demand perfection in every small detail. The teacher said, “Do not demand every kind of excellence from one person,” and “Be exacting with yourself and ask little of others”: generosity toward others, rigor toward oneself. Zixia's saying points the same way. It has an opening for trouble, too: who decides what is great and what is small? Once you provide a category labeled “deviation permitted,” people love putting things into it. But the teacher's path was never one that locked down every point. Holding firm here, allowing room there: that is precisely its shape.

Now to this essay's title.

Most of the material we have used to understand Confucius begins “The Master said.” Yet much of it is spoken by students: Zigong's “Why should he have any fixed teacher?” Zizhang's “respect the worthy and make room for the many,” Zixia's “the craftsmen stay in their workshops,” Zengzi's “sorrow and compassion, not delight.” Confucius did not personally utter these words. But they carry his structure: treating people as people, leaving room, refusing to rank them into different orders. **We seek to understand not only Confucius as an individual, but his school: what that group learned from him, and how far each person's understanding reached.** Whether a saying helps us understand him depends on whether it has that structure, not on whether it begins “The Master said” or “Zixia said.”

That is why, throughout this series, the question “Did Confucius really say it?” has remained secondary. Something a student says rightly counts. Something labeled “The Master said” but arranged into a table does not serve our understanding of him. We judge the words, not the name.

Can a book without the teacher let you see the teacher? Look at what the students say, how they say it, how they argue. He is there in them.

Can what you learned from your teacher still be seen in your own words?
