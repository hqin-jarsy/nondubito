# The Sage Admits His Mistake in Front of the Class

“What I said before was only a joke.”

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

“The Master said” eventually became a seal. Stamp it on something and the matter was settled, beyond discussion. Emperors cited it, teachers cited it, examinations cited it. The sage cannot be wrong; what the sage said cannot be wrong. That was the seal's entire meaning.

Yet once in the *Analects*, “The Master said” is immediately challenged by a student using another “The Master said.”

> The Master went to Wucheng and heard strings and singing. He smiled and said, “Why use an ox-cleaver to kill a chicken?” Ziyou replied, “Formerly I, Yan, heard you say: ‘When those who govern learn the Way, they love people; when ordinary people learn the Way, they are easy to govern.’” The Master said, “My students! Yan is right. What I said before was only a joke.”

He arrives in Wucheng, where his student Ziyou serves as local governor. As he enters, he hears music and singing. Ziyou is educating the people through ritual and music. Confucius smiles: do you need an ox-cleaver to slaughter a chicken?

Ziyou replies: I once heard you say that those who govern learn to love people through the Way, and ordinary people become easier to govern through the Way.

Confucius says: students! Yan Yan is right. My previous remark was a joke. Yan Yan, also called simply Yan in the exchange, is Ziyou.

Look at the exchange. The teacher sounds slightly dismissive: such an elaborate undertaking for such a small place. The student neither sulks nor makes excuses. He brings the teacher's own words back unchanged: you said the Way benefits everyone, governors and ordinary people alike. Why should Wucheng's people not learn it? The challenge lands exactly. Ziyou does not invent another doctrine to refute the teacher. He uses the teacher's principle, applying it where the teacher just failed to apply it. **The sign that a student has truly learned from a teacher is not the ability to repeat the teaching, but the ability to use it to correct the teacher.**

Now consider the response. The teacher has many options. He can ignore it: teachers need not answer students. He can explain it away: “By ox-cleaver I meant something else.” He can concede privately, taking Ziyou aside later to say he was right. He chooses none of these. He calls out, “My students!” Everyone, listen. Then: Yan Yan is right; I was joking. Publicly, by name, without an elaborate defense.

That “My students!” deserves another look. What he wants everyone to hear is not that he admits a mistake, but that Ziyou's words make more sense than what he just said. **I read less defeat here than pride.** A student has used his principle to defeat his remark. That is precisely what he most wants to see. He has complained that Yan Hui is delighted by everything he says and therefore does not help him. Now a student pushes back. Of course he wants the whole class to hear.

This is not his only admission of error. Someone from another state once asks whether Duke Zhao of Lu understands ritual. He says yes. Afterward, the man points out that Duke Zhao married a woman of his own surname, a violation of ritual. How can Confucius say he understands it? When this is reported to him, Confucius replies, “I, Qiu, am fortunate. Whenever I make a mistake, someone is sure to know it.” Another time, Zai Yu talks well, and Confucius trusts him, only to find him sleeping in the daytime. He says: formerly I listened to people's words and trusted their actions; now I listen to their words and observe their actions. Zai Yu taught me to change. **He credits an improvement in his own method to a student's failure.** He also says something firmer still: “To make a mistake and not correct it: that is a mistake.” Error itself is not the fault; refusing to correct it is.

The students learn this. Zigong later says: “The exemplary person's faults are like eclipses of the sun and moon. When he errs, everyone sees it; when he changes, everyone looks up to him.” This is not an attempt to cover for the teacher. It is a posture learned from him. The error can be public, and so can the correction.

An infallible sage would need none of these statements. To err, acknowledge it, correct it, and let people know: this is someone placing himself where he can be corrected.

The recorders could easily have omitted the scene. A school intent on manufacturing a sage would never preserve the whole sequence: teacher misspeaks, student corrects him, teacher concedes in public. It would write, “The Master arrived at Wucheng, heard strings and singing, and approved.” They did not. They kept the dismissive joke, Ziyou's objection, and the teacher's admission without deleting a sentence. They even kept the smile.

I call this cultivation: at the end of your teaching, a student can use your principles to challenge your words, and you are glad. Without that step, everything before it teaches only repetition.

“The Master said,” then, is not a seal. The first person in the *Analects* who refuses to treat “The Master said” as an imperial decree is Confucius himself.

Do you allow other people to bring your own words back and challenge you with them?
