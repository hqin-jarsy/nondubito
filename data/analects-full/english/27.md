# The Man Who Always Keeps His Word: Confucius Called Him Small-Minded

The danger lies not in faithfulness, but in the unconditional “must.”

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

Humaneness, rightness, ritual, wisdom, faithfulness: the Five Constants, five words associated with Confucianism. Faithfulness comes last, but later its weight grew ever greater. A promise worth a thousand pieces of gold. Once a word is spoken, it must be acted on. Say it, do it. “One's words must always be trustworthy; one's actions must always be carried through.” How many people have made these six Chinese characters a creed for life, believing this is what the sage taught?

Here is what the sage said:

> Zigong asked, “What must someone be like to be called a person of worth?” The Master said, “Someone who conducts himself with a sense of shame and, when sent on missions in the four directions, does not disgrace his ruler's commission may be called a person of worth.” “May I ask about the next rank?” “His clan calls him filial, and his community calls him respectful toward his elders.” “May I ask about the next rank?” “Someone whose words must always be trustworthy and whose actions must always be carried through: a hard, clattering, small-minded fellow! Still, he may be counted in the next rank.”

Zigong asks what makes a person worthy of the name *shi*. Confucius answers: know shame in your conduct, and do not betray the ruler's charge when sent abroad. What about a lower rank? Your clan praises your devotion to parents, your community your respect for elder brothers. And lower still? Always keep your word; always carry an action through. What a rigid, small-minded fellow! Nevertheless, he may qualify for the lowest rank.

“Always keep your word; always carry an action through” ranks third for Confucius, and comes with a comment: a hard, clattering, small-minded fellow. *Keng-keng* evokes stones knocking together: hard, brittle, incapable of change. “Small-minded” here does not mean wicked. It means narrow in scope, unable to judge.

Why? The fault lies in “must.” Keeping one's word is good, but “must always” closes down judgment. Whether the promise ought to be kept or not, keep it. Whether the situation has changed or not, carry it through. Faithfulness should answer to rightness: keep the promise when it ought to be kept, do not when it ought not. Resolve should answer to the affair itself: finish when it should be finished, stop when the circumstances have changed. Turn these into unconditional “musts,” and a matter calling for judgment becomes a rule forbidding judgment. **What he opposes is not faithfulness but “faithfulness at all costs”; not carrying something through, but “carrying it through at all costs.”**

He stated the same principle several times. Among the four things he rejected was “insistence on certainty.” He said: “In dealing with the world, the exemplary person is unconditionally for nothing and unconditionally against nothing; he stands with what is right.” There is no fixed “must do this” or “must not do this.” He follows rightness alone. Defending the statesman Guan Zhong, Confucius also said that keeping faith unto death for one particular master was “the scrupulous fidelity of ordinary men and women”: a petty faithfulness.

Where, then, did the “faithfulness” of the Five Constants come from? The *Analects* has this sentence: “The Master taught four things: cultural learning, conduct, wholehearted commitment, and faithfulness.” He taught his students four things: the classics, ethical conduct, giving oneself fully, and honesty. Commitment and faithfulness have a place here. They are two forms of inward sincerity in a teacher's work with students, paired with learning and conduct. Later readers lifted “faithfulness” out of that place and elevated it into a free-floating law of promise-keeping applicable everywhere, putting it alongside *ren*, humaneness. Something with a place is alive and operates according to circumstances. Something without a place is dead and is applied uniformly. Treating “always keep your word and always carry an action through” as a virtue is precisely the result of elevating faithfulness into an absolute law. He had already passed judgment on that result: small-minded.

The Five Constants did something else: they put wisdom fourth. Look at his own ordering:

> The Master said, “If wisdom reaches it but humaneness cannot hold it, though it is gained it will certainly be lost. If wisdom reaches it and humaneness can hold it, but one does not preside over it with dignity, the people will not be respectful. If wisdom reaches it, humaneness can hold it, and one presides over it with dignity, but sets the people in motion without ritual, it is not yet good.”

If wisdom reaches it but humaneness cannot preserve it, what has been gained will still be lost. If wisdom is sufficient and humaneness can preserve it, but one's bearing lacks dignity, the people will not show respect. If wisdom is sufficient, humaneness can preserve it, and one's bearing is dignified, but one's actions do not accord with ritual, things are still not good.

Four levels, with wisdom first. Without wisdom, nothing that follows can even be discussed: you cannot see clearly what ought to be preserved. For him, wisdom is not a virtue. It is the precondition of every virtue: humaneness needs eyes. He said: “The humane person can be made to go, but cannot be trapped; can be deceived, but cannot be utterly deluded.” The humane person may be fooled for a time, but cannot have his mind blinded. Humaneness without wisdom is something other people will put to their own use.

The Five Constants demote wisdom to fourth place and elevate faithfulness into an absolute. Both moves point in the same direction. Judgment is lowered from “precondition” to “one item among the virtues”; one virtue is raised from “requiring judgment” to “forbidding judgment.” **Once judgment is demoted to a virtue on a list, humaneness loses its eyes.** A humaneness without eyes is just the partner for a faithfulness that allows no judgment. Simply do as instructed.

That is the distance between the Five Constants and Confucius. The words are all his. The arrangement is not.

Has your creed of “doing what you say” ever made you finish something that should not have been done?
