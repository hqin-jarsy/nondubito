# Later Generations Drew His Pedigree of the Way

The opening of Yao Yue and the uses of an orthodox succession.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

The first passage in the final book of the Analects does not begin “The Master said.”

> Yao said, “Ah, Shun! Heaven's succession has fallen upon your person. Hold faithfully to the middle. If all within the four seas suffer hardship and destitution, Heaven's bounty will end forever.” Shun likewise charged Yu. It is said: “I, the young man Lü, dare to offer a black bull and make this solemn declaration to the august Supreme Lord. ... If there is guilt in my person, do not implicate the myriad lands; if the myriad lands are guilty, let the guilt rest in my person.” Zhou made great grants, and good people prospered. “Though there are close kinsmen, they are no match for benevolent people. If the people commit a fault, let it rest on me alone.” ... What mattered: the people, food, mourning, sacrifice. Generosity wins the multitude; trustworthiness wins the people's confidence; diligence brings achievement; fairness brings delight.

Yao tells Shun: Heaven's succession has fallen to you; hold fast to the middle way. Shun passes the same charge to Yu. Tang, the king who calls himself Lü in the quotation, says: if I am guilty, do not implicate the myriad lands; if the myriad lands are guilty, the guilt is mine alone. Zhou makes great grants, and good people prosper. Close kin are less valuable than benevolent people. If the people err, the fault is mine alone. ... What is given weight: the people, food, mourning, sacrifice. Generosity wins people's hearts; trustworthiness earns their trust; diligence brings results; fairness makes everyone glad.

What is this passage? Words handed from Yao to Shun and from Shun to Yu; Tang's prayer; proclamations of the Zhou king; finally, an outline of government. Its style resembles the Book of Documents. A collection of one person's sayings suddenly contains a book of royal proclamations. At the opening of the last book, it seems to give the whole work an origin: from Yao to Shun to Yu to Tang to Zhou, handed down all the way until it reaches him.

First, its merits, which are many. “If there is guilt in my person, do not implicate the myriad lands; if the myriad lands are guilty, let the guilt rest in my person”: the ruler takes responsibility rather than passing it to the people. “What mattered: the people, food, mourning, sacrifice”: the people come first. “Restore extinguished states, continue severed lineages, bring forward overlooked men”: do not let others' lines die out; do not discard talent. These sayings point in the direction of his consistent advice to rulers: hold yourself responsible first, and put the people first. As principles of government, they stand up.

What, then, are we saying? We are asking what the passage has been used to do.

To establish an orthodox succession of the Way. Yao, Shun, Yu, Tang, Kings Wen and Wu, the Duke of Zhou, then Confucius: a pedigree, one continuous line. Placing these proclamations at the book's end gives that pedigree a foothold in the text. Later readers go further and further. To the four characters “hold faithfully to the middle,” they add another twelve from the Book of Documents, making the “sixteen-character transmission of the mind.” They call it an inner doctrine transmitted from Yao through Shun and Yu to Confucius, and then into the hands of a few particular successors.

Once an orthodox succession is established, something happens. The test of whether a statement is right shifts from “Where does its rightness lie?” to “Who transmitted it?” Those on the pedigree have the final say; those outside it are heretics. Yet he says, “People can enlarge the Way; the Way does not enlarge people.” People make the Way great, not the other way around. The Way survives not through a pedigree, but only through people practicing it. Throughout his life, he establishes no inner doctrine to be transmitted: he transmits rather than creates; speaks of one thread running through his teaching without spelling out the “one”; dismantles “Heaven has endowed him as a sage”; says, “As for sagehood and benevolence, how could I dare claim them?” **A man who never agreed to establish an inner doctrine is entered into a pedigree of such a doctrine, placed at its end, and made the point at which the pedigree arrives.**

We must be precise about our objection. Recounting Yao, Shun, Yu, and Tang is not the same as establishing an exclusive orthodox succession. The former remembers good things earlier people did; the latter makes pedigree the criterion. We do not assert that this book itself necessarily accomplishes the latter. We oppose its use to prove that “the Way has one orthodox line of transmission; inheritors of that line possess authority, and those outside it are heretics.” **That use replaces “where rightness lies” with “who transmitted it,” making a man who refused to be a sage into the endpoint of a sacred succession.**

Why can it not help us know him? The reason is simple: he is not in it. No “The Master said,” no conversation, no particular person asking a question. It is material the compilers placed in the book. Its inclusion shows that they wanted the book to have an origin. We leave it in that place: a compiler's document, much of its content commendable, its uses requiring caution.

**What we oppose:** Reading this passage as “an exclusive orthodox succession and transmission of an inner doctrine,” then establishing that “inheritors of this line possess authority; those who do not conform to it are heretics.”

**Why:** Once the succession is established, the criterion moves from “where rightness lies” to “who transmitted it.” He says people enlarge the Way, not the Way people, and never establishes an inner doctrine for transmission.

**What we do not infer:** We do not determine who composed the passage, equate recounting Yao and Shun with establishing an orthodox succession, or deny the merits of its principles of government. We say only that this use of orthodox succession does not fit Confucius as we understand him.

Do you trust your “orthodoxy” because it is right, or because it is on the pedigree?
