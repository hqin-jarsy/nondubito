# Why He Wrote No Book

“I transmit but do not compose”: a prescription is not a universal formula.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

A man who founded an intellectual tradition did not write a single book. That is rare in the history of thought. Laozi has his five thousand words; Zhuangzi, his thirty-three chapters; Mozi, Mencius, and Xunzi all have books. The *Analects* that Confucius left us was recorded by his students. In this series’ reading, a reason can be found in his own words:

> The Master said, “I transmit but do not compose. I trust and love the ancient. In private, I compare myself to our Old Peng.”

I only transmit; I do not create. I trust and love what is ancient. Privately, I compare myself to Old Peng.

The most common reading is that Confucius was conservative. He merely passed on his predecessors’ words and had no original ideas of his own. Later Confucians followed him, and “transmit but do not compose” became a governing principle: no innovation; whatever is ancient is good. This reading follows the surface of the words and the impression accumulated over two thousand years. Yet it reads the sentence precisely backward.

First consider “trust and love the ancient.” What antiquity did he love? He said he could describe the rites of the Xia, but that the state of Qi (杞) lacked sufficient material to substantiate them; he could describe the rites of the Yin, but Song lacked sufficient material: “because the documents and learned witnesses are insufficient.” He loved an antiquity supported by evidence, not anything merely because it was old. He also said that Yin ritual derived from Xia ritual, with additions and subtractions, and Zhou ritual from Yin ritual, again with additions and subtractions: “Even a hundred generations hence, what succeeds the Zhou can be known.” The context is what can be known about the inheritance and alteration of ritual. He saw ritual as something continually changing. A man who listed obstinacy among the four failings he had eliminated, who said that the young were to be regarded with awe, and who said of himself that he had “no unconditional must and no unconditional must not,” was not a conservative.

Now consider “do not compose.” This is the important part.

This series connects his not writing with a particular limitation: written words say the same thing to everyone who reads them. So many of the preceding essays have been about one thing. He said different things to different people. First he looked to see whether you had struggled far enough to form a question. He showed one corner and left three for you; if you could not work them out, he stopped teaching. This whole practice has a prerequisite: he is present, and opposite him is a particular person. Once it becomes a book, that prerequisite disappears. A book does not know who is reading. It cannot first gauge someone’s readiness. It cannot stop at the point where it ought to stop. It cannot tell Zilu “wait” and Ran You “act.” A book says the same thing to every reader.

**We therefore read “do not compose” this way: once his method of teaching is written down as a universal formula, its nature changes.** It is not that he could not write. A prescription made out for a particular patient becomes, in a book, a prescription for everyone. He did not want to prepare a single medicine in advance for the entire population. Poetry, documents, ritual, and music were the medicinal ingredients. He tended the dispensary; each prescription was written fresh. This is what this series understands by “transmitting.” “Not composing” means refusing to fix the prescription once and for all.

Incidentally, this does not mean that a cultivator, someone who supports others in developing their own judgment and becoming agents of their own actions, cannot write books. Writing cannot respond to each person in real time: that is a structural limitation, not a sin. The problem is fixing the prescription. He chose not to write. That was his choice. Others may choose another route, provided they do not turn their words into something dead.

Others found different answers to the same difficulty. The Athenian, Socrates, also knew the danger: writing cannot choose its readers and cannot answer further questions. His student Plato took another route. He wrote extensively, but in dialogues, using questions, irony, and endings without conclusions to make the text resist being distilled into dogma. This was a defense. Yet it was a more fragile defense: sentences in a dialogue can always be extracted and treated as conclusions. “Philosopher-king” was extracted in just this way. Confucius’s judgment was thus vindicated: once the highest teaching is fixed in writing, its conversion into dogma is almost impossible to escape.

But his own defense failed too.

He did not write; his students wrote for him. They recorded what he said to whom, and did their best to record the scenes and the names. As long as those names remained, the prescriptions were still prescriptions. Later readers, however, stripped away the names and the scenes, leaving bare “The Master said” pronouncements to hang up one by one. What he had tried to prevent by not writing happened after his death nonetheless. And it happened through his own words: **“I transmit but do not compose” itself became a charter for conservatism. The sentence with which he refused to establish dogma became a license for dogma.** The poison and the antidote were the same sentence.

This was not the students’ fault. Without their records, we would see nothing today. They preserved those unrepeatable scenes: “When I hear it, should I act on it?” “If you are at ease, then do it.” “Was anyone hurt?” without asking about the horses. “I wish to say nothing.” Those scenes allow us, two thousand years later, to put the names back together with the prescriptions.

The preceding seventeen essays have been about the kind of person he was. Next comes a different question: how his words were misread; which true statements had their names stripped away, their direction reversed, their open spaces filled with lists. Each essay will use the same method: put the name back, and see to whom the words were originally spoken.

If your words had to be written down for everyone to read, how would you write them?
