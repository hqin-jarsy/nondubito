# They Could Have Left It Out

What the recorders preserved when it made neither teacher nor students look good.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

There is an account a little cleverer than “Confucius was a sage”: the *Analects* is his students' idealization of their teacher, a promotional brochure. After he died, they gathered the fine things he had said, removed the unflattering parts, and assembled a perfect image.

Testing that account requires only one thing: look for anything in the book that they could have left out.

There is plenty.

They recorded the teacher misjudging someone. He spoke with Yan Hui all day; Yan Hui never contradicted him. His judgment was “as if foolish,” like a dullard. Only after observing him quietly afterward did he revise it: “Hui is not foolish.” Someone intent on erecting a statue of the teacher would erase “as if foolish” and simply write that he recognized Yan Hui's excellence at once. They preserved his mistake.

They recorded the teacher losing his temper. Zai Yu slept during the day, and the teacher scolded him: “Rotten wood cannot be carved; a wall of foul earth cannot be plastered.” This is an anger no sacred image can accommodate. They recorded it anyway, and recorded what followed: once, when I heard what someone said, I trusted that he would act accordingly; now I must watch what he does. “It was because of Yu that I changed this.” Zai Yu taught me to change. The teacher admitted that a student's failure had caused him to revise his method. They recorded that too.

They recorded the teacher's despair. “The phoenix does not come; the River brings forth no chart. I am finished!” No phoenix, no River Chart, those auspicious signs of a rightly ordered age. It is over for me. They did not add, “Then he rallied.” They left the sigh there, just as it was.

They recorded the teacher breaking his own rule. He had taught “sorrow without injury”: grief that does not go so far as to harm the grieving person. When Yan Hui died, his weeping became anguish beyond measure. His attendants reminded him, “Master, you are grieving excessively!” He answered: am I? If not for this man, for whom should I grieve so? They preserved both the teaching and his breaking of it.

They recorded their own failures. At Yan Hui's funeral, the teacher said there must be no lavish burial. They gave him one. He said, “It was not I, but you few disciples.” They recorded the words by which he dissociated himself from them. When he was gravely ill, Zilu made the disciples act as household retainers, arranging the display appropriate to a grand officer's funeral. As soon as the teacher improved a little, he rebuked him: “How long You has practiced deception! Having no retainers, yet pretending to have them, whom would I deceive? Would I deceive Heaven?” You is Zilu's personal name. The senior disciple was accused of deception and of deceiving Heaven; they deleted not a word. Zigong said Heaven had ordained the teacher a sage. The teacher dismantled that claim on the spot. They kept Zigong's words and the teacher's dismantling together.

They recorded the teacher being refuted. Ziyou challenged him with his own words, and he called the students together to acknowledge his error: “What I said before was only in jest.” They recorded him failing to persuade a student. Zai Wo, another name for Zai Yu, said three years of mourning was too long. The teacher explained his reasons; Zai Wo remained unconvinced. The teacher said, “If you are at ease with it, do it.” Only after Zai Wo left did he express dissatisfaction. An idealized account would have rewritten this as Zai Wo's sudden repentance. They did not rewrite it.

They recorded others mocking the teacher. A gatekeeper called him someone who “knows it cannot be done and does it anyway.” A recluse jeered that he “does not toil with his limbs and cannot distinguish the five grains.” Weisheng Mu addressed him to his face as “Qiu,” his personal name, and accused him of glibness. They recorded all of it.

They also recorded what the teacher did not know and did not speak about: “I have no knowledge”; “utterly empty”; “The Master seldom spoke of profit, fate, and ren”; “The Master did not speak of strange events, feats of force, disorder, or spirits.” Zigong testified: “The Master's words on human nature and the Way of Heaven could not be heard.” A promotional brochure would say, “He knew everything and answered every question.” They wrote a whole string of negatives.

Taken together, these details mean that “promotional brochure” cannot explain the whole book. Someone intent only on constructing an image would struggle to explain why it preserves mistaken judgments, anger, despair, broken rules, admissions of error, refutations, insults, the students' own disobedience, and the exposure of their own ceremonial pretenses. **Every one of these things could have gone unrecorded. Recording them did nothing for the teacher's image or the students' own dignity. They recorded them.**

Why? Probably because what they wanted to record was not an image but a person. This was their teacher as they saw him: capable of error, anger, despair, breaking his own rules, conceding defeat in public, and being summed up by a gatekeeper in a single sentence. They accepted this person. So they recorded him, including the unflattering parts.

As discussed earlier, he did not write a book; the students wrote for him. Later readers stripped away names and scenes until his words became universal maxims. That was something the students failed to prevent. But fairness requires us to say that it is precisely because they recorded so honestly, preserving who asked, where it happened, the teacher's little smile, his tapping a leg with his staff, Zai Wo's “I am at ease,” Gongxi Hua's “I am confused,” that we can put the names back today. **What they left was not a statue but a collection of scenes with their rough edges intact.** Later readers smoothed those edges away, but the originals remain in the book.

Of course, there were things they could not record: the living instant in which the teacher made each judgment. “Xiang Dang” records how he ate and how he walked. It cannot record why. That is a limit of recording, not a fault of the recorders. They did their best. This was as far as a record could go.

The *Analects* therefore has two layers. One is the students' reverence and handiwork: lavish burials, filling gaps, arranging lists, constructing an image. The other is their honesty: recording everything an idealizing account ought to have omitted. It is the second layer that makes the book readable.

If you were recording someone you revere, would you include the mistaken judgments, the anger, the despair, and the admissions of error?
