# Eight Characters and a Checklist

Five virtues, four evils, and an answer fitted to its questioner.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

One pattern recurs throughout the first thirty-one essays: whenever he answers a question, he addresses the person asking. To the same question, he tells Zilu not to act and Ran You to act. Four people ask about filial conduct and each receives a different prescription; three ask about benevolence and each receives a different medicine. The answer addresses the person, not the question.

This pattern can be used in reverse. If a passage says “Someone asked, and the Master said,” but the answer does not fit that person, suspicion is warranted. Is this a real exchange, or a ready-made piece fitted with the shell of a conversation?

A passage in the final book of the Analects provides the best touchstone.

> Zizhang asked Confucius, “What must one do to take part in government?” The Master said, “Honor five virtues and cast out four evils; then you may take part in government.” Zizhang asked, “What are the five virtues?” The Master said, “The gentleman benefits people without expense, puts them to work without resentment, desires without greed, is at ease without arrogance, and commands respect without fierceness.” ... Zizhang asked, “What are the four evils?” The Master said, “To execute without teaching is cruelty; to demand results without warning is violence; to issue orders slowly but insist on a tight deadline is predation; to give people what is theirs while being grudging in its disbursement is the behavior of a petty official.”

Zizhang asks what qualifies one for government. The answer: honor five virtues and eliminate four evils. What are the five virtues? Benefit others without incurring expense oneself; put people to work without making them resentful; desire without greed; be composed without arrogance; be imposing without fierceness. What are the four evils? Killing without educating is cruelty; demanding results without warning is violence; issuing orders late but imposing a short deadline is predation; giving people something while being stingy in handing it over is petty officialdom.

There is little to fault in the list itself. Every one of the five virtues and four evils is sound. As reminders for governing, they are excellent.

The problem lies in the words “Zizhang asked.”

What sort of person is Zizhang? The book gives a consistent portrait: aloof, with a touch of arrogance. The Master says he “goes too far.” A fellow student says, “How grand Zizhang is! Yet it is hard to practice benevolence alongside him.” Ziyou says, “He does what is difficult to accomplish, but he is not yet benevolent.” His problems are lofty detachment, concern for outward show, and enthusiasm that does not last. Not greed.

Now look at what this list prescribes for him. “Desires without greed”: Zizhang is not greedy, so that item is no use to him. “Commands respect without fierceness”: that concerns one's bearing in governing the people, and Zizhang holds no office. “Puts them to work without resentment”: a technique for employing the populace. The four evils are further away still. Cruelty, violence, predation, petty officialdom are all faults of officeholders controlling the people. Not one applies to Zizhang. Of nine items, only “at ease without arrogance” can even loosely be said to address him: the aloof do indeed need to guard against arrogance. **A prescription of nine items, eight unrelated to the patient.**

Compare how the Master answers Zizhang on government elsewhere:

> Zizhang asked about government. The Master said, “Hold office without flagging; carry out your duties with wholehearted commitment.”

Eight Chinese characters: 居之无倦，行之以忠. Do not slacken in office; put your whole heart into carrying out your work. Exactly fitted to Zizhang's condition: his enthusiasm does not last, so “without flagging”; he attends to appearances rather than substance, so “with wholehearted commitment.”

Eight characters, and a checklist. The eight characters address the person; the checklist addresses the topic “entering government.” **The difference in length is precisely the difference between teaching fitted to a person and a ready-made package.** When a person answers a question, the patient is present in the answer. When a checklist wears the shell of a conversation, only the topic is present.

This is a reverse test of individualized teaching. Wherever it says “Someone asked,” first check whether the answer fits that person. If it does not, suspect that the conversational shell was fitted afterward: a ready-made program of government needed a questioner to make it resemble personal instruction. Zizhang's name supplies the shell here.

The method works elsewhere. “Zizhang asked about benevolence” receives five items: respectfulness, generosity, trustworthiness, diligence, beneficence. The “six sayings and six distortions” addressed to Zilu contain six pairs. “The gentleman has nine things to consider,” though no one asks, belongs to the same class. A name without teaching fitted to the person is a checklist dressed as a conversation. Seen this way, the role of several passages becomes clear. They are outlines compiled by the school, not prescriptions he gave to a particular person.

Let us be clear about what this essay does not oppose. We do not oppose the contents of the five virtues and four evils. Every item is good. We do not call the passage fabricated or determine who spoke it. We say only that it is not an answer fitted to its questioner, and therefore cannot serve as evidence for knowing this person. What he gave Zizhang was eight characters.

**What we oppose:** We do not oppose the contents of this checklist.

**Why it is not an anchor:** The answer misses the person. Eight of its nine items have nothing to do with Zizhang's condition. His other answer to Zizhang has only eight Chinese characters, precisely fitted to that condition.

**What we do not infer:** We do not call the passage fabricated or determine who said it. We say only that this is not how the person we understand answers questions, so we do not use it to know him.

The next time you see “Someone asked, and the Master said,” will you look first at the answer or at the person?
