# People Differ. That Is Why They Must Not Be Ranked

“Those who know from birth rank highest.”

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

One saying in the Analects divides knowledge into four ranks:

> Confucius said, “Those who know from birth rank highest; those who know through learning come next; those who learn when confronted with difficulty come next again. Those who meet difficulty and still do not learn are the lowest.”

Knowing at birth is highest; learning before knowing is a rank below; learning only when difficulty arises is lower still; encountering difficulty and refusing to learn makes one the lowest.

This passage is often cited as proof: see, Confucianism recognizes genius and human hierarchy. Confucius himself drew up the ranks.

Let us first make something clear, and correct ourselves. We once opposed this sentence with an argument of our own: Confucius explicitly said, “I am not someone who knows from birth”; since he did not consider himself born knowing, a saying that ranks inborn knowledge highest does not fit him. That argument does not hold, and we withdraw it. “I am not someone who knows from birth” and “If there is someone who knows from birth, that person ranks highest in this classification” can both be true. Not placing oneself in the highest category does not mean denying that the category exists. To object on that ground was to rush the argument.

What, then, do we oppose?

First consider what the sentence does. It divides “how knowledge is acquired” into four kinds: knowing from birth, knowing through learning, learning under difficulty, and not learning even under difficulty. As a description of modes of acquisition, this is unobjectionable in itself. Some people learn quickly, some slowly, some only when forced, some not even then. These are differences, and differences are real. The first thirty-one essays discuss his teaching according to the person. Such teaching is needed precisely because people differ: opposite prescriptions for Zilu and Ran You; different accounts of benevolence for Yan Yuan, Zhonggong, and Sima Niu. If everyone were alike, teaching fitted to the person would be pointless. **We do not oppose acknowledging differences. Difference is a condition of cultivation, not an obstacle to it.**

We oppose the next step: turning a description of “modes” into a grading of “people.” Highest, next, next again, lowest: four terms of rank turn differences in ways of learning into differences in human grade. Once people have grades, a whole chain follows. Those of lower grade have lower standing as ends; they may be taught less, or not at all; their grade is inborn, so there is no need to expend effort. This entire chain is what he spent his life dismantling. He says teaching admits no distinctions. He does not interrogate the Huxiang boy's past. He says the young deserve respect: how do we know those coming after us will not equal those of today? He says a fault left uncorrected is truly a fault: people can change. **Difference is real, and difference does not authorize hierarchy.**

The final clause matters especially: “Those who meet difficulty and still do not learn, min, are the lowest.” Read as “the common people are the lowest,” it collides directly with “teaching without distinctions.” But there is another reading: min can mean “such people” generally, those who do not learn even when faced with difficulty, rather than the populace specifically. We do not declare “placing the populace lowest” the only reading. But whenever someone reads it that way and uses it to despise ordinary people, we oppose that use.

Why can this saying not serve as evidence for knowing him? Not because it says something wrong, but because of its form: four ranks, itemized in order, with no addressee, no scene, no space for the listener to think. When he speaks of knowledge, he tells Zilu, by name, “To know what you know and acknowledge what you do not know.” With an uncultivated man, he “probes the question from both ends,” there and then. He does not draw up tables. This passage looks like a table.

This essay therefore makes two points. First, we withdraw an argument we once used. It does not hold, and an invalid argument cannot be retained merely because it points in the right direction. Second, we oppose the reading that turns difference into hierarchy, not the acknowledgment of difference. Acknowledging difference is precisely where teaching fitted to the person begins.

**What we oppose:** Reading these four ranks as grades of human beings, then using them to lower some people's standing as ends and withdraw their opportunities to be taught.

**Why:** Difference is the condition for teaching according to the person, not a reason for ranking people. He never divides access to teaching according to a person's capacities.

**What we do not infer:** We do not determine who said this. We also withdraw an earlier argument: “Since he says he did not know from birth, this sentence cannot be true together with his own statement” is invalid. We say only that turning difference into hierarchy does not fit Confucius as we understand him.

If you believe people differ, will you conclude “therefore teach each differently” or “therefore rank them”?
