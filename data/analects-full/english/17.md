# Confucius’s Four Refusals

What his students saw was not four achievements, but four things he would not do.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

Textbooks put a label on each of two men. Confucius: purposeful action, engagement with the world, ritual and moral teaching. Laozi: nonaction, withdrawal from the world, naturalness. One Confucian, one Daoist. One pressing forward, the other stepping back.

In the *Analects*, a sentence records Confucius’s cultivation of himself. Every one of its four terms is a refusal:

> The Master was free of four things: no conjecture, no insistence on certainty, no obstinacy, no self-centeredness.

He had done away with four habits: guessing without grounds, declaring something absolutely certain, clinging to his own opinion, and placing himself at the center.

This is a student’s observation, not his own account of himself. There is something unusual about the observer: what he recorded was four negatives. He did not rewrite them as four positive virtues, such as “good at discerning, capable of deciding, possessed of his own views, attentive to the self.” Students would ordinarily be the people most eager to list their teacher’s accomplishments. Yet what these students recorded was what he did not do.

The four refusals are four sides of one thing. “Conjecture” means forming an idea in advance, before the matter has become clear. “Insistence” means locking a judgment in place and saying it must be so. “Obstinacy” means holding on once that judgment has been established. “Self-centeredness” means taking oneself as the measure of everything. All four describe the same movement: first erect a frame, then fit people and things into it. He did not make that movement. What the four “noes” reject is setting up the frame in advance.

This is consistent with his other practices. He did not initiate lectures on *ren*, the ethical quality conventionally called humaneness. He said he had no knowledge; when someone came with a question, he explored only that person’s question. On two occasions he did not spell out the “one.” Even to Yan Yuan, also known as Yan Hui, his highest-achieving student, what he gave was four prohibitions: do not look, listen, speak, or act contrary to ritual. For those at the highest point, he always used the negative form. He marked boundaries without filling in the contents. The four refusals in his own life are the same practice applied to himself.

Of the four, the refusal of self-centeredness is the deepest. Once a person establishes the “I” as a fixed frame, everyone else becomes an object within it. What suits me is good; what does not is bad. Every person becomes something measured out by my yardstick. Without that frame, others can each be the persons they are, each an end in their own right. Earlier essays discussed how he taught according to the person, and how his answers were for people, not for questions. Beneath all of this lies his refusal to make himself the measure. **“No self-centeredness” does not mean having no self. It means not using the self as a frame to impose on others.**

Now look again at those two labels.

The opening sentence of Laozi’s book says: “A Way that can be spoken is not the constant Way.” The Way that can be put into words is not that enduring Way. He also says: “Reach the utmost emptiness; hold firmly to stillness.” What movement do these words make? They refuse to congeal what is highest into a frame that can be put into words. Confucius’s four refusals, his “I wish to say nothing,” and his “I transmit but do not compose” make the same movement: do not erect a rigid frame.

The two traditions speak differently and come from different places. I am not proposing that they are one school. But in this one movement, refusing to congeal what is highest and refusing to force things into a frame, the two men stand in the same place. One uses *wu* 無, the word for absence or negation; the other uses *wu* 毋, the word of prohibition. **“Nonaction” does not mean doing nothing, and “no insistence, no obstinacy” does not mean having no views of one’s own. Both say the same thing: do not erect a frame in advance and then force living things into it.**

The pair of labels “action versus nonaction” has therefore been attached far too carelessly. Confucius spent his life doing things, but without setting up rigid frames as he did them. Laozi speaks against arbitrary, meddlesome action, but what he describes is precisely acting without a rigid frame. The real division is not between doing and not doing. It is whether something already hardened in advance stands in the way while you act.

The student who recorded this sentence described his teacher’s highest cultivation as four refusals. Later generations built him temples, fashioned his statues, compiled chronologies of his life, and bestowed titles upon him. Each was another positive frame. While he was alive, he had refused them, one by one.

When you judge a person or a situation, is the frame already in place, or are you looking at what is before you?
