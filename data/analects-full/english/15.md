# Why Did He Smile at Zilu?

Four students speak of their ambitions, and one describes the destination.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

The longest passage in the *Analects* is a casual conversation. Four students sit with Confucius, who asks each to speak of his ambitions. Finally he says, “I am with Dian”: I agree with Zeng Dian. Later readers turn this into a position. Deep down, Confucius values Zeng Dian's freedom from worldly concerns, not governing states and bringing peace to the world. Confucianism looks busy, but what it really longs for is bathing in the Yi in late spring and returning home singing. Song-dynasty scholars especially love this reading. “The spirit of Zeng Dian” becomes a phrase.

First see what he actually does.

> Zilu, Zeng Xi, Ran You, and Gongxi Hua were sitting with him. The Master said, “Though I am a little older than you, do not let that constrain you. You often say, ‘No one understands me!’ If someone did understand you, what would you do?”

Four students sit beside him. I am a few years older, he says; do not make anything of it. You complain that no one understands you. If someone understood and employed you, what would you do?

He opens by withdrawing his status. Do not treat me as the teacher. Speak freely.

> Zilu answered impetuously, “A state of a thousand chariots, squeezed between great states, invaded by armies and afflicted by famine: if I, You, governed it, within three years I could make its people courageous and aware of what is right.” The Master smiled at him.

Zilu, whose personal name is You, jumps in: give me a medium-sized state hemmed in by great powers, with war outside and famine within. In three years I can give its people courage and a sense of right direction. Confucius smiles.

> “Qiu, what about you?” He answered, “A territory sixty or seventy li square, or fifty or sixty: if I, Qiu, governed it, within three years I could provide enough for the people. As for ritual and music, I would await an exemplary person.”

Qiu is Ran You, also called Ran Qiu. He says: a small place, sixty or seventy li across, or fifty or sixty. Let me govern it and in three years I can make the people prosperous. For ritual and music, wait for someone better.

> “Chi, what about you?” He answered, “I do not say I can do it, but I would like to learn. In ancestral-temple affairs or meetings of the lords, wearing ceremonial robes and cap, I would like to serve as a minor master of ceremonies.”

Chi is Gongxi Hua. He says: I am not claiming ability; I want to learn. At ancestral sacrifices and meetings of the lords, I would like to put on ceremonial dress and serve as a junior officiant.

> “Dian, what about you?” His playing on the se grew sparse. With a final ringing note, he set the instrument aside and rose. “My choice differs from those three.” The Master said, “What harm is there in that? Each is simply stating his ambition.” He said, “In late spring, once the spring clothes are ready, with five or six capped adults and six or seven boys, I would bathe in the Yi, take the breeze at the rain-dance terrace, and return singing.” The Master sighed deeply and said, “I am with Dian!”

Dian is Zeng Dian, also called Zeng Xi. The notes of his *se*, an ancient Chinese stringed instrument, grow sparse. One ringing sound, then he puts it down and stands. Mine is different from theirs. What does that matter? Each of you is speaking of his own ambition. Zeng Dian says: late spring, spring clothes ready, five or six adults and six or seven children, washing in the Yi River, taking the breeze on the rain-dance terrace, singing on the way home. Confucius gives a long sigh. I agree with Zeng Dian.

So far, the reading that Confucius prizes carefree detachment seems plausible. But there is a second half. The other three leave. Zeng Dian stays and asks what the teacher thinks of them. Each was simply stating his ambition, he says. Zeng Dian asks: why did you smile at Zilu?

> He said, “A state is governed through ritual, but his words showed no deference. That is why I smiled at him.”

Government requires ritual. His manner of speaking lacked modesty, so I smiled.

**He smiles not at Zilu's ambition but at his jumping in first.** He does not say governing a state is bad. Zilu answers impetuously, ahead of everyone else, and says, “I can do it in three years.” The teacher smiles at that lack of deference. It fits his usual diagnosis of Zilu: the drive of two men; he needs pulling back.

Zeng Dian asks again: isn't Ran Qiu also talking about governing a state? Sixty or seventy li is a state, says the teacher. And Gongxi Hua? What are ancestral temples and meetings of lords if not affairs of state? If Gongxi Hua is a minor officiant, who could be a major one? He affirms both Ran You's and Gongxi Hua's ambitions. Three students want to govern, and he tells none of them they are wrong.

Then what does “I am with Dian” praise? Look at what Zeng Dian says. Zilu, Ran You, and Gongxi Hua all say what they want to do: give people courage, give them prosperity, become an officiant. Zeng Dian describes not an undertaking but a picture: spring, adults and children, bathing, taking the breeze, singing home. **The other three describe the means of government. Zeng Dian describes what it looks like once government has succeeded:** everyone has a place, without conflict or fear. Confucius affirms the picture because it is precisely where government should lead. He has said something similar himself: “Give the old ease, give friends grounds for trust, and cherish the young.”

“I am with Dian,” then, does not rank the four, still less declare that governing is inferior to going for a bath. Each states his ambition; he rejects none. He smiles at one person's manner and praises the end another has described. The first three discuss the road; the fourth says where the road leads. He is reminding the first three not to forget the destination.

One further point. This passage is more beautifully written than any other in the book: the strings, spring clothes, the Yi, the rain-dance terrace, singing home. Almost a little prose poem. Such writing is rare in the *Analects*, and deserves a watchful reading. The passage's core is not its elegance, but two judgments: why he smiles, and what he agrees with. Who supplied the verbal beauty is uncertain. The judgments are his.

Later generations elevate “the spirit of Zeng Dian” into a spiritual attainment, taking the picture out of its setting of government and hanging it up alone. Removed, it becomes a taste. Restored to its setting, it is an end.

The things you are doing: toward what picture do they lead?
