# “Man-Eating Ritual Morality”? He Condemned That Ritual First

Without humaneness, what can ritual do with a person?

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

“Man-eating ritual morality.” As soon as the phrase appears, the charge is laid at Confucius's door. Ritual is his signboard: “Overcome oneself and return to ritual”; “Without learning ritual, there is no way to stand.” The pages of the *Analects* are full of it. Later ritual morality used ritual to oppress people and kill them. Trace it upward, and he is the source.

The book of the *Analects* devoted to ritual opens with two cases. The Ji family uses a dance troupe on the scale reserved for the Son of Heaven, the king, in its private courtyard. The three great families use the royal hymn when the offerings are removed at their private sacrifices. Then comes a verdict:

> The Master said, “If a person is not humane, what can ritual do with him? If a person is not humane, what can music do with him?”

If someone has no *ren*, humaneness, what can ritual do with him? What can music do with him?

The sentence places ritual wholly beneath humaneness. Ritual is not self-sufficient. It is the outward shape of humaneness: the act of treating people as people taking form in gestures, ceremonies, and a sense of proportion. Without that humaneness within, the ritual outside is empty. However perfectly the great families' dancers move together, it is machinery turning in a void. **Ritual can do nothing with them, and they can do nothing with ritual.**

What is the root of ritual? Someone asked him directly.

> Lin Fang asked about the root of ritual. The Master said, “A great question! In ritual, rather than extravagance, prefer frugality. In mourning, rather than polished arrangements, prefer grief.”

Lin Fang asked what was fundamental to ritual. What a good question, he answered. Better for ritual to be simple than lavish; better for a funeral to carry sincere grief than to have every arrangement perfectly managed.

He praised the question for reaching the root, then did not give the root. He gave two directions: if you must lean to one side, lean toward simplicity, lean toward grief. Extravagance and polished arrangements are outside; simplicity and grief are closer to the inside. He refused to say what the root of ritual was. He only pointed inward.

Sacrifice is the most weighty element of ritual. “Sacrifice as though they were present; sacrifice to the spirits as though the spirits were present.” Sacrifice to your ancestors as if they were there; to the spirits, as if they were there. “If I do not take part in the sacrifice, it is as though I have not sacrificed.” A sacrifice in which I do not personally participate is no sacrifice at all. Being present cannot be outsourced. You can have someone perform a ceremony for you; you cannot have them feel reverence for you. The great families' ritual consisted precisely of display that could all be outsourced.

His sharpest sentence is this:

> The Master said, “Ritual, ritual! Do we mean jade and silk? Music, music! Do we mean bells and drums?”

Ritual, ritual: are we really talking about jade and silk? Music, music: are we really talking about bells and drums?

Two rhetorical questions lift ritual and music clear of the level of objects. Jade, silk, bells, and drums are the instruments of ritual and music, not ritual and music themselves. He did not go on to say, “Ritual is reverence.” He only asked, “Can it be merely jade and silk?” leaving you to think for yourself what ritual is. **Any account that makes ritual a matter of display, objects, ceremonies, or rules is broken by this sentence in a single stroke.**

What, then, is oppressive “ritual morality”? Ritual without humaneness. It strips humaneness out of his ritual, leaves only the external set of “musts,” and uses it to make demands upon everyone. This is exactly what he condemned at the beginning of the book on ritual: if a person is not humane, what can ritual do with him? Ritual morality devours people; what devours them is ritual without humaneness. He was the first to condemn it, earlier than anyone else.

How was the ritual-moralist portrait of Confucius assembled? Look at a sentence near the end of the same book: “Holding high position without generosity, performing ritual without reverence, attending a funeral without grief: what is there for me to observe?” In a high place, no magnanimity; in ritual, no reverence; in mourning, no sorrow. What is left to look at? Each sentence has its case: generosity toward whom, reverence at which rite, sorrow at which funeral? But the cases were not recorded. Only three verdicts remain. Anguish without a stated case reads as one-sided preaching. **That image of the stern-faced Master lecturing on ritual was probably assembled sentence by sentence in just this way: strip the cases from words that had cases, and everything left sounds like a reprimand.**

How did he himself perform ritual? As an earlier essay described, when ritual caps changed from hemp to silk, he followed the change because it saved expense without diminishing reverence. When ministers changed from bowing to the ruler below the hall to bowing above, he refused to follow, because that economized on reverence itself. In the same passage he follows one change and resists another. He has only one criterion: is the reverence inside still there? In his hands, ritual had to be judged afresh every time. Not once was it a matter of simply following the rulebook.

Thus “Overcome oneself and return to ritual” is not self-repression. It is drawing oneself back into the form of treating people as people. “Without learning ritual, there is no way to stand” does not mean learning rules. It means gaining a way to express reverence. Ritual morality stripped the inside from both sentences, left only the outside, and used that outside to press people down. For the thing doing the oppressing, he has a sentence waiting: if a person is not humane, what can ritual do with him?

Those “rules” you have encountered: are there still people inside them?
