# He Was Scolding Yuan Rang, Not Old People

Remove a name, and words between two friends become a verdict on everyone.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

“To be old and not die is to be a pest.” The sentence has become a familiar saying used to insult old people: they occupy positions and burden those who come after. Those who use it feel justified, because it is “The Master said.” Those insulted have no answer either, because the sage said it.

First find the whole passage.

> Yuan Rang waited with his legs sprawled. The Master said, “As a child you were neither modest nor respectful toward your elders; as an adult you had nothing worth recounting; old and still not dead, you are a pest.” He tapped Yuan Rang's shin with his staff.

Yuan Rang was sitting with his legs spread, waiting for him. Confucius said: as a child you lacked humility and respect for your elder brothers; when grown, you did not do one thing worth speaking of; now you are old and still have not died. What a harmful creature you are. As he spoke, he tapped Yuan Rang's lower leg with his walking stick.

There is a name, an action, a reason, a scene.

Who was Yuan Rang? An old friend Confucius had known since childhood. The *Book of Rites* records an incident: when Yuan Rang's mother died, Confucius helped him prepare the coffin, yet Yuan Rang tapped on it and began to sing. The students asked: do you still associate with a man like this? Confucius answered: kin remain kin; old friends remain old friends. This was Yuan Rang: careless of ritual, never much serious business to his credit, still the same in old age. On this occasion he “waited sprawled,” sitting askew with his legs spread as he waited for Confucius, an unmannerly posture. Confucius came in, saw him, said those three sentences, and tapped his leg with his staff.

Look at the three sentences. “As a child you were neither modest nor respectful toward your elders” refers to Yuan Rang's childhood. “As an adult you had nothing worth recounting” refers to Yuan Rang's life. “Old and still not dead” refers to the Yuan Rang before him, still sitting without manners. The three sentences are one person's biography, a single line from childhood to old age. The final word, “pest,” is harsh, but it lands on one person, a friend of many decades.

Look also at the action. “He tapped his shin with his staff.” Not a blow, but a tap. Two old men: one sits with his legs sprawled; the other touches his shinbone with a stick and says, “How come you still haven't died?” What kind of scene is this? Harsh words between old friends; the frustrated disappointment of “So this is how you will spend your whole life”; words possible only between people who know one another. He would not say this to a stranger. Nor would he tap a stranger's leg.

Now see how the sentence became a proverb. Remove “Yuan Rang.” Remove “waited with his legs sprawled.” Remove “As a child you were neither modest nor respectful toward your elders; as an adult you had nothing worth recounting.” Remove “He tapped his shin with his staff.” Seven Chinese characters remain: “To be old and not die is to be a pest.” Harsh words addressed to one particular, named person, someone with whom the speaker had decades of friendship, become a verdict on all old people. No word needs changing. Only deletion.

**While a sentence carries a name, it is one person speaking to another. Once the name goes, it speaks to everyone.** This is the same technique as with “The people may be made to follow.” No forgery, only stripping away. Strip the recipient, the scene, the reason, and living words become a dead rule, an evil one at that.

This is not an important chapter in the *Analects*. It is only a small scene. Yet it is the cleanest specimen of the technique. The original records everything: who, what sitting posture, what reason, what action. Readers need only not look.

Those who made the record preserved Yuan Rang's name, and preserved that tap of the staff. With those two things, the sentence is one old man speaking to another. Without them, it is the sage insulting every old person in the world. People who use it to insult the elderly two thousand years later delete precisely what the recorders took care to preserve.

That harsh saying you have used: to whom was it originally spoken?
