# The Famous Saying Cut in Half

“Excel in study, then take office”: what happened to its other half?

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

“Excel in study, then take office.” Study well and become an official. Behind these five Chinese characters stand thirteen hundred years of civil-service examinations, the ideal of “studying to become an official,” and generation after generation who treated learning as a key to the door. The saying comes from the *Analects*. Many people think Confucius said it.

First, two things must be made clear. Confucius did not say it; Zixia did. And it is only half a sentence.

> Zixia said, “When serving in office leaves you strength to spare, study; when study leaves you strength to spare, serve in office.”

If you have energy left over from official duties, go and learn. If you have energy left over from learning, go and serve.

The two clauses form a pair. Here *you* does not mean “excellent.” It means “having capacity to spare.” The opening book of the *Analects* contains the same idea: “If, after putting these things into practice, you have strength to spare, use it to study the written arts.” If you have energy left after doing your job, do not forget to learn. If learning leaves you energy, you can try putting it to work. Together, the clauses say one thing: learning and public service should not displace each other; each should support the other. Officials must not let their learning go to seed. Students must not be able to do nothing but study.

Later readers performed two operations. First they changed *you* from “having capacity to spare” to “being excellent.” Then they cut away the first half, “When serving in office leaves you strength to spare, study,” and kept only the second. Not a character was changed. The other half was simply left unsaid. **With both clauses, learning and service run in two directions. Keep only one, and learning becomes a one-way road to office.** “Study in order to become an official” grew out of that half-sentence.

The discarded half is actually the less welcome one. It addresses officials: if your official duties leave you energy, go and learn. In the age of the examinations, no one wanted to quote it, because it sent officials back to their desks. The retained half was far more useful. It gave every student an idea: study well, and there will be an official position for you.

Why did the sentence leave an opening for this cut? Because it says only how learning and office fit together, not why to learn or why to serve. Whenever Confucius speaks of service, he attaches conditions: “Serve the ruler according to the Way; if that is impossible, stop”; “Attend respectfully to the work before thinking of its pay.” Whenever he speaks of learning, he gives it a direction: “The learners of old learned for themselves.” Learning is for becoming oneself, not for putting on a show for others. Zixia's two clauses carry none of these conditions. They say only that the two activities support one another. Without a criterion, later readers can bend them into the shape they want.

But his teacher had already described what the sentence would become once cut. “The learners of old learned for themselves; the learners of today learn for others.” People in antiquity learned to realize themselves. People today learn so that others will see them. Taken on its own, “Excel in study, then take office” is precisely the slogan version of “learning for others”: learning to be noticed, selected, appointed. What the teacher lamented was pushed to its furthest extreme by half a sentence from his student. That was not the student's fault. It was the fault of those who made the cut.

In fact, Zixia himself said what learning was for, in the very same book: “The many craftsmen stay in their workshops to accomplish their work; the exemplary person learns to reach the Way.” Craftsmen remain in their workshops to make things. The exemplary person learns in order to arrive at the Way. The end he gives learning is the Way, not office. Read this alongside “When study leaves you strength to spare, serve in office,” and public service is merely one thing someone may do when learning leaves capacity to spare. It was never learning's final destination. Those who cut away half a sentence also forgot the words spoken nearby by the same man.

To be fair, there is nothing wrong with Zixia's sentence itself. Neither learning nor public service should displace the other. The point still holds today: people who work should not stop learning, and people who study should not be able to do nothing else. The problem lies entirely with the readers. Needing an authority for “study in order to become an official,” they cut half a sentence out of an injunction not to neglect either activity.

This essay concerns a simple, common technique: change not a word; simply omit the other half. Recognizing it requires only one habit. When you encounter a famous saying, first ask: was there another half before it?

That saying you have believed for so many years: what is its other half?
