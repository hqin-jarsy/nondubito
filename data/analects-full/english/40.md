# Two Passages of Praise: What Makes Them Different?

Lofty words, and reasons for calling someone lofty.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

Two consecutive passages in Tai Bo praise ancient sage-kings.

> The Master said, “How lofty were Shun and Yu! They possessed all under Heaven, yet did not make it their own!”

Lofty indeed: Shun and Yu possessed the realm, but did not treat it as their personal possession.

> The Master said, “Great indeed was Yao as a ruler! How lofty! Heaven alone is great, and Yao alone modeled himself upon it. How vast! The people could find no words to describe him. How lofty his achievements! How radiant his cultural institutions!”

Great was Yao as a ruler. Lofty: Heaven alone is greatest, and Yao alone follows Heaven. Vast: the people cannot put him into words. Lofty his accomplishments; radiant his cultural forms and institutions.

Both passages praise. Both say “How lofty!” Yet they are not the same kind of thing.

Look at the first. Fourteen Chinese characters, with the weight in the final three: 不与焉, “did not make it their own.” Shun and Yu possessed the realm but did not treat it as theirs. They did not cling to the position or make the position their own end. These three characters explain why they were lofty: not because they possessed the realm, but because, having it, they did not appropriate it. This is a structural judgment: the people who govern well are precisely those who do not treat office as an asset. It connects with what he says elsewhere: Tai Bo yields the realm three times; “Was it not Shun who governed through non-action?”; “Governing by virtue is like the North Star: it remains in its place while all the stars gather around it.” The praise has a concrete core.

Look at the second. Nearly three times as many characters, with five expressions of praise in succession: great, lofty, vast, lofty, radiant. Yet not a single sentence says why Yao was great. “Heaven alone is great, and Yao alone modeled himself upon it”: this raises Yao to Heaven's side, but supplies no reason. “The people could find no words to describe him”: they cannot say wherein his goodness lies. That is not saying, rather than saying. “His achievements,” “his cultural institutions”: accomplishments and institutions, but which ones? We are not told. **One passage praises five times, without once saying why.**

That is the difference: **whether a reason is given.** The first has few words and gives one; the second has many and does not. Whether praise is substantial or empty depends not on length, but on whether it has a core that lets us see “what makes him lofty.”

Why does this matter? Because the person in this book always gives reasons when he praises someone. Of Yan Hui, he says, “A basket of food, a gourd of water, living in a poor lane: others could not bear the hardship, yet Hui never let it change his joy.” Something happened. Of Guan Zhong, he says, “He brought the feudal lords together nine times, without war chariots”; “The people still benefit from him today.” Something happened. When he praises Tai Bo's “highest virtue,” there is “three times yielding the realm” to support it. Something happened. He does not praise emptily. A person's judgment is most exposed in the act of praise: what they praise tells us what they value. The three characters 不与焉 express a judgment. “Great, lofty, vast” expresses an emotion.

Moreover, the second passage does precisely what he refuses for himself. Zigong says, “Heaven has surely endowed him as a sage,” and he immediately dismantles it: I was poor when young and learned to do menial work; this is no endowment from Heaven. Someone who refuses to be raised to Heaven's side is unlikely to turn around and raise Yao there.

Let us be clear about what this essay does not oppose. The second passage establishes nothing contrary to him. It establishes no hierarchy, authority, or orthodox succession, and excludes nobody. It is merely empty. Empty praise does not warrant opposition; it simply cannot help us know him. We cannot see his judgment in it, only a string of adjectives.

This method, asking whether praise supplies a reason, is the handiest little measure in this book. It does not decide authenticity; it distinguishes substance from emptiness. The same three characters, 巍巍乎, “How lofty!”, appear in both passages. In one, they are followed by “did not make it their own”; in the other, by still more “How lofty!” There lies the difference.

**What we oppose:** We do not oppose the second passage. It is only praise and establishes nothing contrary to him.

**Why it is not an anchor:** Five rounds of praise, not one reason. His praise always points to something done and a reason; the three characters 不与焉 are precisely that reason.

**What we do not infer:** We do not call the second passage fabricated or determine who said it. We say only that praise in which no judgment is visible cannot help us know the person who gives a reason every time he praises.

The last time you praised someone, did you say why?
