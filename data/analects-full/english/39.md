# A Book of the Analects That Reads Like a Slide Deck

Ji Shi: sound teaching, but in what form?

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

The Analects has twenty books. When you reach the sixteenth, Ji Shi, the tone changes.

In the preceding fifteen, most passages run like this: someone asks, he answers; or a sentence, a counterquestion, an image, then a stop. In Ji Shi, a whole string of things like these suddenly appears:

> Confucius said, “There are three beneficial kinds of friends and three harmful kinds. Befriend the upright, the trustworthy, and the widely informed: these are beneficial. Befriend the ingratiating, the falsely accommodating, and the glib: these are harmful.”

> Confucius said, “There are three beneficial kinds of pleasures and three harmful kinds. Delight in keeping ritual and music in proper measure, in speaking of others' goodness, and in having many worthy friends: these are beneficial. Delight in arrogant enjoyment, in idle roaming, and in feasting: these are harmful.”

Three kinds of helpful friends, three kinds of harmful friends; three helpful pleasures, three harmful pleasures. Further on, three errors in attending a gentleman, three things a gentleman guards against, three objects of a gentleman's awe, nine things a gentleman considers, and a historical rule beginning “When the Way prevails under Heaven, ritual, music, and punitive expeditions issue from the Son of Heaven.” A whole book reads like the outline of a set of lecture notes: three, three, three, three, nine, page after page.

First, the content. Considered one by one, these items are all sound. The upright, trustworthy, and widely informed make good friends; the flattering, falsely amiable, and glib make bad ones. Enjoying speaking well of others and having many worthy friends are good pleasures. “Delight in speaking of others' goodness” sounds especially like him. He has said, “Help bring out the good in others, not the bad.” The meaning of this book does not contradict him.

Now, the form. The three friends and three pleasures use exactly the same sentence pattern: “There are three beneficial X and three harmful X. X the upright, X the trustworthy, X the widely informed: beneficial. X the ingratiating, X the falsely accommodating, X the glib: harmful.” One mold, a new topic, then another casting. He does not speak this way. He speaks according to the person and situation, saying different things about the same matter to two different people. Someone whose speech addresses a person every time would not use one mold to produce two consecutive chapters. **Two consecutive chapters cast in one mold bear the mark of arrangement, not the mark of speech.** Anyone who acquires a useful format can produce a whole string from it.

The book also opens with a long passage. The Ji family plans to attack Zhuanyu; Ran You and Zilu come to see him; he refutes them point by point. First he reproaches Ran You, then invokes the charge given by the former kings, then rebuts the excuse “The Ji family wants to attack, not us,” then cites Zhou Ren, then compares the situation to a tiger escaping its cage. Finally: “I fear that the trouble for Jisun lies not in Zhuanyu but within his own walls.” This is the book's most fully developed argumentative essay. Some sentences in it are superb: “Do not worry about scarcity, but about unequal distribution; do not worry about poverty, but about insecurity”; “If distant people do not submit, cultivate civil virtue to draw them here.” Yet the whole piece does not look like him. He never refutes dissenters in so dense a sequence. When Weisheng Mu mocks him, he answers only, “I detest rigidity.” When recluses mock him, he states only his own choice. This passage looks more like several originally independent sayings, on equitable distribution and security, attracting distant people, and the Ji family's troubles, assembled into one scene, with objections and rebuttals supplied between them to make a continuous essay. The assembly is not bad, and the meaning is right. But the scene fixes the scope of every sentence within its frame.

Another detail: throughout this book the formula is “Confucius said,” not “The Master said.” Elsewhere in the Analects, students recording their teacher write “The Master said”: “Master” means our teacher. “Confucius said” stands one remove away, telling outsiders, “Confucius once said this.” Using that designation throughout a book places its speaker a little farther back.

Put these together: repeated molds, an unusual frequency of identical sentence patterns, an extended composed argument, and a designation one remove away. They point toward a judgment: **this book looks more like an organized set of lecture notes than a succession of encounters.** Lecture notes can be good, and these are. They understand his meaning reasonably well. But notes are not an encounter. No particular person is asking a question; no prescription is addressed to anyone; the items stand in tidy rows, leaving little room for you to think for yourself.

We therefore do not use this book to know him, and we do not oppose it. Those are two separate points. We do not use it to know him because we cannot see to whom he speaks within it. We do not oppose it because it establishes nothing contrary to him: the three friends and three pleasures establish neither hierarchy nor authority; they simply arrange his meaning into a table. **The meaning does not contradict him; he simply would not say it that way.** This is the clearest example of a book that is “not an anchor, but need not be opposed.”

As for how this book came into being and who composed it, we do not judge. What we can see is its form; what we can say is “it does not sound like him.” Whether something sounds like a person is a matter of knowing that person. Whether it is authentic is another matter, requiring evidence we do not have.

**What we oppose:** We do not oppose this book's contents. Choosing friends, choosing pleasures, equitable distribution and security, civil virtue: all are sound.

**Why it is not an anchor:** Several chapters cast in the same mold, unusually frequent repeated patterns, an extended argument, and a designation one remove away: organized lecture notes, rather than encounters.

**What we do not infer:** We do not call this book fabricated or determine who composed it. We say only that this is not how the person we understand speaks, so we do not use it to know him.

Can lecture notes that arrange a person's words into tables let you know that person?
