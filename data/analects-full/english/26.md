# The “Conservative” Who Hated Rigidity

He followed what was good, not what was old merely because it was old.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

Confucius was conservative. This hardly seems to need an argument. He wanted to restore Zhou ritual; he “trusted and loved the ancient”; he “transmitted but did not compose”; whenever he opened his mouth, it was “The ancients...” Since China's May Fourth movement of 1919, he has been treated as the supreme representative of resistance to change. Even his defenders usually concede the point, merely defending the benefits of conservatism.

Someone once asked him to his face why he kept traveling everywhere. His answer came down to two Chinese characters.

> Weisheng Mu said to Confucius, “Qiu, why are you so restlessly busy? Are you perhaps trying to win people over with clever talk?” Confucius said, “I would not presume to practice clever talk. I detest rigidity.”

Qiu, Weisheng Mu said, why do you keep bustling about from place to place? Is it to exercise your glib tongue? I would not dare do that, Confucius replied. I hate being fixed and unyielding.

Weisheng Mu was probably a recluse. He addressed Confucius by his personal name, Qiu, and his tone was blunt. Confucius did not argue with him. He stated only his own principle: I detest rigidity. What is rigidity? Obstinacy, being stuck, refusing to change, clinging to something and never letting go. That includes knowing the world is in disorder yet holding fast to “withdraw” and refusing to move. **The man taken as the supreme representative of conservatism said with his own mouth that what he hated most was rigidity.**

This is not an isolated piece of evidence. When the students recorded his cultivation of himself, one of the four refusals was “no obstinacy.” He said: “The young are to be held in awe. How do we know that those who come after will not equal those of today?” The young deserve our respect; how can you know that later people will be inferior to those alive now? A conservative would not say that those who come later might be better. He assessed several ancient recluses: some would never lower their aspirations; some lived in seclusion and spoke freely. Finally, of himself he said: “I differ from these: I have no unconditional must and no unconditional must not.” I am different from all of them. There is no way I must always be, and no way I must never be. A conservative always holds an “unconditionally” in his hand.

What, then, was his love of antiquity? One of his sayings is quoted more often than any other to prove his conservatism: “The Zhou looked to the two preceding dynasties. How rich its culture! I follow the Zhou.” I follow the Zhou. But look at the reason he gives. Zhou institutions were made with reference to the Xia and Shang dynasties, and were culturally rich. He followed Zhou because it was the best achievement of the three dynasties, the finished product of additions and subtractions, not because Zhou was old. He followed what was best at the time, not what was oldest. Later readers quietly replaced “follow” with “restore.” Between following the best available solution and reinstating a vanished order lies an entire difference of method.

“The learners of old learned for themselves; the learners of today learn for others.” This too is often quoted: look, antiquity good, the present bad. But look at what he is saying. People of old learned to realize themselves; people today learn to be seen by others. He praises antiquity because those learners had a good direction: for themselves. He gives a reason. He is not saying “Old is good.” He is saying “Learning that way is good; the ancients happened to learn that way.” **Every time he follows antiquity, there is a reason, a condition, a particular object. He follows because it is good, not because it is ancient.**

How was the conservative image made? Strip away the reasons. Remove “How rich its culture!” from “I follow the Zhou,” and “follow the Zhou” remains. Remove “for themselves” from “The learners of old learned for themselves,” and “the learners of old” remains. Every sentence is left with an “ancient,” while the reason disappears, the condition disappears, the object disappears. A man who insists on antiquity regardless of everything else is assembled. Yet his own words keep the counterevidence before us: detesting rigidity; no obstinacy; awe before the young; no unconditional must or must not; ritual has additions and subtractions. The poison and the antidote are in the same book.

Return once more to Weisheng Mu's question. Asked why he traveled everywhere, he answered, “I detest rigidity.” His traveling was precisely a refusal to remain fixed. He would not hold fast to a pose of withdrawal, to a judgment that could never change once spoken, or to a rule that said, “Whatever the ancients did, I will do.” **A man who traveled everywhere seeking change was made by later generations into the guardian deity of forbidding change.**

That person you have decided is “conservative”: what did he himself say he hated?
