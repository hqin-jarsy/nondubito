# His Three Encounters with Recluses

The Weizi book, and a choice that never hardens into a prescribed form.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

Confucians enter the world; Daoists withdraw from it. Confucius is the one who enters, the recluses are those who withdraw: one man trying to save the world, a group trying to escape it. That is the textbook division. Very neat.

The “Weizi” book records three encounters between him and recluses. Let us see how he actually treats them.

The first. Jieyu, the madman of Chu, walks past his carriage singing: “Phoenix, phoenix! How has your virtue declined? The past cannot be remonstrated with; the future can still be pursued. Give it up, give it up! Those who govern today are in peril!” Phoenix, what has happened to your virtue? What is past cannot be recalled, but there is still time to change what comes next. Enough, enough! Public office today is dangerous. This is an invitation to withdraw.

His response: he gets down from the carriage, wanting to speak to the man. Jieyu hurries away. They do not get to talk.

The second. Changju and Jieni are plowing a field. Confucius sends Zilu to ask where the ford is. On hearing that the man in the carriage is Kong Qiu, Confucius's personal name, Changju says: he should know where the ford is. When Jieni learns that Zilu is a student of Kong Qiu, he replies: “The whole world is swept along in the same flood. With whom will you change it? Rather than follow a man who avoids particular people, would you not do better to follow those who avoid the world?” The flood is everywhere. Who can change it? Better follow someone who withdraws from the whole age than someone who merely avoids bad people. He then goes on plowing.

Jieni is not being unreasonable. He sees clearly: the world is a flood, and one man cannot change it. Kong Qiu's road means leaving one bad ruler to seek another, still circling within human affairs. He calls that “avoiding particular people.” Better to leave altogether: “avoid the world.” This is a clear-sighted person's more radical proposal.

Zilu returns and reports. Confucius is “disconsolate,” visibly at a loss. He understands what has been said and is partly persuaded by it. Then he says:

> “We cannot flock with birds and beasts. If I do not associate with these people, with whom shall I associate? If the world possessed the Way, I would not be taking part in changing it.”

A person cannot live among birds and beasts. If I do not live with these people, with whom can I live? If the world had the Way, there would be no need for me to change it.

These three sentences are his fullest answer to the recluses. The first: retreating into forests and mountains means keeping company with birds and beasts. I cannot do that. The second matters most: **If I do not live with these people, with whom can I live?** My place is among people, among these people. The third: precisely because the Way is absent, I must be here. If it were present, I would not have come.

The third. Zilu falls behind and meets an old man carrying a weeding implement on his staff. Zilu asks whether he has seen his teacher. The old man says: limbs that never labor, unable to tell the five grains apart; who is your teacher? Then he plants his staff in the ground and weeds. Zilu stands respectfully. The old man keeps him overnight, kills a chicken, prepares a meal, and brings his two sons out to meet him. Next day, Zilu catches up with the teacher and reports what happened. The teacher says: he is a recluse. He sends Zilu back to see him again. By the time Zilu arrives, the man is gone.

Zilu speaks to the old man's family. Refusing to serve is not right. The courtesies between old and young cannot be abandoned: you brought your two sons to meet me, which shows that you observe them. How, then, can the obligations between ruler and minister be abandoned? In trying to preserve one's own purity, one disorders the fundamental relations. “An exemplary person takes office to carry out what is right. That the Way will not prevail, we already know.” He serves to fulfill his obligation. We have long known that the Way cannot be put into effect.

Zilu's speech is the student's version of the attitude captured in the gatekeeper's description of his teacher: “knows it cannot be done and does it anyway.” One enters service not to succeed but to do what one ought. We already know it will not work. And his method is exact: he questions the old man through the old man's own conduct. If you maintain the courtesies between old and young at home, on what grounds do you discard the obligations between ruler and minister in the wider world?

Three encounters, three common features.

Each time, he wants to talk. He gets down from his carriage; he answers after being shaken; he sends Zilu back for another meeting. Each time, the recluses leave: Jieyu walks away, Jieni keeps plowing, the old man is no longer there. One man keeps turning toward people; the others keep withdrawing. He never rebukes them, never says they are wrong. He states only his own position: I belong among people.

Each time, he takes in what he hears. “Disconsolate” is the truest word here. It is not that he is unmoved. He knows the weight of that other road. He is partly persuaded, then still chooses his own. This is not failing to hear; it is choosing after having heard.

For the third common feature, look at a remark near the end of this book. He assesses a series of ancient recluses: some never lowered their resolve or dishonored themselves; some lowered their resolve and dishonored themselves but remained proper in speech and action; some lived in seclusion and spoke freely. Then he says:

> “I am different from these: nothing is invariably permissible, and nothing invariably impermissible.”

I differ from them all. There is no one thing I must always do, and no one thing I must always refuse to do.

This raises the three encounters to another level. Earlier, two roads lay side by side: he chose engagement; the recluses chose withdrawal. Here he says that engagement is not mandatory, and neither is withdrawal. Boyi and Shuqi made never lowering their resolve a necessity. Those in seclusion made seclusion a necessity. He makes no form mandatory. He keeps only one thing: judgment in the present situation. This is not having no position. The village conformist has no position and approves of everyone. His own position has one measure, rightness, and therefore presupposes no particular form. **Not a fixed form, but judgment.**

Another saying at the beginning of the same book carries this to its conclusion. At the end of the Yin dynasty, under the tyrannical King Zhou, three people did three contrary things. Weizi left. Jizi feigned madness and became a slave. Bigan remonstrated and was killed. Confucius said, “Yin had three humane men.” The one who left, the one who concealed himself, the one who died: he granted ren to all three. He judged not the form of the deed, but whether each person, in his own position, had done what he could. It is the same as his attitude toward the recluses: your choice differs from mine; that does not make you wrong.

So he himself stands on neither side of the dividing line between engagement and withdrawal. He stands among people because he judges that this is where he ought to be. In another time, for another person, the judgment may differ. The recluses fixed their judgments into a form. That is the sole difference between him and them.

Have you ever fixed a judgment into a form?
