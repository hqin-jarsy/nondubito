# Four People Ask About Filial Care

One subject, four prescriptions, and none says simply to obey.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

What does filial devotion mean to many people? Obedience. Do whatever your parents say. Do not resist, do not answer back. All those stories in the *Twenty-Four Filial Exemplars*. The *Analects* does indeed contain two Chinese characters quoted for two thousand years: “Do not transgress.” Meng Yizi asks about filial care. The Master says: do not transgress. There you are: do not disobey your parents.

Not so fast. More follows those two characters.

> Meng Yizi asked about filial care. The Master said, “Do not transgress.” When Fan Chi was driving, the Master told him, “Mengsun asked me about filial care, and I answered, ‘Do not transgress.’” Fan Chi said, “What does that mean?” The Master said, “While they live, serve them according to ritual. When they die, bury them according to ritual and offer sacrifices to them according to ritual.”

Meng Yizi asks, and Confucius says not to transgress. Later, while Fan Chi drives his carriage, he raises it himself: Mengsun asked me about filial care, and I said, “Do not transgress.” Mengsun here refers to Meng Yizi. Fan Chi asks what it means. While your parents live, serve them according to ritual; after death, bury and honor them according to ritual.

What must not be transgressed is ritual, not parental wishes. That is a major difference. Who is Meng Yizi? Head of the Mengsun family, one of Lu's Three Huan houses. What are those houses known for? Usurpation: dancing with the Son of Heaven's formations, clearing ceremonial vessels to the Son of Heaven's music, sacrificing to a mountain reserved for the Son of Heaven. Addressed to the head of such a family, “Do not transgress” has its cutting edge in “do not overstep.” Before dying, his father entrusted him to Confucius to learn ritual. He would immediately understand and not go astray. Fan Chi lacks that background, so Confucius waits for his question before expanding: according to ritual.

Notice that Confucius brings this up with Fan Chi on his own initiative. Why? Probably because he knows what those bare words might become once circulated. Events proved the concern justified: they really did become “do not disobey your parents.” Without Fan Chi's question, we would have only the two characters today.

Immediately after this passage, three more people ask about filial care.

> Meng Wubo asked about filial care. The Master said, “Let your parents have only your illness to worry about.”

Meng Wubo is Meng Yizi's son, the same family's next generation. The answer changes. Let your parents worry only about illness. Except for sickness, something beyond your control, nothing else, your behavior, associates, spending, should burden them with worry. For a young aristocrat whose posthumous title contains “martial,” a single sentence closes off every possible source of trouble. “Only” is the cutting edge.

> Ziyou asked about filial care. The Master said, “What is called filial care nowadays means being able to provide nourishment. But dogs and horses receive nourishment too. Without respect, what is the difference?”

Ziyou is his student. The answer changes again. Nowadays being filial means feeding one's parents. But dogs and horses are fed as well. Without respect, what distinguishes it? A stinging remark. Providing nourishment is lowered to the minimum, something humans and animals can both do. Respect becomes the dividing line between human and animal.

> Zixia asked about filial care. The Master said, “The expression on your face is difficult. When there is work, the young do it; when there is wine and food, the elders eat. Do you really take that to be filial care?”

Zixia is also a student, the diligent, capable sort found throughout the book. The answer changes again: the difficult thing is your expression toward your parents. Work needs doing, you do it; food and wine arrive, the elders eat first. You actually call that filial care? Everything Zixia does best, working and attending to needs, gets pushed outside the boundary. One thing remains: your face. What shows there cannot be faked.

Four people, one term, four prescriptions. The two aristocratic household heads receive prohibitions and limits: do not overstep, do not worry your parents. Their problem is excess, usurpation and indulgence. The two students receive directions inward: respect, facial expression. Their problem is turning filial care into accomplishment, a series of tasks to finish.

**Filial care is not a rule. It is a way of seeing the person.** Not one answer says, “Obey your parents.” Elsewhere he does say, “In serving your parents, remonstrate gently.” When parents are wrong, gently urge them to change. Urging them to change is disobedience.

The compilers place the four passages side by side rather than squeeze them into a “definition of filial care.” Four different answers stand together, waiting for you to notice that the answer changes with the person. This is the book's first cross-section of the method. Later, the opposite answers to “On hearing it, should I act?” and the three answers about humaneness develop the same practice.

How did “Do not transgress” become “Do not disobey your parents”? Simple: delete Fan Chi's question. Not one character needs changing.

The teachings about filial devotion you heard growing up: to whom were they addressed?
