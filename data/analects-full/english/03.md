# He Said He Knew Nothing

“Utterly empty”: knowledge without a stock of ready-made answers.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

The Confucius you imagine probably knows everything and insists on explaining everything. He opens his mouth and out comes another complete account of humaneness, rightness, and morality. Students need only take notes. “Old Master Kong is going to lecture us on humaneness again” sounds to many people like the beginning of a joke.

His own description runs in exactly the opposite direction:

> The Master said, “Do I possess knowledge? I do not. An uncultivated man asks me something, and there is utter emptiness. I probe the two ends of his question and pursue them to the limit.”

Do I have knowledge? No. An uneducated fellow comes with a question, and my mind is empty, with nothing ready-made in it. I work at both ends of his question, all the way through.

First, be clear: this is not modesty. Modesty means having goods and saying you have none. He is describing a practice: **keeping no stock.** When someone asks, he does not draw from inventory because he keeps none. He probes the other person's question, letting the answer emerge from the questioner. Someone in Athens said something similar: the only thing I know is that I know nothing. The two approaches arose independently; there is no need to invent a family connection. That saying, too, is a method, not modesty.

Look next at “an uncultivated man.” The visitor has no education. Surely he is the person most in need of a ready-made, simple, take-it-and-go answer. Confucius refuses. With this man too, he probes; with this man too, he lets the other person reach the end himself. This is more radical than “I do not know”: even for those at the very bottom, he will not lower teaching into indoctrination.

That is his own account. Is there corroboration? Yes, and it takes the form of negative records: records of what he does not say.

“The Master seldom spoke of profit, fate, and humaneness.” He seldom discussed profit, seldom fate, seldom *ren*, or humaneness. **Humaneness, the most important word of his life, was something he seldom raised on his own.** Read through the *Analects* and something strange appears: nearly every discussion of humaneness answers a question. Yan Yuan asks, Zhonggong asks, Sima Niu asks, Fan Chi asks, Zigong asks. Only then does he answer, differently each time. There is no public lecture course on humaneness. Why say least about what matters most? Because it matters most, it must least of all be made into a system of statements. Once systematized, it becomes something to memorize, and memorized humaneness is not humaneness.

Zigong later testifies: “The Master's cultural accomplishments can be heard about; his statements on human nature and the Way of Heaven cannot be heard.” We can hear about what is manifest in our teacher. We cannot hear him expound human nature and the Way of Heaven. It is not that Zigong missed class. There was no such class. Elsewhere the disciples record: “The Master did not speak of strange phenomena, feats of force, disorder, or spirits.” They even recorded his unspoken subjects. Zilu asks about death. “You do not yet understand life. How could you understand death?” You have not worked out living; why ask about dying? It is not that he has never thought about death. He will not lay out a system for you in territory he does not know.

His definition of knowing is not knowing a great deal, either. “You! Shall I teach you what knowing is? To know that you know what you know, and to know that you do not know what you do not know: that is knowing.” “You!” names Zilu, the most self-confident person in the book. Let me teach you what knowing means: say you know when you know; say you do not when you do not. He redefines knowledge. It is not the quantity stored in your head, but whether you tell the truth about your limits.

He lives this way, too. Entering the Grand Ancestral Temple, he asks about everything. Someone sneers: who says this son of a man from Zou understands ritual? He goes into the temple and asks about every single thing. “This is ritual,” he replies. Asking is ritual. The person who understands ritual is not the one who walks in issuing instructions, but the one who walks in asking questions. On the spot, he reverses the expert's posture. Another person's praise is half compliment, half jab: “Great is Confucius! Widely learned, yet distinguished in no particular field.” Marvelous: studies everything, excels at nothing. He tells his students: what specialty should I choose, then? Charioteering? Archery? I will choose charioteering. The last-ranking of the Six Arts. He does not argue. He makes a joke.

He also honestly reports a failure: “I once went all day without food and all night without sleep, thinking. It was no use. Better to learn.” I tried thinking through an entire day without eating and an entire night without sleeping. Useless. Better to study. A supposedly all-knowing man leaves a record of pulling an all-nighter and getting nowhere.

Put these together, and the prejudice collapses. He is not someone who stores many answers. He is someone who stores none. **He remains empty not because there is nothing in him, but because, if he were full, there would be no room for the person facing him.** Emptiness is the condition of his tireless learning: fullness would make him refuse to learn again. It is also the condition of his teaching: fullness would leave him only pouring things out. He keeps himself empty so that, whenever someone comes, the question remains that person's question, and the answer grows from that person as well. I call this practice cultivation: not putting things inside you, but probing your own question until you reach the end yourself.

Finally, consider the recorders. A student intent on making a statue of the teacher would write, “The Master knew everything and answered every question.” They wrote “I do not know,” “utterly empty,” “seldom spoke,” “did not speak,” and “cannot be heard.” Item by item, they recorded what the teacher did not know and did not say. **The statue of an all-knowing sage was made by later generations. The self-introduction he submitted was simply: utterly empty.**

Next time someone brings you a question, will you reach first for your inventory, or first explore their question?
