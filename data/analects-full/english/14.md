# Twice He Defended a “Traitor”

The case of Guan Zhong, and the measure of humaneness.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

Loyalty to the ruler. Giving one's life to accomplish humaneness. A gentleman dies for the person who recognizes him. When a lord dies, a minister follows him in death: integrity. When a lord dies and a minister survives, then serves the man who killed his lord: treachery. Many people imagine Confucius as the founding master of this set of values.

In two passages of the *Analects*, two students bring those values to him with the same question about the same person: Guan Zhong.

The story is this. Prince Jiu and Prince Xiaobai contend for the throne of Qi. Guan Zhong and Shao Hu serve Prince Jiu. Xiaobai wins, becomes Duke Huan of Qi, and Prince Jiu is killed. Shao Hu dies for his lord. Guan Zhong does not. Instead, he becomes Duke Huan's chief minister. By the standards above, a textbook traitor.

> Zilu said, “Duke Huan killed Prince Jiu. Shao Hu died for him; Guan Zhong did not.” He asked, “Was that not a failure of humaneness?” The Master said, “Duke Huan brought the lords together repeatedly without war chariots. That was Guan Zhong's achievement. Such was his humaneness! Such was his humaneness!”

Zilu says: Duke Huan killed Prince Jiu. Shao Hu died for him. Guan Zhong did not. Surely that is inhumane? Confucius answers: Duke Huan repeatedly assembled the feudal lords without using war chariots. Guan Zhong made that possible. Such was his humaneness! Such was his humaneness!

Twice. Zilu asks whether refusing to die for one's lord is inhumane. The answer is that uniting the lords without fighting is humaneness. The teacher changes the measuring stick. Not how loyal he was to one lord, but how many people he spared from war.

> Zigong said, “Was Guan Zhong not lacking in humaneness? Duke Huan killed Prince Jiu; Guan Zhong could not die for him and even became Huan's minister.” The Master said, “Guan Zhong served Duke Huan, made him leader of the lords, and set the world right. To this day, the people receive his benefits. Without Guan Zhong, we would probably wear our hair loose and fasten our robes on the left. How could he be like ordinary men and women in their petty fidelity, hanging themselves in a ditch with no one knowing?”

Zigong asks: surely Guan Zhong was not humane? When Duke Huan killed Prince Jiu, Guan Zhong failed to die and then helped Huan. Confucius answers: he helped Duke Huan become hegemon over the lords and restore order to the world. Ordinary people still benefit today. Without him, we would probably wear loose hair and left-fastened robes, an image here of losing their own cultural customs. Should he instead cling to petty promises like ordinary men and women and hang himself in a ditch, unknown to anyone?

Here the point is fully stated. “To this day, the people receive his benefits”: whether people benefit determines humaneness, not whether someone follows a lord into death. “Petty fidelity”: that is his name for keeping faith with one person to the point of death. Dying for a lord is small fidelity. Saving the world from war is humaneness. **Humaneness is not whom you die for. It is how many people you enable to flourish.**

This does not mean he makes no demands concerning trustworthiness. Elsewhere he says, “Insisting that every word be kept and every action carried through: what a rigidly narrow person!” Every word must be honored; every action must reach its end. Such unbending smallness. The “must” is the problem. Whether to keep faith depends on whether it ought to be kept; whether to carry through depends on whether one ought to. Someone who never asks whether it is right, but simply persists to the end in every case, is a small person. Not an evil person, but someone unable to judge. This is the “must” Shao Hu upholds.

To be clear, Confucius is not a Guan Zhong fan. Elsewhere he criticizes Guan Zhong's limited capacity, ignorance of ritual, and extravagant household arrangements that overstep his rank. The same person receives blame by the measure of ritual and praise by the measure of humaneness. Two measures, applied separately, not canceling each other out. That matters. He does not whitewash everything because Guan Zhong achieves something great. Nor does he deny that Guan Zhong saved the people because he violated ritual.

Return to the prejudice. The code of loyalty to the ruler and death for one's lord receives his verdict: “ordinary people's petty fidelity.” Elevating loyalty above everything and making death for a lord the measure of integrity belongs to other people and other ages. **His own surviving verdict is: the man who did not die and thereby enabled the people of the world to flourish, such was his humaneness.**

The record also leaves a painful detail. Of the two students asking this question, one later dies and the other saves a state. Zilu dies in Wei's civil strife, fastening his severed cap-strings before death. With his life, he upholds the kind of fidelity he has asked about. Zigong, when Lu is in danger, travels as an envoy among the states and saves Lu through diplomacy. In Guan Zhong's manner, he makes life possible for a country's people. From the same lesson, the two hear two different paths. That is a story for another essay.

That act of dying for someone which you admire: whom did it enable to flourish?
