# Two Thousand Years of “Rule by Virtue”: Has the Sign Been Hung Backward?

A new reading of “they will have shame and ge,” with its risks left visible.

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

If asked for an ancestral maxim supporting Confucian “rule by virtue,” most people would turn to this:

> The Master said, “Guide them through government, align them through punishments, and the people will evade punishment but have no shame. Guide them through virtue, align them through ritual, and they will have shame and arrive at what is right.”

That is the customary reading: use administrative orders to guide and punishments to regulate, and people will merely avoid punishment without acquiring a sense of shame. Use morality to guide and ritual to regulate, and they will acquire shame and return to the right path. The first half is bad, the second good. Punishment is inferior to moral education; rule by law is inferior to rule by virtue. Textbooks teach it this way, and so have two thousand years of interpretation. The sentence is the signboard of “moral education.”

Our reading is the reverse. Let us say this at the outset: this is a new reading, different from the received interpretation of two thousand years. We will lay out every ground for it, and mark its risks as well.

First ask the question we ask in every essay: to whom is this sentence addressed?

It appears in the book *On Government*. That book consists from beginning to end of prescriptions for those who govern: “To govern through virtue is like the North Star”; “Raise the upright above the crooked”; “The exemplary person is not a vessel”; “Act before you speak.” Not one of its twenty-four chapters addresses the common people. This sentence likewise has a ruler as its recipient. In “guide them” and “align them,” the subject is the ruler and the object is the people. It is the ruler who guides and regulates.

Now consider the crucial word: shame.

Elsewhere in the *Analects*, the same word appears when Zigong asks what makes a person a *shi*, a person of worth. Confucius says, “In conducting yourself, have a sense of shame.” You act, and you know for yourself what is shameful. This is a demand a person of worth makes of themselves; the sense of shame grows from within. But in 2.3, shame is something the ruler is to “guide” into the people. He uses virtue and ritual to guide them, and they then “have shame.” This shame is installed by someone else.

Here is the dividing line. **Shame that grows from within is judgment: a voice inside says, “This must not be done,” and the voice is your own. Shame installed by someone else is a shackle: the voice belongs to someone else, impersonating yours.** The same word is positive in 13.20 because it is something one gives oneself; in 2.3 it is negative because it is handed down from above. The dictionary entry for “shame” is neither praise nor blame. Its value depends on who gives it to whom.

With this key, the whole sentence turns around.

“Guide them through government, align them through punishments, and the people will be free and without imposed shame.” The ruler attends diligently to government and gives the people a clear boundary. Punishment specifies only what may not be done, not how one must live. Judgment within that boundary remains with the people themselves. Within it, they grow on their own. *Mian*, conventionally understood as escaping punishment, here means being allowed freedom. “Without shame” does not mean lacking a sense of shame. It means not having someone else’s shame installed in oneself. The mind remains one’s own; one’s judgment is still alive. This is what 8.9 calls “letting them follow”: allowing them to walk for themselves.

“Guide them through virtue, align them through ritual, and they will have shame and be blocked.” The ruler neglects the work of government, merely preaching virtue and telling the people how they ought to be. He turns “virtue” and “ritual” into a body of content the people must accept, pouring it into them item by item. Filled with “oughts,” the people are so ashamed they dare not move. This is “having shame.” *Ge* means blocked: not returning to the right path, but being stopped where one stands, unable to move. This is what 8.9 calls “making them know”: installing your knowledge in them.

**The ruler Confucius condemns is not the one who uses punishment. It is the one who can only preach lofty principles and pour “oughts” into the people’s hearts.**

This reading agrees with what he says to rulers elsewhere. “When he himself is upright, things proceed without commands”: make yourself upright, and you need issue no orders. “If you desire goodness, the people will be good”: if you want goodness, the people will become good, so why kill? “To govern through virtue is like the North Star, remaining in its place while the other stars turn toward it”: virtue means that you stand there yourself, and the stars circle of their own accord. **Every prescription he gives a ruler lands on the ruler himself: what you are like, not how you are to teach the people.** “Governing through virtue” in 2.1 is positive because virtue is the ruler’s own position. “Guiding them through virtue” in 2.3 is negative because virtue has become content he installs in the people. The two uses do not contradict one another. One concerns what is in oneself; the other, what one puts into someone else.

Now let us mark the risks. This reading changes the interpretation of three words. Shame is usually read positively; we read it negatively here. *Ge* is usually read as “arriving at what is right”; we read it as “obstruction.” Virtue is usually read positively; here we read it negatively. None of these is an arbitrary substitution of meaning. Each follows from the line of reasoning that the addressee is a ruler and the shame is shame he gives to others. Once the first follows, the next two fall into place. But these are inferences, not conclusive proof. The received reading has two thousand years behind it. Ours has no variant manuscript in its support, only consistency across the book as a whole. Excavated evidence could overturn it. If it does, we will change this judgment.

One more consideration gives us the confidence to read it this way. Whenever the highest teacher tells rulers not to shape the people, the words are especially liable to be read backward. Because they take negative forms, “without shame,” “without knowing,” “let them follow,” stripping away the premise “addressed to rulers” turns the negatives into techniques for keeping people ignorant. Chapter 3 of the *Laozi* provides material evidence. The silk manuscript reads, “Make those who know dare not fail to act, and that is all”: do not allow knowledgeable people to withdraw. The received text reads, “Make the clever dare not act”: silence intelligent people. A difference of one character reverses the direction, and the earlier text does not support the later reading. Chapters 2.3 and 8.9 very probably suffered the same fate. Two injunctions to rulers, “Do not indoctrinate,” were read as two instructions for “governing the people.” On the Laozi side, the silk manuscript has already overturned the verdict; the *Analects* is still waiting.

Why devote a whole essay to this sentence? Because it is the most important point in the reading against keeping the people ignorant. Under the customary interpretation, Confucius is the founding master of moral indoctrination: the state tells the people what should shame them and what they ought to do. This is precisely the blueprint of the later machinery of ritual morality. When the May Fourth movement of 1919 denounced “man-eating ritual morality,” this is what it denounced. In our reading, he is warning rulers against precisely that: give the people boundaries, not shame; govern yourself well, rather than filling other people’s minds. **The signboard of two thousand years of “rule by virtue” has very probably been hung backward. What he opposed is precisely what is written on that sign.**

Still, the same sentence applies: the position of judgment belongs to you. Both the received reading and ours are here, along with their reasons. Decide for yourself which better resembles the man in the preceding eighteen essays.

Of the shame you carry, which parts grew within you, and which were installed by someone else?
