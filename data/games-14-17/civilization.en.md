# One Settler

A game of *Civilization* begins on a mostly black map. One Settler stands on the visible ground, often beside a Warrior. You choose a tile, found a city, and end the turn.

Hundreds of turns later, the small clearing may have become half the known world.

Every expansion happened because you clicked.

## Population as an assignable number

A city’s people appear as a population count. Citizens can be assigned to tiles that produce food, production, gold, science, or culture. Reassigning them changes the output immediately. If too little food is produced, growth stops and population may fall.

At this scale, “people” are movable units of capacity.

The abstraction is not uniquely callous. A game spanning millennia, continents, and civilizations cannot display the interior life of every person. Statistics, economic reports, maps, and survey histories perform the same compression.

*Civilization* adds one crucial feature: it makes the compressed scale operable. The player does not merely read that a population has been redirected toward industry. The player moves the citizen icon.

## The immortal viewpoint

The player governs through a named historical leader. Qin Shi Huang, Gandhi, Caesar, Elizabeth, or Montezuma may remain on the screen from 4000 BCE to the near future.

Cities rise and fall; citizens are born and disappear; religions, governments, and languages change. The leader does not age. Strategy requires this impossible continuity. Someone must remember why a city was founded beside a river three thousand years ago.

The player also occupies the only viewpoint capable of seeing the whole system. Maps, yields, diplomacy, research, troop locations, and city reports appear in one interface. The citizens represented by the numbers cannot know that they live inside it.

This asymmetry is not established by censorship within the story. It is established by the camera.

## A world with no refusal

Individual citizens do not have projects the player must discover. They do not resign from assigned tiles, explain that a different life matters more, or reject the purpose the interface gives them. Even catastrophe is absorbed into totals: a city disappears, a unit icon vanishes, a population count falls.

One might conclude that a reading centered on people as ends has no foothold here. The game’s scale appears to contain no persons in the relevant sense.

But a total conversion of people into output creates a design problem. If every new city and citizen adds production without producing any counterforce, expansion becomes a simple arithmetic exercise. External enemies and scarce terrain can resist the player, but once defeated or acquired, those obstacles disappear.

A strategy game lasting six thousand years needs resistance that grows with success.

The most plausible source is internal: large empires become harder to coordinate, long wars exhaust populations, distant cities resist central rule, and more citizens produce more demands.

So the designers create numbers for happiness, unrest, amenities, war weariness, corruption, and loyalty.

The people do not become full subjects. Yet the system cannot function until something inside it represents the fact that they may refuse to cooperate.

# Happiness Is What Pushes Back

In *Civilization V*, founding another city and adding population consumes global happiness. When unhappiness exceeds happiness, growth, production, and military effectiveness suffer. Earlier games used corruption to make distant cities lose part of their output. *Civilization VI* localizes much of the question through Amenities and adds Loyalty. Across the series, war weariness grows even when the war is being won.

The mechanics differ. They occupy one structural family.

## Resistance that grows with success

External difficulty comes from stronger rivals, poor terrain, or limited resources. Internal resistance grows from your own achievements. More cities increase demands. A larger population requires more amenities. A wider empire becomes harder to hold. A longer war makes citizens less willing to bear it.

These numbers are “yours” in the possessive language of empire, but they push back against you.

That counterpressure gives strategy its shape. Expansion now has an opportunity cost. A player must decide not only whether another city can be founded, but whether the empire can absorb what the city asks from the rest of the system.

The games do not give each citizen a voice. They create a parameter for the aggregate fact that citizens cannot be treated as output without remainder.

This is not necessarily a moral commitment by the designers. It is a mechanical requirement. Remove every internal cost and the decision space collapses into addition.

## The language of wanting

Loyalty in *Civilization VI: Rise and Fall* is defined as a city’s devotion to its civilization and reflects how much its citizens want to remain part of it.

“Want” is unusual language in an interface dominated by yield, strength, cost, and progress. The number is still something the player will optimize, but its name asks the player to imagine a relation rather than a resource.

The name also has consequences. When Loyalty falls below 75, growth and yields receive a 25 percent penalty. Below 25, yield penalties deepen and population growth falls by 75 percent. At zero, the city revolts and becomes a Free City capable of defending itself.

A city can leave without an enemy army conquering it.

For a period, it belongs to no major civilization. On a map organized almost entirely through colored ownership, a territory appears whose defining feature is that it has withdrawn from the previous color without yet accepting another.

The independence may not last. But the game has created a mechanical location for refusal, and that refusal has teeth.

The next question is what players do once refusal becomes a visible number.

# Then Players Learned to Use It

Search for advice on flipping a city through Loyalty in *Civilization VI*, and the result is a practical list. Settle populous cities nearby. Assign governors and policies. Use a spy to foment unrest. If you founded a religion, alter the religious pressure affecting the city. Surround it with your own centers of population.

The number that represents whether citizens want to remain under one civilization becomes a tool for acquiring the city on behalf of another.

## A visible will becomes a target

The transformation does not require malicious players. Strategy games teach players to read every visible number, identify its sources, and move it toward an advantage.

If a quantity can be displayed, it can be measured. If it can be measured, it can be optimized. Loyalty therefore becomes not only an acknowledgment of resistance but a dial that someone else can turn.

This is not an exploit. Population pressure, espionage, religion, governors, and policy cards belong to the designed rules. The game explicitly gives players means to change what its citizens “want.”

## The logic of the Free City

At zero Loyalty, a city revolts and becomes free. If the Free City later loses its own Loyalty, it seeks to join the civilization that exerted the greatest pressure during its independence.

Mechanically, the rule is coherent. The strongest surrounding pressure likely comes from the civilization whose cities, people, and culture are closest.

Read in ordinary language, it produces a harsher sentence: a failed independence ends by joining whoever pressed most strongly on the independent city.

The system has room for refusal, but its model of what follows is still organized by competing external claims.

## From global happiness to local amenities

The series has repeatedly redesigned its internal checks on expansion. *Civilization V* used a powerful global happiness system that made every new city a burden on the whole empire. *Civilization VI* moved much of the pressure into local Amenities and let population pressure among nearby cities stabilize dense clusters.

The restriction did not disappear. Its shape changed, and many common strategies in the later game again reward broad, closely packed expansion.

This history matters because the number representing internal resistance is also a balance parameter. Designers adjust it to produce multiple viable strategies and an enjoyable pace. Players learn to budget it as a cost and weaponize it against rivals.

None of these actions is corrupt in the context of game design. Together they reveal a limit: once “their willingness” becomes a calculable variable inside someone else’s system, the variable remains vulnerable to being minimized, manipulated, and retuned.

The game first creates a place for refusal because strategy requires it. It then demonstrates how quickly a system can convert refusal into another instrument of control.

# The People Who Have Names

A notification announces the arrival of a Great Person: Confucius, Sun Tzu, Newton, Curie, Michelangelo.

Ordinary citizens remain anonymous population. Historical individuals receive names—and abilities the player can spend.

## Biography compressed into output

In *Civilization V*, Great Scientists can accelerate research or create Academies; Great Engineers can speed construction or build Manufactories; Great Artists create Great Works or begin Golden Ages; Great Generals support armies or establish Citadels. *Civilization VI* gives named Great People more individual abilities, sometimes with several charges. When the ability is exhausted, the unit leaves play.

This is not a cheap joke at the game’s expense. At a scale of six thousand years, biographies are routinely compressed into contributions: a discovery, a book, a battle, a building. Survey histories must perform some version of that reduction.

The game makes the reduction explicit. A named life becomes a cost in Great Person points and an effect activated from the interface.

The honesty is useful. Clicking the button reveals what a phrase such as “Newton advanced science” normally conceals: an entire life has been replaced by the output history remembers.

## Leaders have faces

Leaders occupy a different layer. AI-controlled rivals have voices, expressions, agendas, grievances, and memories of diplomatic injury. They may dislike your expansion, denounce you, praise a shared policy, or declare war.

The leader controlled by the player, however, becomes strangely silent. The same historical figure who has a vivid temperament as an opponent turns into a viewpoint when selected by you.

Personality is something the system displays in others. At the player’s position sits a pair of eyes.

## One shared tree

Civilizations have unique units, buildings, abilities, and ways of accelerating progress. Yet they advance through the same main technology tree: pottery, mining, writing, engineering, gunpowder, industrialization, electricity, flight, computers, rocketry.

The tree is not only an interface. It is a historical claim. It represents development as a common route with prerequisites, arrows, and a recognizable destination. Cultural difference appears largely as variation in speed, emphasis, or special tools along that route.

Any game that turns millennia into a learnable system needs strong abstraction. The danger begins when the abstraction becomes the player’s intuitive picture of history: one road, one sequence, some civilizations ahead and others behind.

A diagram with arrows is difficult to remember as only one possible diagram.

## One more turn

The series is famous for keeping players awake through “one more turn.” Its strongest hook is not merely that each turn is enjoyable. Something is always almost complete: a technology in three turns, a city’s growth in two, an army’s arrival in four, a wonder in six.

Completing one countdown begins several others.

A system that converts worlds into progress bars also converts the player into someone watching progress bars. The player is not outside the model. Attention becomes one more resource organized by “almost.”

Across these essays, *Civilization* has compressed citizens into output, preserved internal refusal as Happiness or Loyalty, allowed players to manipulate that refusal, and repeatedly rebalanced how much resistance the system should contain.

The final irony is not that the people refuse too little. It is that the size of the number representing their refusal is chosen by designers and judged by players according to whether it produces a good game.

The citizens do not decide how much room their “no” receives. The person on this side of the screen does.
