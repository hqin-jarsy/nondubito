Strickland’s letter is brief. He has decided to live apart from his wife and is going to Paris. He does not supply the explanation everyone is waiting for. There is no accusation, no account of a marriage gone wrong, no reason that the people left behind can discuss and answer.

He is forty, a stockbroker, married for seventeen years, with a respectable wife, two children, and a comfortable home. In their circle he has been dull but dependable, so dull that nobody remembers an interesting remark of his. Then he leaves.

## Everyone supplies a reason

His wife thinks there must be another woman. Relatives and acquaintances make the event intelligible in their own ways: a middle-aged man has gone mad; there must have been signs all along. Looking backward, people discover what they now need to have noticed.

The narrator is sent to Paris to persuade him to return. The assumed assignment is straightforward: find the woman, explain the cost, and bring him to his senses. But the hotel is shabby, the room small and dirty, and Strickland unwashed and badly dressed. There is no woman to find.

He says he wants to paint. The narrator brings up his wife’s distress, the children, and what everyone will say. Strickland does not dispute the injury or try to make himself look better. Asked how his wife will live, he says, in effect, that after seventeen years of his support she can try supporting herself.

This is not an ingenious defence. A defence is addressed to someone whose judgment matters. He is not trying to persuade the person before him.

## A reason that does not do the expected work

But you are forty, the narrator says. That is precisely why he must begin now. What makes him think he has talent? His answer is not evidence of talent. He says he has to paint.

He did want to paint as a boy; his father made him enter business. He has also spent the past year attending evening classes, while his wife thought he was playing cards. His departure is not the birth of an interest with no history.

The distinction is elsewhere. Childhood interest and a year of practice do not provide a guarantee that this will work. He does not offer a portfolio of achievements, a teacher’s endorsement, or a promise that his later greatness will justify leaving. The ordinary account of a dream would turn these into credentials: I have always loved it, I am exceptionally gifted, one day you will understand. He refuses that application procedure.

Nor does painting appear as a reliably happy activity. He struggles with his work, destroys much of it, and is angry at what he cannot yet do. Whatever pleasure it may bring, pleasure is not the answer he gives to the demand for justification.

What remains is necessity. His comparison is with someone in the water: whether he swims well or badly is not the first question; he must swim or drown. The comparison does not prove that abandoning others is right. It tells us what kind of answer he is making.

## What is this necessity?

It cannot be reduced to liking. We can compare preferences, prefer one thing today and another tomorrow. His insistence is not presented as the winner of such a comparison.

It is not simply ambition. Ambition normally names a destination: a position, an audience, an award. Strickland does not organize his life around the recognition that might arrive. Nor is it the knowledge that he is a genius. Other people give him that name; the demand to paint does not wait for their verdict.

And it is not an accusation against his wife. Asked what she has done wrong, he has no charge to make. His earlier frustration and his father’s prohibition matter to his history, but they do not turn the Paris conversation into a case against the people he is leaving.

There is something he cannot set aside, and the explanation others ask for does not reach it. They want an external warrant: what result, what reward, whose approval will make this reasonable? He cannot supply one that determines the weight the act has for him.

That is the sense in which it is his own. It does not mean that nothing in his childhood contributed to it, or that a marriage and a job are literally handed down without choice. It means that the authority of this demand cannot be borrowed from the social roles by which everyone has known him. The respectable stockbroker has an intelligible place. This necessity does not need that place before it can exist.

## Not the usual story of following a dream

A familiar story moves from an oppressed passion through sacrifice to success. The final achievement vindicates the departure. Someone waits at the finish: family, former classmates, the person who once laughed. The story’s meaning comes together when they finally see.

Strickland’s history contains some of those materials, including an early wish frustrated by his father. But the novel refuses to let public vindication become the governing structure. His leaving is not staged as a noble appeal for our sympathy. He does not promise to return with proof.

At the end, in Tahiti, he has painted the walls of his home. Later he is blind. Before dying he makes Ata promise to burn the house after his burial. She keeps that promise.

For now, remember the destruction. If the essential meaning of the work were that a future audience must see it, the burning would be incomprehensible. His own completion is not the same thing as a successful exhibition.

## A sentence he never said

A popular Chinese slogan about the novel imagines sixpences scattered everywhere and one man looking up at the moon. It is not a sentence in Maugham’s book. It is a later way of packaging the book, and an exceptionally convenient one.

It translates the story into worldly security against ideals, petty necessity against distant poetry. It gives us a romantic figure to admire. But Strickland is coarse, rude, and cruel. He does not graciously suffer for an ideal. He offers neither a defence of himself nor sustained attention to other people. The narrator does not learn to like him.

Read with that slogan already in place, and you may find a prettier book, but it will not quite be this one.

## What the departure leaves behind

So far the question has been what he finds. The novel’s weight also lies in what remains when he walks out. The home does not cease to exist. His wife starts a typing business and brings up the children. Maugham does not need a thunderous condemnation to give that effort its weight.

And London is not the end of the damage. The first person to recognize his genius is Dirk Stroeve, a mediocre painter, warm-hearted and ridiculous, mocked by almost everybody and especially by Strickland. When Strickland is dangerously ill in Paris, Stroeve brings him home despite his wife’s vehement opposition.

He prepares to strike. Then he stops.

## A man everyone laughs at

Dirk Stroeve is Dutch, short, plump, and absurd-looking. His pictures are sweet, attractive, saleable, and bad. He is painfully easy to turn into a joke. People laugh at his talk, his accidents, his excitement. He often joins the laughter.

His wife Blanche is younger, quiet, and beautiful. He loves her without reserve, with an extravagance that embarrasses those who watch. It is easy to believe that this explains everything about him: a foolish, soft man, useful chiefly as a contrast to someone stronger.

But his eye for other people’s painting is extraordinary. Mediocrity in his own work has not made him blind to greatness elsewhere. He recognizes Strickland before the people around him know what to make of those strange pictures. He is excited by them, speaks for them, and persists when nobody agrees.

Strickland repays him with contempt. He insults him, mocks his pictures, takes his help, and has little regard for the person giving it. Stroeve continues to help. His recognition is not a bargain for respect.

## He brings him home

When Strickland becomes seriously ill, Stroeve wants to nurse him in his apartment. Blanche refuses with unusual force. She is frightened of the man. She says disaster will follow if he enters. She cries and threatens to leave.

Stroeve pleads: the man may die; they cannot let him die; he is a great painter. At last she yields. Strickland is brought into the studio, and Blanche nurses him attentively, feeding him and watching over him. Once he recovers, he stays to paint. The studio’s owner makes room for him.

Then Blanche chooses Strickland. Stroeve begs her to remain, humiliates himself, tries to discover whether the other man even wants her. Strickland’s response is devastatingly indifferent. She may come if she wants. He is not offering the future that her choice seems to require.

## He leaves them the apartment

Stroeve does not reclaim the place by force. He moves out. Blanche is accustomed to those rooms and her possessions; he cannot bear to think of her discomfort elsewhere. He leaves money too, fearing what poverty with Strickland might mean for her.

He goes to a small hotel and watches the windows of his former home from the street. The narrator protests, appeals to his dignity, tells him how intolerable his behaviour is. Stroeve has no persuasive account to offer. He loves her.

Strickland eventually leaves. Blanche takes oxalic acid and dies after days in hospital. Stroeve waits, but she will not see him. His care does not purchase even a last meeting.

## The tool in his hand

After the funeral, the familiar order of the apartment makes the absence worse. Her things are still in their places. In the studio he finds the canvas turned toward the wall. Once he sees the nude properly, grief, jealousy, and rage overwhelm him.

He searches for something with which to destroy it and seizes the scraper. At the moment of the blow he suddenly sees the painting, not merely what its making has done to his life. He is terrified by its greatness. His hand lets the weapon go.

Later, telling the narrator the story, he reenacts the movement with a dinner knife.

The painting survives. Strickland gives it to him. Stroeve sells his household things and takes his pictures home to Holland.

## Why this moment matters

For me this is the heart of the book, not because it proves Stroeve infinitely forgiving, but because two truths remain present at once.

One is the injury: the man he helped has devastated his marriage; Blanche is dead; his generosity and his own rooms have become part of what he cannot bear to remember. The other is that the painting is extraordinary.

The second truth does not cancel the first. Stroeve need not decide that art matters more than a human life. Nor does the scene require us to claim he can never forgive: later he even invites Strickland to Holland, an offer the other man declines. What the halted blow itself shows is narrower and more difficult. He sees something that his hatred does not determine.

The painting’s greatness is not a credit in Strickland’s moral account. It cannot be placed opposite Blanche’s death and subtracted from it. These are not entries in one ledger.

The narrator’s dislike has impeded his response to the work. Later critics can move the other way, turning cruelty into an entertaining sign of genius. To make wrongdoing evidence of greatness, or greatness a defence of wrongdoing, is to make the same conversion in opposite directions. Stroeve’s hand stops before that conversion is needed.

## Not merely a foil

A common reading treats him as the weak good man crushed by genius. I think it misses what he can do. He recognizes something real before others recognize it. He helps the person who despises him. After losing his wife, he worries about her comfort. After her death, he does not destroy the picture.

These acts bring him no dependable reward. Nobody has appointed him to perform them. Strickland does not learn gratitude; Blanche refuses to see him; the laughter does not stop. His actions cannot be explained by what they will earn.

Yet that is not a reason to make him a saint.

## His goodness also fails to listen

Blanche’s refusal was clear. She said she was afraid. Stroeve believed in the rightness of rescue, and in the greatness of the painter, so firmly that he pressed past her refusal. His distress became pressure on her to yield.

The good deed was accomplished over another person’s no. The opened door belongs to the story’s chain of consequences, although that does not make Stroeve the sole cause of Blanche’s death or remove anyone else’s agency. A charitable motive cannot erase the fact that she was not heard.

The point is therefore not that the best person wins. Stroeve can be foolish, weak, and disastrously mistaken. He can fail to recognize a limit in his own home and still, at another moment, recognize a truth that does not depend on him.

He is the person whose hand stops in front of that canvas. We do not need to turn his whole life into a certificate of virtue to see what that moment contains.

## The familiar defence

Strickland is a bastard, people say, but he is a genius, so it was worth it. There are more polished versions. Great art has a cost. Had he remained a London stockbroker, the world would have lost those paintings. Ordinary moral standards cannot apply to an exceptional artist.

The problem is not merely that such claims sound cruel. We should look at what the sentence is doing.

## An account he never submitted

It makes a balance sheet. On one side: the abandoned family, the ruined marriage, the death. On the other: the paintings. Then it announces that the gain exceeds the loss.

That calculation assumes he exchanged the former for the latter. But he did not describe his departure as an undertaking to compensate his family with art. Asked about his wife, he did not say that her suffering would be worthwhile. He behaved as though it were irrelevant to his decision.

Nor did he promise masterpieces. When challenged about his ability, he did not produce genius as a reason. Stroeve supplies that word, then critics, then readers. They provide a justification that was not his own.

We calculate on someone’s behalf and use the result to defend him, although he has not asked to be defended on those terms. Our defence may tell us more about our need to reconcile the story than about what drove him.

## No application for an exemption

The usual exemption for genius says: this person has done harm, but possesses something special that places him outside ordinary rules. Strickland does not appeal to that provision. He does not patiently explain that the misunderstood artist deserves a different standard.

He paints. He treats Stroeve with contempt. When confronted, he often refuses the moral conversation rather than arguing for a privileged place within it. He can even agree that his conduct is monstrous without changing course.

That refusal does not grant him immunity. It simply means that the licence later issued in his name is not the reason he offered for acting. We should not mistake indifference to judgment for a successful answer to it.

## The burned walls

In Tahiti, he lives with Ata and their children away from European society. He develops leprosy, and the household becomes increasingly isolated. Dr. Coutras visits. Years later, called back when Strickland is dying, the doctor arrives after his death.

Inside the house the walls are covered in painting. Coutras, no trained connoisseur, is shaken by what he sees: a world at once beautiful, bodily, mysterious, and frightening. He feels that someone has reached into things ordinarily concealed.

Strickland had been blind for almost a year. The next chapter says he sat in the rooms he had painted, facing the work with sightless eyes.

Before his death he made Ata promise to burn the house after the burial. Coutras tries to dissuade her. She keeps the promise. The work has had witnesses, including Ata and the doctor, but it will have no continuing public life.

## What the fire removes

The destruction is not simply one more picturesque detail in an artist’s legend. It breaks the familiar structure of an achievement preserved for a future audience. If the murals were intended as humanity’s eventual compensation, why remove the very thing that was supposed to pay the debt?

This does not mean all his paintings disappear, or that nobody ever gains anything from them. Works survive, and his reputation grows. The narrower point concerns the final work: its meaning for him does not require that the world inherit it.

On this reading, completion is enough. There is no commissioner to satisfy, no public acceptance to secure, no final recipient whose benefit explains the act. Doing the work and delivering a valuable product to posterity are not the same thing.

So the fire undermines the particular defence that presents him as sacrificing people to give us a masterpiece. He has not agreed to that contract, and here he withholds the supposed payment.

## This is not a better excuse

It would be easy to make another defence out of that refusal: how pure, how independent of fame, therefore how justified. That would merely repeat the conversion with a more flattering currency.

The picture cannot cancel Blanche’s death, not because it is insufficiently valuable, but because these are not exchangeable things. His necessity can be real, and the injury to his wife, to Blanche, and to Stroeve can also be real. Neither proves or disproves the other.

The fire does not absolve him. It makes one of our preferred ways of absolving him harder to sustain.

## What Maugham leaves unresolved

The novel provides no neat final judgment. Strickland’s illness can be read by a character as punishment, but the book does not thereby certify divine retribution. It gives us no repentance that resolves the injury, and no declaration that the paintings paid for it.

The narrator’s distaste matters, but it is not a court’s concluding verdict. He continues to describe things that do not fit his liking. Maugham lets the necessity and the damage remain visible together.

That can feel evasive only if we think a novel must finish by clearing the account. Here the refusal of closure is part of its force. The reader has to keep holding what a tidy answer would let us set down.

## The question that remains

Finding something entirely one’s own is one thing. Recognizing that other people contain something no less their own is another. There is no guaranteed passage from the first to the second.

The next essay stays with that separation. It will not offer a new total score for Strickland as a human being. Refusing that score does not make the harm weightless. It means we must say both things without using either to silence the other.

Her son Robert appears in khaki with a clerical collar: a clergyman serving in the war. After hearing about his father’s terrible end, he invokes the slow mills of God. The narrator suspects that the family takes the saying for Scripture. The irony matters: the novel does not present it as an actual biblical quotation.

## Everything has been made to fit

These pages are quiet, almost domestic, and unusually bitter. The man who refused to explain himself, seek approval, or preserve his final achievement is now accommodated by fame, prices, and a family legend. The scandal supplies a little flavour to greatness.

His wife can inhabit the reflected prestige. His son can locate punishment in the death. Both have found a story that allows the facts to settle. The dead man can no longer refuse their accounts.

Maugham also lets the narrator imagine Strickland’s other son, Ata’s boy, working on a schooner and dancing on deck beneath the Pacific sky. That contrast interrupts the London room’s certainty. A life need not be contained by the conclusion that this room has reached.

## Two different things

The first is finding something of one’s own whose weight does not wait for other people’s permission. One need not pass an examination, win approval, or present a convincing explanation before it can matter.

The second is recognizing that someone else may carry a necessity of the same kind. Their inability to explain it does not make it less real. Its existence is not conditional on our agreement.

There is no necessary bridge between the two. To assume that authenticity naturally produces respect is to give ourselves a reassuring story, not to describe a reliable fact. Strickland exposes the gap with exceptional force. His own demand is inescapable; other people’s demands scarcely register.

Blanche’s attachment is dismissed as foolishness. Stroeve’s discernment and generosity are treated with contempt. The fact that Strickland cannot accept or understand what moves them does not establish that nothing is there.

## Not a final grade for the man

It would be easy to say he reached the first stage but failed the second, and therefore was an incomplete person. But then we would be building another external scorecard. First society graded the respectable broker and husband. Then critics graded the genius and the villain. Finally his son graded the death as punishment.

Adding our own test would not escape that habit simply because we chose a better test. I do not want to turn the novel into the calculation of one man’s total human worth.

That is not a refusal to name harm. To decline a final grade is not to say that the abandoned wife, the dead woman, and the bereaved husband do not matter. Their injuries do not become less real because the person who caused them cannot be summarized in a verdict.

## Say both things to the end

Return to Stroeve and the scraper. The moment matters because neither truth is required to disappear. In the same way, we can say what Strickland found without romanticizing what he did.

He found a necessity whose force was not assigned by an audience. Childhood wishes and later practice help explain its history, but no certificate of talent or promise of fame confers its meaning. That remains true even when we dislike him.

His departure also leaves actual people carrying actual burdens. A woman in London has children to raise and a living to earn. Blanche dies after terrible suffering. The man who rescued him loses the marriage and home around which his life was built. The greatness of a painting subtracts nothing from that.

The two things do not cancel each other or serve as each other’s evidence. We are asked to hold them together without settling the difference. The discomfort is not an accidental obstacle to the novel’s effect. It is part of it.

## Why the second step is difficult

Recognizing an unexplained necessity in someone else can sound like a simple injunction to be kind. But kindness does not guarantee it. Stroeve is abundantly kind, yet presses past Blanche’s explicit refusal when he brings Strickland home.

Intelligence does not guarantee it either. The narrator is observant and lucid, often the most measured person in the room. That does not mean he fully sees either Strickland’s necessity or Blanche’s desire.

The demand is stranger: acknowledge something you do not understand, do not endorse, and may even find absurd, without insisting that it first pass through your authority. Understanding would give you a reason. Sympathy would give you an affinity. Here neither may be available.

Not everybody reaches that point. And reaching it once does not make a person infallible thereafter. The ridiculous Dutch painter reaches something like it in front of the nude, with the tool in his hand. He does not suddenly cease to grieve or become a different kind of painter. He returns to Holland; the narrator imagines the many bad pictures still ahead of him.

For one moment he sees something his hatred cannot determine, and does not destroy it. The scene does not need a saint to bear its weight.

## At the end

The four essays began with a man leaving and a reason that would not work as a public justification. They followed the injuries, the generosity, and the accounts later submitted on his behalf. They reached the walls of a house whose final paintings were burned.

That destruction does not make all his work useless to everyone, nor does it mean nobody saw the murals. Ata and Coutras did. It means that the final work’s preservation for us was not a condition of its meaning for him.

Afterward everything is made to fit again: the famous painter, the colourful misconduct, the reproductions in the drawing room, the son’s account of retribution. Meanwhile the painted walls are ash.

The book leaves us with two things. A person can find something wholly his own whose weight is not awarded by others. He can also fail to see the people around him, and that failure cannot be offset by what he makes.

Both are true. They do not join into an uplifting moral or a reassuring conclusion. Perhaps that is why the novel leaves us uneasy, and why it remains worth reading.
