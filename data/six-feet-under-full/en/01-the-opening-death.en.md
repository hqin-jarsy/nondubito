# A Death Before the Titles

*English edition, expanded from the Chinese essay and revised alongside it. Full-series spoilers.*

## 1.1 The Pattern

Six Feet Under has a pattern that runs through all five seasons.

In most episodes, someone dies before the opening titles. In the last episode, a child is born.

It's worth looking at how the pattern works. It doesn't start by presenting a body. It gives us a little time.

The person is still alive in those moments and doing something in particular. Washing vegetables, driving, arguing, talking on the phone, putting on sunscreen by the pool.

Then they die.

The image stops. A name appears, and two dates.

Often it's that death that takes us into the Fishers' funeral home.

## 1.2 How They Die

Look at a few of the openings. A woman is on the phone in her backyard when a chunk of blue ice falls from a plane and strikes her. A baker reaches into a mixer while working late, and his sleeve catches. A man's heart stops during sex. A woman sees inflatable figures floating up into the sky, takes them for angels and walks into the road, where she's hit by a car. A child is beside a swimming pool at home.

There's nothing stately about these deaths. Most are absurd. A few are funny.

The show often plays them in an even voice. It doesn't hurry to give the moment the weight of a slow-motion shot or a set of last words.

Someone is doing a very ordinary thing, and then they're gone.

## 1.3 What the Pattern Does

What the opening puts together, abruptly, is a dead body and a person.

Usually we come to a death in the other direction. First there's an obituary. Only then do we get the person. We learn who they've been through what others remember, photographs they left and the things they owned. By then a life is already complete, something that can be summed up and talked about.

Six Feet Under reverses the order.

You see someone alive first. You see them washing vegetables, driving, arguing with a spouse over something small. You see that they don't know.

Then you see them die.

When the dates appear, what you've just had in front of you is a person who was still making plans for tomorrow.

## 1.4 It Isn't an Ending

In this show, a death asks one very short question.

Did you live a life that was your own?

There's no answer to be given now. It's already too late. What the woman was doing at the instant the ice came down is what she leaves behind as an answer. She doesn't get to change it.

The opening puts all the days that came before that instant in front of us at once. Then it stops. What the show keeps coming back to is the ordinary time that just ran out.

## 1.5 The House

A lot of the dead are brought through the doors of an old house in Los Angeles. There's a sign out front: Fisher & Sons.

It's a family home too. People live upstairs; bodies wait downstairs. A flight of stairs separates the kitchen from the embalming room. While the Fishers eat around the table, one of that day's clients lies in a cabinet below them.

Here the question can be asked every day.

Every day, the Fishers handle other people's answers. A bereaved relative sits opposite them and talks about somebody who has died. They listen, go through the arrangements, quote a price, prepare the body and see the service through. A whole life comes in to be settled.

And all the while, their own days are passing.

That's the thread these nine essays follow. Four people, and later five or six, watch the question arrive for somebody else. One by one they have a little time to answer it themselves, or they find they haven't.

## 1.6 The Man in the First Episode

The first death in the series is the father of that family, Nathaniel Fisher, who runs the funeral home in the house.

On Christmas Eve he's driving a brand-new hearse to the airport. Ruth is cooking at home and talking with him on the phone. He lights a cigarette at the wheel. He says something.

Then comes the sound of brakes. A city bus. Ruth is at the other end of the line.

It's worth stopping there for a second. For the first time the question lands on somebody in particular. What is Nathaniel Fisher doing when he dies?

He's driving a new hearse and smoking a cigarette his wife doesn't want him to smoke. That's the answer he leaves. Over the next five seasons the show lets us learn who that man was, through the different versions his three children remember. We'll return to them in the third essay.

What unsettles us most about these opening deaths isn't how bizarre they are. It's that nobody gets to arrange one. You can't choose what you're doing when the last moment arrives. You can only do something about the days before it, the ones that still seem to have a tomorrow.

If your name and dates appeared today, would you be willing to leave what you're doing now as an answer? If not, you can't wait until you know how much time you have left.

Six Feet Under was created by Alan Ball and aired from 2001 to 2005, for five seasons and sixty-three episodes.
