# Everyone’s Waiting

*English edition, expanded from the Chinese essay and revised alongside it. Full-series spoilers.*

## 9.1 The Last Six Minutes

Claire's car pulls away. She puts on a CD from Ted, a mix of songs he likes and that nobody would call cool. The music starts. She gets onto the freeway.

Then the images begin jumping ahead. We see a few of the days to come: Willa's birthday, David and Keith's wedding. Then we see six people die. For each, two dates appear.

## 9.2 The Six People

Ruth Fisher, 1946 to 2025. A hospital room. Her children around her. David holds her hand. Claire has come back from New York. When Ruth dies, she has people in the room with her.

Keith Charles, 1968 to 2029. He started a security company. He's on the job, getting out of an armored truck. Two men open fire. He dies on the sidewalk.

David Fisher, 1969 to 2044. A family gathering outdoors, a crowd on a lawn. He sees Keith as a young man, smiling at him. David falls.

Federico Diaz, 1974 to 2049. He's with his wife on a cruise ship, standing on the deck with a drink. He collapses.

Brenda Chenowith, 1969 to 2051. She's old and sitting in a chair at home. Billy is beside her, talking without a pause. She's listening, and then she's gone.

Claire Fisher, 1983 to 2085. A hundred and one, in her bed at home. Her eyes have failed her. The wall is full of photographs. We see the road again. The young Claire is still driving.

## 9.3 This Isn't About How They Die

At first the sequence looks like it's about ways to die. If that's all you look at, the six seem random. Some die among friends and family, some fall suddenly, and one is shot by robbers on the job. There's no pattern of reward or punishment to any of it.

Look at something else and the sequence takes shape. What is in the frame when the question reaches each person?

Ruth has a room full of people: children, grandchildren, her companion. Her separate HBO obituary adds the business she started twenty years earlier. David has a family picnic that includes both boys he raised with Keith. Rico has the ship and his wife; he went on to buy a funeral home of his own. Brenda has her house and the brother she spent a life looking after. She has written books and made a place in a field. Claire has a wall of photographs, work that remains.

When the question comes for these five, they have something in their hands.

## 9.4 And Keith Has Nothing in His Hands

Keith Charles dies in 2029, aged sixty-one. He's at work. He's shot after getting out of an armored truck.

What's in the frame then? A street. A truck. Two people with guns.

No family, no life's work, no room full of people. He dies on the job in a place that tells us nothing about his life.

It matters that the show puts him among the others. If all six were shown with something in their hands, the last minutes would offer a rule: live well, and you'll get a good ending.

The show has resisted that rule from the first episode. Remember the blue ice, the mixer, the inflatable figures. None of those deaths passed judgment on a life. Keith is the last person the show treats this way.

It's not that he wasn't ready. He married David, raised two children and started a company. He was ready as well as anyone could be. And that day he happened to be on that street.

Being ready doesn't make the last moment come out well. It means the years before it are yours. On that street in 2029, Keith loses the years he might have had. The two men don't get to take the years he already lived.

## 9.5 The Last Time We See the Pattern

In the last six minutes the show brings back a form it has used at the beginning many times.

The opening of most episodes: a person is doing an ordinary thing, and dies. A name appears, and two dates.

The closing minutes: a person goes about an ordinary thing, and dies. A name appears, and two dates.

The finale itself begins, of all things, with a birth. Only in the montage does the old pattern return. This time, you know the people.

Over sixty-three episodes you've been given a little time with a lot of strangers, then a name, then dates. You get accustomed to it. It becomes a rhythm, part of the opening. The show uses it at the end on people you've come to know, and it lets you notice something.

Somebody knew the people at the beginning too.

## 9.6 It Shows Us the Six Deaths

Six Feet Under and The Sopranos aired on the same network. Six Feet Under ended in August 2005. The Sopranos ended in June 2007, less than two years later. Both come up against the same question and answer it in opposite ways.

The last ten seconds of The Sopranos are black. It doesn't tell you whether the man died. It leaves a blank each viewer can fill. Six Feet Under spends its last minutes showing six people die, one after another, with the years given. It leaves no suspense about that.

Both endings refuse a kind of consolation. If The Sopranos gave us an answer, those ten seconds might become a verdict. If he died, call it payback. If he didn't, say the world has no payback. Either way there's a conclusion ready to carry out of the room.

Six Feet Under gives all the answers of one kind. Suspense might itself be consoling. Until something has been said, you can imagine it might not happen. The show puts up the dates, one person at a time.

Neither ending gives us an easy account of what it all means.

## 9.7 The Question

The first essay began with the ordinary time that runs out. The question still fits in one sentence.

Did you live a life that was your own?

Nathaniel ran a business for many years and kept a small room he got in exchange for a funeral. That room doesn't tell us whether he could be himself when anyone was looking.

Nate knew early that he might not have long. He never made that knowledge into preparation. He knew, but he made little he could leave as an answer.

David spent decades watching himself. He came out, and then something happened that left a lasting wound. He raised the children and had years with the man he loved. He could be ready without being whole.

Ruth didn't have an answer when, in her fifties, she was asked what now. She spent five years gathering the pieces, started a pet care business. By the time the obituary was written, that beginning was twenty years behind her. She had time.

Brenda was written into somebody else's book. She became someone able to write books and make sense of experiences like hers. The old book is still in the obituary, as something that once happened to her.

At twenty-two Claire doesn't know if her road will get her anywhere. Years later there is a wall of photographs.

Keith was ready. He happened to be on that street that day.

Nobody here makes it just by figuring the question out. The ones who had time used a lot of ordinary, often boring days whose use wouldn't have been obvious at the time.

Three questions stay after the last six minutes.

First: The opening lets a person do a very ordinary thing, and then the question comes for them. You can't arrange the second when it comes. If it happened today, would you want the thing you're doing now to stand as your answer?

Second: In her fifties Ruth had nothing she could show. At seventy-nine she had a business she'd opened twenty years earlier. The years leading to that beginning looked like a mess while she was living them. Think of something you've done lately that didn't seem much use. What did it teach you? Something small will do.

Third: Between Claire at twenty-two and Claire at a hundred and one are nearly eighty years. We see a few moments. At twenty-two she and Nate both look as if they're running and neither knows what they want. Nate dies at forty with little to show for the running. Claire has a wall full of photographs. On the day the show lets her drive away, there's no way to see the difference.

What do you have in your hands now? Not assets or a title or what people think of you. Something you've made that's still there and doesn't need anyone else's agreement to count.

It's all right if there isn't anything yet. Claire didn't have it at twenty-two. The years she had later came a day at a time.

Did you do that thing today?

Six Feet Under was created by Alan Ball and aired from 2001 to 2005, for five seasons and sixty-three episodes. These nine essays read the television series; some later details also draw on the fictional obituaries HBO released for the finale.
