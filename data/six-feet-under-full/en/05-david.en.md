# David

*English edition, expanded from the Chinese essay and revised alongside it. Full-series spoilers.*

## 5.1 He Did Everything Right

David Fisher was the one who stayed. His brother went to Seattle. His sister was in high school. He was in the house.

Before the first episode he'd finished mortuary school and got his license. He went into the business and learned it from his father. He served at the family church and became a deacon early in the first season. He looked after his mother. He'd had a girlfriend for a long time.

It's a very tidy résumé. Everything on it is the right thing to do. Meanwhile, all those years, there was something else he was doing every day.

## 5.2 The Closet

David is gay and has known it for a long time. In the first episode nobody in his family knows. He has a boyfriend, Keith, a Los Angeles police officer. They've been together for some time. In the Fisher house, Keith is a friend of his.

Look at what this asks of him in an ordinary day. At dinner somebody asks when he's going to find a girl and he has to come up with an answer. When Keith visits, David has to manage the distance between them. At church, in a room full of people, he has to be the person they take him for. If he and Keith fight, he can't tell anyone why he's upset.

Dozens of times a day he does the same thing. He checks the difference between how he just acted and how he's supposed to look. It's where the sharp, appraising father of the third essay comes from. David has trained himself to watch himself. If he stops for a second, somebody might see.

## 5.3 He Comes Out

David comes out in the first season. It doesn't go cleanly. Keith has had enough of being introduced as a friend. He wants to break up. David falls apart.

Something slips out in front of his brother early on, and he takes it back. Only later does he tell his mother. Telling his family isn't something a single sentence can finish. The self he has worked to maintain for years won't disappear as soon as the words are spoken.

The harder part is the church. He tells the people who run it, too. The responses are mixed, and in the end he has to step down as deacon.

The show is especially honest here. Some people accept him and some don't. Telling the truth doesn't make the division in the room go away.

## 5.4 Those Hours

“That’s My Dog,” the fifth episode of the fourth season, is one of the hardest episodes to watch.

David is driving. He stops for a young hitchhiker.

The man gets in. Then he pulls out a gun.

The show takes its time with the next several hours. The man makes David drive, withdraw money and drive to an empty lot. He forces him to take drugs, threatens him with the gun and pours gasoline over him. Later he leaves David there and goes.

The show gives David no reason that explains why this happened. He survives it.

## 5.5 The Show Doesn't Fix Him

In most stories an episode like this would be a test. The main character goes through something terrible, comes out changed and is stronger for it. For a long time afterward this show keeps filming how the experience hasn't gone away.

David starts to lose his grip at work. He has trouble at a funeral. He shakes in the embalming room. He starts seeing the man on the street, in the supermarket and in the hallway at home. The man isn't there. David sees him.

His relationship with Keith nearly falls apart. What David can't talk about already shapes the way they live together. By the fifth season there are two boys in their lives, and fear shapes how David is with them too.

Trying to get help isn't any prettier. David tries to talk to someone, tries to make what happened something he can speak about in the past tense. Then Nate dies. That loss meets the fear already there.

The show doesn't give him a scene in which he gets better. It gives him a long, uneasy stretch of time in which things keep coming back.

## 5.6 What the Voice Says

His father appears to him at the end of the fourth season. In the third essay, we saw the old voice say something different. It reminds him that he's still alive.

By the end of the fifth season, Nate has died. David has spent some time away from Keith and the children, living with Ruth again. He meets the man in the red hoodie in a dream. This time he goes toward him. The figure turns out to be a terrified David.

Afterward he returns to Keith and the boys.

There's a way in which this is harder than coming out. Coming out says something about who I am. Here he has to face something that happened to him, something he couldn't stop, and he still has to go on living.

## 5.7 Was He Ready?

When the question comes for David Fisher, what does he have? The show gives a very particular answer. He dies in 2044 at an outdoor family gathering. He is among his family. Before he goes, he sees Keith as a young man, smiling at him.

He and Keith married and spent years together. They adopted two boys. There is the house and the business he spent his working life in. There's a family gathering.

What he doesn't have is a life in which everything has been put right.

The show doesn't tell us what finally became of those hours. It doesn't tell us he healed. It doesn't tell us he carried them in the same way forever.

Being ready doesn't require being whole. A hurt can leave a permanent mark and a person can still make a life they'd be willing to leave as an answer. There's no contradiction in that.

David's answer isn't that he got over it. It's that after it happened he still raised those children, still spent those years with the man he loved and still did that work. Coming out didn't fix him; the life he made afterward didn't automatically fix him either. The watchfulness he learned to survive kept finding something to watch once the old danger had passed. Love didn't simply remove the fear of what happened.

He didn't have to be made whole first to live those years.

Six Feet Under was created by Alan Ball and aired from 2001 to 2005, for five seasons and sixty-three episodes.
