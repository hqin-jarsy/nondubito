# A Business

*English edition, expanded from the Chinese essay and revised alongside it. Full-series spoilers.*

## 2.1 The Showroom

At Fisher & Sons there's a room full of caskets. At one end, a box made of particleboard and covered with cloth. At the other, solid mahogany, metal fittings and silk inside, at many times the price.

The show takes us into this room again and again. The family comes in. They probably lost someone that morning or the day before. They haven't slept. Their faces are swollen. It's hard to get a sentence out. Then somebody starts showing them what they can buy.

It's a hard scene to write, and the show doesn't turn away from it. There's a kind of talk that goes with this room. This model has a better seal. Many families feel it's the last thing they can do for him. Of course, the decision is entirely yours.

That last sentence is the one to listen to. The decision goes back to the family. Something goes with it: if you choose the cheaper one, what will that say about you?

## 2.2 The Fishers Aren't Swindlers

Most of what the Fishers do is genuinely useful. Somebody has to take care of the body and do it properly. In a few days somebody has to see to notices, registration, papers, the cemetery, the service, religious arrangements and where the relatives will sit. Barely able to manage the day, a family isn't in a position to do all that on its own.

More than that, someone has to be there with them through those days.

David Fisher takes that work seriously. He remembers names. He can sit with someone for a long time. When a mother comes apart, he doesn't hurry to console her. He just stays. He believes his work is worth doing, and he's right.

The trouble lies elsewhere.

## 2.3 The Bill

It's also a business.

The price difference in the showroom can't be explained by materials alone. After two years underground, the expensive casket and the cheap one are both underground. What the extra money buys in that room is the chance to say, “I did everything I could for him.”

That's exactly what the family most needs to be able to say. Someone has lost a person and can do nothing about it. They can't save them. They can't change anything that has already happened. They can still spend money.

The trade comes into view here. At the moment when a person feels they can do nothing, the business sells them one thing they can still do.

## 2.4 Rico

One part of this business is unmistakably a craft. Federico Diaz, known as Rico, is a restorative artist. Say a body is brought in after a car crash and the family wants an open casket. He has to give the person a face they can recognize.

Wax, thread and color; a photograph taken while the person was alive. He spends hours restoring the shape of a cheekbone, closing torn skin and bringing the coloring close to what it was. The show lets us watch him work and doesn't spare us the detail.

It's a reconstruction. Rico is proud of it. When he's finished he stands still and looks for a while. If something isn't right he does it again. If someone tells him the work doesn't matter, he'll let them know what he thinks.

He does this so that the people who are still alive can take one last look. It does nothing for the dead person.

## 2.5 What Rico Wants

What Rico wants has to do with his place in the house, not just his craft. He works for a wage. The Fishers count him as one of their own. He'd been in the business since he was young, taught by Nathaniel himself. Apart from the children, few have been around the house so long.

When Rico asks for a share in the business, Nate and David want time to think. They mean it when they say he's family. But that word can turn a question about ownership into one about affection. As Rico puts it at one point, you say I'm family, but your names are on the door and mine isn't.

In the first season Rico leaves for Kroehner, then comes back to the Fishers. At the end of the second season a surprise inheritance lets him put in seventy-five thousand dollars and buy a quarter of the business. The sign eventually says Fisher & Diaz.

He couldn't get that place just by asking for it. He could leave, and he could come back to the table with money of his own and terms to discuss.

## 2.6 Kroehner

Kroehner Service International is a chain that buys family funeral homes across the country. A chain can offer to buy you out, then compete next door if you won't sell. Its scale gives it a way to lower prices. It can buy in bulk, advertise and give every office the same procedures.

The person Kroehner sends to talk is very polite. They'll keep the Fisher name. You'll still be in charge here. Nothing will change.

One thing will, of course. Someone else will own the place.

The point isn't that big companies are bad. A chain can have better facilities, lower prices, clearer procedures and even someone to take calls around the clock. The question is whether the work itself survives when it's done the same way everywhere.

Think of the two hours David spends beside a grieving mother without talking. Where on a report would anyone put those two hours?

## 2.7 Two Prices in One House

The Fishers do two things in the same house every day. They stay with people through some of the hardest days they'll ever have. And they send those people a bill.

You can't pull the two apart. Without the bill, the family can't make a living, the house goes, and there isn't any place for those two hours with David to happen.

The show doesn't resolve that difficulty or dismiss it as hypocrisy. It keeps both within view: the showroom, and the person crying outside it.

A lot of work that involves looking after people depends on one part that doesn't make it onto a bill. That part can matter most to the person being looked after. When nobody can see it on the report, it's also the part somebody can most easily decide to cut.
