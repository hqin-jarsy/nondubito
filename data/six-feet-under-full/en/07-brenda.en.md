# Brenda

*English edition, expanded from the Chinese essay and revised alongside it. Full-series spoilers.*

## 7.1 Charlotte Light and Dark

As a child, Brenda Chenowith was the subject of a book.

It was called Charlotte Light and Dark. Charlotte was the name they gave her in it. Her parents didn't write it. Gareth Feinberg did. Her parents were the ones who made her available to him.

Both were psychotherapists. They saw that their daughter scored remarkably high on intelligence tests. She was more than the bright kid people remark on at dinner. She was the sort of child who would make an impression in a professional journal.

She was watched, tested, written up and discussed. The book came out and made a name for itself. In one corner of psychology, Charlotte was a case.

## 7.2 What That Did

“Childhood trauma” is too broad to say what it did.

First, at a very young age Brenda knew she was being watched. Most children do something and that's the thing they've done. Brenda does it and something else happens at the same time: how will they write it down? Anything she does might become a piece of material.

Second, her understanding of herself comes from outside. Most people acquire it slowly, from things they've done, from how others respond and from what it felt like to them. Very early on, Brenda already has a professional account of herself in writing. It knows more about her than she does.

Third, and most important, her being special is something her parents have accomplished. She was written about because she was different. Being different, from that day on, doesn't belong to her alone. It's been certified. It's an asset in the family.

## 7.3 What She Does

Adult Brenda, in the first two seasons, can be very hard to be around. She's extremely smart. She can see through someone in three sentences. She's read everything and can pick apart any conversation in the language of a therapist.

She uses that ability to attack. A family gathering with her parents is a massacre. With Nate she can name exactly what he most wants not to hear. If something tries to get close, she goes after it.

In the second season she starts having sex with strangers, one after another, in one place after another. The show stays with this for a long time and makes it uncomfortable to watch. It looks less and less like something she's doing for pleasure.

Follow what she does and you can hear a question occurring over and over: is there something I could do that would finally make you not want me?

It looks like self-destruction. You can also see a test. Someone who grows up being recorded, assessed and made into a case learns first that what she's worth depends on what she shows. Brenda keeps pushing it to the limit.

If I behave badly enough, will you still want me?

## 7.4 Her Brother

There's someone else in this story: her younger brother, Billy. He has bipolar disorder. How the two of them came to be what they are for each other matters here.

Her parents left the care of Billy to Brenda. For years she watched over him, took his calls and tried to figure out what to do when he was unwell. There wasn't a formal moment when anyone told her to take this on. It slowly became hers to handle.

So the family gave her two things. You're special. And you're the one responsible for your brother. She asked for neither. Both were supposed to say something about who she was.

## 7.5 What She Does Later

In the fictional obituary HBO released with the finale, you can read what Brenda Chenowith does later.

She earns a master's in social work at California State University, Los Angeles, and a doctorate at the University of Southern California. She writes several books on the role of gifted children in families. She becomes one of the leading scholars in the field, adds courses to the social work curriculum at USC and develops ways of doing research.

Then the obituary mentions that as a child she was the subject of Gareth Feinberg's Charlotte Light and Dark.

## 7.6 She Takes It Back

In the course of her life Brenda becomes the person who writes the book.

There's no need to call it revenge. She studies what had happened to her: the uses a family can find for a child with a gift. She takes a hand in writing about it. She was once a piece of material in somebody else's book. She spends a life getting to the place where she can write about the material herself.

Look where the obituary puts the old book. It comes after the degrees, the work and her place in a field of her own. Now it's something you learn in the background.

She hasn't escaped Charlotte Light and Dark. The book still exists and it's still there in the obituary. What she has changed is where it belongs in the sentence. It used to say what she was. Now it says what she went through.

The distance between those two things is a life's work.

## 7.7 When the Question Comes for Her

The question comes for Brenda in 2051. According to the same obituary, she dies at home at an advanced age.

What the show gives us is almost a joke. She sits in a chair. Billy is beside her talking on and on, about Claire and Ted. She listens and listens, and then she's gone.

It's worth staying with that for a second. She spent a life dealing with Billy. As she dies, he's beside her, still talking.

What does she have? Three children: Maya, whom she raised with Nate; Willa, their daughter; and Forrest, born after she remarries. Her husband Daniel. The books she wrote. A place in a field of study. And the brother she spent a life looking after, talking right alongside her when she dies.

There are two ways to see that last thing. She never got free of the task, even at the end. Or the brother she was made responsible for so young lived all the way to the end and was still with her.

The show doesn't say which.

Brenda began with an expert's name for her and an outside account of who she was. She went on to study, work and write. The celebrated case is still in the obituary; it no longer occupies the place of the person doing those things.

A change in the grammar doesn't make the harm disappear. It can make what happened something in a past you're still able to think about, rather than a permanent answer to who you are.

Six Feet Under was created by Alan Ball and aired from 2001 to 2005, for five seasons and sixty-three episodes.
