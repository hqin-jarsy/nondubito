# Chapter Eighty-six: Timid Ying-chun meets her end at the hands of the wolf of Zhongshan; unbending Lady Xing examines the accounts and uncovers old wrongdoing

<!-- P1 -->
After the official vessel carrying Tan-chun had gone, the doors of Autumn Studio were sealed and the keys handed to Lady Wang. Remembering how Adamantina had lately kept her door shut and refused visitors, Lady Wang let a few more days pass, then sent Zhou Rui's wife with two old women to Green Bower Hermitage, intending first to ask whether she lacked rice or charcoal. When they reached the hermitage, however, they found its two doors standing ajar. A fine layer of pine needles lay on the stone steps. The bronze chime still hung in its old place beneath the eaves; it stirred a little in the wind, but made no sound.

<!-- P2 -->
Zhou Rui's wife called several times from outside. Receiving no answer, she pushed the door open and went in. The bedding on the meditation couch was neatly folded; an inch of cold ash lay in the incense-burner, and an old rush cushion leaned against the wall. Several ordinary tea vessels were still on the shelf, but Adamantina's own little tea stove, her travelling water bowl and two volumes of scripture were all gone. The ritual vase beneath the window stood empty, with half a dried plum leaf beside it. All the drawers were open. There was no letter in them, not a single written word.

<!-- P3 -->
“Only yesterday I saw the young lady gathering leaves on the slope behind,” a little maid said. “How can she be gone today?”

<!-- P4 -->
The old woman who looked after the hermitage came hurrying up. “I thought I heard the side gate make a noise just before the fifth watch,” she said hastily. “I took it for the wind and didn't get up to look.”

<!-- P5 -->
“Did a carriage or chair come for her?” Zhou Rui's wife asked.

<!-- P6 -->
“Not that I could see,” the woman replied. “There was only one trail of footprints on the muddy path to the south. Beyond the bamboos they faded away.”

<!-- P7 -->
They searched behind the hermitage and found the bolt of the little gate lying on the ground. Outside it a few blades of grass had bowed beneath the dew. Farther south lay the back street, with people coming and going in the distance. How could anyone tell one from another now? Zhou Rui's wife went back and reported to Lady Wang. “Did she leave any message?” Lady Wang asked.

<!-- P8 -->
“Not a word,” Zhou Rui's wife replied.

<!-- P9 -->
Lady Wang sat for a while. “Very well,” she said at last. “Have the hermitage locked. Make an inventory of what is inside and keep it safe.”

<!-- P10 -->
From then on Green Bower Hermitage too stood empty. For the moment we shall leave it there.

<!-- P11 -->
Now Ying-chun had said little ever since marrying into the Sun family. On her earlier visit the old woman had reported to her that her go stones at Amaryllis Eyot were all still there, and Ying-chun had sent word to Bao-yu asking him to send the set. Bao-yu had gone into the Garden himself to fetch it, counted the stones one by one, and packed them in their red sandalwood box, inlaid with mother-of-pearl and flush designs of gold and silver, before entrusting them to a reliable person to deliver to the Suns'. These red and green ivory go stones, carved with designs of flowers and birds, had once belonged to Emperor Minghuang of the Tang. Some years before, someone had offered through an intermediary to buy them for ten thousand taels, but Ying-chun had refused. Now that they had been sent to her, she merely put them away in her chest. She had not laid out a single game.

<!-- P12 -->
Sun Shao-zu drank and gambled outside, sometimes staying away for three or five days, sometimes returning in the middle of the night reeking of wine. At the slightest displeasure he would smash cups, beat people and curse them. Ying-chun stayed hidden in her room, copying scriptures morning and evening, or occasionally reading the Treatise of the Most High on Retribution.

<!-- P13 -->
One evening Sun Shao-zu came home drunk. The moment he entered he cursed the page for forgetting to raise the lamp-wick; then, finding his soup cold, he flung a teacup to the ground. The maids and old women all slipped away. Ying-chun sat beneath the lamp with the same book before her. At the sight of it Sun Shao-zu sneered. “You wretched bitch! Reading that useless rubbish again.”

<!-- P14 -->
Ying-chun frowned, but held her breath and said nothing.

<!-- P15 -->
There was a clatter as Sun Shao-zu kicked a small stool aside and sat down opposite her. “Do you still think you're the Second Miss in a duke's mansion?” he sneered. “Your father took five thousand taels of mine and kept promising to get me a good appointment. In the end there wasn't so much as a shadow of one. I had to send another five thousand to Jia Yu-cun's door before I got the official's hat I wear today. Ten thousand taels altogether. I bought my post, and you were sold to me in settlement of the debt. What right have you to put on your fine-lady airs with me?”

<!-- P16 -->
Ying-chun only kept her head lowered, her hand pressed tightly against the page.

<!-- P17 -->
The more Sun Shao-zu cursed, the angrier he grew. He snatched the book away, but at the words Treatise of the Most High on Retribution on its cover his anger turned to loud laughter. “Still reading this fucking thing!” He tossed it beside the brazier, then raised his right foot and scraped the mud from his boot on a corner of the book. “If this book has any power, let it protect you first. I've paid out my silver and got my post. Did so much as half a copper cash fall from heaven to stop me?”

<!-- P18 -->
The charcoal caught one corner of the book. Its paper curled, sending up a thin thread of blue smoke. Ying-chun hurriedly bent to retrieve it and pressed out the sparks with her hand. Her fingers burned red, but she made no sound. She merely smoothed the blackened place gently and put the book back where it had been.

<!-- P19 -->
Finding still less satisfaction in this, Sun Shao-zu stood up. “My superior Mr Zhou's wife is coming to visit tomorrow. You're to come out and keep her company. At New Year and every festival she goes round to all the houses, and remembers what cakes each old lady likes. There are any number of matters in our office that a single word from her has smoothed over. Learn from her how to deal with people, instead of sitting here all day with those damned ducal airs. Try pleading illness and refusing to see her again, and see whether I let you off.” He kicked the curtain aside and left.

<!-- P20 -->
What followed was another state of affairs, which the Sun servants dared not speak of outside and no one from the Jia household came to ask about. Every few days Tangerine sent someone to fetch a doctor, but those at the gate always said that their mistress was peculiar in her ways and refused to take medicine.

<!-- P21 -->
Late one night, Sun Shao-zu was again out drinking, and most of those in the inner courtyard had gone to sleep. Ying-chun quietly told Tangerine to close the door, then asked her to bring over her dressing-case. Her voice was so calm that Tangerine could not guess what was wanted. She did as she was told and set it beneath the lamp.

<!-- P22 -->
From its lowest compartment Ying-chun took an old purse of stout satin and tipped out its pieces of silver one by one onto the table. She drew a plain gold pin from her hair, slipped the silver bracelet from her wrist, and set these down with two pairs of earrings. Counting the silver, she said, “There are thirty-two taels in ready money here. The gold pin is light, but it is worth a few more. Keep the bracelet for expenses on the road. Don't sell the earrings unless you are in real difficulty.”

<!-- P23 -->
Tangerine started. “What road do you mean, miss?”

<!-- P24 -->
“At the third watch tonight the watch changes at the back side gate,” Ying-chun said. “The water cart will be going out. The driver is the nephew of Mother Zhang in the kitchen. I've already made Mother Zhang take the pearl-set hairpin. Put on his wife's old jacket and go round behind the woodshed. He'll think you're going home with a message and won't stop you.”

<!-- P25 -->
Tangerine dropped to her knees. “You want me to leave? But what will happen to you, miss? Let us go together.”

<!-- P26 -->
Ying-chun shook her head. “You can go. I cannot. My father took five thousand taels from him, did not do what he had promised, and would not return the money. I was given to settle that account.”

<!-- P27 -->
Tangerine clasped her knees. “Then I won't go. Wherever you are, I'll be there too. At worst we can die here together.”

<!-- P28 -->
Ying-chun reached out and drew her up. Putting the silver, bracelet and earrings into the purse, she pressed it into Tangerine's hand. “If you stay, it will only mean another good girl's life thrown away for nothing. If you leave, you can find a way forward for yourself.”

<!-- P29 -->
“If you know there is a way out,” Tangerine wept, “why won't you take it?”

<!-- P30 -->
Ying-chun picked up the gold pin from the table and slipped it into Tangerine's hair. With a smile she said, “The world is wide, but where in it is there room for me?”

<!-- P31 -->
“Go back to the mansion,” Tangerine wept. “Go to the Second Master, to Her Ladyship, to Miss Bao. Someone is bound to take you in.”

<!-- P32 -->
Ying-chun did not answer. She only took back the purse, brought out a cotton-padded waist-wrap, and divided the silver among several places, sewing it into the lining. Usually she was slow even with a needle; that night her stitches were very steady. Whenever she finished one place she pressed it with her hand, making sure that nothing showed from outside before beginning the next. Then she brought out a gold phoenix ornament of filigree and clustered pearls and sewed that in too.

<!-- P33 -->
Tangerine stood beside her, watching and weeping. “Don't make a sound,” Ying-chun said. “If anyone outside hears, even you won't be able to leave.”

<!-- P34 -->
Then she added, “If anyone asks, say I sent you to my family's house to fetch something. Once you are through the back gate, go south. Don't go to the Rong mansion, and don't turn back. Find some relatives of your own to stay with first. When things have quietened down in a few days, you can decide what to do.”

<!-- P35 -->
“Once I'm settled,” Tangerine wept, “I'll come and fetch you.”

<!-- P36 -->
Ying-chun straightened her collar. “You must never come back.”

<!-- P37 -->
Tangerine wept and shook her head again and again.

<!-- P38 -->
“If anyone asks you later,” Ying-chun added, “say that I told you to go. Don't let them call you a thief.”

<!-- P39 -->
Tangerine pushed the waist-wrap and purse towards the table. “Keep these yourself, miss. With money in your hands you'll fare better outside.”

<!-- P40 -->
But Ying-chun pressed them back against her breast. “I shall have no use for them.”

<!-- P41 -->
She spoke very softly. Tangerine stood motionless, her tears falling on the back of Ying-chun's hand. Ying-chun withdrew it and took a handkerchief to wipe her face. “Your eyes are swollen with crying. They'll be suspicious at the gate. Sit in the outer room for a while, until your face looks better, before you go.”

<!-- P42 -->
As the third watch approached, a faint sound of wooden wheels duly came from the back courtyard. Ying-chun put the old jacket round Tangerine with her own hands, then drew the gold pin from her hair and hid it inside the upper of her shoe. “There will be people on the road. You mustn't wear this in your hair.”

<!-- P43 -->
At the door Tangerine knelt again and bowed her head to the ground three times. Ying-chun stood watching without helping her up. Tangerine rose and opened the door. She had only just crossed the threshold when she looked back. “Wait for me, miss.”

<!-- P44 -->
“Go quickly,” said Ying-chun.

<!-- P45 -->
Tangerine left the courtyard along the shadowed part of the covered walk. The old woman on duty at the back side gate coughed once outside the woodshed, and the gate opened a crack. Tangerine squeezed through and followed the water cart down two lanes. At the little bridge to the south she could not help looking back. Beyond the Suns' high wall, the lamp in the principal room was still burning. She stood for a while, then at last turned and went on.

<!-- P46 -->
When she heard the distant watchman's clapper fall silent, Ying-chun bolted the door and the back window too. She took the red sandalwood go box from the bottom of her chest, opened it and gently dusted it, then closed it again and set it on the table where Sun Shao-zu usually sat. Returning to her seat beneath the lamp, she took the scorched copy of the Treatise of the Most High on Retribution from the drawer and turned its pages from beginning to end. The few lines she had traced in vermilion as a child were still there. She smiled faintly, yet closed the book, laid it squarely back in the drawer and pushed the drawer shut. Then she poured the stale tea from the table into the basin, turned the cup upside down, and set her two shoes neatly together on the footstep before the bed. Only when all this was done did she untie the ribbon she ordinarily used to fasten back the bed-curtains.

<!-- P47 -->
The lamp in the outer room went out first. The one in the principal room burned until the fourth watch.

<!-- P48 -->
Next day the servants at the Suns' gate said only that their mistress's old illness had returned, and admitted no outsiders. A few days later a thin coffin was quietly carried into the back courtyard. No mourning was hung at the gate, nor was word sent to the Jia family. Sun Shao-zu merely told his servants, “Her family is already in mourning. Wait a while before telling them.” He also had a new lock put on the back side gate. At the Rong mansion they knew nothing of any of this. We shall leave it there.

<!-- P49 -->
At the Rong mansion, meanwhile, the monthly allowances of every household had been cut again and again since Grandmother Jia's funeral. Aunt Zhao's original allowance had now been reduced by another half-month, and the rice sent from the kitchen was mixed with stale bran. After she had spent several days cursing in her room, a number of married women servants came to complain that their wages were late. One after another, they began talking about the ways of the household stewards.

<!-- P50 -->
One of the younger women clapped her hands. “If we're short by a single string of cash, they hound us to death. How much silver has passed through Lai Da's hands? Has anyone ever said one word to hurry him? That garden of his, with its pavilions and waterside terraces, is better fitted out than the one here!”

<!-- P51 -->
“Why stop at Lai Da?” another said with a laugh. “There's Lai Sheng and Yu Lu at the Ning mansion, and Zhang Cai's wife here. Which of them hasn't tucked away several boxes? When Zhang Cai's daughter married the year before last, her dowry took sixteen loads to carry. I counted them at the gate myself! Where does a mere woman servant in charge get such things?”

<!-- P52 -->
An old woman beside them held up three fingers. “Blessed Buddha! And Nurse Zhao's two sons need no mentioning. In those years when they went down to Suzhou with Master Qiang, buying theatrical costumes and hiring boats, they put down three taels for every tael they spent. The costumes stayed in their trunks, but the money grew in other people's sleeves.”

<!-- P53 -->
Aunt Zhao had been listening stretched out on the kang. At this she sat up. “Merciful heavens! If you all know this, why don't you report it to those above?”

<!-- P54 -->
“To whom?” said the first woman. Another quickly put in, “Who dared open her mouth while Mrs Lian was about?” The old woman added, “It's only now, with Mrs Lian ill and the Elder Lady not troubling about such things, that we dare come and complain.”

<!-- P55 -->
Aunt Zhao spat towards them. “Shameless creatures! If she won't trouble about it, I'll make her.”

<!-- P56 -->
She hurriedly called for a little maid to bring her shoes, put them on, and led the women straight to Lady Xing. Lady Xing was having the outstanding lists of funeral offerings from Grandmother Jia's funeral collected. Seeing Aunt Zhao come in with a crowd behind her, she frowned. “What is it now?”

<!-- P57 -->
Aunt Zhao began to weep. “Your Ladyship must stand up for those of us with nobody to care for us. They've cut our allowances and cut our rice, and when we ask, they only say there's no silver in the common purse. Blessed Buddha, where has it all gone? It certainly isn't honest people like us who've swallowed it! How much did Lai Da swallow to build his garden? How much have Lai Sheng and Yu Lu hidden away at the Ning mansion? And that black-hearted Wu Xin-deng! Master Lian has told him time and again to cough up the silver he's stolen, but he talks back and denies it, and accuses other people instead. Now we're the ones who must economize! Economize for what? To pay for his garden?”

<!-- P58 -->
“Who told you Lai Da had swallowed any silver?” Lady Xing asked.

<!-- P59 -->
Aunt Zhao turned and pointed at the women behind her. “It's not just one person saying it. Who here doesn't know? His garden is there for everyone to see. It didn't fall from heaven, did it?”

<!-- P60 -->
“And Nurse Zhao's two sons,” a woman behind her hurried to add. “When they went down to Suzhou to buy things, they put any number of false entries in the accounts.” The others vied with one another in telling how Zhang Cai's wife lent money out privately at interest.

<!-- P61 -->
When she had heard them out, Lady Xing merely told Wang Shan-bao's wife to write down a few names. “I am aware of it now,” she said. “Accounts are not investigated just because you say so. Go back to your rooms. If you gather in a crowd and make a disturbance again, it is your wages I shall dock first.”

<!-- P62 -->
“Now Your Ladyship has heard,” Aunt Zhao said, “you mustn't simply let it drop!”

<!-- P63 -->
“When I say I am aware of it,” said Lady Xing, “I mean I am aware of it.”

<!-- P64 -->
Seeing her displeasure, the women could only withdraw. Once they were all gone, Wang Shan-bao's wife handed her the sheet covered with names. “They certainly dared to speak,” she said with a smile. “If there really is an inquiry, Lai Da will be the first who can't escape.”

<!-- P65 -->
Lady Xing folded the paper and weighted it with her teacup. “Lai Da and his sort were indulged beyond all restraint while the old lady lived,” she said with a cold smile. “If I wanted to question him now, whose authorization should I take? But who has handled most of the household money all these years? Lian's wife has been running things, hasn't she? While the old lady lived, she always favoured the second branch. I was the wife of the eldest son, and couldn't so much as get a word in. Now the old lady is gone, and I hear Wang Zi-teng has been ordered away from the capital. With that great prop of her own family removed, who in the capital is left to stand behind her? Lian's wife is the Second Lady's brother's daughter, and runs the house for her. Investigate the one and the inquiry leads straight to the other. Let the second branch learn that theirs is not the only voice in this house. She's ridden roughshod over everyone for years. It's time someone straightened out the accounts for the family.”

<!-- P66 -->
Wang Shan-bao's wife understood. She hurried closer with a flattering smile. “Your Ladyship has considered it justly. After all, Lai Da's garden dates back years. There are warrants signed by His Lordship and Master Zhen, and the standing he enjoyed in Her Old Ladyship's time. Who can touch that? Lai Sheng belongs to the Ning mansion. Inquire there and they'll say we're overstepping our bounds. Investigate Yu Lu or Zhang Cai's wife, and you draw in the purchases of every branch. Wu Xin-deng has kept the storehouse for years. Whose requisition is not in his hands? If we really went through this whole list, we'd seize one man today and implicate a whole household tomorrow. We wouldn't unravel it by next year, and we'd offend both mansions before we'd begun. It's different if we investigate only Mrs Lian. She's Your Ladyship's own daughter-in-law, and she's managed the household. The accounts all passed through her hands. You have every right to question her; no one can say otherwise. And she's offended more people than anyone. Find one charge against her and the servants will have what they want, and clap their hands for joy. No one will go on asking after the others' accounts.”

<!-- P67 -->
Lady Xing shifted her hand-warmer to the other hand. “What has implicating people to do with it? I am merely establishing proper rules for this family. Seal her accounts office tomorrow.”

<!-- P68 -->
Next day Lady Xing had the little accounts room where Xi-feng had formerly managed affairs sealed. Sunshine was ill and could not come; he merely handed the keys to Patience. Patience brought them to Lady Xing. “My mistress cannot get out of bed now. The account-books are all there, not one missing. Your Ladyship is welcome to examine them. Only, some of the vouchers for what each household drew are still in those households' hands. They will have to be checked together.”

<!-- P69 -->
“If there are books, we shall read the books first,” Lady Xing said. “Where vouchers are missing, we shall ask the people who received the things.”

<!-- P70 -->
Wang Shan-bao's wife led the servants in opening the cupboard. On the upper shelf were the monthly household accounts; below were several small boxes containing estate-rent records, pawn tickets and loan bonds. Every book had a dated label, and little slips in Sunshine's handwriting were tucked along the edges. Lady Xing began with Grandmother Jia's funeral. Paper offerings, canopies for scripture-reading, rice and flour, and sacrificial banquets each had their expenses entered. The kitchen had reported a hundred and twenty tables, but payment was recorded for a hundred and fifty. “Thirty tables' difference here,” said Wang Shan-bao's wife. The woman in charge of the kitchen pressed both hands to the floor. “Some were meals for the attendants, and some were added at the last moment.” “If they were added at the last moment, why are there no names?” Lady Xing asked. The woman could not answer. After stammering for a while she said only that everything had been in confusion that day. Lady Xing folded down the corner of the page. “Make a note. Have the kitchen send in the purchase lists for that day as well.”

<!-- P71 -->
They turned next to the monthly allowances. The amounts due each month, what had actually been paid, and how many days payment had been held back were all recorded. As Wang Shan-bao's wife read, she watched Lady Xing's face, pausing at significant places. In several months the money had been drawn but not immediately distributed; a separate entry read “transferred out.” “Lent at interest, that is,” she said with a smile. “The household often needed money urgently,” Patience said. “My mistress lent the allowances out for a short time, and when she collected principal and interest she used them to fill the gaps in the common funds. Every receipt and payment is on the pages that follow.” “I did not ask you,” Lady Xing said. “Read those pages first.” The accountant read from the book: “Third month of such-and-such year: eight hundred taels in allowance money transferred out. Sixth month: eight hundred taels principal recovered, with sixty-four taels interest. Paid for Her Old Ladyship's medicines, twenty taels; ice for the several households, thirty taels; repairs to the east side gate, twelve taels.” He read another entry: “Tenth month of such-and-such year: twelve hundred taels transferred out. The following year, owing to poor harvests on the farms, only four hundred taels recovered. The balance deferred until this spring.” “She's lost the capital,” said Wang Shan-bao's wife. “The bonds are all there,” Patience replied. “It isn't unaccounted for. The people outside cannot repay it.” Lady Xing leafed through the bonds. “Whether they can repay it or not, she handled it. What right had she to take the household's silver outside?” Patience lowered her head and said nothing.

<!-- P72 -->
Lady Xing ordered these books put aside. Several private ledgers and further loan bonds were also found in a search of Xi-feng's room. The bonds stated plainly: on such a day of such a month, so many taels lent to such a person, at such a monthly rate; below were signatures and thumbprints. The interest ledger recorded each monthly receipt. Taken together, the sums amounted to several hundred taels. Wang Shan-bao's wife collected the private ledgers, bonds and interest book and presented them to Lady Xing along with the household accounts that would not balance. Every entry in those books was perfectly clear: to whom money had been lent, when, how much interest was due, and whether it had been repaid. What had gone out was recorded, and what could not be recovered was recorded too. Several still earlier transactions were also entered.

<!-- P73 -->
When Lai Da was summoned, he came in wearing a half-worn mourning robe and carrying several yellowed warrants. “The garden was built years ago,” he said. “Timber, stone, plants and trees each had someone separately in charge. I only supervised the whole. Here are the warrants signed by His Lordship and Master Zhen, and the amounts Master Lian signed for at the time.” “When did you build your own garden?” Lady Xing asked. Lai Da smiled. “The money for my few wretched rooms was put together by my children and grandchildren. As for a garden, there's merely a stage in front and a few trees behind. How would I dare compare it with this mansion?” Lady Xing examined the warrants. Every item bore a master's signature. “You may go for now,” she said. “Do not move the old accounts. Come again when you are called.” Lai Da kowtowed and left.

<!-- P74 -->
Lady Xing had intended also to call in the old accounts from the other mansion for the Imperial Visitation. Wang Shan-bao's wife hastily said, “Master Zhen manages that house, and he's head of the clan as well. Your Ladyship cannot summon his people from here.” Lady Xing had to abandon the idea. Wu Xin-deng came in bearing the storehouse keys and requisition vouchers. As he set the vouchers in neat order on the table, he said with an ingratiating smile, “If Your Ladyship wishes to investigate, it will save me a great deal of arguing. Every tael that has gone in or out over these years has a voucher. Your Ladyship need only point out a single item and I'll answer for it to your face. If not even one can be found, then someone is taking advantage of this confusion to frame me. I cannot be made to bear the charge for nothing.” Hearing his defiant tone, Lady Xing was about to have him put in chains for questioning. Wang Shan-bao's wife quickly signalled to her and bent close. “Wait, Your Ladyship. He holds the vouchers for every household. Push him too hard, and he'll shout out who drew too much and who claimed what wasn't theirs. Who knows how many new troubles we'll have today? Keep the vouchers and let him go for now. There are all these books; we shall certainly need him back to check them.” Lady Xing considered. “Very well. Leave the vouchers and let him go.” Only after Wu Xin-deng had passed through the door did he wipe the sweat from his forehead with his sleeve.

<!-- P75 -->
The inquiry went on for several days. Lai Da, Wu Xin-deng and the others all gave grounds for suspicion, but each had warrants and authorized headings to shield him. Xi-feng's little account-books, by contrast, grew clearer with each volume, and the clearer they were, the more evidence they supplied. Wang Shan-bao's wife drew up daily slips listing the sums discovered. When the women waiting outside heard that Xi-feng was being investigated, they exchanged looks of secret delight. Aunt Zhao came again to ask about Lai Da. “We have found that Mrs Lian lent the allowance money at interest,” Wang Shan-bao's wife told her. “Several thousand taels of the principal alone are still tied up outside, and we're looking into even bigger old accounts. Who has time for Lai Da now?” On hearing that they had come to Xi-feng, Aunt Zhao at once smiled. “I always said her hands weren't clean. There was no need to suspect other people for nothing. This time Her Ladyship really has stood up for us!” Without waiting for an answer, she took her little maid and left. When Lai Da had first heard of the inquiry he had kept a smile on his face, and hidden away a few conspicuous objects at home. Later, seeing that their searching and searching never went beyond Mrs Lian and that no one questioned him again, he set his mind at ease and went back to managing his affairs as before.

<!-- P76 -->
Going back through several more years of accounts, they found, hidden between the layers of an old ledger, a receipt for silver sent by Jing-xu of the Mantou Nunnery. It was for three thousand taels. “What is there to investigate?” Lady Xing said at once. “A nun doesn't send three thousand taels for nothing. She must have been paying for a favour.” Wang Shan-bao's wife took the receipt, then turned to the later pages. “Look farther on, Your Ladyship. These three thousand weren't entered in the accounts at first. Later, when the mansion was short of money, they were finally paid into the common funds. First they paid two months' wages in the Garden, then several old purchase debts, and the remaining few hundred taels went towards Her Old Ladyship's birthday gifts. On paper it is all quite clean.” “If it went into the household accounts afterwards,” Lady Xing said, “we can hardly hold this against her.” “That is true, but the money's source was still improper. In my humble opinion, precisely because it entered the common funds, we ought to ask who had it sent to her. That is what we should investigate, not the three thousand taels.” Lady Xing nodded. “Quite right. Find the people Jing-xu used to deal with.”

<!-- P77 -->
Lady Xing had an old woman brought in who had often dealt with Jing-xu in former days. At first she said so many years had passed that she had forgotten, but when she saw that the receipt still existed, she confessed. “That year Mother Jing-xu came to Mrs Lian about the marriage of a Miss Zhang in Chang'an. The Zhang daughter was already promised to the son of a garrison commander. Then a young Master Li used his influence to try to force her to marry him instead. Mrs Lian had someone send a word to the military governor's office in Chang'an, and the Zhangs broke off the engagement. Afterwards I heard that both the girl and the young man she was originally promised to were dead.” “Who took the message outside for Mrs Lian?” Lady Xing asked. “I don't know. I only saw Wang-er come twice.” Lady Xing had Wang-er's name written down, and circled the three characters of Zhang Jin-ge's name as well.

<!-- P78 -->
Wang Shan-bao's wife again led a search of Xi-feng's room. Behind a cupboard they found several damaged letters. One, of which only half a page remained, mentioned Er-jie's medicines during her illness, Autumn's quarrelling day and night, and Zhang Hua's lawsuit outside. Lady Xing had Bao Er's wife summoned. She had formerly waited on Er-jie outside the mansion in Little Flower Lane. Seeing Lady Xing seated above her, she was frightened into dropping to her knees. “You were often in and out while Er-jie was living here,” Lady Xing said. “Do you know how she died?” “Several doctors certainly came when she was ill. Later, after she took a dose prescribed by Doctor Hu, she grew worse in the night. Shan-jie said she had always been frail, and no one else could be blamed. That is all I know.” “Who called Doctor Hu?” Lady Xing asked. Bao Er's wife shook her head. “I heard someone outside had recommended him. Who it was, I don't know.”

<!-- P79 -->
Wang Shan-bao's wife sent for Shan-jie as well. Having heard that she was wanted, Shan-jie had already hidden herself elsewhere and could not immediately be found. Someone did, however, find Trinket. She had once served at the House of Green Delights; since being dismissed, she had eked out a living doing odd jobs for others. Hearing that the Elder Lady was looking into old accounts and that a useful service might earn her a place in the mansion again, she came in a blue cotton jacket washed almost white. Below her cuffs showed the backs of her hands, still marked with a few old puncture scars. Wang Shan-bao's wife asked whether, during her time at Green Delights, she had seen people from Xi-feng's rooms coming and going. “After Sister Crimson went to Mrs Lian,” Trinket said, “Wang-er's wife often came to Green Delights with messages. There was also a married woman with a blue silk headcloth who came twice at night. She always used the back passage and wouldn't let herself be seen.” They then sent Trinket with several others to identify old acquaintances around the house, and outside to find the shop where the medicines had been dispensed. After two days of errands she did find a former assistant, who said that Doctor Hu's prescription had contained very heavy quantities of safflower and peach kernels, and that the woman who fetched it had a slight limp. Trinket returned and gave her report, but did not leave. She stood beneath the covered walk as if she had more to say. Coming out, Wang Shan-bao's wife asked, “Why are you still here?” Trinket smiled ingratiatingly. “Mother, something from years ago has weighed on my heart all this time. While Her Ladyship is investigating, I'd like it cleared up.” And she went straight on: “When I was at Green Delights, I was accused of stealing Miss Patience's bracelet, and Miss Skybright turned me out. I wasn't trying to steal it. Mrs Lian told me to take it. She told me to put Miss Patience's bracelet secretly by Master Bao's pillow. I didn't understand what she meant by it, but I dared not disobey. I'd only just got it, before I'd had time to put it there, when Mother Song saw me and took it back to Miss Patience. I've borne this wrong for years. I only ask Her Ladyship to bear witness that I wasn't someone who coveted the thing.” Several women copying accounts beneath the covered walk stopped their hands. Wang Shan-bao's wife went in and reported to Lady Xing. Lady Xing sat without moving. After a long time she ordered a few taels of silver brought for the girl. “I have noted what you said. Take this silver, and never mention a single word of this matter to anyone outside again.” Trinket still wanted to speak, but they had already coaxed and pushed her out through the side gate. In the end she did not regain her place in the mansion. Clutching the few pieces of silver, she went away with her head bowed.

<!-- P80 -->
Lady Xing then had Wang-er confined in an empty room. At first he admitted only to lending money for Xi-feng; of everything else he professed complete ignorance. Lady Xing looked somewhat pleased and was about to order him beaten, but Wang Shan-bao's wife hastily stopped her. “If you beat him, he'll insist he knows nothing, and you'll only be accused of forcing a confession. Better starve him for a few days, then bluff him with that half-page of letter. If he can't say where Zhang Hua's body is, he has deceived both his master and the authorities. Your Ladyship need only threaten to report him, and he'll be afraid.” Lady Xing agreed. She had a guard posted outside and allowed him only one bowl of cold rice a day. On the third day she came herself to question him. “Where is Zhang Hua buried?” Wang-er prostrated himself. “I don't know.” Lady Xing spread out the fragment of letter and smiled. “When Mrs Lian ordered you to deal with Zhang Hua, didn't you come back and say robbers had struck him down on the road and left him dead at the wayside? If he died there, where is his grave? Who collected the body? For years you've shielded your mistress with a story whose dead witness could never contradict you. If you cannot tell me where the body is now, I shall take it that you deceived your mistress and the authorities, and report you.” At the threat of the authorities, sweat broke out repeatedly on Wang-er's forehead. He could only kowtow. “Zhang Hua isn't dead. My mistress told me to make a clean job of it that year. But a human life is no light matter, and I couldn't bring myself to do it. I merely spent twenty taels staying out of the way for a few days, then came back and said he'd died on the road.” “Twenty taels?” Lady Xing asked. “Whose money?” “Drawn from the accounts office.” “Write that down,” said Lady Xing.

<!-- P81 -->
The inquiry had begun over the household's money. By its later stages, little of the missing silver had been recovered, but several old affairs involving human lives, buried beneath it, had been turned up one by one. Lady Xing made no public announcement. She merely had the accounts that had passed through Xi-feng's hands packed separately in a chest and told Wang Shan-bao's wife to seal it. As for Lai Da's accounts and the others', she said only that they would be investigated later.

<!-- P82 -->
News of the inquiry reached Xi-feng's room day after day. First they said the little accounts office had been sealed, then that the lending of the monthly allowances at interest had been discovered. Later still, people began mentioning Jing-xu, Er-jie and Zhang Hua. Whenever anyone came to report on the inquiry, Patience intercepted them outside and would not let the news pass within. But Xi-feng kept asking, “Whom did they call today?” Patience could only say, “Wang Shan-bao's wife sent for Wang-er.” With her eyes closed, Xi-feng asked, “What did he say?” “I don't know yet.” Presently someone came for Xi-feng's old private seal. Patience refused to hand it over and came in to tell her. “Give it to them,” Xi-feng said. “Now that they mean to investigate, if I keep it they'll only say I'm hiding something.” Patience had to send it out.

<!-- P83 -->
Xi-feng lay for a while, then suddenly asked for paper and brush and told them to bring Sunshine in. He spread the paper, dipped the brush in ink and waited. “Write ‘Your Ladyship,’” Xi-feng said. Sunshine wrote the two characters and looked up for what was to follow. Xi-feng gazed at them. After a while she said, “There is no need to send it.” Sunshine turned the sheet over, tucked it beneath the spittoon and withdrew. “If Her Ladyship knew that most of the interest went to make up the household funds,” Patience said, “perhaps she wouldn't go so far.” Xi-feng opened her eyes and looked at her, and Patience stopped. “It's all on the later pages,” Xi-feng said. “They can read it if they choose. If they don't choose, let it be.” Then she asked, “How much allowance money is still tied up outside?” “Principal and interest together, a little over four thousand taels. The harvests have been poor. Some families truly cannot repay it.” “Don't lose the bonds,” Xi-feng said. “They're all sealed up with the accounts.” Xi-feng nodded and closed her eyes. Outside, beneath the window, old women were whispering that Jing-xu's three thousand taels had now been traced. Hearing them, Xi-feng merely drew the edge of her quilt a little higher. Beside the bed lay an unsettled account-book from Grandmother Jia's funeral. She reached out and opened it, but after two pages her strength failed and it slipped from her hand to the edge of the mattress. Patience hurried to pick it up and put it back beside her pillow. Xi-feng lay like that. Whenever another name reached her from outside, her eyes moved a little, but she never once sent anyone to plead with anybody.

<!-- P84 -->
That day Bao-yu went to the Naiad's House. The qin table was empty, and the instrument's bag hung on the wall. “Adamantina came to hear you play the other day,” he asked Dai-yu. “How is it that once she left, she simply vanished? She didn't leave a word at the hermitage. I went to look. The other tea vessels are all still on the shelf, but she's taken the green jade bowl she drank from herself.” Dai-yu sat beneath the window arranging an old roll of music. At this she gave a cold smile. “If she meant to go, why leave anything written?” Bao-yu hastened to smile. “She told you she was leaving, but not where she was going. That hardly counts as saying goodbye.” “If kindred understanding depends on a spoken goodbye,” Dai-yu said, “it is no kindred understanding at all.” Bao-yu sat by the table, picked up the roll and turned a couple of pages. “What did the two of you play that day, you Bo Ya and Ziqi?” he asked with a smile. Dai-yu spat lightly. “If you put it like that, Ziqi has gone, and I, as Bo Ya, can only break my strings.” Then she added, “The last piece was ‘Thoughts of the Worthy.’” “‘Lament for Yan Hui’? What did she say?” Dai-yu looked out of the window. “She said it was too sorrowful. But the music is the music. If the listener hears sorrow in it, that is hardly the player's fault.” Bao-yu thought for a while and asked again, “When they say the qin and se sound in harmony, does that mean the two instruments must be tuned to one note?” Dai-yu turned and looked at him. “You can hear their harmony only because each has its own strings. If you twist all the strings of one to match the other, there may be no discord, but there is only one voice left.” Bao-yu rested his hand on the music. After a long silence he said, “What if other people tune the strings for them?” “Other people can only listen,” Dai-yu said. “How can they tune them in their place?” Slowly she rolled up the music. “But this harmony is very hard to speak of. Those who can harmonize may not be able to form a pair.” Bao-yu said nothing. For a moment the room was still. Dai-yu put the music in its case and pressed the clasp. She had to press twice before it shut. “Rest now, cousin,” Bao-yu said. “I'll come again tomorrow.” “It's windy outside,” Dai-yu said. He answered and went out.
