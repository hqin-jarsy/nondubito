# Four Variables Arrive Together

## 1. A measurement that could not be made

18 October 1968. Mexico City. The men's long-jump final.

For Beamon's jump, the wind reading was exactly two metres per second, the upper limit for record eligibility. The distance was 8.90 metres.

The jump exceeded the range of the apparatus then in use. The optical measuring device's rail was not long enough to reach the landing point. Officials reverted to an old-fashioned steel tape and took about twenty minutes to establish the official distance.

It is an apt opening because it gives the essay's entire problem a visible form.

An apparatus built for precision encountered something too large to measure and had to retreat to the tape. The device represented the greatest precision this construct could achieve in 1968. It failed at the moment of greatest need, not because it broke, but because the jump exceeded its design range.

There is an unobtrusive irony here. Designers choose an instrument's range from the range of previous performances. The length of that rail contained the farthest anyone had jumped, plus a reasonable margin. Beamon jumped beyond the margin, beyond the boundary of possibility drawn by all previous history. The apparatus could not measure him not because it was poorly made, but because it had been made to fit the past, and that jump did not belong to the past.

What happened afterward was equally beyond measurement. Not the distance of 8.90 metres established by the tape, but its causes.

More than Beamon arrived that day. Four factors had to be considered together: altitude, wind, a new track, and the athlete's own condition. They contributed to the jump, but the surviving evidence does not let us cleanly apportion their contributions. The same meeting also used a new timing apparatus. That changed the recording of sprint results, not the physical distance of a long jump. Later we will see how one institution dealt with these two kinds of problem.

This essay therefore does not try to assign a definitive number to Mexico City's help. The surviving evidence is insufficient for a precise answer independent of a particular model. It asks why the answer remains conditional, and what a construct devoted to precise measurement does when it encounters an event it cannot yet fully explain.

Begin with the four variables.

## 2. One loss, one gain

Mexico City's Olympic athletics venue stood about 2,240 metres above sea level.

Using standard-atmosphere relationships, air density there was roughly 0.98 kilograms per cubic metre, about eighty per cent of sea-level density. Atmospheric pressure was close to seventy-six per cent of standard sea-level pressure.

Those two figures contain the division running through the entire meeting.

Thin air has two consequences, pointing in opposite directions. One is reduced aerodynamic resistance. For a fast-moving body, drag falls as air density falls, so thin air helps sprinting and jumping. The other is less oxygen. Maximum oxygen uptake declines with altitude, so thin air impedes endurance performance.

The same condition is a gain for one family of events and a loss for another.

Both consequences involve the same air molecules, yet point in opposite directions because they serve different processes. Drag concerns how a moving body passes through air; oxygen concerns how the body obtains energy from it. Thin air clears a path for the first and creates a shortage for the second. A meeting puts the 100-metre runner and the 10,000-metre runner beneath the same sky, but that sky means completely different things to them. The sky is not playing favourites. The events ask different things of it.

This had been calculated before the Games. Pugh's work published in 1965 emphasized that altitude impaired aerobic performance because maximal oxygen uptake declined, while reduced air density lowered drag. Before competition began, someone had already predicted that results would split by event family: faster sprints and jumps, slower endurance events.

The meeting's performances enacted that prediction.

In the sprints, Smith ran the 200 metres in 19.83, a world record. Evans ran 43.86 in the 400, a world record and the first time anyone had broken forty-four seconds. Hemery's 48.12 in the 400-metre hurdles broke the pre-Games record of 48.8. In the long jump, Beamon sent the old record far into the distance.

In endurance, Keino's 3:34.91 for 1,500 metres was an Olympic record but slower than the pre-Games world record of 3:33.1. The official account of the 10,000 metres gives a halfway split of 15:00.6, slower than the pace of the 1924 Olympic final.

Throwing occupied an intermediate position. Matson's 20.54-metre shot put was below the existing world record of 21.78. Lūsis's 90.10-metre javelin throw was an Olympic record and a very large throw, but not the explosive breakthrough seen in sprinting and jumping.

Arrange the results by event and a clear boundary appears. Thin air did not improve everything uniformly. It raised some performances and depressed others, with the dividing line falling precisely where Pugh's calculations had placed it.

For the construct, this was awkward. The record system assumed that performances in the same event could be compared across venues. Mexico City demonstrated that changing altitude shifted different events in opposite directions and by different amounts. The venue was no negligible background. It was a variable systematically rewriting performances in event-specific ways. A construct founded on comparability encountered comparability's physical limits.

This is the essential point about altitude. It is not an advantage in one direction. It cuts both ways, depending on whether an event chiefly battles air resistance or consumes oxygen.

## 3. The dispute began with the choice of host

These physical relationships were not discovered in 1968. They provoked controversy from the day Mexico City was selected in 1963.

Contemporary reporting shows particular concern among coaches and sports physicians from low-altitude countries about endurance events and acclimatization. The IOC did not change the venue.

There were genuine proposals before the Games to move events to sea level. One influential contemporary report described British and Belgian sporting figures suggesting that endurance events be relocated, expressly mentioning coastal Veracruz as the kind of alternative they envisaged. This establishes that relocation was discussed, although it never became IOC policy.

National Olympic and sports-medicine research institutions also mobilized. The clearest example recovered in this research is the British Olympic Association's 1965 Mexico altitude research project, preserved in Olympic and BOA archival catalogues. It is strong evidence that the altitude problem had become institutional work, not merely newspaper discussion.

We must distinguish what is established from what is not.

The BOA project existed, broad pre-Games anxiety existed, and relocation discussions existed. What cannot be established here is the complete archive of medical advice submitted by every country to the IOC, or the full original trail of every formal relocation proposal. The concerns are certain. A comprehensive comparative inventory of national submissions was not recovered.

One feature of the gap deserves attention. Opposition chiefly came from low-altitude countries, while the host supporting the Games as planned was itself on a plateau. National positions on altitude may therefore have followed the altitude of the countries concerned, just as in Essay 04 the strongest defender of an event was the country that had benefited most. Positions probably followed gains and losses here too, but establishing that requires the missing complete inventory. It can be proposed as a possibility, not asserted as a finding.

Scholars differ on how far science reassured the IOC beforehand. One interpretation says accumulating physiological research made the Games appear manageable through acclimatization and planning. Another says the IOC politically absorbed the science without resolving the fairness problem for endurance athletes. Both readings occur in secondary literature.

Pause over the disagreement, because it is a miniature of the essay.

Science did not solve the problem. It was absorbed. Altitude's disadvantage for endurance was a calculable physical fact; Pugh had calculated it. The fact was neither refuted nor removed. It was covered over by a meeting proceeding as planned. When a construct confronts an inconvenient fact already worked out, it need not disprove it. It can continue operating and leave the fact where it stands.

This will recur more clearly in the essay's second half.

## 4. Four variables, none removable

Now place the four variables against Beamon's jump.

First, altitude reduces air resistance, which acts both during approach acceleration and during flight. Altitude therefore influences approach and flight alike.

Second, wind. The legal following wind that day was two metres per second. Wind also acts during approach and flight.

Altitude and wind create a crucial attribution problem: both influence speed and distance through aerodynamic drag, and their effects interact. But sharing a mechanism does not make two variables necessarily collinear in the data. Air density and the athlete's speed relative to the air are distinct quantities in the drag relationship; suitable observations and a model can in principle distinguish their effects. The difficulty is that this particular historical jump left insufficient evidence. One study estimates that at roughly 2,250 metres, the altitude advantage for the 100 metres is approximately equivalent to a legal tailwind of two metres per second. That sprint-model comparison suggests that neither influence should be ignored; it does not establish equal contributions to Beamon's long jump.

This attribution problem deserves a closer look. A jump's final distance carries no labels showing which centimetres came from altitude and which from wind. Separating those influences requires observations of the movement, comparison conditions and an explicit model, not simply more decimal places in the final distance. Even when two factors work along related pathways, that alone does not establish that they can never be distinguished. What is missing here is the evidence needed to test a decomposition of that particular jump.

Third, the track. It was an all-weather polyurethane surface replacing the earlier cinders. It changed the approach and take-off without directly changing aerodynamics. Synthetic surfacing gave more secure traction, reduced speed lost during ground contact, and removed many uncertainties of cinders. Unlike the first two variables, it acted between athlete and ground, not athlete and air.

Fourth, the athlete's own condition: competitive form, arousal, execution on the day. These affect everything and cannot be observed mechanically in full.

None of these four variables can be lifted out of the jump and weighed separately.

The difficulty is not simply that the final distance was recorded too coarsely. Altitude and wind both influence drag; the runway changes the approach, which feeds through take-off into flight; and the athlete's condition runs through the movement. Attribution needs a model of these processes and evidence suited to it. Measuring 8.90 metres more precisely cannot by itself recover that evidence. But this difficulty does not establish that no possible observations or experimental design could ever distinguish the factors.

Precision and identifiability are different deficiencies, and distinguishing them matters throughout this essay. Precision concerns how finely a quantity is measured. Identifiability asks whether, given stated assumptions and data, the target effect can be uniquely determined. A more precise tape cannot substitute for evidence about the movement and causal assumptions. More suitable data, different comparisons or experimental designs can sometimes improve identification. They cannot make the unrecorded full process of that 1968 jump happen again.

A distinction central to the essay must be explicit.

Modelability does not mean that a model is adequately supported by the evidence. Modelling makes mechanisms and assumptions explicit and calculates from them. Identification asks whether the target effect is uniquely determined by those stated assumptions together with the available data. Beamon's jump can be modelled, and different studies have produced conditional estimates. The surviving material does not precisely reconstruct the four contributions as one uniquely established set of numbers. A computable estimate must be distinguished from an allocation determined by the evidence of that historical jump.

Nor were the necessary observations preserved in 1968. Today we lack Beamon's complete take-off kinematics, a precise wind field across the whole runway during the jump, shoe–surface interaction variables, and the local gust structure over the board and sandpit at that instant. These are exactly what a modern causal model would most want. Their absence is a central reason that a four-variable decomposition remains unresolved.

## 5. How large the jump was depends on the question

Attempts to decompose Beamon's jump have yielded not one agreed value, but a range of estimates.

Ward-Smith's frequently cited 1986 analysis directly modelled altitude and wind for Beamon. Later aerodynamic work summarized the combined benefit of legal tailwind and altitude as approximately thirty-one centimetres. Popular and semi-technical accounts offer a wider range of supposed sea-level, still-air equivalents. These are model outputs, not measurements.

Public reception almost always loses the distinction. A concrete number such as thirty-one centimetres reads like a measured conclusion, as though someone had actually measured how much altitude and wind jointly helped. In fact it is a calculation under assumptions. Change the assumptions and the number changes. Treating model output as measurement is the commonest misunderstanding in these arguments. It makes something fundamentally undetermined appear settled. The definiteness of the number conceals the uncertainty of the assumptions.

Estimates differ for reasons deeper than disagreement over numerical size. They also differ over the unit of comparison.

One approach asks: with the same athlete in the same condition making the same jump, how far would he have gone at sea level in still air?

Another asks: how far did Mexico City increase the probability that this particular athletic state would occur at all?

These are different causal questions. The first already assumes a great deal, imagining a sea-level version of a jump that never happened. The second is almost impossible to answer from surviving data because it concerns the effect of an entire setting on whether a person could enter that state that day.

The existence of both questions reveals something. When a performance cannot be cleanly attributed, the formulation of attribution branches too. We do not simply have one clear question without an answer. Different formulations ask about different effects and need different evidence. Neither question is already settled by the surviving material.

Notice that all this inquiry concerns a performance already officially recognized.

Beamon's jump was valid throughout. It remained real. Its wind was legal; it was measured, approved, and entered according to the rules. Coaches, statisticians, and physicists asked how much came from Mexico City's conditions without disturbing its standing in the ledger for a single second.

This is a basic property of the construct, already encountered repeatedly. The ledger records a number, not its causes. Admission of 8.90 metres required a wind reading, measurement, and signatures. It did not require an account of how the number arose. Causes belong outside the ledger, where debate may continue forever while nothing inside moves.

This is a condition of the construct's continued operation, not simply a defect. If a performance had to have its causes decomposed before recognition, scarcely any could be recognized. Behind every performance stand wind, a venue, the athlete's condition, and innumerable inseparable factors. Recording results rather than causes may look crude, but it is what allows bookkeeping to continue in a world of permanently entangled causes. The ability to record is purchased by relinquishing explanation.

Beamon's record lasted until Powell's legal 8.95 metres in 1991: twenty-three years. That is why attribution became a lasting question rather than a passing one. For twenty-three years the mark hung at the top of the table, and no one could say how much belonged to the man and how much to Mexico City that day.

## 6. The governing body solved another problem

What the construct subsequently did is among the most revealing features of the story.

It did not regulate altitude away.

The longstanding policy of world athletics is that altitude performances retain record eligibility. An official record compilation explains that the federation makes no distinction between performances at high- and low-altitude venues, although statisticians privately maintain unofficial low-altitude lists because altitude can materially assist sprinting and jumping.

Two ledgers appear. The official table makes no altitude distinction. The statisticians' unofficial low-altitude list separates out high-altitude marks. They coexist, without the second receiving the first's official status. Essay 04 has just shown parallel accounts; here is another version. Practitioners supplement the construct's formal accounts with a shadow ledger recording precisely what the formal accounts decline to distinguish.

The shadow ledger's mode of existence deserves thought. Statisticians maintain it without official standing, yet continue to maintain it, showing that a substantial group needs it. They need it because they think the official account omits something real: altitude helped. The official ledger's completeness is compensated for by people keeping another alongside it. The more firmly a construct insists on the purity of its own book, the more such shadow accounts grow around it to shelter realities it declines to recognize.

Whether the federation ever came close to formally establishing a separate high-altitude record book is less clear than many later summaries suggest. Unofficial low-altitude progressions certainly existed because official policy continued approving altitude marks. What the primary material recovered here does not securely establish is an itemized history of a formal proposal nearing adoption, followed by the exact debate rejecting it. The policy outcome is certain. The complete proposal history is more fragmented in public sources than many overviews imply.

What the construct actually did lay elsewhere.

By the mid-1970s the federation selected another boundary: stricter timing rules rather than a separate altitude ledger. World Athletics's historical milestones record that in 1976 the federation agreed to accept only automatic timing for sprint world records.

That is a decisive archival clue.

Faced with the conditions affecting comparability in 1968, the governing body later refined a timing standard that could be enforced consistently. Legislation cannot change altitude itself, but it can restrict venue eligibility, separate record categories, or continue admitting altitude performances. Wind already had a threshold, while synthetic tracks were becoming standard. The institution chose one workable route among these possibilities. The outcome shows what it addressed; it does not by itself establish every motive behind that choice.

So it regulated timing.

The pattern deserves a full statement. Faced with several difficulties, an institution may find it easier to concentrate on apparatus it can control consistently. But timing was not the fourth factor in the account of Beamon's jump; that was the athlete's condition. Accurate timing matters greatly to sprinting. It did not alter this jump's physical distance. One meeting brought both kinds of problem together. Standardizing one did not disentangle the causes of the other.

A possible institutional tendency appears, not confined to athletics. Faced with more and less tractable problems, an institution may concentrate attention and resources on the former and refine its work there. Its capabilities may grow asymmetrically, well developed in controllable directions and incomplete elsewhere. Observers can mistake the direction of greatest effort for the most important one, though it may simply be where effort can most readily take effect. That is an interpretation open to further examination, not an inevitable law of all institutions.

Controllability and importance are different things. For Beamon's jump, altitude, wind and runway conditions helped shape the performance; timing addressed the recording of results in other events. Making those records more reliable did not explain how this distance arose, nor does that make accurate timing unimportant. The question is not whether the institution selected the wrong uniquely correct priority. It is how precision in one direction can be mistaken for an answer to the whole group of problems.

The next essay concerns its solution to timing, and the remainders that solution created.

## 7. A precedent that counted

High-altitude record eligibility was no empty provision. Concrete precedents established it.

In the men's 100-metre progression, official or highly authoritative record histories include Hines's 9.95 in Mexico City and Calvin Smith's later 9.93 at Colorado Springs, both high-altitude venues. Those precedents are precisely why statisticians maintained unofficial low-altitude lists.

Hines's 9.95 carried another meaning. It is a repeatedly cited milestone in men's 100-metre history, one of the official markers of the first sub-ten-second breakthrough under formal conditions. That milestone was achieved in thin air. Some of the construct's most treasured breakthroughs occurred under conditions known to help, and it chose to accept them in full. Their official standing did not disappear because of altitude. Recording a condition and withdrawing eligibility are different things; the decision to retain the records cannot be traced to one unique motive from its outcome alone.

The precedents mean that altitude marks were not grudgingly tolerated. They were fully accepted official records. Hines's 9.95 was a genuine world record despite having been run in thin air, and despite everyone knowing that thin air helped.

For other high-altitude venues, including Johannesburg, Nairobi, El Paso, and Denver, this research did not recover equally strong ratification evidence for each city separately. The principle can be stated confidently: altitude marks are record-eligible, and the principle has been applied in practice. Venue-by-venue precedent files remain incomplete in the primary materials assembled here.

A connection to earlier essays should be marked.

The first record table of 1914 admitted older marks achieved across twenty years under different conditions at different venues. It was internally uneven from birth. Hines's 9.95 and a sea-level 9.95 could occupy the same 100-metre progression while everyone knew they were not the same thing. The table chose not to distinguish. The price was that no adjacent pair of performances came with guaranteed comparability.

This runs opposite to the decathlon–heptathlon example at the end of Essay 04. There, maintaining comparability within each table required refusing to merge them, purchasing internal integrity through incommensurability. Here, maintaining a single table required relinquishing comparability within it, purchasing an undivided list with incomparable conditions.

The approaches look contradictory but reveal different trade-offs: separating unlike events in one case, admitting performances at different altitudes to one progression in the other. Converting every altitude to an equivalent performance would require additional models and conventions. Simply separating high- and low-altitude lists would still require choosing a boundary and accepting its new problems. Keeping one official progression is one possible arrangement, not proof that every alternative is logically unavailable.

## 8. All contributed, and cannot be separated

Let us bring the essay together.

Two popular claims about Beamon's jump both outrun the evidence.

One says that Mexico City made the jump. That is too strong. In the same final, Beer won silver with 8.19 metres and Boston bronze with 8.16. They used the same runway, the same altitude, and legal wind conditions, but only one man jumped anything close to 8.90. If Mexico City made the jump, why did it not make the other two men's? The setting was available to all. The jump occurred once.

The comparison supports insufficiency: the same venue and altitude did not make everyone jump 8.90 metres. It does not by itself establish that these conditions were counterfactually necessary, or that Beamon could not have reached that distance elsewhere. There is reason to investigate how the environment helped him, but other athletes' performances are not the missing controlled version of his jump. What survives is a performance produced by athlete and setting together, not four measured percentages.

The opposite claim says conditions were irrelevant because everyone used the same runway at the same altitude. That is also too strong. The essay has explained an altitude advantage estimated for sprinting as equivalent to a two-metre-per-second tailwind, while Beamon also had a real tailwind of that strength. Calling these irrelevant ignores physics.

The durable historical truth is narrower and stronger than either simplification: all four variables mattered, and the data do not permit clean separation.

This leaves an explanation unfinished, but not one awaiting a more precise tape. Two facts must be held together: the jump really happened, and its causes cannot simply be read off the final distance. Models can continue to investigate it. Their assumptions, gaps in the evidence and unresolved questions must travel with their estimates.

Earlier essays often located the remainder outside the construct: names absent from a list, discarded timing readings, performances not retrospectively recognized. They were outside the ledger, but individually clear. A name was a name; a reading was a reading.

The remainder in Beamon's jump is different. It is not outside the ledger: the jump stood legitimately at its summit. It concerns the causes of that number. Altitude, wind, runway and the athlete's condition did not exist as four separate lengths; they acted together in one jump. The surviving evidence does not uniquely reconstruct their interactions as four contributions already measured apart. Further research remains possible. The official performance simply does not arrive with a completed causal allocation.

Set this beside earlier remainders to see its distinctiveness. Earlier, the remainder concerned a construct's relationship with what it missed, outside a clear boundary. Here it lies within, inside the entry of which the construct is proudest. It is not an excluded other. It is the unexplained part of a cherished performance itself. The construct can exclude something from its accounts. It cannot untangle the causes inside a performance already entered. The more solemnly it records the mark, the more tightly it embraces that tangle.

This is a new form of remainder. Earlier remainders waited outside because the construct had failed to receive them. Here it received, recorded and approved the performance without thereby explaining the four interacting influences on the number. The construct can hold the performance in its ledger. It cannot therefore claim already to possess its final explanation.

Return to the measuring apparatus unable to reach the landing point.

It represented the construct's peak precision in 1968, yet retreated to a steel tape before that jump. The tape established 8.90 metres as the official measurement. That tape, and the timing and measuring instruments subsequently refined, directly recorded performances, not itemized causes. Physical models can still offer conditional estimates of why the jump went so far. They cannot turn an unrecorded process into four contributions already measured separately. Four factors met in one action. The institution could record the result without thereby closing the account of its explanation.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
