# A System Must Classify to Operate

## I. A decision that must precede the race

Before a women's event begins, something must already have happened. The organizers must have decided who may enter the category.

The decision cannot wait until afterward. Entry, call-room procedures, record ratification, and medal allocation all depend on it. Before the starting gun sounds, everyone on the line has already been judged eligible to stand there. The women's category is, first of all, an eligibility classification: a binary, enforceable decision that must be made before competition.

This essay concerns that decision, and the repeated replacement over sixty years of the line on which it rests.

Essay 13 concerned a construct unable to observe the counterfactual body with biological limbs directly. It still had to assess indirect evidence and allocate the risk of insufficient proof. Here the problem is adjacent but different. The construct faces not an absent fact but a classification it must make. The physiological reality from which its criteria are drawn is continuous in some respects, overlapping, and jointly determined by multiple factors; it cannot be divided cleanly by a single proxy.

The essay's purpose and limits should be clear.

It does not decide which side is right or offer an ultimate definition of womanhood. It records several approaches to women’s eligibility over roughly sixty years, organized below into seven historical groupings. They involve different governing bodies and include returning or combined criteria, not seven neat, successive replacements by one institution. The important point is that many changes altered what was examined, rather than merely measuring the same object more accurately.

The historical changes can be checked, while their grouping remains an editorial arrangement. Whatever the reader’s position, each criterion, governing body and scope can be examined. The essay concerns the institutional predicament shown by those changes, not a tidy count standing in for the history itself.

This distinction governs the essay's restraint. Someone may firmly believe that the women's category needs a clear boundary, or firmly believe that the current boundary is misplaced. Those positions, and the many between them, are not adjudicated here. The subject is the difficulty shared by attempts to draw any such boundary: a clear, enforceable, pre-competition eligibility decision must be derived from physiological traits that are not all reducible to one perfectly separating measurement. That difficulty confronts every position.

## II. Seven different criteria

First set out the sequence on which the essay rests.

Around 1966, some international competitions introduced physician-conducted visual, physical, or gynaecological examinations. The criterion was bodily appearance.

In 1967 international athletics began sex-chromatin screening, the Barr-body method. Cells from the lining of the mouth were examined for chromatin associated with an inactivated sex chromosome. The criterion shifted from appearance to a cellular chromosome marker.

In the 1990s the IOC adopted polymerase chain reaction testing for sequences specific to a sex chromosome, commonly targeting a sex-determining gene sequence or other specific DNA. The criterion shifted from sex chromatin to a particular genetic sequence.

In 2011 the IAAF introduced eligibility regulations for women with hyperandrogenism. The criterion shifted to serum testosterone together with assessment of androgen sensitivity.

In 2018 the rules targeted particular differences of sex development. The conditions included specified bodily conditions, testosterone at or above a threshold, and sufficient androgen sensitivity. The criterion narrowed from high testosterone generally to a particular group of bodily conditions.

In 2023 the testosterone ceiling was lowered further, and regulated events expanded from selected middle distances to all world-ranking competitions. The parallel transgender rules ceased to depend only on years of testosterone suppression and instead considered whether the athlete had undergone male puberty. Developmental history entered the criteria.

In 2025 World Athletics introduced a one-time genetic-sequence test in place of the previously separate DSD and transgender eligibility regimes. The sequence was SRY. The criterion had come back to a genetic sequence, this time as a unified preliminary screen.

Appearance, sex chromatin, a particular genetic sequence, testosterone with androgen sensitivity, specified DSD conditions, pubertal history, and genetic screening again: seven useful historical groupings, not seven entirely different, non-repeating single measurements. They show how eligibility systems replace, combine or return to different bases.

This history is not merely refinement of a single test. Some changes adjusted an existing threshold, some retained or combined conditions, and others changed what was examined. Appearance, chromatin, testosterone and pubertal history are not the same quantity. The continued existence of the category does not establish unchanged eligibility criteria; neither does change mean that every step replaced the object entirely.

The distinction has practical consequences. The new genetic screen does not measure testosterone or chromatin more precisely; it uses another marker. Regulations may nevertheless retain developmental or functional conditions alongside it. A future change therefore calls for separate questions: does it improve an assay, adjust a threshold or replace a proxy? The construct sometimes refines a route and sometimes changes it. Not every change is automatically scientific progress, nor is every change merely retracing its steps.

This connects with the decathlon table in Essay 7, but more sharply. There, the same question, how to convert performances across events, had received five different answers. Here, the eligibility question had been approached through the historical criteria set out here. The table altered a conversion rule. These changes alter the basis of a person's entrance into competition.

## III. No criterion directly measures its intended object

The scientific limits reveal a recurring feature: none of these criteria directly measures the complete object the rule seeks to judge.

As supporters of the regulations formulate it, that object is whether an athlete has a sufficiently large, event-specific competitive advantage arising from androgen effects associated with male puberty.

Each criterion measures something else.

The Barr-body test identifies visible chromatin associated with an inactive sex chromosome. It measures neither athletic ability, gonadal function, nor androgen action. Some women without Barr bodies could fail an initial screen, while some individuals with Barr bodies who had experienced substantial androgen effects could pass. It was neither a reliable standalone test of womanhood nor a test of athletic advantage. A 1991 paper estimated that such screening might unjustly exclude approximately one in 504 female athletes.

PCR identifies a particular genetic sequence. It is more direct in that analytical sense, but cannot alone determine whether the sequence functions normally, whether the body responds to androgens, what phenotypic changes occurred at puberty, or the size of any advantage in a particular event. Greater sensitivity for detecting DNA does not settle how the result should translate into eligibility. At Atlanta 1996, 3,387 women athletes were screened; eight initial results required review, and all eight athletes were ultimately allowed to compete. Detection of chromosome-specific material did not automatically mean ineligibility.

Testosterone appears closer to the physiological mechanism, but remains an incomplete proxy. It does not directly measure muscle response to androgens, pubertal development, or event performance. An analysis of post-competition samples from 693 elite athletes found overlapping male and female testosterone distributions under particular measurement conditions and thresholds. That challenges the absolute proposition that no male and female single serum values can ever overlap. It does not establish each person's androgen responsiveness, pubertal history, or performance advantage within the overlapping range.

The latest genetic criterion makes the separation from individual advantage particularly visible. The first gate asks whether the target sequence is detected, rather than measuring current testosterone or estimating personal competitive advantage. A test can answer the sequence-presence question with high analytical accuracy without estimating athletic performance with equal accuracy. The rules themselves need an exception for complete androgen insensitivity, demonstrating that a genetic marker and functional androgen action do not correspond one to one.

The criteria therefore share a property. They measure identifiable traits, appearance, chromatin, genes, hormones, while none directly measures the full event-specific advantage at issue. Each is a proxy: a measurable substitute for something harder to measure. A gap remains between proxy and target.

That gap is not simply a temporary deficiency in science. It has a structural source. A sufficiently large advantage in a particular event is not a single quantity captured by one measurement. It results from interacting factors: pubertal development, muscle response to hormones, skeletal and cardiopulmonary structures, and the conversion of those features into performance in a specific event. A criterion based on appearance, genes, or hormones can capture only part of that chain. Using one link as a proxy for the whole necessarily omits others. A better assay alone cannot remove a gap rooted in the target's composite nature.

## IV. The line was not calculated from an effect size

During the testosterone-based period, the gap took a concrete form: how was the threshold chosen?

The 2011 regulations did not convert a fixed percentage advantage into one publicly derived numerical cutoff. They used the normal male range as a basic boundary, supplemented by androgen sensitivity. The threshold came from clinical reference ranges and category concepts, not a calculation of how many seconds each additional unit of testosterone would produce.

The 2018 rules specified a number. The IAAF described it as an enforceable boundary clearly above the levels of the great majority of women without DSD and below the usual male range, allowing room for measurement error and individual fluctuation. In the arbitration evidence, experts described a low range for women without polycystic ovary syndrome and a much higher male range. The chosen number was neither the arithmetic midpoint nor a mathematical output of the event study's effect sizes.

That is the section's central point. No public dose-response model demonstrated a necessary minimum percentage advantage above the cutoff and its absence just below. The applicants' experts expressly challenged this in arbitration. The institutional response relied principally on reference ranges, androgen physiology, and administrative workability. The threshold can therefore be described as a normative and clinical boundary, but not as a precise calculation from the reported effects of roughly one to four percent.

The number was lowered in 2023. Official explanations placed the new standard closer to the upper end of the usual female reference range. The published rules specified obligations and time limits but supplied no calculation mapping the new value to a particular percentage change in competition performance. Nor did a public model explain why identical hormone values should apply across every athletics event.

With the 2025 genetic criterion, the same issue continued in another form. The assay's accuracy is analytical: it answers whether the sequence is present. It does not establish a quantitative relation between sequence detection and an individual's advantage in a particular event. A continuous hormone value became a presence-or-absence genetic screen, without filling the missing derivation of performance effect.

The same line runs through earlier essays. Essay 7 found no complete public derivation of decathlon coefficients. Essay 12 found none for the forty-millimetre shoe limit. In Essay 13, overall net prosthetic advantage was a legal standard applied to evidence, not a directly measured variable. Here the testosterone thresholds were not calculated from a demonstrated event effect size.

Four precise boundaries, none uniquely located by the kind of derivation one might expect. This is not simply one institution's oversight. It is the common condition of boundaries imposed on continuous effects without a natural point at which everything changes from acceptable to unacceptable. Such lines can be explained and defended without being proved uniquely necessary.

## V. A population difference that cannot be assigned to an individual

The essay offers no final verdict. That does not mean withholding the strongest arguments on either side. This section and the next set them alongside one another without deciding between them.

Begin with a fact both sides recognize but use differently.

Across many strength, speed, and endurance events, adult elite male and female performances differ by roughly ten to twelve percent. Supporters of the rules identify puberty-related testosterone effects on muscle mass, haemoglobin, skeletal development, and cardiopulmonary systems as major mechanisms. This population-level gap supplies a central empirical rationale for the women's category.

The shared starting point has an important limitation: the overall gap does not directly establish a ten-to-twelve-percent advantage for any particular woman.

Populations and individuals are different objects. A twelve-percent average difference does not imply that every member of one group is twelve percent faster than every member of another. Individuals vary and distributions can overlap. Moving from a population difference to the advantage of a particular person requires a further step the aggregate number cannot itself supply.

Consider how each side uses that limitation.

Supporters argue that precisely because no accurate individual-percentage test is available for every athlete, a manageable category rule is necessary. Eligibility requires an enforceable pre-competition decision, while scientific variables are often continuous, overlapping, and imperfect. Every boundary produces borderline cases. The institution therefore argues that it need not prove an identical advantage for every affected athlete in every race; it must show that the category-level proxy is reasonable, the evidence adequate, and the procedure consistent.

Opponents argue that the general male–female gap cannot simply be assigned to particular women. Serum testosterone is not a complete proxy for muscle responsiveness, pubertal development, or event performance. Athletes with DSD form a very small sample, and key studies are observational or conducted in populations without DSD. The rule should demonstrate that the regulated characteristic produces an advantage sufficiently large, event-specific, and reproducible to justify exclusion, rather than infer it from general male data.

Both arguments begin with the same population gap and the same limit on assigning it to individuals. They reach opposite conclusions. One says: because individual advantage cannot be measured accurately, categories are necessary. The other says: because individual advantage cannot be measured accurately, categories inferred from group data should not exclude individuals.

The essay does not decide which response is correct. Both are responses to the same limitation.

Why does that matter? Their dispute is not primarily over whether the overall gap exists. It concerns what should happen when a well-established population fact cannot be allocated precisely to individuals. One response retreats to categories as administratively workable. The other rejects category-based exclusion where an individual's circumstances may differ greatly from the average. These are attitudes toward uncertainty, not merely conflicting factual measurements. The disagreement arises where scientific description has ended and a decision is still required. No higher measurement automatically resolves that choice.

## VI. Evidence and coverage that do not line up

Disagreement over the specific science is equally clear. One mismatch is particularly concrete.

A central study supporting the regulations was published in 2017. It analysed more than two thousand athlete observations from two World Athletics Championships, grouped male and female athletes separately by free testosterone, and compared performances. In several women's events, the higher-testosterone group performed approximately one to four percent better than the lower group.

This was observational competition-data analysis, not a randomized experiment. It attracted several kinds of criticism.

One concerned data quality. Researchers checking public data identified duplicate records, impossible or erroneous performances, and opaque revisions. Arbitration evidence also recorded substantial overlap of athletes between the championships, some samples that could not be classified, and the absence of separate analysis of doping effects. Researchers supporting the rules corrected some errors but denied that they overturned the overall relationships.

Another criticism deserves particular attention.

The study's significant findings included certain middle-distance events, hammer throw, and pole vault. The 2018 regulations covered events from 400 metres to one mile but excluded hammer and pole vault. Conversely, the original study did not report corresponding significant differences for 1,500 metres and the mile, both covered by the rule.

The study said to support the rule and the events actually regulated did not line up. Some events showing an effect were excluded; some included events lacked a corresponding significant result in that study.

This was not only an external allegation. The arbitral tribunal recorded the concern. The majority upheld the regulations but specifically questioned the evidence for 1,500 metres and the mile. Supporters said event coverage also reflected physiology, adjacent distances, and other evidence. Critics argued that this retrospective selection weakened any claim that the rule followed directly from the study.

This connects with Essay 11, with its qualification intact. A participant in the javelin committee identified clear landings as the primary goal, while distance reduction also shaped the specification; one change serving several purposes does not leave every motive unknowable. Likewise, a mismatch in event coverage does not by itself refute the eligibility rule’s reasons. It identifies choices between research findings and regulatory scope that still require explanation.

The gap need not mean deception. More plausibly, a rule rarely follows in a straight line from one study. General physiology, analogy with neighbouring events, administrative convenience, and negotiation also shape it. Research is one input. A mismatch need not show deliberate distortion; it may show that the rule was never research's direct product, despite presentations suggesting otherwise. The decathlon coefficients in Essay 7 offered a similar lesson: something precise and scientific-looking may have more mixed origins than its surface reveals. The appearance of precision can conceal the gap precisely where an exact justification is most needed.

Other evidence remained contested. A randomized trial gave non-elite young women testosterone for ten weeks and found improved running time to exhaustion, but no significant between-group difference in some strength and power measures. Supporters regarded randomization as stronger causal evidence than observational work. Critics noted the small sample, non-elite participants, short intervention, use of time to exhaustion rather than race time, and inability of short-term administration to healthy women to reproduce the lifelong development of athletes with DSD.

The competing evidence stands side by side. Neither side has completely displaced the other. This essay does not supply an adjudication the evidence has not itself supplied.

## VII. Four judicial stages, different questions

Semenya's case passed through four judicial stages. Understanding what each considered helps explain why this essay does not pronounce on the ultimate category question.

The first was CAS. In 2019 its majority held that the 2018 regulations were discriminatory but, on the evidence then available, necessary, reasonable, and proportionate to protecting the women's competitive category. It dismissed the challenge. At the same time, it expressed serious concerns about fair implementation, athletes' ability to maintain the threshold, medication side effects, confidentiality, and the evidence for 1,500 metres and the mile.

Be clear about what it did. It asked whether the available evidence justified upholding this rule. It did not establish that every affected athlete necessarily had the same advantage, or offer a formula converting personal testosterone values into seconds. It expressly allowed that implementation proving impossible or excessively difficult could change the proportionality assessment. It adjudicated a particular regulation under the evidence before it, not a universal definition of womanhood.

The second stage was the Swiss Federal Supreme Court. In 2020 it dismissed the application to set the award aside. Its review was exceptionally narrow. The question was not a fresh assessment of all scientific evidence but whether the award violated Swiss public policy under a demanding threshold. It confirmed that the award could not be annulled on the limited grounds available under Swiss arbitration law. It did not independently verify every scientific conclusion.

The third and fourth stages were the European Court of Human Rights. In 2023 a Chamber found, by four votes to three, that Switzerland had violated the Convention. The Swiss government's referral request was accepted, so the Chamber judgment did not become final.

On 10 July 2025 the Grand Chamber delivered its final judgment. By fifteen votes to two it found a violation of Article 6 §1, the right to a fair hearing. Semenya had effectively been compelled to accept CAS's exclusive jurisdiction; the dispute implicated fundamental personal and bodily rights; and the Swiss court's exceptionally narrow public-policy review had not provided adequate safeguards.

This stage is especially easy to misread.

The Grand Chamber found a fair-hearing violation by Switzerland. It also held the discrimination, private-life, and effective-remedy complaints inadmissible for lack of territorial jurisdiction. That was a jurisdictional ruling, not a merits finding that the DSD rule was non-discriminatory. The Grand Chamber did not annul the CAS award, invalidate the athletics regulations, or directly grant Semenya eligibility for women's events.

The stages therefore addressed different questions. CAS considered the rule's validity on the evidence before it. The Swiss court considered whether the award could be set aside. The final Strasbourg judgment considered whether Switzerland's judicial procedure was fair. None supplied an abstract, final answer to who ought to be eligible for the women's category. A procedural victory is not the same as a substantive invalidation. The ultimate classification question remained without the kind of final universal judgment the essay is asking about.

This division of work reveals a broader feature. Each institution confined itself to a narrower question within its authority: this regulation, this arbitral award, this judicial procedure. The largest question remained outside that limited adjudication. This is not necessarily evasion. A legal or arbitral body cannot resolve the category's meaning once and for all. Each addresses the portion within its competence while the broader issue remains open.

## VIII. A line drawn through complex physiological variation

Bring the essay together, still without supplying a final answer.

The construct faces a task it must perform but cannot perform without difficulty. Before competition, it must decide who is eligible for the women's category. Competition requires an enforceable binary eligibility outcome. Yet appearance, chromatin, genes, hormones, and pubertal history each capture only part of a multifactorial physiological reality.

This is the remainder, different from every preceding form.

Earlier remainders included omitted names, unresolved causes, techniques not decided by a performance table, unchosen arrangements and unobservable counterfactual bodies. Here it is what a single criterion leaves unanswered about bodies, development and athletic effects. A genetic sequence can yield a categorical presence-or-absence result; hormone levels, functional responses and performance are different variables. Connecting them to eligibility requires evidence and an explanation of the institutional choice.

These changes are not merely successive approaches to one waiting eligibility line. They also concern institutional goals, proxies, enforcement and consequences for individuals. A clear categorical test result does not finish the justification of eligibility; repeated changes do not prove that every boundary lacks empirical grounds either. What no single assay decides is why the boundary ought to fall at this particular place.

This extends the argument of Essay 12. There, continuous footwear advantages did not contain a natural forty-millimetre boundary. Here, the relationship between physiological traits and eligibility likewise does not yield a uniquely compelled cutoff. The difference is the weight of the consequence. A shoe boundary determines whether equipment-related performances count. This boundary affects a person's body, career, dignity, identity, and ability to compete as she understands herself. Moving it carries far greater weight than changing an implement specification.

Precisely because the stakes are so high, restraint matters. The essay could easily become a declaration of allegiance in an already noisy dispute. That is not the series' purpose. It observes what the construct does when closure fails, rather than supplying the judgment the construct cannot finally settle. Here its movement is clear: it must classify; it cannot scientifically derive a uniquely justified eligibility boundary; it repeatedly changes criteria, gives reasons, and faces challenges without reaching an uncontested endpoint. Observing that movement and taking a side are different activities. This essay undertakes the former.

Keep the discipline established at the start: this essay does not decide where the eligibility boundary should fall. It reviews seven historical groupings across organizations and periods. Each approach requires reasons and remains open to questions about evidence, procedure and individual consequences. That history is not seven tidy replacements, and its count supplies no final judgment for either side.

When discussing particular people, the essay relies only on what they have publicly said. One athlete described routine genetic screening in her own 2005 article as an additional barrier to women's entry into sport. Another emphasized in arbitral testimony that she was a woman, not a man. Another argued that her natural body should not exclude her without adequate evidence of advantage. These are publicly expressed positions and experiences, not medical information disclosed by institutions. The essay does not reproduce any medical record the athletes themselves have not made public, just as the tribunals did not reduce the whole question to an answer readable from a single test.

Organizations use differing eligibility arrangements: pubertal history, genetic screening, open categories or continuing revisions. That shows the absence of an agreed institutional answer in practice. It does not logically prove that no uniquely better arrangement could exist. The defensible point is that assays and empirical research do not automatically make every choice among institutional aims, rights and risks.

The construct can measure a person's testosterone, detect a genetic sequence, and estimate a population's average performance gap. It cannot measure the single point at which the women's eligibility boundary ought to be placed. That point is not simply a measurable fact. It is a boundary that must be chosen, whose unique normative justification cannot be established merely by a single assay or by historical changes. Across roughly sixty years, the history above follows several forms of that choice.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
