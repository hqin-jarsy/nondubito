# Making the Incommensurable Commensurable

## I. One Competition, Five Totals

Thorpe's ten performances in the 1912 Stockholm decathlon were these: 11.2 seconds in the hundred metres; 6.79 metres in the long jump; 12.89 metres in the shot put; 1.87 metres in the high jump; 52.2 seconds in the four hundred metres; 15.6 seconds in the 110-metre hurdles; 36.98 metres in the discus; 3.25 metres in the pole vault; 45.70 metres in the javelin; and 4 minutes 40.1 seconds in the fifteen hundred metres.

Those ten numbers have not changed in more than a century. They record what actually happened over those two days, measured by officials, entered in the record, and never subsequently altered.

What changed was the total score.

Under the 1912 table, those performances totalled 8,412.955 points.

Recalculated directly with the 1920 table's formulas, they yield approximately 7,751 points.

Looked up in the printed 1934 table, approximately 6,972.

Looked up in the 1950 table, approximately 6,267.

Calculated with the current formulas in the table used since 1985, 6,564.

The same competition, the same person, ten identical performances. The total falls from 8,412 to 6,267, then rises to 6,564. It loses more than two thousand points, then regains some.

Notice that the curve is not monotonic. It does not move steadily downward or upward, but descends to a trough before rising. This tells us that no single direction governs the rewriting of these tables: they become neither consistently stricter nor consistently more generous. The 1950 table is the least favourable to Thorpe; the current table treats him somewhat more kindly. Each has its own inclinations, with no continuous line connecting them. An athlete's historical score fluctuates through this directionless succession of revisions like a curve being moved by different hands in turn.

The athlete himself has done nothing. He is long dead, those two days long past. None of those ten runs, jumps, and throws can be repeated, nor need they be. What changed was not him, but the ruler used to calculate his total.

The preceding essay argued that every number the construct records is not the event itself, but an imprint left in the ledger after the event passes through a particular apparatus. Whether Hines's run is 9.9 or 9.95 depends on the cloth used to measure it. Here the issue takes a more radical form. A decathlon total depends not on which cloth measures one performance, but on which table converts ten unrelated performances onto the same numerical scale and then adds them together. And that table has been revised repeatedly. The five versions selected above illustrate those changes; they are not an exhaustive history.

First notice the numerical distance between the five totals: more than 2,100 points separate 8,412 from 6,267. This is not a gap between two athletes scored on the same table, so it cannot simply be translated into a difference in competitive ability. Nor does a decathlon ordinarily progress through qualification into a final. The point is that the same athlete's same performances acquire a different total when the scale changes. Nothing has been performed again, yet the number in the ledger has shifted dramatically.

## II. A Table, Not a Measurement

We must first be clear about what this table is.

It is not a natural measurement waiting to be discovered. It is an apparatus constructed by a governing institution to define equivalences between different events. Nature provides no answer to the question of how much faster running counts as equivalent to jumping how many centimetres farther. There is no exchange rate between the hundred metres and the long jump. The table stipulates one.

The official history clearly records repeated committee work, congress adoption, and the replacement of one scheme of equivalences by another. In Prague in 1983, a working group explicitly treated the table as something to be redesigned according to policy objectives, including comparability, applicability at different levels, and future stability.

Its makers, in other words, have never treated the table as a truth, but as a tool. Tools serve purposes. When the purpose changes, or the understanding of it changes, the tool is remade.

In the official record, revision has at least four different meanings.

First, changing the benchmark performances to which scores are anchored. Which performance counts as a thousand points can change.

Second, changing the mathematical form of the conversion. The function relating performance to points can be linear, progressive, strongly progressive, or curved in different directions for different events.

Third, changing whether the table serves only combined events or also general comparisons between disciplines.

Fourth, changing the direction and strength of progression. How many points a fixed improvement earns at a low level, and how many at a high level, can be adjusted.

Each of these four meanings offers an opportunity for rewriting. The table has used them all.

Consider them together and something becomes apparent: almost no part of this table is immovable. Its benchmark performances, mathematical shape, scope, and direction of progression can all change. What remains fixed? Only the shell: converting ten events into points and adding them. Every specific element within that framework is an adjustable parameter. Something whose anchors, shape, scope, and direction can all be altered can hardly be said to measure an objective quantity. It looks more like a convention that can be repeatedly reconfigured.

A popular simplification says decathlon points were settled once and for all in 1912, when someone decided what they should be. This is misleading. Official records show successive institutional rewritings in 1920–21, 1934, 1950–52, 1962, and 1984, followed by several limited adjustments to the tables.

As for who compiled the original 1912 table, no definite answer is available. The accessible formula sheet explains its method and benchmarks but does not reliably identify a sole compiler. Later official histories describe proposals by the organisers, consultation with experts, and the eventual adoption of a linear table. The safest account is that the Stockholm organisers made the initial proposal, consulted experts, and arrived at an adopted linear table. Available primary sources do not justify confidently attributing authorship of the mathematics to one person.

This missing author is more revealing than it may seem. The table is the sport's central rule. It has determined the fate of every decathlete for more than a century, yet we cannot clearly say who first established it. By contrast, the names, performances, and placings of the athletes it scores are all recorded meticulously. The construct records its objects of measurement with extraordinary precision, but records the basis of its own measuring ambiguously. This asymmetry recurs throughout the series. It is especially striking here because what remains ambiguous is the ruler's own origin.

This is the same kind of gap encountered in preceding essays. A construct that demands evidence for every performance lacks sufficient evidence concerning the authorship of its most foundational table.

## III. Every Revision Changes the Component Scores

The clearest way to see how the table changes is to hold an athlete's ten performances constant and vary the table. That is how Thorpe's five totals were produced. More revealing than the changing totals, however, are the changing component scores.

Under the 1912 table, Thorpe's 1.87-metre high jump earned 958 points, and his 15.6-second hurdles earned 943. Both were enormous scores under that table.

It is worth putting 958 high-jump points in perspective. The 1912 table assigned a thousand points to each event's 1908 Olympic record. Thorpe's 1.87 earning 958 meant that on this linear table it was already very close to the Olympic-record level of the time. The same 1.87 is worth only 687 today, not because the height has become lower, but because the benchmarks and conversion have changed. A height close to the Olympic record in 1912 is not thereby equally close to the record today. What this comparison directly shows is that an unchanged height earns fewer points. On its own, it does not establish that high jumping has been devalued against every other event at every performance level.

Under the present table, those same two performances earn 687 for the high jump and 751 for the hurdles.

His pole vault falls hardest. 3.25 metres earned 751.6 points in 1912; under the current formula it earns 418.

751 to 418. The same 3.25 metres. More than three hundred points lost, nearly half its value.

This is not a change in athletic performance but in the conversion apparatus. The 1912 table valued a 3.25-metre vault at about 750 points. The present table values it at just over four hundred. One table's valuation of the same vault is nearly twice the other's.

Which is right? There is no answer, because no objectively correct valuation awaits measurement here. How many points a 3.25-metre vault should earn depends on how much weight the table wants pole vaulting to carry within the ten events. That is a policy choice, not a fact.

There is a directional consequence worth noticing. Thorpe's total fell to its minimum of 6,267 under the 1950 table, then rose to 6,564 under the current one. Rewriting can raise historical performances as well as lower them.

There are other upward examples. C. K. Yang's Olympic silver-medal performance in 1960 is worth 8,010 under a later scoring system. Thompson's winning performance in 1984 earned 8,798 under the table then in force, while current statistical presentations give it 8,847. The same decathlon gains several dozen points when the table changes.

Rewriting therefore does not uniformly devalue the past or uniformly elevate it. It redistributes. Every revision reallocates the points available to the ten events. Applied to a particular historical performance, that redistribution may raise or lower the total depending on the athlete's strengths at the time.

In Thorpe's set of performances, the high jump and hurdles earn fewer points under the current table, and his total falls. A different combination of performances need not change in the same way: the result depends on the actual marks and both scoring curves, not simply on which event is an athlete's strength. Revising the table can therefore change the relative positions of different kinds of athletes in cross-era comparisons.

An unsettling implication follows. When an athlete's career ends, he believes he knows his place in history, because he has a total that can be compared with predecessors and successors. But that place is not fixed. Years later, if past marks are recalculated for cross-era comparison under a new table, his comparative score and those of other athletes may change. His position in that recalculated sequence can rise or fall; the medals and official results of the original event do not thereby change automatically. He cannot determine which scale a later comparison will use. He may no longer be alive. The number into which a lifetime's effort has been compressed is not his to secure. It lies in the hands of those who revise the table afterward.

## IV. The Curves Keep Changing

These five tables differ not only in numerical values. Their shapes have changed fundamentally several times. Those changes record the construct's changing understanding of fairness.

The 1912 table was linear. Each event's 1908 Olympic record was assigned a thousand points. Performances better or worse than it moved up or down from that benchmark at a fixed slope.

What does linear mean? It means that, whatever your level, a fixed improvement earns the same additional points. Improving from very poor to slightly less poor earns as much as improving from very good to even better.

The 1920 table remained linear, merely replacing the 1908 benchmarks with the 1912 Olympic records, still worth a thousand points. The anchor changed, not the shape.

The 1934 table broke with linearity. It came from Finnish work, calculated by J. Ohls in 1931, used in Finland from 1932, and adopted by the IAAF in 1934. It was progressive, pursuing the principle that equally good performances in different disciplines should receive equal scores.

What does progressive mean? The higher the level, the more additional points a fixed improvement earns. A tiny advance at the top is worth much more than the same advance at a low level.

Why move from linear to progressive? Because people recognised that the difficulty of improvement increases with performance level at different rates in different disciplines, and linearity did not reflect this. Progression was an attempt to make points correspond more closely to actual difficulty.

The 1950–52 table retained that philosophy but intensified it considerably. A scoring-table committee was appointed in 1946, and the redesign was undertaken by Sweden's Holmér and Jörbeck. They raised the top of each table to fifteen hundred points, placed a key equivalence at thirteen hundred, and tried to replace some previous expert judgment with empirical curves, particularly curves of world-record running speeds.

A turning point appears here and deserves a pause. The 1962 table reacted against the excessive progression of the 1950–52 men's table. The new men's table adopted a principle already used in the women's tables: moderate progression in track events, moderate regression in field events.

Regression is the opposite of progression. Moderate regression in field events means that, as the level rises, a fixed additional improvement earns fewer points rather than more.

Within just thirty years, then, the construct offered three answers to the same question. Linearity says every step is worth the same. Progression says the higher step is worth more. The regression adopted for field events in 1962 says the higher step is worth less.

None of these answers is wrong, because the question is not factual but political: what does this sport want to reward? Evenly distributed all-round ability, or breakthroughs approaching the human limit in one discipline? Different answers produce differently shaped tables. The construct changed its position three times in thirty years. If past marks are recalculated under each policy, their totals and comparative order can change. That is distinct from revising the original competition results.

Here something general about the construct becomes visible. Many questions it faces appear technical, requiring a correct formula, but are actually questions of value, requiring a choice of position. There is a fundamental difference: technical questions have right and wrong answers; questions of value involve trade-offs. When a construct treats a question of value as technical, it searches for a correct formula. The search never ends, because no correct formula awaits discovery, only choices with different costs. One reason the decathlon table has been revised repeatedly is that it is searching for a correct answer that does not exist.

## V. There Is More Than One Way Even to Ask What Someone Should Score

The table left by the 1984 revision still governs scoring today. Criticism of it exposes the deepest layer of the problem: there is no agreed answer even to how “fairness” should be defined.

The 1984 revision was the decisive modern rewriting. The working group said the new tables should serve combined events alone; comparable performances should earn comparable points; all events should be mildly progressive; and top world-class totals should remain broadly unchanged. The Los Angeles congress adopted the tables for use from 1985.

Notice the working group's definition of fairness. It did not define fairness as equal statistical rarity, equal training investment, or equal physiological cost. Its definition was more of a compromise: comparable performances should receive comparable scores; the table should work from beginners to elites; data from specialist athletes should inform the design, but no individual discipline should overwhelm the combined competition.

That compromise was subsequently criticised as having become unbalanced.

The strongest modern criticism says the 1984 table is now out of balance, not merely old. Some research argues that the system is inherently unstable, structurally favouring certain events, especially the long jump, 110-metre hurdles, and hundred metres, while undervaluing throws and the fifteen hundred metres. The deeper criticism is philosophical: equal points should represent genuinely comparable difficulty.

The difficulty is that “genuinely comparable difficulty” itself has several mutually incompatible meanings.

The main competing doctrines of fairness in the literature are these.

One is equal difficulty: performances equally difficult to achieve in different events should earn the same points.

Another is equal rarity: equally uncommon performances in different events should earn the same points.

Another is equal all-roundness: no event should systematically dominate the total, and scoring should reward breadth.

An older doctrine is an equal law of training, measured by consistency in the progression of training loads. Critics argue that linear or near-linear conversions violate it.

The literature does not converge on any one of these doctrines.

This is the essay's most important point. In preceding essays, a performance could not be causally disentangled, or a number depended on the cloth used to measure it. Those were still cases of a definite question without a definite answer. Here the difficulty goes further: the question itself is not unique. Equal difficulty and equal rarity are different standards that produce different tables. Equal all-roundness is a third; an equal law of training a fourth. Before calling a table fair or unfair, one must choose what fairness means. There is no higher standard that can adjudicate that choice.

This deserves fuller explanation, because it touches a root of the whole series. Measurement presupposes a definite object to be measured. Length can be measured because what length is is clear. But what combined events seek to measure, “all-round ability,” is itself unclear. Is it the average difficulty of ten events, the combined rarity of ten performances, or a balance without specialisation? These candidates differ, and none can be identified as the true all-round ability. The problem is therefore not insufficient accuracy of measurement. It is that the object being measured lacks a definition. The construct assigns precise scores to something ill-defined, and precision conceals the missing definition.

Thus the demand that equal points represent genuinely comparable difficulty sounds clear, but conceals an unanswered question: comparable in what sense? In 1984 the construct chose a compromise definition, not because it had found the correct answer, but because it had to select something workable among standards none of which could be proved.

“Had to select something workable” is the key to the entire table. The construct is not unaware of the different standards of fairness, nor incapable of argument. Its predicament is that whichever it chooses will appear unfair under another, yet it cannot decline to supply a table, because the decathlon cannot proceed unless a total can be calculated. It therefore does not find the correct answer. It picks, among options none of which stands securely, something people can just about accept and that permits the sport to continue. This is not failure. It is the only thing an operational construct can do when faced with an insoluble problem.

## VI. The Coefficients Are Official; No One Can Fully Explain Their Origin

The current men's decathlon uses one form of formula for track events and another for field events. Both have sets of coefficients, and the result is rounded down to a whole number.

The coefficients are public. The three coefficients for every event are printed in the rules.

But there is no official derivation of how they were produced.

No published official derivation has been found that would allow someone to reconstruct every coefficient in the current 1984 system from the beginning. Later researchers repeatedly note the absence of a transparent derivation. The coefficients are official; the process that produced them remains largely opaque in the accessible literature and cannot be fully reverse-engineered.

To be clear, opacity does not necessarily imply deliberate concealment. More likely, a working group combined extensive performance data, expert opinions, and several principles, then arrived at the coefficients through repeated adjustments. That process may have lacked a clean logic expressible as a formula. It was an engineering exercise, not a derivation. Engineering can yield precise results without leaving a reproducible path. Intentional or not, however, the outcome is the same: the governing rule can be used, but cannot be traced to first principles.

This peculiar condition deserves separate attention.

Every number in a table governing the sport is public. Anyone can use it to calculate a score precise to the individual point. The public material located here does not allow the choice of those numbers to be reconstructed step by step. That does not establish that no complete account was ever written or that no one within the institution knew it. The remaining gap is between being able to execute a precise public rule and being able to retrace its development from the available public record.

This connects with Essay Three. Amateurism once defended itself by invoking an ancient past that did not exist, and the falsity of that past never diminished its force for a single day. Here the situation is similar but subtler: no fabricated origin of the scoring coefficients has been exposed here; a fully traceable derivation is missing from the public material available here. They still operate precisely, determine every decathlon's winner, and turn Thorpe's pole vault from roughly 750 points into roughly four hundred. A construct's rules can be indisputably exact while this essay cannot fully explain from those materials why these numbers, rather than others, were chosen.

Precision and justification are different things. The table is extraordinarily precise. Any score difference between two performances can be calculated without ambiguity. But precision does not mean these numbers have a traceable justification. It means only that, once the numbers are accepted, every subsequent calculation is unambiguous. The construct conceals its lack of grounding through precision, and users often mistake precision for justification.

## VII. How Other Sports Work Around the Problem

The decathlon is not the only sport that must combine different events for comparison. Looking at how other sports handle the problem shows how distinctive its chosen path is.

Modern pentathlon does things differently. It still uses points in its earlier disciplines, but in the final laser run it converts the points gap into a starting-time gap. The leader starts first; pursuers start behind at a rate of one second per point. The competition therefore ends in a race whose finishing order directly determines the medals.

Individual Gundersen events in Nordic combined use a similar approach: each point of the ski-jumping deficit becomes four seconds of delayed cross-country start time. This describes that format, not all Nordic combined competitions; formats such as the Compact use different arrangements. Again, the competition ends with someone crossing the line first, rather than with a final sum of heterogeneous component scores.

At the level of competition results, triathlon largely avoids the decathlon's deepest commensurability problem. It does not convert swimming, cycling, and running into separate point units and add them. Athletes are ranked by their time and finishing order on one continuous course. The rules define a finisher as an athlete who completes the course and crosses the finish line, with results ordered by finishing time.

Together these three examples reveal a common feature. None allows heterogeneity to remain permanently in the form of points. Modern pentathlon and Nordic combined convert differences between disciplines into starting-time differences for a final pursuit, then let an actual race settle matters. Triathlon directly uses cumulative time. In all three, conversions ultimately flow into something everyone recognises: who crosses the line first.

The decathlon did not take this route.

The choice may well be historical accident rather than deliberate design. The decathlon originated before electronic timing and precise pursuit-race mechanisms. Converting performances into points and adding them was probably the only practical solution. By the time other sports developed the cleaner pursuit format, the decathlon had accumulated decades of point-scoring tradition, with a long succession of records and placings expressed as totals. Adopting a pursuit then would have meant abandoning that entire history. It retained points not because points were better, but because it had travelled too far down that road to turn back.

It leaves heterogeneity where it is, resolving it through a permanent abstract scale. Long jump and shot put are not converted into seconds within the same race. They become points that are added together, producing a sum that corresponds to no actual race.

The distinction is fundamental. In modern pentathlon, changing the conversion table alters who concedes how many seconds to whom in that competition's pursuit. Once the race ends, the table's work for that occasion is done. It does not retrospectively change the previous edition's champion, because that champion was the first person across the line in that particular pursuit. The fact is fixed.

The comparison reveals an advantage of the finish line that an abstract score can never possess. What happens at the line is a physical fact: whose torso first reaches that vertical plane. Once it has happened, it cannot be changed and depends on no subsequent conversion. An abstract total is not a physical fact but a calculation, and calculations can be repeated. A later conversion table does not change who crossed the line first, although official results can still involve rulings on infringements. The decathlon adds another layer: even with ten unchanged marks, a different table can produce a different comparative total. That comparison can be recalculated; it does not follow that every original official result is automatically rewritten.

In the decathlon, a revised table can be applied to the performances of past competitions. The comparison concerns not just who crossed a final line first, but a total calculated from ten marks. If past performances are fed into a new table, their comparative totals change. Thorpe's five totals are the outputs of different tables, not five successive official verdicts replacing the original one.

The decathlon's distinctive feature is therefore that the conversion table itself has become the sport's central constitutional object. Other sports treat conversion as a disposable instrument: use it once, then let the finish line decide. The decathlon turns conversion into a permanent ledger whose accounting rules can be rewritten. Rewriting them makes a parallel historical comparison possible; it does not automatically replace the official results established under the rules of each original event.

## VIII. A Ledger That Can Be Rewritten Retrospectively

Let us bring the argument together.

The construct faces a genuinely insoluble problem. There is no exchange rate between the hundred metres and the long jump. This is not something temporarily undiscovered; it simply does not exist. Running fast and jumping far are different things, with no objective rate of exchange waiting in nature.

Yet the decathlon requires them to be added into a total. It must therefore invent an exchange rate. Invent, not discover.

The table is that invented exchange rate. Because it is invented, it can be reinvented. It has indeed been rewritten repeatedly, as the five selected versions show. Each reinvention changes a policy rather than correcting an error: this version rewards all-roundness, that one breakthroughs; this one values pole vaulting highly, that one less so.

Something shared with the whole series appears here. What the table seeks is closure: making ten incommensurable events commensurable in one comparable total. It succeeds. Any two decathlons can be ranked by their totals. Closure has been achieved.

The price is that this closure rests on a concealed choice. The total can be calculated because someone has assigned exchange rates to the ten events, and those rates are policy choices without an objective foundation. Closure depends on not asking why the rates take this particular form. Ask, and it becomes clear that they could be otherwise. If they were, every total, ranking, and history would be different.

Essay Five described one kind of remainder: the construct admitted a performance, then discovered that the number it recorded contained four inseparable things. Here the remainder is different. The construct calculates a precise total that depends on an exchange rate whose origin it cannot itself explain. The total is precise; the exchange rate arbitrary. The precise total rests on the arbitrary rate, while users see only precision, not the arbitrariness beneath it.

Look again at the nature of the ledger.

In Essay Two, the first thing the construct learned was how to add; it had never been taught to subtract. Here the situation grows more complicated. The decathlon ledger can not only receive additions but be recalculated retrospectively. Change the table and all past totals become new numbers without altering a single performance. This is a power not previously encountered: changing every conclusion without changing any original record.

The construct has chosen not to exercise this power fully. It has not recalculated every historical decathlon under the new table and legally substituted the new scores for the old. No congress record explicitly debating whether to do so was found in the available material. Actual practice introduced the new table in 1985, while historical accounts often retain original scores and also display an adjusted score under the current table.

This is parallel historical presentation, not complete legal replacement.

Thorpe's competition therefore carries two totals side by side today: 8,412.955, the original score under the 1912 table, and 6,564, the score under the present one. Neither is deleted. Both remain.

The two numbers tell different stories. 8,412 tells how extraordinary an all-round athlete Thorpe was within the understanding of 1912, approaching the Olympic standard of the time in almost every event. 6,564 tells what the same competition is worth under today's valuation. Neither story is false; they stand on different exchange rates. A later reader seeing only the two numbers cannot discover from the numbers themselves that the entire difference comes from the exchange rate, not the quality of the performances. Numbers conceal the rate and leave two apparently contradictory results.

These are again the parallel ledgers encountered in Essay Four, and the coexistence of hand and electronic readings in Essay Six. When the construct cannot make something singular, its solution is often not to choose one version but to preserve both side by side. One performance, two totals; one run, two readings; one event, two tables. What it cannot close becomes two adjacent columns.

This is perhaps the construct's most characteristic manoeuvre throughout the series. Genuine closure is impossible because what is to be closed has no unique answer. It therefore settles for listing several answers side by side and allowing them to coexist, rather than imposing a single one. That looks like honesty, an admission that it cannot establish one supreme authority. Yet it is also an evasion, leaving the judgment to those reading the ledger. The parallel columns are both where the construct acknowledges non-closure and where it transfers the burden of non-closure to others. Readers confronted with 8,412 beside 6,564 must decide which to trust. The construct need not decide.

Return to the ten numbers that have never changed.

11.2 in the hundred metres, 3.25 metres in the pole vault, 4 minutes 40.1 seconds in the fifteen hundred. These are things Thorpe actually did: accurate, verifiable, unchanged for more than a century. But the total that adds those ten things into one number has five different values under the five selected tables, while historical accounts may display both an original and a current-table total. What actually happened remains fixed; its valuation keeps changing. The construct can record precisely what someone did. It cannot settle once and for all how much each of those things is worth.

This observation extends across the seven essays so far. From the ancient list of names to record registration, eligibility boundaries, measurement, conversion between hand and electronic timing, and finally the table making ten things commensurable in one number, the construct's powers have continually grown. What it records becomes ever more precise and detailed. Yet one thing remains beyond it: a standard that requires no choice. Every valuation demands an unprovable prior decision: which performance is worth a thousand points, how much faster counts as equally good, which kind of fairness counts as fair. Once those decisions are made, everything afterward is supremely precise. But none of the decisions themselves has an objective foundation. The more precise the construct becomes, the more deeply it conceals the choice beneath it, until users imagine a fact lies there when there is only a decision once made.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
