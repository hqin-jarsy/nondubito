# Narrative Is Also a Construct

## I. Two real concentrations

Two things are real, and official performances can be used to check them.

One is in East Africa. Since 1964, Kalenjin athletes from Kenya's Rift Valley have accumulated an exceptionally dense concentration of middle- and long-distance achievements. One researcher-coded analysis counted men's Olympic medals, World Championship medals, and annual top-twenty-five marathon performances from 800 metres through the marathon. It assigned Kalenjin athletes fifty-four Olympic medals, seventy-seven World Championship medals, and 206 top-twenty-five marathon performances. Within Kenya, Kalenjin athletes accounted for approximately eighty-four percent of major track medals.

The other concentration is in the Caribbean. Since the 2008 Beijing Olympics, Jamaica has accumulated an equally striking concentration in sprinting: men and women, 100 and 200 metres, championship after championship. The Jamaican α-actinin-3 paper itself noted that Jamaicans won seven of Beijing's twelve individual sprint medals.

Both concentrations are real. They are too prominent to dismiss as anecdotes. This essay neither denies nor intends to deny them.

It asks something else: how do people explain them?

Explanations have grown around both concentrations, and they share a form. They begin with something observable: running barefoot to school, altitude, a gene, a school. They turn it into a cause sufficient to account for the whole phenomenon. The subject of this essay is that these explanations are themselves constructs.

The constructs of the previous fourteen essays were visible in rules, scoring tables, record books, and thresholds dividing people into categories. This one operates less visibly. It exists not in a rulebook but in the causal language used to narrate performances. A bodily characteristic is presented as the cause of a population-level phenomenon. Like every earlier construct, that narration seeks closure and cannot fully achieve it.

Its position needs clarification, because the essay could be mistaken for either of two extremes. It does not say that bodies are irrelevant or biological variation nonexistent; that would be as inaccurate as determinism. Nor does it say every proposed explanation is prejudice and should be discarded. Its claim is narrower: these explanations contain a common logical leap, turning a genuine observation into an unproved causal assertion. The leap, not the observation, is being challenged. Many observations are real. The leap is the construct.

## II. A distinction that must come first

Understanding what goes wrong requires a distinction that forms the essay's foundation.

Two different questions are often treated as one.

First: within the Kalenjin population, what makes some people faster than others?

Second: what accounts for Kalenjin athletes' much greater concentration of international middle- and long-distance achievements relative to other populations?

These are separate questions. The first can examine factors differentiating faster and slower individuals within the population: genes, Achilles tendons, training. The second asks what produces differences between populations. Its answer cannot simply be read from the answer to the first.

Within-population heritability does not identify the causes of between-population differences. That is the key sentence of this essay.

An example makes the distinction clear. Suppose a tendon characteristic explains some variation in running ability among Kalenjin individuals. That is possible. It does not establish how much of the international performance difference between Kalenjin athletes and other populations is caused by that characteristic. Geography, economics, schools, recruitment, and training systems also differ between groups and are entangled with ancestry. Within-group and between-group variation operate at different analytical levels. Transferring an answer from one to the other is a basic error.

The distinction immediately exposes a problem. Many genetic and environmental explanations answer only the first question, or measure an intermediate trait, before being presented as answers to the second. For the between-population concentration that actually needs explaining, the source reports no identified percentage contribution from any single proposed factor.

This distinction is repeatedly missed because it cuts against intuition. If something creates differences between individuals, surely it also creates differences between groups? That does not follow. Groups differ in factors that may scarcely vary within the local population being studied. Differences between two children in one Kalenjin village might mainly reflect locally varying factors such as individual aptitude and training. Comparing that population with a northern European country also introduces economics, school density, role models, agents' networks, and the whole system turning running into an international profession. These may vary enormously between populations while varying little within the local sample. Between-population outcomes could therefore depend heavily on factors invisible to a within-population comparison.

The answer must be explicit. How much of the population-level achievement difference has been causally apportioned? The defensible answer offered here is that it remains unknown, with the separate contribution of each proposed factor unidentified.

## III. One real number standing in for another

The explanations are particularly persuasive when they carry a real number. The number may be genuine while measuring something other than the claim it is used to support.

Consider several examples.

Research on an angiotensin-converting-enzyme variant found that it could explain up to twenty-four percent of circulating enzyme activity. That is a substantial figure. But the same research found no significant association between the variant and Kenyan elite-athlete status. Twenty-four percent therefore describes enzyme activity in blood, not a proportion of Kenyan sporting success.

A study of α-actinin-3 found that it explained 2.3 percent of variation in forty-metre sprint times among Greek adolescent males. In Jamaica, genotype differences between athletes and controls were not significant. The 2.3 percent came from Greek adolescents' sprint variation, not the achievements of Jamaican Olympic sprinters.

The tendon example is especially revealing. A study of thirty-two Kenyan runners found that an Achilles-tendon moment-arm measurement explained thirty percent of variation in running economy within the sample. Thirty percent sounds substantial. Yet in that same study, running economy was almost unrelated to the performance measure used: the correlation coefficient was −0.01.

Pause over the distinction. The tendon moment arm accounted for about thirty percent of running-economy variation in the sample, while economy was almost uncorrelated with the performance measure, at −0.01. The former percentage therefore cannot be relabelled as thirty percent of performance. Nor does this prove that the tendon trait’s own relationship with performance must be zero: correlations do not automatically transmit through three variables. Evidence is still needed to connect the intermediate measure to performance and then to population success.

That is the numerical substitution, whether intentional or not. A percentage associated with an intermediate phenotype is relabelled as a percentage of national or ethnic achievement. “The tendon explains thirty percent of running-economy variation” becomes “the tendon explains thirty percent of Kalenjin success.” The first was measured. The second was not. The link supposedly joining them, economy to performance, was precisely the link absent in that study.

This continues an earlier theme in a new form. Essay 7 found no public derivation of scoring coefficients. Essay 12 found none for the forty-millimetre shoe line. Essay 14 found that testosterone thresholds were not calculated from performance effect sizes. There the problem was precision without the required derivation. Here a genuine number exists, but measures the wrong object for the explanation being offered. A real but non-equivalent statistic stands in for the missing causal statistic.

The substitution is difficult to detect because it satisfies the desire for a concrete number. A precise percentage makes a complicated phenomenon feel quantified and understood. Listeners often do not ask what the percentage actually describes. “The tendon explains thirty percent” sounds like an explanation of thirty percent of sporting success, though it may concern an intermediate variable with almost no performance association in the sample. Numerical specificity conceals the mismatch between the measured object and the explained object. A true number in the wrong place can mislead more effectively than a vague assertion because it carries the authority of quantification.

## IV. Every story is narrower than its claim

Place the familiar deterministic stories against that standard, and each reveals its limits.

The first is running barefoot to school.

The story says Kalenjin children develop their ability by running long distances to school every day. What has been established is an association. In a survey of 404 elite runners and controls, approximately twenty-two percent of controls, seventy-three percent of national-level runners, and eighty-one percent of international-level runners reported running to school as children.

The association is real. But it permits two directions of explanation. The study's authors acknowledged that their design could not determine whether distance running made the children stronger or whether children who already found running easier were more likely to run. Selection into running and the effect of running were not separated. Earlier interviews also found that fourteen of twenty elite runners had not regularly run long distances to school. The evidence therefore supports a higher prevalence of running to school among the sampled elites, not a claim that every Kalenjin champion ran ten kilometres barefoot, still less that this commute caused later success.

The second story is that altitude makes champions.

Parts of the Rift Valley lie around two thousand metres above sea level, and altitude has genuine physiological effects. But altitude is neither sufficient nor universally necessary for championship success. Tibetans and Andean populations live at much greater elevations and show established physiological adaptations without producing Kenya's concentration of international distance champions. Altitude adaptation is widespread; industrial-scale production of elite distance runners is not. This does not establish that altitude contributes nothing. It shows that altitude alone does not determine international outcomes. Classic Kenyan studies also found that sedentary boys living at altitude did not automatically have high maximal oxygen uptake. Activity and training sharply differentiated the groups.

The third story is the sprint gene.

The story gives Jamaican champions a sprint gene ordinary people lack. The study found instead that the ACTN3 XX genotype was rare in both athletes and controls: about two percent in Jamaican controls and three percent in athletes, four percent in African-American controls and two percent in US athletes. Neither population’s athlete–control comparison showed a significant genotype-distribution difference. What was widely shared was the low prevalence of XX, not near-universal possession of the rare genotype. That pattern cannot by itself explain why only a few become elite; it does not prove that the variant has no physiological effect.

The fourth story is that one person made everything happen.

A missionary coach, a school championship, or a club is credited with producing the national phenomenon. Chronology directly challenges that account. The next section considers it.

Each story begins with a real correlate, school travel, altitude, genotype frequency, or an institution, then turns it into a sufficient cause without an identified counterfactual. That is the recurring recipe: take a genuine association, omit the missing comparison, and declare causation.

## V. Chronology refuses

The claim that one person or institution created everything encounters a particularly hard obstacle: the order of events.

Begin with Kenya. St Patrick's High School in Iten and its coach, Brother Colm O'Connell, became important nodes in Kalenjin running. But O'Connell arrived in Iten only in 1976, eight years after Keino's 1968 Olympic victory and twelve years after Kenya's first Olympic athletics medal.

He therefore cannot explain the initial breakthrough of the 1960s, which preceded his arrival. His contribution concerns later reproduction of success, continuity of coaching, and international visibility. Origin and reproduction are different questions. His own account emphasizes creating an environment in which athletes could develop, consistent with selection, guidance, and continuity, rather than a coach creating innate ability.

Jamaica's chronology is clearer still.

The inter-school championships began in 1910. The particularly visible intensification of Jamaican sprint dominance dates to Beijing 2008, about ninety-eight years later. A school competition that had existed for nearly a century cannot alone explain why dominance intensified at that particular moment.

What about the later clubs? MVP Track & Field Club was founded in 1999, about nine years before Beijing, offering a possible developmental stage for athletes leaving school. It addressed a concrete bottleneck: enabling athletes and coaches to develop in Jamaica rather than all having to leave for the United States. Another club led by Mills had a coaching lineage preceding Bolt’s breakthrough, but its formal 2009 relaunch came after Beijing.

Together these chronologies challenge two kinds of single-cause account. They challenge institutional sufficiency: the school championships existed for almost a century, much of it without comparable dominance. They also challenge an account relying solely on changes in population genetics: international achievements rose over decades, faster than a population's allele frequencies would plausibly change enough to explain that rise. A gene pool did not have to transform over three or four generations to produce it.

The temporal argument needs precision. A long-stable genetic distribution alone does not explain why achievements changed over a few decades; one must also explain why more people entered training, were discovered and reached international competition then. Markets, coaching, migration and incentives can change quickly. That does not show inherited variation contributed nothing: an existing predisposition may gain different opportunities for expression in a new environment. Chronology challenges a closed single-cause story; it does not settle the division between genes and environment in advance.

Nor did Jamaica become a sprint nation in 1999. It had a major Olympic tradition long before the club, from the 1948 generation through Quarrie and Ottey. Any story making one later institution the absolute beginning collides with achievements that preceded it.

## VI. The strongest arguments on both sides

This essay does not declare genetics or institutions the principal cause. It presents the strongest versions of both. Its target is the deterministic narrative form, not either field of explanation as such.

Begin with the strongest genetic argument.

It is not a single speed gene. It argues that the concentration is geographically narrow enough to resist easy explanation through national policy or altitude alone; elite performance is an extreme, highly polygenic phenotype; even samples of several hundred elite athletes may lack power to identify combinations of thousands of small effects; α-actinin-3 demonstrates that performance-relevant functional variation exists; and a genetic contribution need not change during the historical growth period. An already existing distribution may become more visible when markets, coaching, and migration routes allow more people to express it.

The strongest version acknowledges its limit: it establishes plausibility, not a quantified causal share. It is a gene–environment interaction hypothesis awaiting sufficiently powerful genomic, developmental, and institutional evidence from African and Caribbean populations.

Now consider the strongest institutional and selection argument.

International achievement rose within decades, faster than substantial allele-frequency change. Successful regions developed dense, self-reinforcing systems: visible children's competitions, specialist schools, camps, coaching lineages, role models, agents, travel networks, and very large international rewards. Those systems repeatedly selected from thousands of participants. Extreme performers can emerge from a large, intensively selected pool without requiring a large initial difference in population means. Jamaican athlete–control genetic comparisons yielded null findings for the prominent variants, and multinational endurance genomics produced weak results. The best-known variants do not reliably distinguish champions from their local populations.

This argument also has a limit. Institutions tend to concentrate where success already exists, creating reverse causation. No study has randomly assigned an Iten- or Jamaica-equivalent system to a matched population for a generation.

Both arguments are defensible, and both have honest limits. The essay does not adjudicate between them because the evidence needed to partition their causal shares is unavailable.

Neither argument is the target. The target is the move that turns either into determinism. Whether genetic effects are large or small, whether institutional effects are primary or secondary, a probabilistic tendency is not an inevitable outcome. Even a real average tendency does not predetermine every individual's fate, nor establish a phenomenon's only cause. Determinism's mistake is not choosing genes rather than institutions. It is turning “more likely” into “destined.”

That is the balance the essay must preserve. Criticizing biological determinism does not require claiming that everything is environmental and bodies irrelevant. That would merely replace one determinism with another, compressing a complex system into a different single cause. Bodies matter, environments matter, institutions matter. Their separate causal contributions have not been established here. No one can therefore declare one factor decisive on the strength of the evidence presented. Whatever its flavour, determinism pretends that the causal allocation is already known when it is not.

## VII. The people who were not measured

Deterministic narratives share a particularly damaging structure hidden in the direction of inference.

They begin with successful athletes, identify a characteristic, and narrate that characteristic as the cause of success.

The direction is reversed.

The problem is reasoning backward from the selected outcome while failing to measure adequately the people who share the characteristic without achieving the outcome.

Thousands of children run to school barefoot; very few become international runners. Certain tendon traits are not exclusive to East African champions, and not everyone with them becomes one. The ACTN3 XX genotype is rare in Jamaican athletes and controls alike, yet most controls are not Olympians. Many features narrated as sufficient causes are also found among people who do not achieve elite success.

These people are not necessarily absent from research: athlete–control comparisons have already appeared above. What leaves them out is the closed story that turns the successful athletes’ shared feature into a sufficient cause.

The narrative reads a feature from the successful, calls it the cause, and stops. It does not examine the unsuccessful people who share it. That comparison would immediately defeat the claim of sufficiency: if most people with the characteristic do not succeed, the characteristic cannot be a sufficient cause of success. The deterministic story depends on not making that comparison.

This is the remainder, in a new form.

Earlier remainders were omitted names, unquantifiable causes, performances belonging to different progressions, counterfactual bodies unavailable for direct observation, and bodily differences not exhausted by one criterion. Here the remainder consists of people who share the narrated feature but did not succeed and consequently never entered the story's field of observation. They are the comparison group the narrative must neglect to close itself.

Earlier constructs failed to close because their objects would not fit, causes could not be calculated, or no unique answer existed. Here the narrative can appear complete precisely because it excludes the people who would defeat its sufficiency claim. It achieves closure not by solving the problem but by looking away from the comparison group. A construct tells a closed story by systematically neglecting those who would open it again.

The structure is widespread. It is an extreme form of survivorship bias: observe the successful, identify shared features, and call them the secrets of success while overlooking unsuccessful people with the same features. The error is familiar in other fields, but particularly persistent in narratives about bodies and ancestry. A visible bodily feature feels intuitively causal. Slender lower legs or an apparently running-suited physique are easy to cast as explanations. People with the same slender legs who never reach a final disappear from photographs, samples, and stories of champions.

This also explains the weakness of slavery-based narratives. One version claims that selection in the slave trade, survival on plantations, or a particular West African source population biologically produced modern Jamaican sprinters. It lacks a demonstrated chain from historical selection pressures to measured allele-frequency changes and then to present sprint performance. It retrospectively fits history to an outcome rather than establishing population genetics. Structurally it resembles the other deterministic stories, merely moving the proposed cause several centuries backward while leaving the intervening links unmeasured.

## VIII. A story that compresses the system into the body

Bring the essay together.

Three things are established, and this essay denies none of them. First, the Kalenjin and Jamaican elite concentrations are real. Second, measured biological variation is real: genotype frequencies, limb morphology, tendon geometry, altitude adaptation, and running economy can differ. Denying variation is no more accurate than turning it into destiny. Third, institutional differences are real. Iten's schools and camps, international agents, Jamaican school championships, clubs, and coaching networks affect who trains, for how long, with what support, and who reaches international competition.

One thing remains unresolved: causal shares. No research cited here establishes a percentage of Kalenjin or Jamaican dominance attributable to genes, morphology, altitude, running to school, school championships, camps, clubs, economic incentives, or training-related migration. Percentages attached to intermediate phenotypes must not be relabelled as percentages of national or ethnic achievement.

The fuller picture is therefore not a bodily trait single-handedly causing a population phenomenon. It is a recursive system: small or unknown individual differences can be amplified through recruitment, training, economic competition, migration channels, coaching knowledge, and repeated selection. The individual starting difference is uncertain and may be small. The system can amplify it into a visible concentration.

The deterministic narrative compresses that whole system into the body.

“Born to run,” “natural sprinters,” “a gifted tribe,” “the speed gene”: such phrases reduce a multistage selection system to one bodily feature. They turn a decades-long process spanning schools, camps, markets, and migration into something a person supposedly possessed at birth. That compression loses the system, loses time, and loses the people who share the feature but never pass through the whole process.

That is narrative as construct. Like a rule, table, or threshold, it seeks closure. It wants one bodily feature to close the explanation of an entire phenomenon. It cannot, because the causal contribution connecting feature to phenomenon has not been identified. It substitutes the outcome needing explanation for a cause, substitutes a genuine intermediate statistic for a missing causal one, and turns probability into necessity. Its apparent completeness depends on overlooking the people who would challenge that story.

The construct can measure a tendon, a genotype frequency, and a medal count. It cannot, on the evidence presented, allocate those medals among the tendon, the gene, the schools, the camps, the agents, and decades of selection. That allocation has not been established. In its place, a narrative quietly inserts an answer shaped like a body. The body is real and its variation real. But the population-level causal chain connecting it to the concentration has not been measured as a complete explanation. The narrative skips that chain, joins the endpoints, and says: look, it is this body.

Here the essay connects more deeply with the series. The previous fourteen constructs operated institutionally: deliberately created rules, tables, books, and thresholds. This one operates cognitively, through causal stories people almost involuntarily construct to understand the world. An unexplained phenomenon is difficult to tolerate, so an explanation is made. The easiest places the cause in something visible, a body. Like an institutional construct, it seeks to settle what remains open. Yet closure fails because the chain it assumes has not been established. It seems real because both endpoints are real: a body and a concentration of achievement. Between them lies a causal chain not yet fully identified by these measurements. It may not hold in the proposed form, yet the narrative treats it as self-evident. This is the series' quietest construct. It is written in no rulebook. It lives in the confident tone of the sentence: they succeed because they were born that way.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
