# One Problem, Two Conclusions

## I. A contrast that must first be dismantled

This essay was originally going to present a clean contrast: running shoes kept their records, swimsuits had theirs annulled. Two structurally identical equipment disputes, two opposite decisions.

That contrast is wrong. It must first be dismantled, or the whole essay will rest on sand.

Here is what actually happened. World records set in the polyurethane swimsuits of 2009 were not annulled. FINA retained them, provided they had been achieved in recognized competitions while the suits remained approved. Most of the forty-three world records set at the Rome World Championships remain in the historical progression.

Running shoes were treated similarly. World Athletics retained eligible road performances achieved in shoes later restricted or superseded by new rules. It did not delete performances achieved in thick-soled, carbon-plated shoes.

What about Kipchoge's 1:59:40.2 in Vienna? It is often described as an annulled world record. But it was never a world record. It was a specially arranged attempt, announced and understood beforehand to be ineligible for record recognition. The reasons it could not enter the record book were independent of the shoes.

The neat contrast therefore fails. It was not retention in one case and annulment in the other. Both retained their records. The real question is not why the construct gave opposite answers to the same problem, but **why it chose not to annul the performances, when the opposite choice was available**.

The preceding two essays concerned a construct rewriting rules and resetting records when an implement definition was crossed. The 1986 javelin change closed the old-specification ledger and opened a new one. Here we encounter the reverse: faced with equipment that similarly crossed an established boundary, the construct declined to reset the records and kept old performances and new technology in the same book. That choice is defensible, just as the opposite javelin choice was defensible. Its opposite would also have been defensible.

This essay has no answer. It is the first in the series, so far, that genuinely has none. That absence is its subject.

Why this essay in particular? Earlier technical questions retained a factual core. How much of Beamon's jump came from altitude? There is no answer, but it is at least a question about facts, even if their contributions cannot be calculated. The question here concerns value. Retain or annul is not something more data can settle. No amount of experimental evidence tells us whether a legal but non-comparable performance ought to count, because “ought” is not something data can answer. For the first time, the distinction between fact and value becomes the centre of an entire essay. And value is precisely where the construct is least capable of achieving closure.

## II. A real advantage that has been exaggerated

First establish the facts about running shoes, since both sides begin there.

Thick-soled, carbon-plated shoes do produce a measurable advantage. Repeated controlled experiments have demonstrated it. The earliest advanced shoes generally reduced submaximal metabolic cost by approximately two to four percent relative to conventional racing shoes.

That is a population average. It is real.

But two qualifications must immediately be added, or the number will be misread.

First, a four-percent reduction in metabolic cost does not mean a four-percent faster race. Racing involves fatigue, turns, gradients, surfaces, weather, fuelling, and tactics. A four-percent oxygen saving does not mechanically become a four-percent faster marathon. Observational studies have estimated marathon-time effects between 0.6 and 2.8 percent, depending on sex and modelling assumptions, generally smaller than the laboratory's four percent.

Second, individual differences are enormous, and they are observed differences, not merely hypothetical ones. In one controlled experiment with world-class Kenyan runners, laboratory running-economy responses across the seven Kenyan runners and tested shoe models ranged from an 11.3-percent detriment to an 11.4-percent benefit. The same shoe could be a substantial help to one person and a burden to another. Some athletes respond strongly; others improve hardly at all; some use more energy at the fixed test speed in a particular model.

Keep this second point in mind. It will become one of the weightiest elements of the argument.

The two sides disagree over whether the advantage constitutes a discontinuity or ordinary footwear improvement. One calls it a rupture: unusually resilient foam could be used in much larger volumes because the plate and geometry stabilized the platform, producing a metabolic effect several times larger than the marginal differences usually observed between traditional racing shoes. The other calls it continuous innovation: every racing shoe modifies cushioning, stiffness, traction, and geometry. There is no motor or external energy source. The benefit still depends on the athlete.

Another popular claim should be dismantled: that the carbon plate alone returns enough energy to explain the whole advantage. That does not hold. Removing or reducing longitudinal bending stiffness in an otherwise similar shoe did not eliminate its economy benefit. The plate's isolated contribution is not the system's total effect. The advantage arises from interactions among foam energy return, stack height, rocker geometry, mass, and altered joint mechanics, not from the plate alone.

This connects with Beamon's jump in Essay 5. Several interacting factors combine to produce an effect, and one cannot simply isolate a single factor and assign it a definite contribution. The carbon plate is not a motor, just as altitude was not the sole cause of Beamon's jump.

## III. The forty-millimetre line has no published derivation

From 2017 to 2019, road performances accelerated unusually sharply, coinciding with the rapid spread of advanced footwear.

A study spanning 2001–2019 and including 11,400 top-one-hundred performances found that 2017–2019 was the three-year window with the greatest average improvement in almost two decades. Those three years accounted for approximately forty-four percent of the improvement in women's marathon performance since 2001 and thirty-five percent of the men's.

The acceleration is real. Its cause is disputed.

The shoe-centred explanation points to mutually reinforcing evidence: timing, adoption patterns, laboratory effects, changes within athletes, and the exceptionally sharp three-year jump. The multifactor explanation points to faster courses, a deeper East African field, improved pacing and fuelling, weather selection, appearance fees, training, and the preferential adoption of new shoes by runners who were already faster. Shoe allocation in observational data was not random.

Without long-term randomized assignment, confounding in observational data needs careful treatment. The evidence supports a footwear contribution without uniquely and exactly apportioning the entire acceleration between shoes and other influences. This does not invalidate all causal research or rule out better estimates. Again we encounter Essay 5's structure: an effect cannot simply be divided into independent contributions.

World Athletics responded on 31 January 2020. Road-shoe soles could be no thicker than forty millimetres and could contain no more than one embedded rigid plate.

The January 2020 announcement examined here supplied no complete quantitative derivation uniquely fixing the limit at forty millimetres.

World Athletics cited its internal Assistance Review Group's assessment of independent research that had found sufficient cause for concern and announced a working group to guide further research, certification, and long-term regulation. But the January 2020 announcement published no formal evidence table, model, threshold-validation study, or calculation explaining why the limit was forty rather than thirty-five, thirty, or forty-five millimetres.

Legal analysis characterized the line as regulatory and precautionary, not as a result directly demonstrated by a particular experiment.

The connection with earlier essays is especially clear. In Essay 7, the public material located here did not fully reconstruct the development of the decathlon coefficients. Here the January 2020 announcement supplied no quantitative derivation uniquely fixing forty millimetres. It did publish policy reasons: protecting competition integrity, pausing more radical designs and allowing further research. Lacking a unique experimental derivation is not the same as lacking justification. A line can be precise to the millimetre while readers still need to ask how those reasons support it, and how it compares with other possible limits.

A further point matters. The forty-millimetre limit separates acceptable from unacceptable shoes, but a benefit does not naturally change from reasonable to unreasonable at precisely forty millimetres. Height, foam and geometry interact; adjacent numbers need not mark a difference in kind. Experiments can inform the consequences of forty, thirty-nine or other limits, and a rule can explain its trade-offs. An experiment alone cannot turn a changing effect into the one boundary that ought to be adopted. Having to decide is not the same as having to decide arbitrarily. A choice can be reasoned and contestable without being uniquely compelled by the data.

Other information is also unavailable. Was a stricter proposal, such as thirty millimetres or a complete ban, formally put forward and voted down? No meeting record has been located. A complete named membership list for every phase of the working group is also unavailable. The gaps have a familiar shape: the conclusion and some reasons are public, while the available material does not reconstruct the full proposal and deliberation process. The initial Assistance Review Group membership was published; lacking a complete roster for every phase does not mean no names were ever released.

## IV. Vienna: a performance that was never a record

Now consider the most frequently misdescribed performance: Kipchoge's 1:59:40.2 in Vienna.

The familiar version says that he broke two hours and set a record, only for World Athletics to annul it because of his shoes.

Every link in that story is wrong.

It was not an annulled record because it was never an eligible record. The obstacle was not the footwear but the organization of the attempt.

More than forty elite pacemakers were arranged in rotating teams. A group ran around Kipchoge in an aerodynamic formation, changing approximately every 4.8 kilometres. Computational fluid dynamics and wind-tunnel research informed the formation. Drinks were handed to him from a moving bicycle rather than collected by him at fixed stations. A lead vehicle projected visual pacing guidance.

Together these arrangements made a specially organized attempt that did not meet ordinary marathon record conditions; that does not make every favourable condition a violation in itself.

World-record recognition requires a genuine competition, properly arranged, advertised, authorized, and conducted under the rules. The Vienna arrangement, with rotating helpers who were not competitors in the race, did not meet the usual conditions for world-record ratification; having one runner as the target is not by itself a separate violation. Legal pacemakers are competitors who start the race and remain subject to its rules. Assistance by people who are not competing, who leave and re-enter, or who are stationed to pace an athlete without being competitors in that race is prohibited. Rotating fresh teams in and out is fundamentally different from ordinary legal pacing.

Vienna was ineligible for reasons independent of the shoes. Rotating non-competing pacemakers and moving refreshments raised the assistance and feeding problems that made this specially organized attempt ineligible under ordinary record conditions. Ideal pacing, a suitable course and favourable weather are not each a violation merely because they help; the actual competition, course and assistance conditions matter. Whatever the precise legal status of the footwear, it would not turn the attempt into a ratifiable record.

An honest qualification must also be added about the shoes themselves. The exact engineering specifications of the Vienna prototype are unavailable. No complete manufacturer's specification or public destructive inspection of Kipchoge's actual Vienna shoes has been located. The later retail model was publicly described as having one full-length carbon plate, but that does not prove the prototype had the same construction. Media widely speculated about stack heights above forty millimetres and as many as three plates. Those remain unconfirmed claims.

The accurate description is therefore this: a real and extraordinary performance, an attempt explicitly understood beforehand to be ineligible for record recognition, excluded because it failed competition and assistance conditions independent of the unresolved details of its shoe construction.

Calling Vienna an annulled record merges two separate matters: the performance's ineligibility and the controversy over the shoes. The first does not depend on the second. Even perfectly compliant footwear would not have made Vienna record-eligible.

## V. Swimming retained its records too

Swimming likewise did not annul the records, despite the popular story.

In 2008, a suit combined densely woven elastane fabric, polyurethane panels, body compression, a water-repellent surface, and ultrasonically welded seams. Development testing claimed roughly twenty-four percent less skin-friction drag than older fabric. That was a manufacturer's development-test claim, not an independently verified twenty-four-percent improvement in race performance.

In 2009, competing suits intensified impermeable polyurethane coverage, compression, trapped-air effects, and buoyancy effects. Some leading designs were effectively all-polyurethane full-body suits, rather than textile suits with limited panels.

The Rome World Championships produced forty-three world records.

To appreciate how unusual that was, set it against the ordinary scale. A few world records at one championship would already be remarkable. Forty-three meant broad sections of the record book being rewritten within days. Retrospective studies estimated gains of several percentage points in certain events, an extraordinary quantity in a sport decided by hundredths of a second. The number's abnormality became one of the strongest arguments for annulment: a technological change accompanying forty-three world records is difficult to describe as merely routine equipment evolution.

FINA then acted. On 24 July 2009 its congress approved a return to textile suits and limits on body coverage. For existing records, however, it chose no retrospective cancellation. On 31 July it confirmed that the old approved list would remain valid through 31 December, with new requirements applying from 1 January 2010.

On this point FINA and World Athletics did the same thing: restrict the future without rewriting the past. Earlier records stayed in the progression and remained current until surpassed.

FINA's public reason was that the records had been set under the rules and conditions applicable at the time, and it would not reconsider them.

Another popular explanation must be dismantled: that FINA judged the records fair because everyone had equal suits.

That was not its claim. The formal public reason was narrower: the performances were legal under the rules then in force. Access was not literally equal. Contracts, national-team arrangements, supply, fit, approval timing, and model performance differed. The construct declared legality, not fairness, still less universal equality. Legality and fairness are separate judgments. It made only the former.

## VI. Both sides have a case

The real question is now clear. Running shoes and swimsuits did not receive opposite treatment. Both retained their records. That choice can be strongly opposed and strongly defended. This section presents both cases. The essay will not adjudicate between them.

Begin with the strongest case for retention.

First, legality and institutional responsibility. Athletes are entitled to rely on governing bodies' rules and approved lists. FINA approved the suits and kept those approvals effective through 31 December. World Athletics permitted compliant carbon-plated shoes in recognized road races. Retrospectively penalizing athletes would transfer the regulator's delay or error onto competitors who followed the rules at the time.

Second, records have never been technologically pure. Tracks, pools, timing, lane ropes, starting blocks, training science, nutrition, bicycles, rackets, and shoes continually change. A record measures the best performance under conditions recognized on its date, not an abstract constant of unaided humanity. Retrospectively drawing a line at polyurethane or thick foam requires a defensible explanation of why those innovations differ in kind from every previously accepted improvement. That explanation is difficult to give.

Third, no scientifically valid subtraction exists. Average effects cannot be subtracted from individual performances. Shoe responses range from disadvantages to double-digit laboratory benefits. Suit effects vary with fit, body shape, stroke, and distance. An individual record cannot reliably be converted into a hypothetical textile-suit or traditional-shoe performance. Here the enormous individual differences from Section II matter. If the same shoe affects different runners so differently, there is no fixed bonus that can be assigned to everyone wearing it and then deducted.

Fourth, selective annulment would rest on uneven evidence. Photographs and surviving product records cannot conclusively identify every shoe or suit variant. Prototypes changed; customized fit varied; approval status evolved rapidly. Annulment limited to clearly identified users might leave equally assisted but unidentified performances untouched, producing a less accurate record book.

Together these four arguments make a strong case for retention. The opposing case is comparably strong.

Consider the strongest arguments for annulment or reclassification.

First, a record is an institution of comparability, not merely a legal competition result. A championship determines who won under the day's rules. A record claims a best performance across history. A governing body could preserve medals and places while declining to make a technologically discontinuous performance the benchmark for athletes obliged to use materially slower equipment.

Second, governing bodies can correct their own category errors, and have done so. Cycling's hour record supplies a precedent. The UCI moved technologically assisted performances into a “Best Human Effort” category and reinstated an earlier benchmark achieved with conventional equipment. Retrospective reclassification is institutionally possible even when the performances were celebrated at the time. This matters because it establishes that non-annulment is not an incapacity. The opposite can be done and has been done. Retention is a choice.

Third, the performance discontinuity was exceptionally large. Rome produced forty-three world records; retrospective studies estimated gains of several percentage points in certain events. The top-one-hundred running acceleration of 2017–2019 was the largest three-year improvement in the two-decade dataset. Such synchronized jumps can be read as evidence that the record category had materially changed before the rules caught up.

Fourth, formal legality is insufficient when the approval process itself is defective. FINA repeatedly revised its 2009 approvals and struggled to measure buoyancy, permeability, and material composition. World Athletics' earlier rules prohibited unfair assistance without an operational advance test. Provisional regulatory approval does not necessarily establish that the resulting records belong to the same historical category.

Fifth, unequal access was intrinsic rather than incidental. Early advanced shoes went first to selected sponsored athletes. Access to polyurethane suits depended on contracts, federations, supply, and last-minute approvals. When a technology has substantial benefits, exclusive or unstable access affects both competition between contemporaries and the hierarchy of records.

Together these five arguments also make a strong case for annulment.

Like the retention case, this is not necessarily an extreme position indifferent to athletes. It rests on an equally serious principle: records mean something because they are comparable. A record that cannot be compared with successors loses its central function. On this view, a mark that later athletes in compliant equipment could never approach would cease to be a monument worth aspiring to and become an impassable wall. It would hold everyone below a height partly produced by something they were no longer permitted to use. The annulment side defends the principle that records should be comparable human benchmarks, not equipment's unreachable residue. That principle is as legitimate as non-retroactivity.

Both sides have a case. How they have one is the heart of the essay.

## VII. Why there is no adjudication here

These are not a correct side and an incorrect side, one defensible and one indefensible. Both are defensible, but they stand on different ground.

Retention rests on contemporaneous legality and finality. On that foundation, a performance achieved in compliant equipment at an official competition is a valid record, and retrospectively changing its status is unjust.

Annulment rests on longitudinal comparability within a record category. On that foundation, a performance that prevents meaningful comparison between generations should not be their benchmark, even if it was entirely legal when achieved.

Both foundations are real, and they point in opposite directions. Which one is chosen depends less on proving one uniquely correct than on what the construct prioritizes: legality and finality at the time, or long-term comparability of the category.

That is why this essay offers no answer. It is not a problem that more evidence alone would automatically dissolve. The record still has gaps; even a fuller account would require an explanation of how contemporaneous legality and long-term comparability should be weighed. The available material does not uniquely exclude every other reasonable option. Primary records establish three institutionally coherent options: prospective restrictions with retention, retrospective reclassification, and parallel records for equipment eras. They supply no rule demonstrating that one is universally more legitimate.

Other sports give different answers, which is precisely the point. Golf and tennis tend toward prospective rules preserving earlier legal achievements. Cycling reclassified records when equipment fundamentally changed the historical test. Archery maintains parallel, technically defined record categories: compound-bow records do not replace recurve records simply because both are archery. Three approaches, with no cross-sport principle establishing which alone is right.

This connects with Essay 9. There the construct declined to decide whether glide or rotation was superior because no universal optimum had been established. Here it did decide: it retained the records. But no objective standard uniquely compelled that decision. It could have chosen the opposite. Essay 9 concerned a construct declining to adjudicate; here the adjudication hangs between two defensible foundations.

The difference deserves another look. Facing glide and rotation, the construct did not intervene because it lacked a single scale that could rank the techniques universally. Its silence acknowledged that absence. Here it intervened and chose retention. But contemporaneous legality, the foundation it chose, had no demonstrably superior claim over longitudinal comparability. The decision was therefore not uniquely grounded by an objective rule. It selected one of two equally available grounds. It could have selected the other. Silence and choice reveal something in common: without an objective standard, silence honestly refrains from choosing, while choice selects a ground that could have been otherwise. Neither is the sort of reproducible judgment compelled by a shared criterion.

Essay 7 offers another connection. The decathlon scoring table faced incompatible definitions of fairness, so even the proper question was not unique. Here retention and annulment both work because they express different reasonable understandings of a record. One sees it as the best competition result under the conditions of its time. The other sees it as a comparable benchmark across eras. Like the definitions of fairness in Essay 7, neither can be demonstrated to be the uniquely correct understanding.

## VIII. A conclusion chosen, not compelled

Bring the essay together.

Running shoes and swimsuits generated structurally similar equipment disputes. Both combined interacting features rather than relying on one magical component. Both produced measurable average improvements with large individual differences. Both spread rapidly after early competitive success. Both governing bodies initially operated with broad, qualitative fairness or equipment rules, then adopted numerical or material restrictions. Both applied the new restrictions prospectively and retained performances achieved in recognized, compliant competitions.

Faced with these similar cases, the construct made the same choice: retention.

But the choice is contestable. It is not the only correct course, not a conclusion forced by evidence, not a decision with no alternative. The construct selected one of several defensible possibilities. Opposite retention stood comparably strong reasons for annulment or reclassification, and an actual precedent for the contrary choice: cycling's hour record.

This joins the essay to its two predecessors.

In Essay 10, the construct discovered a boundary when it was crossed and decided whether to defend it or open it. I noted that the decision could have gone the other way. In Essay 11, redefining an implement reset the records and closed the old javelin ledger. Both involved choices, though they could appear natural or almost compelled. This essay turns “it could have been otherwise” from a side remark into its whole subject. It shows clearly that structurally similar equipment disputes can lead either to a reset, as in javelin, or to retention, as with shoes and swimsuits. Both have complete arguments and precedents. The construct chose the latter.

The remainder takes another new form.

Earlier remainders were excluded names, unquantifiable causes, unadjudicated techniques, actions that might have been admitted, and legal but non-comparable performances. Here the remainder is the unchosen option itself. Annulment, reclassification, parallel era records: these were workable possibilities with precedents. The construct chose retention, leaving them as paths not taken. They were not wrong. They were unchosen.

This remainder is especially difficult to identify because it does not appear in the ledger at all. It is another form the entire ledger could have taken. Had the construct chosen annulment, today's record book would look different. Rome's forty-three records would carry different designations or occupy a separate era sequence. That alternative book was a real possibility. It failed to materialize not because it was unworkable, but because the construct took the other road.

There was no signpost at the fork. No single principle supplied by these materials excluded every other reasonable direction. It chose retention with a complete set of reasons; it could have chosen annulment with another complete set. Both roads led to coherent record institutions, but different ones. One was selected. The other remained across the fork, equally defensible but untravelled.

To see how unusual this remainder is, place it beside the others. An omitted name can be spoken. Unquantifiable causes can still be recognized as present. An incomparable javelin performance can be seen in the old book. Each has an object one can point to. This remainder has none. It is a record book that never happened, the book that would exist today had the construct chosen annulment. Nothing in the actual world can be identified as that book, precisely because it is the unrealized version. It is an absent road, a choice's reverse side, a possibility diverted away. Of all the things the construct cannot close, this is the lightest, too light even to enter the ledger. Yet it is also the most comprehensive, because it is another possible form of the whole ledger.

The construct can measure a shoe's energy saving, a swimsuit's drag reduction, and a performance to the hundredth of a second. It cannot measure whether those performances ought to count. “Ought” is not a measurable quantity. It is a judgment that must be chosen, with no uniquely correct answer.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
