# Which Bodies May Be Measured

## 1. All nine finished

Amsterdam, 1928. The women's 800-metre final.

Nine athletes started, and all nine were listed as finishers. The first three ran faster than the pre-race world record. Radke won in 2:16.8, followed by Kinue Hitomi in 2:17.6 and Gentzel in 2:17.8. Then came Thompson, Rosenfeld, MacDonald, Dollinger, and Kilosówna.

The ninth finisher was Wever. Commonly reproduced results do not give her precise time.

We need not invest this fact with too much weight. A missing last-place time in an early results table could have many explanations: an omission in recording, a loss in copying, or a failure to time it originally. What matters is its position. In a race where three women bettered the world record, the only athlete without a number was the one who finished last. The construct's attention declines with placing, and the bottom cell is where its grip most readily loosens.

By any measure, this was a successful race. An event entered the Olympics for the first time; nine women finished; three beat the existing world record. If measurement was the construct's purpose, what happened in this final was the best result it could have hoped for.

One further point follows. Three leading athletes bettering the pre-race record in the same final shows a recorded frontier being advanced together. That record had already been improving, while participation and documentation in the event continued to expand. One final alone cannot establish how narrow the earlier field had been. The table nevertheless carried two meanings: athletes demonstrated real competitive ability, and an event was accumulating its own history. One was athletic, the other institutional. This should have been a development the construct was prepared to take seriously.

Surviving film can still be viewed. It shows several athletes briefly sitting down, lying on the grass, or visibly gasping after the finish. None of this is unusual after an 800-metre race.

The doctor examining the women afterward was Bergmann. A few minutes later she checked their pulses, blood pressure, and general condition. Her report was that everything was normal.

The evidentiary position was therefore clear from the outset: an official results table, film, and testimony from the doctor who examined the athletes on the spot. All three corroborated one another and pointed to the same conclusion. Nine women ran a fast 800 metres, then were tired.

Their different natures strengthen that corroboration. The results table was institutional, produced by procedure without evaluative commentary. The film was mechanical; it did not know what it was filming. The testimony was professional, given at the scene by someone qualified to judge. Three independent sources pointed to the same event. Evidentially, that is a fairly strong state of confirmation.

What happened next had nothing to do with those three things.

## 2. The newspapers said five, or six

Around 3 August 1928, several major newspapers reported the race.

*The Times* described the aftermath as a spectacle of prostrate figures in evident distress, inferring that the women's 800 metres was dangerous.

Williams of *The New York Times* wrote that the event demanded too much of women's physical powers. He also reported that six of the nine collapsed after finishing and several had to be helped away.

Shirer of the *Chicago Tribune* put the claim directly in his headline: five women track stars had collapsed in an Olympic race.

In Canada's *Toronto Star*, Marsh wrote about Thompson and Rosenfeld on the front page of 3 August, turning exhaustion into a selling point for the story.

Set these reports beside the three sources in the previous section.

The official table says nine finished. *The New York Times* says six fell. The *Chicago Tribune* says five collapsed.

These numbers do not directly cancel one another out. Nine finishers and five or six athletes lying down afterward could coexist: finishing and post-race collapse are different indicators. What needs checking is which movements were called falling or collapsing, and whether they supported the conclusion that the distance was dangerous.

We must not overstate the point. There is descriptive latitude between briefly sitting down and collapsing. The same movement can be described as resting or giving way. The numbers need not have been invented. They may have classified what was seen by a standard much looser than the results table's. The point is not that someone lied. It is that when description and counting conflicted, description took effect.

Something else needs stating, and it is the most important point in this section.

None of these four reporters can be securely placed beside the finish line at the time. Modern research can establish their bylines, newspapers, and publication dates. It cannot determine from the surviving material whether they saw the scene themselves or wrote from reports passed around the stadium. Four articles, four vantage points: all unresolved.

Nor did the present research recover directly quotable facsimiles of the original reports in leading German and French newspapers. Modern scholarship broadly agrees that the catastrophic narrative was international, not confined to Britain and North America. But establishing that directly requires national-library microfilms and facsimiles. We cannot now describe in fine detail how widely, and in exactly what language, the story circulated.

That gap has a practical effect. Seeing only four English-language reports makes it difficult to decide whether this was a few newspapers independently dramatizing the same story or a shared framework spanning languages. The former looks more like a journalistic contingency; the latter more like an age's common preconception. Scholarship favours the latter, but the direct texts supporting it were not recovered in this research. The analysis below must therefore rest on the four visible articles, not pronounce on the invisible ones.

The preceding essay's observation applies directly: what a construct can see depends on the form in which an event remains on paper.

Here the difficulty is that the same event remained on paper in two forms, each emphasizing different aspects of it.

One was a results table, produced by timekeepers, judges, and the meeting's recording procedures, whose entire function was to record accurately what had happened. The other was news, produced by pages that had to sell, whose function was to persuade readers that the story was worth reading.

The latter took effect.

This must be stated fully, because it can sound like a complaint about journalism. It is not. Essay 02 explained that the modern record system's first ledger was itself a commercial newspaper. Wilkinson selected it because it was the only archive large enough and continuous enough at the time. Newspapers had helped bring the construct into existence. The same medium gave it a body in one place and overturned its measurements in another. These are two sides of one property: whatever can bring news before everyone can also bring an erroneous number before everyone, just as quickly.

## 3. The vote did not survive

After Amsterdam, the international athletics federation decided to remove the women's 800 metres from the 1932 programme.

An accessible first-hand account of the rationale comes from Bergmann. She summarized it this way: delegates were disturbed by the image of the prostrate girls and voted on humanitarian grounds. She also said that the German delegates had tried to preserve the event.

The German effort deserves notice. Germany had won the title and set the new world record, giving it a direct reason to defend the event. But Japan and Sweden also won medals, and reasons to preserve an event extend beyond medals; Germany was not its only beneficiary. This neither praises nor condemns anyone. It reminds us that gains and losses may influence voting positions without allowing us to infer everyone’s motives from the identity of the champion.

A structural question follows. New events may not yet have established training resources, competitive pathways, and networks of support across many countries. When programmes contract, they may therefore find their place harder to defend than older events. This is no law governing every new event, nor does it reduce every vote to an interest calculation. It shows that, beyond an event’s intrinsic merits, the distribution of existing investment and support may help determine what survives.

Then there is the more widespread claim that the IOC immediately voted to limit women's racing distances to 200 metres.

No matching decision appears in the publicly verifiable minutes.

What can be established is another vote. In 1931 the IOC voted on women's participation in athletics overall. By sixteen votes to three, it retained women's athletics in the 1932 programme. The minutes preserve neither the debate point by point nor a directly verifiable separate vote count for the women's 800 metres.

The familiar claim must therefore remain unresolved. This does not mean it is necessarily wrong. It means the original record supporting it is not currently available.

A pattern appearing for the third time deserves marking.

In Essay 02, the earliest original record-application form could not be found. In Essay 03, the locations of Thorpe's admission letter, his coach's internal correspondence, and the complete exchange between committees were unknown. Here a decision governing thirty-two years has left no itemized record of its reasoning.

None of these three cases requires deliberate destruction. What survived shares a property: it was the routine output of a continuing machine. Annual results books were printed every year; student files were classified; aggregate vote totals entered minutes. Systems performed those tasks. What failed to survive also shares a property: it was one-off. A discussion, a letter, a debate. Once concluded, it dispersed.

A construct therefore usually leaves posterity its conclusions, not its reasoning.

The consequences for judgment are concrete. To understand what lay behind the 1928 deletion, we have a decision's result, a doctor's recollections, and later accounts. We lack what those present actually said that day. Conclusions about motives must therefore infer from outcomes and context. Inference cannot attain the certainty of meeting records. This is a limit to accept, not an obstacle to overcome by interpreting harder.

## 4. The reasons from within

We must now work through both directions of the argument. Here both have established evidence; neither needs to be contrived.

One direction says that removing the event rested chiefly on a mistaken assessment of women's abilities. The supporting evidence has already appeared: nine official finishers, three bettering the pre-race world record, surviving film that does not support the familiar story of multiple fainting athletes, and the examining doctor's judgment that the prohibition was hasty and emotional, with normal physiological findings. Besides Bergmann, Messerli later said he had been present and that the supposed collapse looked more like crying and emotional reactions to defeat than pathological breakdown. Taylor interpreted brief post-race falls by individual competitors as tension and competitive emotion, not proof that the distance was unbearable.

Bergmann made a more important observation: nervous strain in sprints might be more harmful than longer-distance running.

Its significance is that the medical opinion available on the spot did not point toward the decision. The doctor examined the athletes and judged in the opposite direction.

Of course, her suggestion was itself one doctor's opinion, not an established finding. Caution is necessary before using it as proof that the decision was wrong: that would counter one untested professional judgment with another. Its force lies elsewhere. It shows that medical opinion was not unified, although later accounts often portray the removal as following contemporary medical consensus. “Consensus” is inapplicable here. There was none.

The other direction says that the decision had reasons within its contemporary medical and organizational setting, and those reasons were coherent within that framework of knowledge.

That framework had roughly three layers. First, women's bodies were understood primarily as bodies for reproduction and child-rearing. Moderate exercise was beneficial, while excessive or competitive endurance exertion might harm health and fertility. Second, an unbecoming public spectacle after a race was treated as a governing problem: the concern was what spectators saw, not whether athletes were injured. Third, there was a preference for women's Olympic events described as aesthetic. IOC president Baillet-Latour's position in 1930 pointed this way.

The second layer is particularly revealing because it is independent of the athletes' physical condition. It concerns an image, precisely what the newspapers supplied.

There is another reason to isolate this point. The first layer concerns physical harm to athletes, a claim that evidence can test. The second concerns public appearance, the third a preference for aesthetic events. Those are not the same kind of bodily assertion and cannot be decided by a medical examination alone. This does not exempt impressions and preferences from criticism. It means that showing athletes can withstand the distance does not automatically remove the other two reasons. A decision may lose its bodily rationale yet persist through programme preferences. This is why accumulating evidence does not always change a decision.

These are consequently not two opposing explanations. They interlock within one event. Newspapers magnified the image; the image entered delegates' judgments; and the contemporary framework already offered a channel through which public appearance counted as a legitimate reason. None of the three alone need have produced the deletion. Together they were sufficient.

A limitation must be explicit. The present public-source research did not produce an item-by-item, edition-by-edition bibliography of medical articles and textbooks around 1928. It recovered the intellectual structure of the restrictive position and the on-site medical testimony against restriction, but not a bibliography whose individual entries could all be checked. That does not mean no such bibliography exists. It lies outside what this research recovered.

Finally, consider the difference in timing between the two directions.

Evidence later systematically weakened the mistaken assessment of ability, while the logic of programme governance persisted in the history of the rules. The latter half of this essay will show it: by 1978, the reason for rejecting women's 5,000 and 10,000 metres was no longer physical incapacity but insufficient worldwide participation. The reason changed. The gate stayed where it was.

## 5. Another record book

Long before Amsterdam, another system was already operating.

During the 1920s, Milliat organized independent international women's competitions to pressure the IOC and the athletics federation. The four central competitions staged by the Fédération Sportive Féminine Internationale were Paris in 1922, Gothenburg in 1926, Prague in 1930, and London in 1934.

The federation did more than hold meetings. From 1922 onward, it recognized women's world records.

That is the central fact here. Recognizing records is not ancillary to organizing competitions. Establishing, ratifying, and publishing them are among an international sport federation's core functions. Doing this declares that one is an institution entitled to say: this number counts.

Notice that no one had conferred the power on it. The FSFI did not obtain record authority from a higher body. It began doing the work, and because athletes competed, newspapers reported, and people took the numbers seriously, it acquired the function in practice. Essay 02 explained that a table needs a source whose credentials it cannot grant itself. Here is the other side: an institution can become that source by doing the work continuously.

Between 1922 and 1936, women's athletic performances therefore belonged to two books.

A compromise was reached in 1926. Milliat agreed to replace the name “Women's Olympic Games” with “Women's World Games” in exchange for women's athletics entering the 1928 Olympics. The bargain reveals her strategy. The parallel system was not intended to endure forever. It was bargaining power.

The compromise was only partly honoured. Women's athletics entered Amsterdam as a limited set of events, not a full programme. The Women's Amateur Athletic Association in Britain consequently refused to send a team: ten events had been promised, but only five were offered.

This was a rare boycott by women for programme equality in modern Olympic history. Its target was precisely the admission usually narrated as a victory.

In 1936 the FSFI was absorbed by the international athletics federation.

Absorption differed from the treatments seen in earlier essays. It neither wiped out the other ledger nor preserved two books side by side. World Athletics's present accounts of women's record progressions still state that certain earliest world records were recognized by the FSFI in 1922. Some entries from the absorbed book therefore remain within the official genealogy.

We do not, however, possess an itemized list of which entries were taken over intact and which were later silently invalidated. Public evidence establishes a transfer of authority, but cannot produce a line-by-line handover inventory.

The current book thus contains entries from an institution that no longer exists, without their origins necessarily being visible on its face. After swallowing a rival, a construct does not label which lines were swallowed.

The parallel ledger was never declared invalid while it existed. It was real, recognized by a functioning international organization, pursued by real athletes. It disappeared not because it was refuted, but because part of its purpose was achieved and its organizers had never intended it to last indefinitely. A construct can end through completion rather than defeat.

Yet that formulation must not be too tidy. What was completed was Milliat's layer of purpose. For athletes whose performances appeared in the four Women's World Games, the relevant question was not whether the ledger had fulfilled its purpose but whether their own names remained in it. We cannot fully answer that today because the itemized transfer inventory does not exist. A construct's ending is not the same event for its designers and for the people it records.

## 6. The woman without a number and the woman with one

The women's 800 metres returned at Rome in 1960.

From 1928 to 1960: thirty-two years.

The restoration has no single identifiable date. We know the event was on Rome's programme. Public materials do not securely pin down who completed the final act of reinstatement, in which meeting and year. More cautiously, it resulted from the continuing expansion of women's events during the 1950s and reconsideration of the programme by the athletics federation and IOC, rather than one definitive meeting of vindication.

Pause over this.

Closing the gate had concrete acts: a vote, an occasion on which reasons could be stated, a conclusion that could enter minutes. Opening it did not. The opening was dispersed across more than a decade, accumulating through incomplete individual actions until we can no longer identify the moment it was accomplished.

A construct closes and opens gates asymmetrically. Closing requires a decision because it resists the status quo. Opening may require only that the status quo gradually cease to need the gate. The latter produces no single record.

This also explains why commemoration concentrates on closure. In 1928 there is an identifiable object: a vote, reasons, quotable sentences. The reopening offers no equivalent. Later accounts therefore revolve around the moment of closure, leaving the decade or more of opening almost blank for lack of quotable milestones. The construct's method of recording determines what posterity remembers, and what it records is often precisely its worst decisions.

The later completion of the women's programme can be traced through official results: 1,500 metres at Munich in 1972; 3,000 metres and the marathon at Los Angeles in 1984; 10,000 metres at Seoul in 1988; triple jump at Atlanta in 1996; pole vault and hammer at Sydney in 2000; and the 3,000-metre steeplechase at Beijing in 2008.

From 1928 to 2008: eighty years.

Along that line sit two events close together, offering a particularly clear comparison.

In 1966 Gibb joined the Boston Marathon from bushes beside the start and ran 3:21:40. She had no race number.

In 1967 Switzer obtained a number using her initials and became the first woman to complete Boston wearing one.

The construct responded to the two women quite differently.

For Gibb, the dispute concerned the organizers' doubt that she had covered the whole course. The response was to deny that the event had happened. She was not registered, so nothing could be cancelled. All that remained was to challenge whether the performance was valid, a challenge not readily resolved within the system because she had never been part of its timing and check-in procedures.

For Switzer, the response was a physical attempt to remove her from the course. She wore a number, appeared in the register, and was an identifiable entry.

The Thorpe pattern repeats. The person using his own name was processed; the aliases escaped. Here it is more direct: the registered person could be seized. The construct could do little to the unregistered person, and precisely for that reason could say she had not finished.

The two responses carried different costs. The attempt to seize Switzer produced a sequence of photographs. Those images became the incident's best-known evidence and worked strongly against the organizers. Doubt about Gibb left no such images, because doubt was a statement, not a physical act. When the construct acted on a visible entry, its own action became visible. When it dealt with an invisible person, its action remained invisible too. Visibility runs both ways, something it may not have anticipated.

A formal women's division was established in 1972. The organizers later recognized Gibb's pioneering-era victories.

## 7. Records first, admission afterward

In 1978 the international federation's women's committee requested world-record recognition and international competition status for the 5,000 and 10,000 metres.

The federation refused, saying worldwide participation had not reached a sufficiently high level.

In 1980 it recognized world records in both, but still did not include them in its then-existing world-championship and international-competition programme. Under the rule governing new Olympic events, they consequently could not make the 1984 Los Angeles programme.

Two things must be separated.

First is the sequence. Records came first; programme eligibility followed. For several years there were officially recognized world records in events that the recognizing institution did not admit to its major competitions. Measurement was operating before the gate of admission opened.

Essay 03 showed the reverse sequence: first draw the eligibility line, then measure those who crossed it. Here measurement came first, and admission only much later. They were the same two books in reversed order. Either way, some people remained caught between them.

Second is the reason itself.

Insufficient participation has a structural problem as a ground for refusal: an event's worldwide participation depends heavily on Olympic status. Without an Olympic event, countries do not allocate national-team places or training resources to it; young athletes do not choose that path. Participation remains low, and the threshold stays unmet.

The gate requires applicants to possess something obtainable only by passing through the gate.

This need not be a deliberately designed trap. Any institution controlling its own size needs a standard for what deserves inclusion. Popularity is among the most neutral and readily measurable standards available. The difficulty lies not in its intention but in its mathematical form: it makes an outcome into a prerequisite.

Such forms occur elsewhere. A technology needs widespread adoption to become cheap, and needs to become cheap to be widely adopted. A language needs users to be worth learning, and learners to acquire users. The difference is that those situations do not ordinarily place a switch in one institution's hands. This one does. When a loop contains an institution able to authorize an exception, it is no longer only a natural delay. It is also a choice that could have been made and was not. That matters in judgment without imputing malice to anyone.

In 1984 several women distance runners sued in California, asking the appellate court to require the addition of women's 5,000 and 10,000 metres to that year's Los Angeles Olympics. They argued that the IOC's new-event rule historically locked women into an inferior position and perpetuated structural discrimination against female athletes.

The Ninth Circuit upheld the refusal of a preliminary injunction. The majority held that the rule applied on its face equally to men and women, and therefore was not the kind of legal sex classification it understood to be at issue.

Allegations and judgment must be read separately.

The dissent expressly acknowledged that the early modern Olympics were marred by blatant discrimination against women, and argued for applying California's anti-discrimination law more favourably to the plaintiffs.

The case therefore did not find that the IOC had never discriminated. The majority found that, within this lawsuit's particular structure, the current rule did not justify immediately compelling additional events by judicial order. These are entirely different propositions, often collapsed in later retellings.

The majority's reasoning itself illustrates a recurring form in this series. A rule treats everyone equally in wording, but acts on a status quo made by an unequal history. It inherits the existing distribution and treats it neutrally. The neutrality is not necessarily a disguise; it is genuine. The important point is that neutrality toward an asymmetric starting point does not produce a symmetric result.

The majority must also be allowed its own place. The court faced not the general question of whether the rule was fair, but whether it could immediately order an international organization to add two events in this litigation. The answers may differ. Restricting itself to the second question was not necessarily evasion. The dissent addressed history; the majority addressed what this judicial intervention could accomplish. They were not discussing precisely the same thing, and their coexistence is no contradiction.

Whether the situation amounted to discrimination in law is another question, to which the 1984 majority and dissent gave different answers.

## 8. Two rooms

The gate closed in 1928 is open today.

The Paris Olympics achieved gender parity in athlete quota places. The IOC's own event totals were 157 men's events, 152 women's events, and twenty mixed events.

In athletics, the remaining asymmetry is chiefly no longer that women lack an event, but that corresponding events take different forms. Men run the 110-metre hurdles; women run the 100-metre hurdles, with different spacings and heights. Men's combined competition is the decathlon; women's is the heptathlon.

That change is one of the essay's most important points: exclusion on grounds of capacity has become differentiation through rules.

The rationale in 1928 was that women could not withstand the distance. Today's rationale invokes historical tradition, competitive comparability, and continuity of rules. The first makes a bodily claim that evidence can refute, and has refuted. The second cannot be refuted in that way because it makes no bodily claim. It says only that these events have always had these forms, and changing them would make earlier and later results incomparable.

This transition deserves fair treatment. Replacing a false assertion about bodies with a true assertion about continuity in the accounts is progress in every sense, forced by decades of evidence. The construct abandoned an untenable reason for a defensible one. What must remain visible is that the gate's position largely stayed fixed while its inscription changed. A construct can change its grounds without changing its shape, and criticism itself often drives the change of grounds.

And the continuity claim is true.

Decathlon and heptathlon are two event sets, two scoring tables, and two record lists. Their performances cannot be converted into one another. This is design, not oversight. To preserve comparisons across time within each, incomparability between them must be maintained indefinitely.

The structure will recur after Essay 05, but appears here with unusual clarity. The two ledgers run in parallel not because one defeated the other or one was invalidated, but because comparability can be achieved only within a ledger. Extending one column of numbers requires refusing to merge it with another. Each ledger purchases its internal integrity through incommensurability with the others.

Here the essay reconnects with Essay 01. The ancient victor list produced names rather than comparability, so four tracks of different lengths posed no problem. The later construct chose comparability. Once that became its purpose, it had continually to draw boundaries: which performances could stand together and which could not. Every boundary left something outside. Across eighty years, the grounds shifted from bodies to participation, then to tradition and continuity. The number of boundaries did not diminish.

Return to that afternoon in 1928.

Nine women finished a fast 800 metres. The results table recorded nine names and eight times; the ninth finisher Wever's time does not appear in common compilations. Film survived; the examining doctor left testimony. Neither determined what happened afterward.

What took effect was a handful of newspaper stories. Whether their authors stood at the finish line cannot now be established.

The construct possessed its own measurements. Its entire apparatus existed to produce the results table: timekeepers, judges, recording procedures. Then it looked at the athletes on the grass and set its own measurements aside.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
