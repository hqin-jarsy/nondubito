# The Construct Offered Closure; the Athletes Declined

## 1. The Official Said They Could Continue Before the Athlete Asked Whether They Could Stop

In the men's high-jump final at the Tokyo Olympics in 2021, Barshim and Tamberi had no failures through 2.37 metres, then each failed three times at 2.39. Even the existing countback rules left them exactly equal.

The official came over and spoke first.

What he said was not “I cannot decide” or “You should tie.” It was that they could proceed to a jump-off.

The first information the official supplied was that another procedure remained available. The construct held a means of continuing to separate winner from loser, and the official first laid that means before them.

Then Barshim asked a question.

He asked whether they could have two gold medals.

The official confirmed that this was possible.

That sequence is the whole key to this essay. It was not an official saying they could not be separated and therefore had to tie. It was an official saying that they could continue to be separated, and athletes asking whether they could choose not to be. The construct first explained that it still possessed the means to produce a winner; the athletes then asked whether they could decline to use those means.

This differs from the direction emphasized in many earlier essays. Measurements, records, eligibility lines, scoring tables and starting thresholds turned unsettled matters into actionable decisions. Here a way to continue was also ready: the jump-off. Yet the rules left the choice of taking that next step to the athletes still tied for first, acting jointly.

That is what this essay concerns. The construct has prepared the means of closure, and people may choose not to use them.

The episode turns a recurring question in this series around. Many earlier essays concerned how institutions turn complex situations into an actionable result. Here, another procedure was available, yet the institution allowed the athletes concerned jointly to decline it. What remained was not an uncertain placing but a definite, lawful shared first place. The absence of a sole winner is not the absence of a final answer.

Several popular accounts must first be corrected, because almost every stage of this episode has been misreported.

## 2. Several Accounts That Must First Be Dismantled

The first account to dismantle is that the Tokyo official proactively suggested awarding two gold medals.

He did not. The official first explained that a jump-off could continue; Barshim then asked whether two gold medals were possible. The athletes proposed not separating the result. The official supplied the option of continuing to separate it.

The second account is that the official could not tell who was stronger, so had no choice but to award two gold medals.

That is not what happened. This was not a problem of adjudicative capacity. The construct was fully capable of continuing to distinguish them. A jump-off was the mechanism: place the bar at a given height, see who clears it and who does not, and the distinction emerges. The construct was not unable to distinguish them; the athletes chose not to enter the procedure that would do so.

The third account is that the rules made an ad hoc exception because of their friendship.

They did not. The two men were good friends with shared experiences of injury. That helps explain why they wanted to share, but it does not explain the result's legality. Its validity rested on a rule that already existed. Even if the athletes had been strangers, or had hated one another, the rule should have produced the same result if they had made the same joint decision. Friendship was the motive, not the legal basis.

The fourth account, and the most important, is that World Athletics permitted shared gold medals only after Tokyo.

It did not. The provision allowing a tie for first place to stand appeared at least as early as the rules cycle effective from 1 November 2009, roughly twelve years before Tokyo.

This point deserves separate attention, because it is the case's institutional premise.

Under the 2008 rules, if athletes remained equal for first place after countback, they had to continue one jump at a time through the prescribed raising and lowering of the bar. Athletes had to jump in each round of the tie-break until the tie was resolved. There was no exit of the kind available today, in which everyone decides not to jump again and the tie for first remains.

The 2009 amendment introduced that exit: if no jump-off took place, including when the athletes concerned decided not to jump further, the tie for first place would stand. The new rule took effect on 1 November 2009.

Tokyo was therefore neither an innovation nor an exception. It was the use in 2021 of a rule written in 2009. That rule had lain quietly in the rulebook for twelve years. Tokyo merely let the whole world see it for the first time.

## 3. What Was Authorized Was Not Awarding Gold, but Leaving the Result Open

To describe this case accurately, we must see exactly what the rule authorized. It did not authorize an official to award two gold medals. It authorized something more precise and more fundamental.

The rule's legal structure is this: athletes still tied for first after countback should in principle have a jump-off. If no jump-off occurs, with the rule expressly including the athletes' decision not to continue among the possible circumstances, the tie for first place remains.

Look closely at that structure. The rule does not say that an official may award two gold medals. It says that in certain circumstances the tie for first remains. The Olympic medal system then awards a gold medal to each first-place finisher. Two gold medals are therefore a consequence of the placing, not a direct act of awarding an extra medal. The rule first preserves two first places; the medal system consequently gives each a gold. The athletes are not applying to an official for an additional gold medal.

That causal order determines where the power lies. If gold medals were the official's to distribute, this would be an exercise of discretion: the decision would belong to the construct, which could award or withhold them and decide how many. The actual mechanism is different. The rule stipulates that the tie remains in these circumstances. This is an automatic placing consequence, not an exercise of discretion. The official has no discretion here to choose one gold or two. He can only confirm the rule-prescribed placing triggered by the athletes' joint decision. The source of the decision therefore lies not in the official's discretion but in the athletes' shared choice. The official is the intermediate link translating that choice into a placing, which is then translated into a number of medals.

This is not verbal hair-splitting. It determines where the essay's subject lies. If an official awards the gold medals, the decision belongs to the official, to the construct. In fact, the construct preserves the placing of two firsts, and the athletes' joint decision not to jump again triggers that result. The decision therefore rests with the athletes.

That choice must be defined still more precisely, because it is easily described inaccurately.

The inaccurate version is: athletes can refuse a tie-break and keep their gold medal.

The accurate version is: all the athletes concerned who remain tied for the same first place may jointly terminate the tie-breaking procedure.

The difference is substantial. This is not a unilateral veto available to any individual. If one athlete alone decides not to jump and another wishes to continue, the one who declines forfeits the opportunity to take the higher place, and the willing jumper wins. Only when all athletes still tied for first jointly decide not to jump does the tie for first remain. This is a collective right to terminate, not an individual right to veto.

In Tokyo, only Barshim and Tamberi occupied that same first place. Nedasekau, who finished third, also cleared 2.37 metres, but earlier failures had placed him third on countback. It was not a choice among three athletes about sharing gold. The two athletes who were exactly equal, Barshim and Tamberi, jointly decided not to jump again.

## 4. The Athletes Did Not Rewrite the Rules. They Chose a Path the Rules Supplied.

We must guard here against portraying the athletes as rising above the rules, or the rules as ceasing to apply at this moment.

Neither happened. The athletes rewrote no rule. They chose one of two lawful paths that the rules already provided.

The first path: continue to a jump-off, allowing the construct to close the result into a single first place through further attempts.

The second path: all athletes still concerned in the tie for first jointly stop, and the construct accepts a shared first place.

Both paths had been written into the rules in advance. That declining further jumps could preserve the tie was itself an institutional provision, not a concession won by the athletes on the spot. They stood before two already laid paths and chose the second. Their choice was real, but they chose an option supplied by the construct, not one they had invented.

The essay's subject can therefore be stated precisely: the construct holds firmly onto the capacity for closure, but hands the switch determining whether to keep using that capacity to the people it judges.

Every part of that formulation matters.

The construct retains the capacity for closure. That is true. The jump-off is entirely its mechanism. Raising and lowering the bar, judging clearances and failures: these are all the construct's work. The athletes have no power to invent a tie-breaking method themselves.

But the switch that activates the mechanism is handed to the athletes. The construct says neither that they must be separated nor that they must remain tied. It relinquishes that meta-decision, the decision about whether to continue.

Those whom it judges receive the switch. This is the subtlest point. The athletes are the objects of the construct's judgment. It measures their performances and assigns their places. Yet at this one point, those objects of judgment receive the switch that determines whether the construct will complete its last step. The judge hands a decision to the judged.

## 5. One Institution, Four Occasions, Two Outcomes

The clearest evidence of this rule's character lies in the opposite results produced by its use in different competitions. The construct stays the same; the people choosing change, and so does the output.

In the men's high jump at the 2015 World Championships in Beijing, the institution was used to the end. Drouin, Bondarenko and Zhang Guowei remained tied for first after the main competition, and all three entered a jump-off. All failed first at 2.36 metres. The bar was lowered to 2.34, which Drouin cleared while the other two failed. Drouin became sole world champion, and the other two shared silver. The athletes chose to continue, and the construct closed the result into a single champion.

Tokyo 2021 was the case described above. The athletes jointly chose not to continue; the construct accepted a shared first place and two gold medals.

In the women's pole vault at the 2023 World Championships in Budapest, Tokyo's structure reappeared in another event. Moon and Kennedy remained equal after countback, chose not to have a jump-off, and shared the world title. World Athletics subsequently stated explicitly that the two agreed to share the title rather than continue with a jump-off.

In the men's high jump at the Paris Olympics in 2024, the same rule produced the opposite of Tokyo's result. Kerr and McEwen both cleared 2.36 metres in the main competition and remained equal on countback. They chose to continue with a jump-off. After further failures, the bar came down, and Kerr cleared 2.34 metres to become the sole gold medallist. McEwen later explicitly defended the choice to contest a single championship.

Place the four together and a pattern emerges. In 2015 they used the mechanism; in 2021 they did not; in 2023 they did not; in 2024 they did. The same institution, the same rule, four competitions, two outcomes. What determined which outcome appeared was not the rule, but the path chosen by the athletes standing at the fork.

That is the proof that Tokyo was not a one-off exception. It was no unrepeatable Olympic accident. It was a general, executable option within the vertical-jump rules. Paris supplies the most valuable counterexample: with the same institutional exit available, Kerr and McEwen chose to continue, turning the outcome from two golds into gold and silver. The construct remained unchanged; the choosers changed; the output changed from two gold medals to one gold and one silver.

Tokyo and Paris should therefore not be framed as warmth against cruelty, or friendship against competition. They embody two lawful normative judgments within the same institution. The Tokyo judgment is that the main competition has already left the athletes equal, and that equality itself is sufficient for them to be first together. The Paris judgment is that the construct should continue closing first place into a single value. The rule recognizes both judgments. Both are lawful.

Something deep deserves a pause here. The disagreement is not about who is right or wrong, but about what a tie itself means. The Tokyo view regards the tie as a genuine competitive result. If two athletes are exactly equal in the main competition and on countback, then they really are equal. That equality is the competition's true conclusion. A jump-off is an extra procedure artificially producing a single placing, rather than a means of revealing that the athletes were actually different. The Paris view treats a tie as a suspension awaiting resolution: the athletes are temporarily level, but the position of champion is inherently singular, so competition must continue until one wins. Both interpretations make sense. Their disagreement corresponds exactly to the question this series has kept asking: is equality a deadlock that must be broken further, or a result valid in its own right? The rule offers no single answer. It leaves the answer to those concerned.

## 6. A Man Who Endorsed His Choice but Opposed the Rule

The shared gold medal had supporters and critics. The real focus of their dispute is not whether the episode was moving, but who should possess the power of closure.

The supporting side is represented chiefly by official accounts. World Athletics described the result that day as shared success, and its year-end review placed it among the year's outstanding moments. Barshim's own argument was not that they could not be separated, but that the main competition had already demonstrated equal performances on the day and further competition was unnecessary. Tamberi emphasized shared experience, mutual respect and the value of sharing a championship. These interpretations all treat the act as an active choice, not a physical inability to continue or an official's instruction to stop.

The opposing side's central argument is that an Olympic championship should be closed into one person. Some commentators said at the time that a jump-off should have taken place, because Olympic finals should not share gold medals. Some athletics observers likewise expressed disappointment, believing that athletes should fight on until there was a single winner. Note that this opposition does not allege that Tokyo's decision was unlawful. It objects to the rule's values. It accepts the result's legality while arguing that the rule should not have this form.

One of the most substantial criticisms came from Tamberi himself.

A little over a month after Tokyo, Tamberi stated clearly that he did not regret sharing gold with Barshim. At the same time, he said that athletes should not have the opportunity to make that choice.

The statement almost directly strikes the subject of this essay. Tamberi endorsed the specific, personal decision made in Tokyo; he would choose that way. Yet he opposed the rule that handed this institutional decision to athletes. He believed the rule should not ask him to choose.

Those are two entirely different propositions. “I would choose this way” concerns how an individual acts under given rules. “The rules should let me choose” concerns how the rules themselves ought to be designed. Tamberi answered yes to the first and no to the second. Through his own example, he separated the propositions with complete clarity.

Someone who personally benefited from the rule and defends his choice simultaneously opposes the rule's existence. That alone shows that the real issue in this case is not the particular decision but whether the power to make it should be handed over. That is precisely the essay's subject: should the construct entrust its final step to the people it judges?

It is worth placing this question of the power of closure briefly within an earlier history, because two different roles of closure were already at issue when finish photography appeared. At Los Angeles in 1932 there were two cases of opposite character that must be kept separate. In the men's 100 metres, Tolan and Metcalfe both received official times of 10.3 seconds. The finish was extremely close, and officials carefully examined the photographic record. The official result was never changed to a tie: Tolan was first. A later claim often says that Metcalfe would have won under today's rules. That is a historically grounded counterfactual, because the old standard then required the entire torso to pass beyond the finish line, and Tolan fulfilled that standard earlier. But the counterfactual cannot be merged with the actual ruling. The stronger claim that the photography proved Metcalfe had won and the officials deliberately awarded Tolan the title lacks reliable support. What can be established is the controversy, the difference in rules, and the final decision for Tolan. At the same Games, the men's 110-metre hurdles presented the opposite case. Keller was initially placed third; after film review, Finlay was awarded third place and the bronze medal. Here photography did not manufacture controversy. It supplied the evidence that closed it. From the start, then, the construct's interpretive tools operated in two directions. They could be accused of failing to close a dispute that should have been closed, and they could genuinely settle a placing misjudged by the human eye. Those must be distinguished, not lumped together as “controversial cases.”

## 7. The Construct Can Look More Closely, and Can Also Choose Not To

To understand the weight of Tokyo's step, we must return it to the whole history of the construct's capacity for closure. That history has two directions: the construct continuously acquires stronger powers of closure, and the construct deliberately relinquishes powers it already possesses.

First consider growing capacity.

The Stockholm Olympics in 1912 used finish photography, which the IOC identifies as the technology's Olympic debut. This essay has not obtained enough material to reconstruct all the early system's parameters, so it assigns neither a frame rate nor millisecond accuracy to it. What can be established is that officials gained a recorded tool they could consult after observation by the human eye.

At Los Angeles in 1932, Kirby's two-eye system marked a qualitative change. Its camera ran at approximately 128 frames per second, a nominal frame interval of roughly 0.0078 seconds. Coupled with an electric clock, it could provide readings at the hundredth-of-a-second level. By Paris 2024, the official finish-line scanning system was reportedly capable of 40,000 recordings per second, a nominal sampling interval of approximately 25 microseconds.

The construct sees ever more finely. Its resolving capacity at the finish line now far exceeds the number of decimal places it publishes in official results.

There is also a crucial early phenomenon. The construct's capacity to see more finely does not necessarily mean that official results are published at the same resolution. In the 1932 men's 100 metres, Tolan and Metcalfe both had official times of 10.3 seconds, although automatic timing could yield readings at roughly the 10.38 level. One central function of photography was to establish order, not to turn every finer internal measurement into another official decimal place.

Today this is explicit. World Athletics' own photo-finish guidelines expressly allow rereading to the thousandth of a second for certain ties affecting qualification. Modern Olympic finish-line scanning samples far more finely still. It is therefore no longer tenable to say that athletics does not use thousandths because its machines are insufficiently precise. Not generally publishing official sprint times to thousandths is a choice of rules, not a camera's inability.

Two things need separating. Equipment can supply finer images than official results display, and rules use thousandths in certain qualification ties. But a scanning interval is not the accuracy of the entire measurement system: calibration, synchronization and interpretation still matter. Hundredths are therefore not simply the camera's ceiling, nor should every finer sample automatically become an official decimal place. Rules governing published times and placings decide which information has adjudicative force on which occasions. This has something in common with Tokyo: possessing more means of distinction does not mean always having to use them to the limit.

Now consider deliberate relinquishment, for which swimming provides the clearest example.

In the men's 400-metre individual medley at the 1972 Munich Olympics, Larsson and McKee were both timed at 4:31.98 to the hundredth. The equipment read further: 4:31.981 for Larsson and 4:31.983 for McKee. Larsson won gold by two thousandths of a second.

International swimming subsequently did something: it transformed the third decimal place from data capable of determining a placing into data that must not be used even if the equipment can read it. Its rules expressly require automatic times to be recorded only to hundredths and stipulate that the third decimal place must not be recorded or used to determine time or placing.

Swimming has a strong metrological reason for doing this. Pool geometry admits tolerances. For a 50-metre pool fitted with automatic touchpads, the standard length is 50 metres, with an allowed positive variation of approximately one centimetre. A top swimmer travels only a few millimetres in a thousandth of a second. A timer's ability to read thousandths therefore does not automatically guarantee that the race distances in two lanes are known accurately enough to support a fair comparison at that decimal place.

Caution is needed, however. To say that international swimming formally abolished thousandths because of pool tolerances makes a stronger causal claim than is usually acknowledged. What can be established are the two ends: rules explicitly prohibit using the third decimal place to decide times and placings, and facility specifications allow length tolerances. The policy change after 1972 is also widely associated with that race. But no sentence has been found in the meeting minutes from the time explicitly stating that thousandths were abolished because the pool tolerance was a particular amount. That rationale should therefore remain unresolved, rather than be presented as a fact supported by an original resolution already located. The often-quoted three-centimetre difference between lanes describes historical older specifications, not the present figure. Current tolerances are stricter.

Swimming and athletics thus exhibit two different forms of the construct's self-limitation. In swimming, the equipment has a third decimal place, but the rules deliberately deny it adjudicative force. In athletics, equipment and internal reading can extend to thousandths or finer, while official sprint times remain recorded to hundredths and placings are settled by the finish image. Both demonstrate that the capacity to measure and the legal force an institution chooses to give measurement are not the same thing.

## 8. A Reserved Box That May Lawfully Remain Unfilled

Let us draw the essay together.

Across the preceding sixteen essays, the construct repeatedly turned complex situations into names, records, placings and victories. Sometimes measurement and explanation remained apart; sometimes eligibility and definition left disputes; sometimes the institution chose not to make a certain judgment. What remained was not always a failure. It showed what a ledger could answer and what it left outside.

Here, for the first time, the construct does the opposite. It has not lost the capacity for closure. It holds the jump-off in its hands and can at any moment turn the Tokyo competition toward a single champion. It has deliberately and in advance legislated permission for an unclosed result to exist. It wrote a path into its own rules that leads to shared first place, a result not reduced to a single value.

That brings us to this essay's remainder, whose form reverses all the earlier ones.

Earlier remainders had different origins. Eligibility rules might exclude a name; surviving evidence might not separate causes; bodily categories might fail to accommodate continuous differences; a start reading might not explain how a movement began. Some involved limits of capacity, others institutional choices. They were not all things the construct wanted but could not obtain. Here the angle changes: the rules reserved a place in advance for the participants' joint choice.

This essay's remainder is what remains when the construct can close but chooses to make closure unnecessary. Shared first place is not the construct's failure, not an inability to distinguish winner from loser. Quite the opposite: it can distinguish them, and chooses not to require it. It is a box reserved in advance in the construct's own ledger that may lawfully remain incompletely filled.

Here a remainder takes another form. It need not be only a limit of measurement or a difficulty of classification; it can be space preserved when an institution deliberately stops pursuing a sole winner. The construct was not confounded by shared first place. It had reserved a lawful place for it. A result not reduced to one person can nevertheless be complete, lawful and final.

That acknowledgment carries weight. An adjudicative institution can easily treat a single answer as the mark of success, making a tie look unfinished. Tokyo shows another possibility: recognize the tie already produced by the competition and let those concerned jointly decline to continue. We need not infer that only powerful institutions dare do this, or that permitting ties signals weakness. The more important question is when further distinction matters, and when stopping together can also be a result an institution takes seriously.

It does so by handing over the switch. It does not decide for itself when a tie is appropriate. It gives that decision to the athletes still equal under the main-competition results and countback rules, allowing them to decide jointly. The construct acknowledges something here: when its own measurements have judged two people exactly equal, when the main-competition results and countback figures are identical, perhaps it should not alone determine whether an additional procedure must force them apart.

This contrasts with Essay 16. There, research recommended lowering the start threshold while the rules retained 0.100 seconds; findings still had to travel into formal revision. Here, a path already written into the rules lets athletes tied for first jointly decide whether to enter a jump-off. One case asks how a standard is reconsidered; the other shows how an institution reserves a choice in advance. Both concern who decides the final step, but not a simple opposition between refusing evidence forever and relinquishing all control.

Retention and delegation are different ways to handle the final step. Some standards need common application while remaining open to evidence and formal revision. Other outcomes already have an exit through which those concerned jointly decide whether to continue. The construct need not pursue a sole winner in every matter. It can also provide that an existing tie is a result, and that the next step is for the people it judges to decide together.

This construct can record every attempt made by two high jumpers, calculate that their results are exactly equal, and raise a bar at any moment for another jump to separate them. It did not do so, not because it could not, but because many years earlier it had written another path for precisely this moment, a path allowing them to say there was no need. It prepared every means of distinguishing winner from loser, then placed the final decision about using those means back in the hands of the two people the main competition and countback had not separated.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
