# Do Not Ask What Was Intended

## 1. A Rule That Does Not Ask About the Mind

There is a rule in the anti-doping system whose core is a refusal to ask.

Article 2.1 of the World Anti-Doping Code defines a violation by the presence of a prohibited substance, its metabolites or markers in an athlete's sample. Article 2.1.1 immediately adds that it is each athlete's personal duty to ensure that no prohibited substance enters their body.

Together these provisions establish strict liability. Within the applicable scope of prohibition and the relevant detection and decision criteria, establishing presence under Article 2.1 generally does not additionally require proof of intent, fault, negligence or knowing use. This does not mean that any trace detected by an instrument automatically constitutes a violation. Decision limits, special reporting or evaluation criteria, and valid therapeutic use exemptions must also be considered. Presence or use consistent with a valid exemption is not a violation, rather than merely a ground for reducing a later penalty. Whenever this essay says presence establishes a violation, it assumes these prerequisites have been satisfied. What the rule does not ask about is intention, not whether its conditions of application are met.

That refusal to ask is our starting point.

Earlier essays examined judgements about things that cannot be directly seen. Essay 13 asked how a body would perform under different conditions; Essay 14 asked which bodily conditions should enter competition eligibility. No ready-made reading supplies the whole answer. Here the choice is different. When establishing the presence of a prohibited substance in a sample, the construct does not make proof of an athlete’s state of mind a necessary condition. It first establishes that violation through external evidence satisfying the applicable standards, leaving intention and fault for later judgements.

But this refusal has a crucial boundary. Ignoring it means misunderstanding the entire rule.

## 2. Not Asking About Intention Does Not Mean Automatic Severe Punishment

One widespread claim is that strict liability means an automatic four-year ban.

Legally, that claim is wrong.

The Code separates whether a violation occurred from what consequences follow. Strict liability means that, once the prerequisites already described are satisfied, establishing presence does not additionally require proof of intent. But not every consequence waits for a finding about fault: Article 9 automatically disqualifies the relevant individual result when the violation is connected with an in-competition test. Intention and fault are highly relevant to the period of ineligibility and its reduction under Article 10. No Fault can eliminate that period without automatically eliminating every competitive consequence.

The two-stage structure needs to be clearly understood. The first stage, establishing the violation, is strict and does not examine intention. In the second stage, determining ineligibility and its reduction requires examination of intention and fault; that does not displace the result-disqualification rules just described. Under Article 10.5, if an athlete establishes No Fault or Negligence, the otherwise applicable period of ineligibility may be eliminated entirely. Under Article 10.6, where there is fault but it is not significant, the period may be reduced.

Strict liability does not make fault irrelevant. It separates establishing a violation from how intention and fault affect ineligibility. A no-fault finding may coexist with disqualification of a competition result while eliminating a ban. To equate strict liability with an automatic four years collapses these different questions into one. It is wrong.

Nor was the principle invented from nothing. Before the World Anti-Doping Code existed, the Court of Arbitration for Sport was already reasoning in terms of strict liability, most famously in the 1995 Quigley case. The 2003 Code harmonised the principle internationally; it did not create it from scratch.

Here the construct has designed something ingenious. It faces a dilemma. If every positive finding requires proof of intention, deliberate users can escape whenever they can plausibly deny intent, because intention is internal and extremely difficult to prove. But imposing uniformly severe penalties on presence alone would wrong the genuinely innocent, such as athletes who inadvertently ingest contaminated supplements. The solution separates the issues. When establishing the violation, the construct does not ask about intent, so denying intent provides no escape. When determining consequences, it carefully examines fault, allowing an athlete without fault an opportunity to avoid a ban, though not automatically every competitive consequence. One stage does not ask; the other does.

## 3. Where the Criticism Falls Between the Two Stages

Whether strict liability is justified is a dispute with substantial arguments on both sides. This essay presents them without adjudicating between them.

The supporting case has four parts.

Evidentiary feasibility. Intention is internal and often extremely difficult to prove. If anti-doping organisations had to prove conscious cheating in every positive case, deliberate doping could escape whenever it could plausibly be denied.

Institutional workability. In Quigley, the tribunal emphasised the cost and complexity that litigation over intention would impose on sporting bodies. Uniform liability based on presence greatly simplifies adjudication.

Competitive fairness. The harm to other competitors exists whether ingestion was intentional or accidental. Disqualification of results therefore protects the field as a whole, rather than merely punishing an individual.

And proportionality achieved through the second stage. Supporters argue that potential unfairness should be addressed not by weakening Article 2.1's finding of a violation, but by using Articles 10.5 and 10.6 to eliminate or reduce ineligibility where fault is absent or low.

The opposing argument is also more complex than a general insistence that intention should always be required.

It distinguishes correcting a competition from blaming a person. Disqualifying a result may be defensible because opponents were objectively disadvantaged. Declaring that an athlete committed a violation, however, may have lasting reputational, sponsorship, psychological and career consequences even when no period of ineligibility is ultimately imposed. The two should not be conflated.

Critics point out that mitigation often requires athletes to reconstruct the source of an accidentally ingested substance, which may be practically impossible in contamination cases. Unequal resources matter: access to specialist lawyers and scientific experts affects an athlete's ability to establish the source and degree of fault. Administrative efficiency alone, they argue, is not sufficient justification for imposing severe disciplinary consequences within an asymmetrical regulatory system.

Both cases have force. They do so in a way familiar from this series: they stand on different values. One gives priority to competitive fairness and institutional workability; the other to protecting individuals against blame without fault. Both values are real.

## 4. The Same Case, Two Opposite Decisions

Strict liability's two-stage structure is clearest in contamination cases. Here allegations and findings must be kept rigorously separate.

Lawson's sample produced a positive finding associated with exposure to an anabolic substance. The first-instance disciplinary tribunal found an Article 2 violation and rejected his arguments about source and absence of fault, imposing a four-year ban.

Notice the wording. The first-instance tribunal found a violation and rejected his defence. At that stage, contamination was his explanation, not an established fact.

He then appealed to the Court of Arbitration for Sport. On source and fault, the tribunal reached the opposite evidentiary conclusion. Applying the relevant balance-of-probabilities standard, it accepted the contaminated-beef explanation, found No Fault or Negligence and eliminated the period of ineligibility.

That reversal did not reject strict liability. On the contrary, it is a textbook example of separating the Article 2.1 violation from the fault-based consequences under Article 10. The violation stood at both stages: the substance was present. What changed was the conclusion about fault. The first tribunal considered him at fault; the appellate tribunal found no fault. The same fact of presence constituted a violation in both proceedings; the same question of source produced opposite conclusions.

Simpson and Powell provide another version of the same principle. Both Jamaican sprinters' positive findings were traced to a supplement. The first-instance decisions imposed eighteen-month bans. On appeal, CAS accepted the relevant evidence about the supplement source and reduced each ban to six months.

Together, these cases show how contamination operates. It does not make a positive finding disappear. Presence was never overturned. Contamination changed the assessment of the sanction. For Lawson, the four-year period of ineligibility was eliminated; for Simpson and Powell, the bans were reduced from eighteen months to six. The violation remained a violation. Fault and consequences changed.

We must also avoid an excessively strong claim: that contamination automatically exonerates an athlete. It does not. Neither supplement nor meat contamination is an automatic defence. Source, credibility and the athlete's degree of fault remain contested questions. Lawson's case shows that different tribunals can reach opposite evidentiary conclusions in the same matter. Supplement contamination also has an empirical basis: an analysis conducted in 2001–2002 examined 634 non-hormonal nutritional supplements and found approximately 14.8 percent contaminated with undeclared anabolic steroids or prohormones. But 14.8 percent is not a valid estimate of the entire global supplement market today, and the wider ranges found subsequently are not a single population prevalence rate.

## 5. An Hour That Must Be Declared Every Day

Strict liability governs what happens once a sample has been collected. Collecting it first requires finding the athlete, which brings us to another system: whereabouts reporting.

Athletes in a Registered Testing Pool who are subject to the full whereabouts regime must designate one specific sixty-minute period every day, at a specified location, during which they must be available and accessible for testing. Current rules confine that slot to the permitted daytime range, presently between 5 a.m. and 11 p.m.

A common misunderstanding needs correcting. Article 2.4 does not say three missed tests. It says any combination of three filing failures and missed tests within twelve months. One filing failure plus two missed tests can therefore constitute a violation just as three qualifying missed tests can. Nor does a testing officer's failure to find an athlete at a random moment itself constitute a missed test. A formal missed test requires a documented testing attempt during the designated sixty-minute slot. A discrepancy in location or an unanswered knock does not automatically become one of the three failures under Article 2.4.

The regime poses a real challenge to privacy, and that challenge has formally reached the courts. French athlete organisations and individual athletes challenged whereabouts requirements before the European Court of Human Rights, arguing that intrusive obligations concerning location and availability interfered with private and family life.

The Court delivered its judgement on 18 January 2018. What it decided must be stated precisely.

It accepted that whereabouts obligations significantly interfered with private life. It did not say there was no interference. It also held, however, that for the purposes of private life and Article 8 of the Convention, the interference was justified and proportionate, serving legitimate aims including health and protection of others. It therefore found no violation of Article 8.

Conflating no violation with no interference misrepresents the judgement. The Court expressly acknowledged interference; it considered that interference justified. These are entirely different statements. The construct did not say whereabouts reporting leaves privacy untouched. It said the intrusion can be justified.

Proportionality remained disputed after the judgement. Supporters emphasise that meaningful out-of-competition testing requires genuinely unannounced access, and that a narrower system would create predictable testing gaps. Critics focus on the burden of surveillance, intrusion into domestic space, data confidentiality and athletes' continuing obligation to arrange private life around regulatory access. Empirical research has found an important middle position: many elite athletes strongly support anti-doping in principle while expressing substantial dissatisfaction with the practical burden of whereabouts requirements.

## 6. A Sample Ten Years Later

Strict liability and whereabouts govern the present. Another system extends the construct's reach into the past: long-term sample storage and retrospective testing.

The relevant time limit was extended from eight years to ten in the 2015 revision. Under the current framework, stored samples may be retained and validly reanalysed for up to ten years after collection.

Reanalysis is targeted, not automatic. Long-term storage creates a legal opportunity. When analytical capabilities improve, new intelligence or investigative information emerges, or a strategic testing rationale makes a stored sample worth revisiting, the construct can analyse it further. Olympic programmes therefore select a subset of stored samples, rather than indiscriminately retesting every tube from a Games.

As to scale, during the major reanalysis waves in 2016, the International Olympic Committee announced a combined total of 1,243 selected samples from Beijing 2008 and London 2012. By December 2020, further analysis associated with those Games had produced more than 130 violations. These numbers must be kept separate. The number of selected samples belongs to the testing stage; the number of violations represents legal outcomes developed through results management. They do not share a denominator and are not interchangeable.

The retrospective system has had concrete consequences for medal allocation. A peer-reviewed analysis found that seventy-six medal results were associated with violations arising from IOC retesting of the 2004, 2008 and 2012 Games. These accounted for 57 percent of all doping-affected medal results in that study's historical dataset.

The analysis also gives a measure of delay for these affected medal results: the mean interval from the Games to announcement of the retrospective violation was 6.8 years, reported together with its dispersion as 6.8 ± 2.0 years. That does not mean every case lay within two years of the mean.

The 6.8-year figure is easily misquoted. It measures the interval from the Games to announcement of a retesting violation, not the wait until an athlete actually receives a reallocated medal. Appeals, federation revisions, IOC Executive Board decisions, return or non-return of original medals and the athlete’s choice of ceremony may still intervene. The study examined here supplies no global mean for actual medal receipt. Its 6.8 years cannot be transferred to answer that different question.

Actual delays can be very long. Paris 2024 hosted a special ceremony for ten Olympians receiving reallocated medals associated with Sydney 2000, Beijing 2008 and London 2012. Some intervals from competition to ceremony thus reached approximately twelve to twenty-four years.

## 7. A Medal More Than a Decade Late

A medal arriving more than a decade late raises a question this series keeps encountering: can a belated correction repair the original wrong?

There are two opposing answers. This essay does not choose between them.

One says yes. Reallocation corrects the official record, restores medals and enables ceremonial recognition of the athlete who should have occupied that position. The IOC now provides formal reallocation ceremonies instead of merely mailing medals or transferring them administratively. That itself acknowledges that a medal deserves a ceremony.

The other says no, or at least not completely. Neither a substitute ceremony nor a corrected database can recreate what has been lost: sponsorship, prize-money opportunities, media exposure, national celebrations and, above all, the original moment on the Olympic podium. That moment occurred more than a decade earlier, at the peak of an athlete's career, on a night when the world was watching. However solemn the reallocation ceremony, it is not that night.

Both answers point towards something recurring in this series. The construct can correct its ledger, but cannot correct what lies outside it. It can reallocate medals, rewrite names in the record and hold a replacement ceremony. These are corrections within the ledger. Everything the athlete lost on the night that should have belonged to them, the real things that happened beyond the accounts, cannot be recovered by the construct. It can make the record right. It cannot turn time back.

The IOC’s provision of formal reallocation ceremonies can be read as a response to that loss. Sending a piece of metal and bringing someone before a public to recognise their achievement are not the same act. Ceremony gives belated recognition some weight and may offer a measure of repair. It still cannot return the original night. That is one meaning we can read in the practice, without presenting it as the institution’s fully stated or exclusive motive.

## 8. An Exception That Takes Not Asking to an Extreme

This essay concerns how the construct operates its anti-doping system by not asking about intention. History also contains an extreme version of not asking. It was not part of this system, but an extreme instance of what the system seeks to prevent. The structure must be made clear, because its nature differs entirely from every earlier refusal to ask.

The earlier refusal was a procedural design. Anti-doping rules do not ask about an athlete's state of mind when establishing presence, preventing deliberate users from escaping by denying intent. At the second stage, they carefully examine fault, leaving a way out for the innocent. This refusal concerns an athlete's conduct and is balanced by a second stage that does ask about fault. Its purpose is to protect clean competition and athletes who have not doped.

In East Germany, however, there was a state-directed programme with the archival designation State Plan 14.25.

Here the manner of narration must change.

The Stasi Records Archive within the German Federal Archives confirms that State Plan Topic 14.25, beginning in 1974, was a state-directed doping programme incorporated into a broader sports-research structure. This is an archival finding, not an inference from medal totals. Documents used terms such as supporting means as euphemistic, coded administrative language for the drug programme. That official terminology must be distinguished from what any individual athlete was told.

The records document covert administration, including administration to minors. Athletes, in some circumstances particularly minors, were sometimes given substances in disguised form, such as tablets described as vitamins. An original archival study independently documented state-funded androgen administration to minors of both sexes, emphasising female and adolescent athletes in particular.

The total number remains a matter of scholarly dispute. From recovered confidential material, that archival study concluded that thousands of athletes annually received androgens, including minors. This is a major archival reconstruction, much stronger than journalistic conjecture, but not a complete register of every affected athlete. Later single-number estimates should therefore be described as estimates. Their results depend on the period, the definition of doping, drug categories and the completeness of surviving records. They are not a number established by a Berlin criminal court. An exact age distribution cannot reliably be calculated from the available archives. The involvement of minors is established; a particular age percentage is not.

The Berlin prosecution initially concerned 142 cases. That is a charging figure, not the number of offences found in the final judgement. On 18 July 2000, the Berlin Regional Court sentenced former East German sports chief Ewald to twenty-two months' imprisonment, suspended, for aiding bodily injury through covert drug administration to twenty high-level female athletes. Sports physician Höppner received eighteen months, also suspended. On 5 September 2001, the Federal Court of Justice dismissed Ewald's appeal, making the conviction final. Its judicial record characterised the relevant practice as centrally state-directed administration of harmful doping substances to athletes without their knowledge.

The Berlin judgement also has limits. It did not judicially establish an exact total, find that every recipient was a minor, or find that every recipient was given precisely the same story about vitamins. Its criminal findings concern the cases proved before that court. The wider system is independently established by archives, but a criminal judgement must not be inflated into a census.

We can now state the structural judgement. It stands here separately, even in its syntax, from the preceding procedural discussion.

The anti-doping system's refusal to ask concerns what an athlete did, leaving fault to the second stage. State Plan 14.25's instruction not to ask did not concern what athletes had done. It demanded that they not ask what they were being given. The former suspends inquiry into a legal category of mental state. The latter strips a person of the right to know what enters their body. An unknowing athlete, including a minor, was administered harmful substances, while the phrase supporting means packaged the act as an administrative procedure. A person was treated as a means to an end. That cannot simply be narrated past in a neutral voice. Once one side violates the fundamental requirement to treat persons as ends, neutrality no longer applies.

## 9. What Krieger Says Himself

One person's experience of State Plan 14.25 has been recounted especially often, and is also especially liable to misrepresentation. Here we therefore use only what he has publicly said himself.

The German National Anti-Doping Agency's archival project records that Krieger entered an elite children's and youth sports school in 1979 at fourteen, later training at Dynamo Berlin. In 1981, when he was approximately fifteen or sixteen, he began receiving blue tablets later identified as an anabolic steroid. They did not come in normal manufacturer packaging. His coach described them as something that would help sporting performance. Krieger did not know the pharmacological identity of what he was given. Establishing these facts requires no dosage information.

In later first-person interviews, Krieger directly described the institutional environment. He said they did not question the tablets because in East Germany they were expected to trust their coaches. In another interview, he described discovering the scale of the deception and realising that officials had treated him as a laboratory rat without informed permission. These statements are presented as Krieger's own testimony, not as an inference about every East German athlete's knowledge.

Krieger has publicly described substantial androgenic, masculinising physical changes during the years of steroid administration, linking this involuntary hormone exposure with a sense that control over the development and interpretation of his own body had been taken away. The appropriate evidentiary formulation is that he reported those changes, while steroid administration itself is documented.

One crucial point must be made explicit.

Krieger has also said that uncertainty concerning gender preceded doping. His own account does not begin with an unambiguous prior gender identity suddenly transformed by drugs. Interview accounts describe discomfort or uncertainty concerning his body and identity before the doping period. His later argument concerns involuntary hormonal intervention depriving him of the ability to discover and develop his identity on his own terms.

The popular headline claim that East German drugs made Krieger transgender therefore goes beyond his account and conflicts with it in an important respect, because he acknowledges that uncertainty about identity preceded doping.

These are the claims that can be made: officials administered androgenic drugs without informed consent; he experienced substantial physical effects; and he believes the intervention impaired his autonomy in understanding his identity. The deterministic claim that drugs caused a transgender identity should not be attributed to him.

This follows the same discipline as Essay 15's dismantling of deterministic narratives. There, a bodily characteristic was narrated as the cause of an outcome without the intervening causal chain ever being measured. Here, drug exposure is narrated as the cause of an identity, while Krieger's own account complicates rather than supports that deterministic assertion. The causal question cannot be settled by Stasi archives, Berlin convictions or Krieger's retrospective testimony. The construct can establish that administration occurred and record physical changes. It cannot establish a counterfactual identity, saying who a person would have been without that exposure. Like the nonexistent body in Essay 13, that counterfactual cannot be observed.

## 10. The System, and the Individual

After State Plan 14.25, Russia offers one of the clearest comparisons. But the comparison requires care.

The World Anti-Doping Agency's independent investigation and the IOC's independent commission both found an institutionalised, state-linked Russian system involving manipulation of anti-doping processes and protection of doped athletes. Core evidence included laboratory records, documents, witness evidence and forensic investigations. This met the threshold for a documented, systematic state role.

A legally essential distinction must nevertheless be maintained: establishing a system and establishing an individual's violation are separate propositions.

In litigation arising from the Russian investigations, CAS repeatedly clarified this distinction. Even where a panel was satisfied that an overarching manipulation scheme existed, the IOC or another prosecuting party still had to present sufficiently compelling evidence connecting the particular athlete to conduct constituting a violation. Some Russian athletes therefore won individual appeals despite the wider systemic evidence. The existence of a scheme cannot serve as collective guilt.

Nor should the Russian and East German systems be described as institutionally identical. The East German evidence includes a named state research and programme structure covering drug research, administration and secrecy. The strongest Russian findings concern state-linked manipulation of doping controls, laboratory results and sample processes, together with protection of athletes. Both meet the threshold for a documented systematic state role, but the documentary architectures and conduct proved are different.

This distinction leads to the final safeguard of this essay. A documented state doping system does not prove that every athlete from that country doped. A state cover-up does not establish that every protected or named athlete knew of it. Nor does a historically clean, negative sample by itself establish that a particular athlete never used prohibited drugs. CAS's insistence on an individual connection is precisely the barrier against turning systemic evidence into guilt based on nationality.

Excluding another country from this highest evidentiary category does not establish that systematic doping never occurred there. It establishes that the required evidentiary threshold has not been met in the available material. Longstanding allegations do not establish a system. This is consistent with the discipline of the essay throughout: allegations are not findings; evidence of a system is not individual guilt; and presence establishes a violation at the first stage, while only the second determines what an individual should bear.

Return to that refusal to ask.

The construct described here establishes violations without asking about intention, then determines consequences by asking about fault. The design aims, in a world where intention cannot readily be proved, both to prevent deliberate users from escaping and to avoid wronging the innocent. It is an ingenious, defensible and continually disputed balance. Behind the need for it, and for such care in drawing the boundary between not asking and asking, lies something it seeks to protect: every athlete, whether or not they have used drugs, should compete in clean conditions, with knowledge and the ability to take responsibility for their own body.

State Plan 14.25 shows what remains when that protection is taken away entirely. It is not a failure of the construct, but the opposite of the reason for the construct's existence. The construct draws its boundary of not asking precisely to prevent the recurrence of that other instruction not to ask, the one that treats a person as a means.

A sample test can identify a substance at an extremely low concentration. That reading alone cannot encompass how an unknowing person was treated. Archives, testimony, medicine and courts can still establish administration and harm, and pursue responsibility; those efforts are not powerless. They cannot, however, fully return lost knowledge, bodily autonomy or years of a life. People who later spent their lives understanding what had happened to them are more than a concentration in a test. The ruler can clarify some facts without settling the whole account of a person’s loss.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
