# The Body the Construct Cannot Place

## I. A question that cannot be observed

First make the question precise. The whole essay hangs on it, and it cannot be answered directly.

An athlete who runs on carbon-fibre prostheses seeks admission to open competition against athletes without prostheses. The governing body must decide whether his prostheses give him an overall competitive advantage over athletes who do not use them.

The standard form of that judgment is counterfactual: if this athlete had intact biological limbs instead of prostheses, how fast would he run in the same event?

That question cannot be observed directly.

The version of him with intact biological limbs does not exist. His lower legs are absent. No experiment can give him biological lower legs, have him run, and compare the result with his prosthetic performance. For an athlete with absent limbs, that state of the world can never be experimentally observed.

Pause over this, because it differs from every earlier uncertainty in the series. Altitude's contribution to Beamon's jump existed in principle but was entangled with other factors. That was a difficulty of separation. A shoe's energy saving can in principle be measured, though individual differences are large. That is a difficulty of variation. Here the difficulty is more fundamental: the object required for comparison is not in the world. It is not hidden or too small to measure. It has never been produced. Once a biological lower leg is absent, the performance it might have produced is absent from the observable world too. The construct's reference point is a vacancy.

Researchers can only approach it indirectly: compare the athlete with others, change his prosthetic configuration, simulate, reconstruct through anthropometry, make statistical predictions, or combine these methods. In the Leeper case, the Court of Arbitration for Sport made the limitation clear: a prediction of an athlete's intact-limb performance is necessarily an informed estimate rather than a scientifically verifiable fact, and includes uncertainty that cannot be eliminated.

This therefore differs from the essays that precede it.

Earlier problems retained some observable factual core. The altitude contribution to Beamon's jump could not be disentangled, but the variables existed and had been measured. Whether advanced shoes should count in Essay 12 was a value question, but both sides relied on observable data. Here the problem is not merely an unresolvable calculation or a value judgment. The fact to be judged does not physically exist and cannot be seen.

How does the construct adjudicate when a fact cannot be observed?

This is a wall every system of measurement will eventually encounter. A construct devoted to measurement bases its authority on what can be seen and weighed, yet some things it must judge fall beyond that reach. Earlier invisible things were still approachable: a lost original document, an entangled cause, an unchosen option. Here the invisible thing is a body that never existed. When the construct's central powers, seeing and measuring, fail because there is no object to see or measure, it must either acknowledge its inability or find a way to decide without observing that object. The burden of proof supplies that way.

That is this essay's subject. The answer is surprising but entirely intelligible: when truth cannot be determined, adjudication retreats to a prior question. Who bears the cost of not being able to determine it? Allocating the burden of proof answers that question. Where the evidence remains unresolved, that allocation becomes the decision.

## II. Three steps that must remain separate

Before discussing Pistorius, establish a discipline. This essay is easiest to misunderstand at precisely this point. Three things must be kept separate.

First, a laboratory measures a mechanical or physiological difference.

Second, a governing body calls the difference an advantage.

Third, a tribunal decides whether the governing body has proved an overall competitive advantage under a particular rule.

These are three different propositions. Measuring a difference does not establish that it is an advantage. Calling it an advantage does not legally prove that it is an overall advantage.

Ordinary discussion almost always collapses the distinctions. People hear that a prosthesis returns more elastic energy than a biological ankle and intuitively conclude that it must confer an advantage, and therefore that its user must benefit unfairly. But greater elastic-energy return is an isolated mechanical fact. To establish an advantage, one must show that its benefits over the whole event exceed accompanying disadvantages such as lower force production, slower acceleration, or poorer stability. To establish an overall advantage legally, positive and negative factors must then be weighed against a standard of proof. Between additional joules returned and an overall competitive benefit lie two wide gaps. The unobservable counterfactual runs through both.

Take Pistorius. In 2007 the IAAF commissioned Brüggemann's team at the German Sport University Cologne to conduct tests. One bilateral below-knee amputee, Pistorius, and five non-amputee sprinters ran sixty metres on an indoor straight. The study identified lower mechanical work during stance, a substantially different distribution of work among the prosthetic ankle, knee, and hip, lower limb mass, and a high ratio of mechanical energy returned to energy absorbed by the blades. The researchers inferred that the prostheses might permit the same sprint performance at a lower metabolic cost.

That is the first step: measurement and inference. The researchers themselves described the measurements as leading to a hypothesis. A hypothesis, not a conclusion. It was an inference from mechanical work, not a direct experimental reconstruction of the nonexistent athlete with biological limbs.

The IAAF's official summary went further, stating that Pistorius used approximately twenty-five percent less energy than runners without prostheses at the same speed. That is the second step: interpreting the measured difference as an advantage.

The disputed question was not whether mechanical differences had been measured. They had. It was whether those measurements established an overall race advantage after starts, acceleration, bend running, fatigue, force-production limits, and all disadvantages were included.

That third step is legal adjudication, not laboratory measurement. Keeping the three apart is the foundation of the essay.

They must remain separate because each supports a different degree of certainty. Measurement can establish mechanical numbers. Calling the difference an advantage is already an interpretation that places it within a complete race. Deciding whether overall advantage has been proved is a legal act weighing evidence in both directions against a proof standard. Collapsing the steps commonly lends the certainty of the first to the third: because the mechanical difference is established, people assume the overall advantage is established too. In fact, certainty diminishes along the route from a definite mechanical difference to a disputed advantage to an overall net advantage that cannot be directly observed.

## III. Who bears the burden of proof?

On 14 January 2008, the IAAF Council found that Pistorius's Cheetah prostheses violated Rule 144.2(e) and declared him ineligible to compete under IAAF rules while using them.

This was an eligibility decision, not merely a scientific statement.

Pistorius appealed to CAS. On 16 May 2008, following a two-day hearing, CAS upheld his appeal, immediately revoked the IAAF Council's decision, and declared him eligible to compete in IAAF-sanctioned events using the prostheses examined in the case.

The crucial point was not that CAS found the prostheses conferred no advantage. It did not make that finding. The crucial point was who bore the burden of proof.

The 2007 Rule 144.2(e) prohibited devices that provided an advantage. Grammatically and legally, the prohibited fact was the existence of advantage. Excluding Pistorius therefore required proving that advantage existed. The party asserting it, the IAAF, had to provide the proof.

The award records that the IAAF accepted the burden of proof, a point the tribunal did not need to discuss further.

That is the essay's first pivot.

Accepting the burden meant the IAAF had to establish an overall net advantage. It failed to do so. CAS observed that the Cologne protocol concentrated on straight-line running at specified speeds without sufficiently incorporating starts, acceleration, bend running, and all countervailing disadvantages. The evidence did not establish overall advantage to the required standard. Under the burden-of-proof rule, the party making the claim lost.

Notice what happened. CAS did not say that the prostheses had no advantage. It said that the party alleging advantage had not proved it to the required standard. Those are entirely different statements. One asserts a fact; the other adjudicates proof. Because the central counterfactual could not be directly observed, the tribunal could make the latter judgment without establishing the former.

The standard was the civil standard, usually called the balance of probabilities. When the evidence is evenly balanced, the party bearing the burden loses.

The rule becomes especially visible when the evidence does not meet the required standard. Prosthesis cases rely on comparison, estimates and models because the central counterfactual cannot be directly observed, leaving uncertainty difficult to eliminate. But unobservability does not condemn the evidence to an eternal tie. Leeper’s case below shows that indirect evidence can still satisfy the balance of probabilities. The burden allocates the consequences of insufficient proof; it does not declare every such case decidable only by default.

Pistorius therefore won not because his prostheses were proved to confer no advantage, but because the IAAF bore the burden and failed to establish advantage on the balance of probabilities. The burden's direction determined who bore the consequences of the unresolved factual question. Those consequences were the decision itself.

## IV. An amendment that reversed the direction

CAS expressly limited the decision's scope. It did not apply to another athlete or another prosthesis. Later cases had to be assessed on their own facts.

Pistorius's victory therefore concerned Pistorius and the specific prostheses examined. It created no general exemption for prosthetic devices.

That narrowness later mattered greatly, because the rule changed.

In 2015, after controversy concerning Rehm and legal advice, the IAAF revised the provision. New Rule 144.3(d) prohibited mechanical aids unless the athlete established, on the balance of probabilities, that the aid would not provide an overall competitive advantage over athletes not using it.

That sentence reversed the direction completely.

Under the old rule, the prohibited fact was the existence of advantage, and the IAAF had to prove it. Under the new rule, the condition for permission was the absence of advantage, and the athlete seeking permission had to prove that absence.

Not one laboratory measurement changed. The science remained the science. The counterfactual remained unobservable. Only the consequences of inconclusive evidence changed. Under the old rule, insufficient evidence meant the IAAF lost and the athlete could compete. Under the new one, insufficient evidence meant the athlete lost and could not compete.

The same unresolved fact could therefore yield opposite outcomes under two rules. No new scientific discovery was required. Reversing the burden of proof was sufficient.

Here the essay's central proposition appears in full: where the decisive fact cannot be observed, the practical content of an inconclusive adjudication is an allocation of the cost of not knowing. A change in a rule's wording can reverse that allocation, leading the same evidence toward the opposite outcome.

The 2015 rule assigned the legal risk of uncertainty to the athlete. Uncertain evidence meant the exception had not been established, so the athlete could not compete.

Fairness requires acknowledging that placing the burden on the athlete had its own rationale. It was not simply obstruction. The IAAF's position was that the athlete controlled the device's use and configuration and possessed the relevant training history and personal performance data. Requiring an applicant for an exception to prove the case was considered administratively workable and protective of competitive integrity. That reasoning has weight. In many contexts, a person seeking an exception can reasonably be asked to show that it is harmless. The problem was this context's unusual feature: what had to be proved depended on an unobservable counterfactual. A burden allocation reasonable elsewhere could here become a demand to prove something that could never be directly tested.

## V. A study that declined to decide

To see the rule's force, consider its effect on a particular study.

Before Rio 2016, Rehm and German sports bodies commissioned a multi-institutional investigation. It tested three below-knee-amputee long jumpers, including Rehm, and seven experienced non-amputee jumpers. It found effects pointing in opposite directions.

One direction was disadvantage. The amputee group's average maximum sprint and approach speeds were lower, although Rehm's own speed fell within the non-amputee group's range.

The other was favourable mechanics. The amputee jumpers lost less horizontal velocity at take-off, and their prostheses returned more elastic energy than the comparison group's joints below the knee.

The study therefore did not point to one answer. Some findings worked against Rehm and others in his favour. They were measured in different units and could not simply be added.

The authors then did something crucial: they explicitly declined to adjudicate.

The researchers said that, at that stage, they could not determine whether Markus Rehm’s prosthesis did or did not give him an overall advantage.

That is stronger than saying no advantage was found. It does not report an unsuccessful search. It says the study could not determine either proposition. The authors explained that something favourable to one technique could be unfavourable to another, while the limiting factors of the different techniques could not yet be fully identified.

Now place that inconclusive study inside the rule assigning the burden to the athlete.

The rule requires proof of no advantage. The study cannot determine whether an advantage exists. The athlete has therefore not discharged the burden, because uncertainty is not proof of absence. Under that rule, admission is denied.

The direction of the burden directly affects the outcome. If the federation must prove advantage, the same inconclusive study leaves it unable to do so and the athlete may compete. If the athlete must prove no advantage, the study leaves him unable to do so and he is excluded. The study itself favours neither side. It honestly acknowledges uncertainty. Opposite burden allocations turn that neutral uncertainty into admission or exclusion.

The parties read the study in opposing ways. At a press conference on 30 May 2016, Rehm said that no advantage from the prosthesis could be established and treated that as support for continuing his Olympic bid. Critics, including Beckmann and colleagues, stressed that inability to establish advantage did not prove its absence under the applicable rule. The research team's own position was that neither proposition had been established.

Three parties, three formulations, one study. The difference was not the measurements but each party's position relative to the burden of proof.

The example brings the proposition into sharp focus. Disagreement over an honestly inconclusive study concerned not what it measured but who should bear its inconclusiveness. Rehm, seeking to compete, understood uncertainty as insufficient reason for exclusion. Critics defending the rule understood it as failure to establish the exception. The researchers stated that neither proposition had been established, and stopped there. The study left judgment to others. Their judgments depended on the burden's allocation.

That year the IAAF president said Rehm had not, at that stage, demonstrated the absence of advantage. After discussions with the IAAF, Rehm confirmed that he would not pursue qualification for Rio 2016.

## VI. A decision that reversed the direction again

The burden later moved back, this time through adjudication.

In 2020 Leeper's case reached CAS. Leeper, a bilateral below-knee-amputee sprinter, sought recognition of his prosthetic performances alongside those of athletes without prostheses.

CAS did two things that must remain separate.

First, it addressed the burden. It held the 2015 clause placing the burden on the athlete unlawful and invalid because of its differential effect on disabled athletes. The reversal was neither necessary nor proportionate, especially when the neighbouring general technology rule placed the burden on the federation. Paragraph 359 invalidated the rule insofar as it required the athlete to establish the absence of overall competitive advantage. CAS severed that clause rather than deleting the entire mechanical-aid prohibition. Following severance, paragraph 362 placed the burden on the IAAF to prove that Leeper, or another disabled athlete seeking to use prostheses, obtained an overall competitive advantage from the particular prostheses concerned.

On burden allocation, the direction returned to the 2008 position: the federation must prove advantage, rather than the athlete prove its absence. Leeper won on that point.

Second, CAS addressed the merits. It found that World Athletics had, on the specific evidence, established an overall competitive advantage from Leeper's particular prostheses, especially their height and resulting leg length, which enabled him to run faster than his estimated intact-limb counterpart. Leeper lost on that point. His appeal succeeded only in part.

Keeping these decisions separate prevents the essay's proposition from collapsing into a simplistic slogan.

The burden determines the starting point and who bears inconclusive evidence. It does not independently determine the destination. In Leeper's case, the burden was assigned to World Athletics, but World Athletics met it on the balance of probabilities. Even with the federation bearing the burden, the athlete lost.

The direction of proof is therefore not the only determining factor. It is a decisive default when evidence remains balanced. When one side genuinely proves its case to the applicable standard, the merits prevail over that default. Leeper's prosthetic-height issue was a case in which the evidence did not remain evenly balanced but met the standard.

Height itself deserves attention because it was one of the more tangible features in this otherwise counterfactual field. A bilateral amputee sprinter's prosthetic height directly determines standing height and leg length; other things being similar, longer legs imply a longer stride. Actual prosthetic dimensions and ordinary anthropometric relationships provide something that can be estimated without wholly reconstructing the absent biological body. In Leeper's case, this relatively tangible feature helped World Athletics satisfy the standard. It illustrates the converse of the earlier argument: when something can be estimated with sufficient confidence, the burden's default recedes and substantive evidence takes over.

A tribunal can therefore reject a federation's burden-allocation rule and still uphold exclusion under a lawfully reassigned burden. Proof allocation and merits are distinct decisions. Leeper's case demonstrates the separation particularly clearly.

## VII. Choosing a direction does not specify how much uncertainty is tolerable

By this point, the burden of proof has passed through three stages and two reversals.

The 2007 rule prohibited devices providing advantage, with the burden on the IAAF. In Pistorius, the IAAF accepted that burden but did not meet it, and the athlete was admitted.

The 2015 rule required the athlete to demonstrate no advantage. Rehm faced a study that declined to decide, did not discharge the burden, and did not proceed to Rio.

The 2020 Leeper award severed the athlete-burden clause and restored the federation's responsibility. Under that restored burden, World Athletics nevertheless established the advantage of Leeper's particular prostheses, and the athlete lost on the merits.

The three stages produced different outcomes and included two reversals of the burden. They show how allocation changes the legal risk of uncertainty. But three athletes, devices and evidentiary records are not an experiment in which identical evidence was merely placed under different rules. Pistorius and Rehm make the allocation’s importance visible; Leeper’s loss on the merits reminds us that evidence can still decide the outcome.

A limitation must be added, or the proposition goes too far.

The burden determines who bears insufficient proof; the balance of probabilities asks whether the proposition is more likely true than not. They are related but distinct. Neither automatically supplies a scientific method, minimum sample size, comparator or algorithm for combining benefits and disadvantages. If the evidence is evenly balanced, the burdened party loses. Before that outcome, however, the tribunal must decide whether the probability standard has been met.

This marks the boundary of what the burden can do. It can ensure an outcome when evidence is inconclusive, preventing a case from remaining unresolved indefinitely because science cannot settle it. It cannot specify how good the science must be to settle the question. Better methods, more appropriate comparators, and ways to aggregate effects across phases of an event are matters for science and policy. The burden is a fallback for adjudication when truth cannot be reached, not a route to that truth.

Moving the burden can change a legal result without changing any laboratory measurement. It cannot determine how much scientific uncertainty is acceptable. The deeper question, which method, comparator, and aggregation procedure should establish a prosthesis's net advantage, remains unanswered. The burden merely allocates the cost when that question cannot be resolved.

This is the construct’s predicament. It cannot directly observe the athlete with biological limbs; it must assess the counterfactual through indirect evidence and allocate the consequences if proof remains insufficient. Science alone does not assign the burden to federation or athlete: legal fairness, workability and risk allocation also matter. Across the three stages, the burden took two directions, eventually returning to the earlier one. Assessing evidence and assigning the cost of insufficient proof must remain distinct.

## VIII. A body the construct cannot accommodate

Bring the essay together.

The construct assumes that performances can be compared and that comparison requires a common basis. Among athletes without prostheses, that basis is the biological body. Bodies differ, but they are of the same broad kind: muscles, tendons, and bones working under nervous control. That shared category makes comparison possible.

An athlete running with prostheses disrupts the category. Part of the interface producing movement is non-biological. A running prosthesis is primarily passive. Its stiffness, geometry, alignment, and loading govern how it stores and returns energy, while it changes limb mass, lever arms, and the distribution of joint work. It is not the same kind of thing as a biological leg.

The construct therefore confronts a body its comparison framework cannot readily place. To admit it into comparison with non-prosthetic bodies, it asks whether the prosthesis provides an overall advantage. That question calls for the unobservable counterfactual athlete with biological limbs.

This is the remainder, in another new form.

Previous remainders included omitted names, unquantifiable causes, unadjudicated techniques, unchosen options, and legal but non-comparable performances. They fell outside the ledger in different ways, but pointed toward something that existed or could be identified. Here the remainder is a physically nonexistent fact: this athlete running with biological limbs. That version has never existed and cannot be observed. What the construct seeks to judge is not merely outside the book. It is absent from the world.

Faced with a counterfactual it cannot directly observe, the construct can still assess indirect evidence. If that evidence fails to meet the standard, it must also decide who bears the cost of insufficient proof.

This is a sharp version of a recurring movement in the series. When causes remain unresolved, record the result; when a performance table cannot choose a technique for each person, leave judgment partly to practice; when science supplies no unique answer about what ought to count, choose among defensible arrangements. Here the absent comparative body requires evidence and models. The burden does not cancel that assessment: it specifies the consequences if it remains insufficient. Overall advantage was not established against Pistorius, but was established to the legal standard against Leeper. Two reversals show that allocation may change an unresolved result even without new measurements. They do not show that evidence can never distinguish the cases.

Care is essential: this essay is not a conclusion about which athlete should win. Pistorius, Rehm, and Leeper have each articulated their own positions. Pistorius sought a fair opportunity for disabled athletes to compete against non-disabled athletes. Rehm argued that an inconclusive study should not be treated as proof of advantage. These are normative positions, not scientific evidence. The essay does not adjudicate between them, just as the tribunals could not directly observe the underlying counterfactual bodies.

Athletes with limb differences have entered open competition before. Natalie du Toit competed in open-water swimming; George Eyser won gymnastics medals with a wooden leg. These are histories of inclusion, but not adjudicated precedents for carbon-fibre prostheses under an overall-net-advantage test. They show that limb difference alone need not exclude someone from open competition. They do not determine who bears the burden when a required mechanical aid may alter an event's mechanics and the central comparison cannot be observed.

The construct can measure elastic-energy return, horizontal velocity lost at take-off and distance to the centimetre. It cannot directly measure how fast this same person would run with biological limbs. That absence requires estimates and leaves uncertainty. Estimates can nevertheless meet a legal proof standard; when they do not, the burden assigns the consequences. Three stages and two reversals leave us not with universal undecidability, but with a distance that cannot be skipped between direct measurement, assessment of evidence and allocation of risk.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
