# Technique Does Not Converge

## I. Two Techniques in One Final

The men's shot-put final at the 2016 Rio Olympics.

Crouser won gold, Kovacs silver, Walsh bronze. All three used the rotational technique.

In the same final stood Majewski and Storl. Both used the glide.

The first three were spinners, yet two emblematic gliders remained in the final. This was not one technique replacing another, but two techniques standing beside the same throwing circle on the same day, competing for the same gold medal.

The previous essay concerned how the flop displaced the straddle, one technique pushing another out of competition over several decades. This essay concerns the opposite. The shot put's two techniques, glide and rotation, have coexisted for more than half a century without a final verdict. Neither has replaced the other. Different athletes continue to use them side by side in the same competitions, neither eliminating its rival.

Throughout, the construct has never declared which is better.

This must be established at the outset, because it is the greatest difference between this essay and the first eight. Whenever two things coexisted in those essays, the construct eventually tried to deal with them: choose one, assign each a domain, or invent a category to contain them. It was always acting. Here, two techniques have coexisted for more than half a century and the construct has not moved. No selection, no partition, no new category. It simply lets them coexist. Something ordinarily eager to adjudicate chooses not to adjudicate. That choice deserves examination.

Previously the construct kept making judgments: which performances count, who qualifies, how many points a performance is worth. Here, facing two techniques that have coexisted for more than half a century, it neither adjudicates nor possesses a mechanism for doing so. World Athletics' event guide presents shot putting as using either glide or rotation. Its technical rules merely clarify fouls and incidental contact for athletes using rotation. Rules govern legality and safety, not superiority. Both techniques are legal. As to which is better, the rules say nothing.

That silence is notable. The construct regulates circle dimensions, shot weight and fouls, and compares legal throws by distance. Yet a longer throw does not automatically rank techniques in the same order for every body, training situation and tolerance for risk. This is not an absence of any common scale. It is a limit on what the performance scale alone can decide for an individual. Rules keep adjudicating results while leaving much technical judgment to coaches, research and athletes themselves.

## II. One Travels Straight, the Other Turns

First we must establish what these two techniques are, because their difference grounds everything that follows.

Before O'Brien, the standard men's technique was an older front-facing or sideways movement: the thrower leaned back on one leg, hopped forward, and pushed the shot.

O'Brien replaced it. He began facing the rear of the circle, turned approximately 180 degrees, and moved into the delivery position at the front. He won Olympic gold in 1952 and produced the first eighteen-metre performance in 1953. His method became the recognised model for later gliders.

The glide's basic mechanical conception is linear acceleration: a low movement across the circle, a rapid transition into the power position, then a long, tightly controlled final delivery. It emphasises preloading the rear leg, moving rapidly with minimal vertical displacement, and establishing a powerful front-side block at release.

Rotation differs. Official Olympic biographies credit Alekseyev with pioneering rotational shot putting and identify Baryshnikov as its first internationally prominent exponent. Baryshnikov was using it in the early 1970s. In 1976 he set an official world record of 22.00 metres and won Olympic bronze in Montreal.

Its mechanical attraction is a longer acceleration path and an opportunity to generate more linear and angular momentum before the final delivery. Rotation can allow greater pre-acceleration and a larger potential increase in shot velocity.

The fundamental difference is therefore this: the glide moves the athlete across the circle in a straight line; rotation turns the athlete through it like a discus thrower. The glide seeks controlled, stable force production. Rotation seeks a longer acceleration path and the possibility of a higher release speed.

A popular misconception needs clarification. Some accounts call O'Brien's innovation rotational because it included a 180-degree turn. This is understandable but mechanically misleading. Governing-body and coaching descriptions identify his movement as the glide, a linear style, not the later fully rotational technique. His half-turn was a bodily rotation within the glide, not the continuous turning of the rotational method. They must not be confused.

This is more than terminological fussiness. It determines the essay's chronology. If O'Brien's half-turn counts as rotation, rotation begins in 1952 and the two techniques appear almost simultaneously. In fact, more than twenty years separated O'Brien's glide from Baryshnikov's later rotation. The glide ruled alone for twenty years before rotation appeared. Confusing their beginnings makes the story of non-convergence unintelligible, turning it into two techniques running abreast from the outset rather than a challenger arriving after twenty years of dominance.

## III. Two Techniques Occupy One Record Line

The official world-record holders show how the two techniques successively reached the sport's summit.

Before O'Brien, the record belonged to an earlier technical family. Rose, Hirschfeld, Kuck, Torrance, and finally Fuchs used pre-O'Brien methods belonging to neither glide nor rotation.

From 1953 to 1973, the official record line followed the glide. O'Brien, Long, Nieder, Matson, and Feuerbach all used versions developed from O'Brien's method. For approximately twenty years, the glide governed the sport's official performance frontier.

Then rotation arrived. Baryshnikov's 22.00 in 1976 was the first officially recognised rotational world record. Thereafter both techniques appeared along the record line: Beyer, Timmermann, and Andrei on the glide side; Baryshnikov, Barnes, and today's Crouser on the rotational side.

One individual requires separate treatment because he exposes an important distinction.

Oldfield threw 22.86 metres in 1975. The performance is central to rotational technique's history, but was not ratified as a world record because of his professional status.

This must be seen clearly. Oldfield was one of the two most conspicuous early promoters of rotation. He began experimenting with it in 1968 and subsequently popularised it in America. His 22.86 was astonishing at the time. Yet it could not enter the official record, not because of the technique or insufficient distance, but because he was a professional.

Essay Three examined the construct's admission boundary: before measurement comes the decision about whose performances may enter. Oldfield's throw fell outside it. He had made the movement, made the throw; the distance really happened. The ledger refused it because of his status.

At Oldfield, then, the adoption history of rotation diverges from official record progression. As a technique, rotation could already produce 22.86 in 1975. As an official record, it entered the ledger only with Baryshnikov's 1976 throw. These are different lines. What a technique can do and what the construct acknowledges it has done are different things. Oldfield stands precisely in the crack between them.

That crack has special significance here. A technique's actual capacity can become completely disconnected from its representation in the official ledger. From official records alone, rotation reached twenty-two metres only in 1976. In reality, a rotational thrower had reached 22.86 in 1975, but his status excluded him. The record line therefore does not faithfully chronicle technical development. It chronicles the eligible portion of that development. A technique may have travelled far outside the ledger before an eligible athlete repeats it and the ledger recognises that it has arrived.

Who invented rotation consequently has two not quite identical answers, depending on what “invention” means. Official Olympic reference sources credit Alekseyev and Baryshnikov with the internationally recognised competitive model. American coaching memory often gives Oldfield a major role as promoter and early demonstrator. These accounts do not wholly contradict each other, but neither do they make the same claim. Inventing something that can enter official records and inventing something that throws far but cannot enter them are already divided by the admission boundary.

This also makes priority more complicated than it appears. Ordinarily, “who invented it?” means who first did it. Within a construct possessing an admission boundary, invention splits into two kinds: first to do it, and first to be recognised as doing it. Oldfield may belong to the former, Baryshnikov to the latter. Whom history remembers depends on which meaning is used. Official records naturally favour the latter, recognising only what enters the ledger. Memory closer to what actually happened preserves the former. Neither memory is necessarily wrong. They stand on opposite sides of the admission line.

## IV. Elite Athletes Are Moving Toward Rotation, But

Look only at elite men today and a clear impression emerges: athletes are moving toward rotation.

Coded data support that impression. Salinero and Del Coso's World Championships dataset found a sharp increase in rotational use across successive championships: from seven gliders among ten athletes in Paris in 2003 to none among twelve in Doha in 2019. Hatase and colleagues examined major-meet champions, finding rotation used by thirty-seven of sixty-four champions in the 2000s and fifty-two of sixty-four after 2011. In a 2021 sample of regional top-eight finishers, forty-six of forty-eight used rotation.

These numbers look like convergence, as though rotation were eliminating the glide and the sport were finally reaching a verdict.

But changing adoption cannot by itself answer whether one method is mechanically better for everyone. The research presents a more complicated picture than the popularity trend alone.

While documenting the collapse in glide usage, Salinero and Del Coso explicitly concluded that earlier mechanical analyses had not demonstrated a sufficiently clear advantage to justify exclusive use of rotation. Elite athletes are moving toward rotation: that is fact. Rotation has been proved mechanically superior: that is not. Athletes' choices and scientific conclusions separate here.

This separation is central and deserves a pause.

Increasing adoption by leading athletes is ordinarily treated as evidence that a technique is better. Athletes have the strongest incentive to choose optimally; if all are moving toward rotation, surely it is better? The reasoning feels natural, but contains a gap. Athletes can choose a technique for many reasons unrelated to absolute superiority: it is easier to teach; coaches know it better; training systems for it are more developed; or leading athletes around them already use it, creating a trend. Increasing adoption records the combined force of these causes, not technical quality alone.

The distinction extends beyond shot putting. Popularity can reflect genuine advantages while also responding to teaching systems, established practice and imitation. Adoption matters, but it is not the final verdict on every athlete’s choice. Shot-put research includes findings of higher rotational performances in some samples and analyses emphasizing that both methods can reach similar distances. The task is not to declare popularity wrong, but to prevent it from replacing a particular comparison.

And when researchers actually measure their mechanics, the findings are not one-sided.

## V. An Event That Does Not Collapse into a Single Formula

A mechanical comparison from the men's shot put at the 2009 World Championships provides this essay's firmest evidence.

Gliders had a lower average release speed: 13.73 metres per second, against 13.90 for rotational throwers. Release speed is the principal determinant of distance. On that measure, rotation wins.

Yet gliders achieved a slightly greater average distance: 21.34 metres against 21.18.

Lower release speed, greater distance. How? Compensation through a steeper release angle and a higher release point. The same study recorded release-height differences of as much as 0.29 metres in the gliders' favour.

Together these numbers show something important. Shot-put distance depends on three elements: release speed, angle, and height. Rotation has the speed advantage; the glide has advantages in angle and height. Each is stronger in different respects, and those strengths compensate, leaving the final distances nearly equal.

This shows why release speed alone cannot settle the comparison. Speed, angle and height work together, and an advantage in one can be offset by another. But trade-offs do not prove that no combination can ever be better overall; an optimum need not dominate every separate component. The evidence here is narrower: different athletes in this competition sample reached similar distances through different combinations.

The distinction between a trade-off and across-the-board dominance remains important for understanding coexistence. A method no worse in any relevant respect would offer a strong reason for replacement; where advantages differ, choice depends more on how a particular athlete combines them. This guarantees neither permanent coexistence nor the impossibility of a better future method. It shows that, in these comparisons, one advantage alone cannot finish the choice for everyone.

The 2017 World Championships biomechanical report shows why simple body-type rules are dangerous. It noted that Kovacs, 1.81 metres tall, produced one of the highest release points in the field, 2.22 metres or 122 percent of his stature, and one of the steepest release angles, 39.9 degrees. The tallest athlete discussed had both a lower relative release height and a lower angle. Body size matters, but does not map one-to-one onto technique choice.

The scientific literature is divided, and that division is precisely what this essay records.

The case for rotation: Kato and colleagues analysed performances by 176 international-level male shot-putters from 2001 to 2022, finding higher averages for best performances and season's bests among rotational throwers. They concluded that rotation was superior in producing higher performances among recent international male athletes.

The case against convergence: Salinero and Del Coso argued that earlier kinetic and kinematic analyses had shown no clear rotational advantage over the glide, and that elite athletes could achieve similar distances with either technique.

More interestingly, Kato's own data favoured the glide on another dimension. Gliders maintained a significantly higher proportion of their season's best, 97.31 percent versus 96.42 percent. The authors interpreted this as better maintenance of consistent throwing actions across rounds. They found no significant difference in best performance as a percentage of season's best, or in foul rate.

Thus even data used to establish rotation's superiority also established greater consistency for the glide. Independent variability research supports the importance of consistency: some studies associate lower technical variability with longer distances. This also explains why some coaches still value the glide in an age crowded with spinners.

Taken together, the evidence does not make every metric a tie, nor does an advantage on one establish that everyone should change technique. Strengths, costs and conditions of use need separate attention. Research can support a general trend while preserving reasons to compare methods for a particular athlete.

## VI. Coaches Have No Single Answer Either

If both techniques can produce high-level performances, how do coaches choose one for a particular athlete? There is no uniform method.

Peer-reviewed evidence has not firmly established that choice should be based primarily on body type. Successful rotational throwing has been demonstrated by athletes with widely differing physiques, and some current coaching material argues that athletes of every body type and strength level can rotate. Yet strong coaching traditions also hold that taller athletes with longer levers and greater static strength often suit the glide, while more rhythmic, agile athletes suit rotation. The literature does not converge on a scientific rule.

The most common practical teaching sequence begins with standing throws, often introducing the glide first because it is simpler and easier to stabilise, then testing natural rhythm and athletic ability before transferring some athletes to rotation. This is a coaching tendency, not a universal doctrine.

Within that tendency lies something connected to the previous essay. The glide is taught first because it is easier to stabilise. This relates to the learnability that helped the flop displace the straddle, but leads to the opposite outcome. In high jump, the easier-to-learn flop eventually pushed the straddle out. In shot put, the more readily stabilised glide has not eliminated rotation; rotation has instead become increasingly prevalent at elite level.

Why has learnability not produced the same landscape in both events? In high jump, learning and transmission, together with safer landings and competitive success, helped the flop become dominant; that does not prove it the only suitable method for every body. In shot put, both techniques have produced high-level performances and offer different training advantages. Coaches may start a beginner with the glide, then consider a switch if the athlete shows the rhythm and other capacities rotation needs. Learnability influences the entrance without deciding the elite athlete’s destination by itself.

The comparison illustrates a broader point: the same factor can work differently in different structures. In high jump, learnability and transmission help explain the flop’s emergence as the elite standard without proving it mechanically optimal for every body. In shot put, an introductory advantage for the glide likewise does not decide the elite athlete’s final method. Learnability is not a button producing the same outcome everywhere. It works alongside bodies, training, resources and actual performance. A factor’s weight depends on the structure containing it.

Shot-put coaches consequently have no consensus equivalent to “everyone should rotate” or “all tall athletes should glide.” The mainstream position is more conditional: choose the technique that gives that athlete the best repeatable release, considering coaching time, rhythm, strength profile, and tolerance for error.

This is an unusually honest position. It acknowledges that no universal answer exists: the answer depends on the particular person. That is precisely where the construct cannot achieve closure.

## VII. Crouser Opens Another Gap Between the Techniques

Crouser, today's best shot putter, pushed the issue further with the technical development that media and coaches began calling the “Crouser Slide” in 2023.

Public reports describe a modification preserving rotational intent while changing his route across the circle. Rather than starting fully parallel to its rear baseline, he begins at roughly sixty degrees and steps across more directly. Crouser connected the change with seeking incremental distance after already dominating through conventional rotation. Public explanations describe not an abandonment of rotation for the glide, but a hybrid entry designed to create a more direct route and better timing into the power position.

One point must be clear. No official technical paper by Crouser or his coach standardises “Crouser Slide” as a formal biomechanical category. In public discussion it is primarily a coaching and media label, not a settled scientific classification. Some call it a slide; others a modified rotational entry.

It should therefore not be treated as a third technique. It is more like a new gap opened between glide and rotation: something belonging wholly to neither side, whose own classification remains unsettled.

This reinforces the essay's theme. If two techniques had converged on one optimum, today's best athlete should be perfecting that optimum rather than opening a new gap between them. Crouser's experiment shows that even at the sport's summit, the optimal route remains unsettled and the middle ground between established techniques remains worth exploring. Someone who has won everything through rotation still seeks a better route. That itself suggests the best route has not been found.

There is another implication concerning why exploration continues. Once an optimum is found, the rational course is for everyone to approach it rather than strike out elsewhere. Yet Crouser, already at the world's summit with existing technique, still searches between two mature methods. In his own judgment, neither has reached the ceiling. The best athlete still exploring is the strongest signal that the event remains unsettled. True convergence will arrive when all leading athletes use one technique and refine only its details. Shot putting is still far from that day.

The construct's response to the Crouser Slide is the same as its response to glide and rotation: none. It need not decide whether this is a new technique, whether it is superior, or whether it merits separate classification. It asks only whether the throw is legal, whether a foul occurred, and whether the shot landed in the valid sector. The route used to produce the throw, its name, and its advantages over other routes are questions the construct never asks.

## VIII. Once the Rules Speak, the Branch Cannot Appear

Comparison with other throwing events reveals where shot putting's non-convergence comes from.

Official discus instruction describes a rotational throw of roughly one and a half turns. There is no enduring elite bifurcation equivalent to glide versus rotation.

Official hammer instruction likewise describes rotation, with three or four turns. Rules and official event instruction present no comparable long-standing contest between different technical families.

Javelin differs from shot, discus, and hammer: its rules actively constrain technique. World Athletics' technical rules prohibit athletes from turning completely around so their back faces the throwing arc before release.

That provision directly blocks the emergence of a rotational-versus-linear branching structure like shot putting's.

This is the essay's destination and deserves explanation. Javelin could conceivably develop a rotational method, turning to obtain a longer acceleration path as shot putters do. But the rules explicitly prohibit facing away from the throwing direction, and any rotational throw necessarily passes through that position. Once the rules speak, the rotational branch cannot emerge. Javelin has only one broad technical direction not because linear throwing is proved optimal, but because the rules prohibit alternatives.

Now place javelin beside shot put.

Shot-put rules mandate neither glide nor rotation. Both are legal, both have produced high-level performances, and they have coexisted for more than half a century. Javelin rules explicitly prohibit turning the back toward the throwing direction, removing the particular rotational route discussed here. That does not eliminate every other technical variation. The contrast is not between an eternal tie and an event without branches. It concerns how an explicit rule changes whether a particular path can be explored.

Together the events show how rules alter the space open to technical exploration. Without one prescribed movement, practice may develop a dominant method or retain several for a long time, through bodies, materials, teaching and training. Dominance does not prove universal optimality, and branching does not prove that no optimum exists. An explicit ban, however, directly removes some otherwise explorable routes. Javelin supplies that contrast here.

This links the two essays. Both concern space left open by rules. In high jump, landing conditions, learning, athletes’ practice and competitive success helped the flop spread; in shot put, different parameter combinations and training paths have sustained users of both methods. We need not first declare a universal optimum present in one event and absent in the other. Similar regulatory freedom can produce different technical landscapes under different conditions.

We can now describe regulatory silence more fully. Silence is not inaction but an act of yielding the decision. Remaining silent about technique effectively says: I will not govern this; other forces will decide. The rules do not control what those forces are. In high jump, material conditions and learning economics combine to produce convergence. In shot put, mechanical trade-offs produce non-convergence. The silence is the same; the delegated power falls to different forces and yields opposite results. Silence does not decide whether convergence occurs. It decides who decides.

## IX. A Question Without an Adjudicator

Let us gather the argument.

The two techniques have coexisted for more than half a century. O’Brien introduced the glide in 1952; rotation developed during the 1960s and 1970s, with Baryshnikov and Oldfield important figures. Both still appeared in the 2016 Olympic final. Such long coexistence warrants serious attention to why different methods retain users. It does not guarantee that they can never converge, or declare their performance exactly equal in every period.

The official rules and event descriptions examined for this essay do not declare one technique universally superior for athletes.

On the narrow question of whether any official body has declared one shot-put technique better, the answer is no. No World Athletics, IOC, or equivalent institutional document doing so has been found. Official texts regulate legality and safety, not superiority. The event page describes shot putting as using glide or rotation; technical rules merely clarify fouls and incidental contact for rotational athletes.

This is the essay's core, and must be linked to the whole series.

Previously the construct constantly adjudicated. It used rules to determine which performances count, admission boundaries to determine who qualifies, and scoring tables to determine what a performance is worth. Every verdict had a basis: a rule, a table, a line. The construct continually judged.

The competition rules nevertheless supply no ranking of glide and rotation that every athlete must obey. Distances can be compared, and technical effects can be researched. The difficulty is converting an advantage for certain athletes under certain conditions into one method for everyone merely from a results table. Rules need not accomplish that task. They require legal throws to travel far, not every athlete to follow the same route.

A different remainder appears here. Which method suits this person is neither an annulled performance nor an accounting error. It is a question the ranking by distance has not answered. Competition can assign places without prescribing a single route for every body and training history. That question remains within continuing research, coaching and athletes’ experiments rather than receiving its final answer from a rule.

The point deserves expansion. The distance column records how far the shot travelled without unfolding how a body, training and technique together produced that throw. Process is not wholly unregulated, and technical research can record it. A ranking of outcomes is simply not a complete account of process. Two athletes reaching similar distances may have followed very different routes. The number in the table can be clear while judgment about a particular method continues beyond it.

Not issuing a final technical verdict reveals the performance scale’s boundary. A world-record progression can preserve the farthest throw without turning that athlete’s route into the optimum for everyone. The methods are close in some competitions and differ in other samples or metrics. Keeping both available does not require proof that no optimum can ever exist. It requires acknowledging that current rules and evidence have not ended the question for each particular person.

This is what non-closure means in this essay. Earlier difficulties concerned things too large, too numerous or mismatched. Here, the number supplied by competition does not answer every question about technique and the person. Glide and rotation have both produced high-level performances. They need not be proved eternally equivalent to retain a place, and one ranking cannot declare technical judgment finished for every athlete.

Return to the Rio final.

Crouser won gold by rotating. Storl stood on the same field using the glide. Two people, two techniques, one task: pushing a 7.26-kilogram shot as far as possible. Decades of training and research have changed many judgments without bringing every athlete to the same movement. The rules have not made that choice for every body either. The methods can stand side by side, submitting their throws to the same measure. Who throws farther this time is not the same question as how each athlete must throw from now on.

The construct can measure every throw to the centimetre. That number alone cannot tell each athlete how to throw next. Measuring an outcome is not the same as finishing judgment on every route to it.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
