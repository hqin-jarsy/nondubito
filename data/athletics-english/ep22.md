# The Construct Invents a Category for the Remainder

## 1. People the gate cannot accommodate

The Olympic Games have a gate called nationality.

Rule 41 of the Olympic Charter stipulates that an Olympic competitor must be a national of the country whose National Olympic Committee the competitor represents. Eligibility is bound to nationality and the National Olympic Committee, or NOC. You compete for a country, and that country's NOC sends you. This is the normal Olympic gate, the one through which the overwhelming majority of athletes pass.

Yet there are always people who cannot pass through it.

For some, their country has been sanctioned or expelled, or does not yet have a recognized NOC. For others, their NOC has been suspended. Others have fled their country and cannot, or will not, represent it again. Their bodies are qualified and their performances good enough, yet the nationality gate keeps them outside. They are people the Olympic system, operating normally, cannot accommodate.

This essay concerns what the construct does when faced with those people.

In the preceding twenty-one essays, the construct has handled what it cannot accommodate in several ways. It has admitted that something will not fit and left it outside the ledger. When it could not untangle causes, it retreated to recording results alone. When it could not reach something, it retreated to allocating the burden of proof. It has even deliberately reserved space in its rules for an outcome that does not close. Here it does something new: it invents a new category for the people it cannot accommodate.

It does not respond solely with blanket exclusion, but leaves some of them a conditional route to participation. Nor does it pretend they do not exist. Alongside its existing classification system, it builds a new compartment specifically for those who cannot pass through the nationality gate. Independent Olympic Athletes, neutral athletes, the Refugee Olympic Team: these names are categories newly created by the construct for people it cannot otherwise accommodate.

This newly created category is what the essay will examine closely. It has two faces, pointing toward opposite judgments, and this essay will not adjudicate between them.

First, why leave the final judgment to the reader? Not because goodwill and tokenism, inclusion and exclusion can never be distinguished, or because further facts would be useless. Essay 12 on what should count and Essay 14 on eligibility boundaries both required attention to evidence and normative reasons. So does this question. We must establish whom a new category admits and what it asks them to give up. Those facts will not automatically rank every aim. This essay sets out what defenders and critics each see, giving readers tools for comparing their reasons.

## 2. Neutral does not mean without nationality

Before going further, we must dismantle a widespread misconception about this new category.

The misconception is that a neutral athlete has no nationality.

That is not an accurate account of the rules.

Take the Individual Neutral Athlete system used for Paris 2024. An athlete holding a Russian or Belarusian passport seeks to compete as neutral, and that passport status is precisely what triggers the eligibility review. In other words, the construct not only knows the athlete's nationality; it initiates the special review because it knows. Nationality has not been erased. It serves as the entrance to the review.

What is removed is not nationality but the representational connection.

The distinction matters. World Athletics’ records can retain the Russian Federation as an athlete’s biographical nationality while recording competition under a neutral code in a particular period. Nationality has not disappeared; the permitted form of representation has changed. Flags, substitute music and inclusion in NOC medal tables depend on the event’s own arrangements: an Olympic medal-table policy is not simply a rule of the World Athletics database. What these arrangements share is a distinction between where a person comes from and the identity under which they are permitted to appear.

Neutrality, then, is not the construct's ignorance of nationality. On the contrary, it knows nationality intimately. Neutrality is a rule about whom an athlete is permitted to represent, not a declaration that the construct does not know where the athlete comes from. It deletes or replaces public symbols: flag, anthem, opening-ceremony formation, medal-table attribution. But it neither erases, nor needs to erase, legal nationality from institutional records.

This distinction underlies everything that follows. The debate between the two faces occurs precisely here: if the construct retains nationality and removes only representation, is that preservation or suppression?

There is a practical consequence worth identifying at the outset. Since nationality is not erased but merely denied public representation, the construct is not making someone stateless. It is moving nationality from the public stage into the backstage archive. Nationality remains, but no longer in the visible places of flags, anthems, and medal tables. It retreats to the less visible athlete database. This movement from front stage to backstage changes no legal fact: the person remains a national of that country. What it changes is whether the fact may be displayed in this competition. That permission to display is the whole issue between the two faces. One side values the person's admission; the other stresses that part of the person has been hidden.

## 3. From exclusion to substitution to a reusable action

The construct did not always possess this ability to invent categories for its remainder. It emerged from an earlier, rougher history whose starting point was not invention but exclusion.

Consider exclusion first.

Because of apartheid, South Africa was excluded from the Tokyo Olympics in 1964. This was exclusion from participation. In 1970, the construct went further: it withdrew recognition from the South African NOC and expelled it from the Olympic Movement, producing roughly twenty-one years of institutional absence, ending only in 1991.

Two legally different acts must immediately be distinguished. The 1964 act concerned participation in that particular Games. The 1970 act was formal expulsion. Collapsing 1964–1970 into a single decision obscures several reversals in between, including the unsuccessful conditional readmission of 1967–1968. Neither act invented a new category. Both kept an entire country outside. This was exclusion, not accommodation.

Next, consider an unsuccessful attempt at substitution.

At Munich in 1972, confronting the Rhodesian question, the construct first attempted an approach resembling substitution. Its initial remedy was not neutrality in today's sense. It was to let the Rhodesian delegation appear under a colonial, British-style identity rather than Rhodesia's own. This was a substitution of identity.

But the substitution failed. African NOCs rejected the identity workaround and threatened a collective withdrawal. Following a vote of 36 to 31, the construct withdrew Rhodesia's invitation before the Games opened. The initial remedy was therefore to substitute identity; when that proved politically insufficient, participation itself was removed. The embryonic substitution was overridden by a stronger exclusion before it could become a stable means of accommodation.

Secondary accounts do not entirely agree on which flag the Rhodesian delegation would have used. Some say the British flag; others say the pre-independence colonial flag. Until the ceremonial documents for that Munich meeting are located, the specific flag should remain uncertain.

Then, after the nation-based boycotts of Moscow and Los Angeles, the construct finally stabilized the action from the 1990s onward.

At Barcelona in 1992, UN sanctions and state dissolution produced Independent Olympic Participants. At Sydney in 2000, athletes from East Timor competed independently because it did not yet have a recognized NOC. At Sochi in 2014, Indian athletes initially competed independently because their NOC was suspended, then resumed Indian identity when the suspension was lifted. At Rio in 2016, Kuwaiti athletes competed as Independent Olympic Athletes because their NOC was suspended. Among them, Al-Deehani won the first Olympic gold medal under that independent category. The medal ceremony used Olympic rather than Kuwaiti symbols.

The legal triggers in these cases were very different: UN sanctions combined with state dissolution, the absence of a recognized NOC, and NOC suspension. They did not constitute a single fixed category written into the Charter.

They nevertheless shared something. Each time, the construct's Executive Board used the same technique: preserve athletes' participation while suppressing or replacing normal NOC representation. The trigger differed each time; the action remained the same.

This is what the construct really invented for the remainder. Less a category than a reusable action. People may fail to pass through the nationality gate for countless reasons, but the construct's response to those differences is the same: retain the performance, remove national representation. The action is applied repeatedly to one trigger after another.

This offers a contrast with Essay 14. Its seven historical groupings followed different organizations changing, combining or returning to sex-eligibility criteria. Here a similar action recurs under different triggers: preserve a route for individual participation while limiting national representation. One history makes changes of criteria visible; the other shows an institutional tool being carried into new situations.

The contrast goes deeper. The changes in Essay 14 involved evidence, controversy, enforcement and institutional aims; not every change was simply forced by the previous test’s failure. Nor are the arrangements here exact copies. State dissolution, NOC suspension, war and refugee displacement carry different conditions. Yet they repeatedly use a related tool, separating individual participation from national representation. Its familiarity deserves scrutiny. The easier it becomes to reuse a tool, the more carefully we must ask what it preserves and removes in each new setting, rather than allowing proficiency to conceal those questions.

## 4. A system of application, review, and selection

With Russian neutral athletes, this action became exceptionally concrete, and its details dismantle another common misconception.

The misconception is that neutrality is a status Russian athletes receive automatically.

It is not. It is a system of application, review, and selection.

World Athletics' Authorized Neutral Athlete scheme initially allowed individual athletes, after the suspension of the Russian athletics federation, to apply and demonstrate that they met special eligibility criteria. It did not automatically render every Russian athlete neutral.

The figures make this clear. In 2018, 73 were approved, while 68 were rejected and six had their status revoked. By 10 September 2019, the cumulative figures were 128 approved, 58 rejected, and one revoked. In 2021, 151 were approved and six rejected.

These figures show case-by-case selection, not automatic substitution of the whole Russian team. Some passed, some did not, and some passed only to have their status revoked later. Neutral status had to be earned individually, and a substantial proportion were kept outside in the process.

The testing requirements were equally specific. The 2022 guidelines required most applicants over fifteen to have at least three unannounced out-of-competition tests in the preceding twelve months, spaced at least three weeks apart. This was the evidentiary price of separating an athlete from a suspended federation.

Then, in March 2022, a second layer of sanctions changed the practical meaning of that status. Because of the invasion of Ukraine, World Athletics excluded Russian and Belarusian athletes from its international competitions. Holding neutral status therefore no longer guaranteed access to the World Athletics Series. The Russian athletics federation's doping-related suspension was finally lifted in March 2023, but the war-related exclusion continued independently.

This layer must be kept firmly in mind. The word “neutral” must be tied to the particular sanction that created it, rather than treated as a timeless, unitary category. Doping-related neutrality and war-related exclusion are different things, and their lifting or continuation follows separate courses.

By 17 August 2026, clearer evidence had appeared: the Olympic construct and World Athletics no longer had the same policy on Russian and Belarusian participation. World Athletics reaffirmed in 2026 that its 2022 exclusion remained in force. The Olympic construct, meanwhile, provisionally lifted the Russian Olympic Committee's suspension in July 2026 and said that its earlier recommendations to international federations concerning Russian participation no longer applied. The IOC nevertheless reserved a later decision on the Russian flag and anthem at future Olympics, while international federations retained their own admission policies. Athletes from the same country occupied two different positions in the eyes of two institutions. This demonstrates precisely that neutral-athlete status is not one global legal status. It is federation-specific, arising, changing, and disappearing with the particular sanction concerned.

## 5. Individual Neutral Athletes at the Olympics

By Paris 2024, the construct had created a later category with a different composition: Individual Neutral Athletes.

It differed from the preceding independent categories. Qualified athletes holding Russian or Belarusian passports could compete only as individuals, and only after additional eligibility review. Teams were excluded. Athletes actively supporting the war were ineligible. Athletes contracted to the Russian or Belarusian military or national-security agencies were ineligible. Normal anti-doping and qualification requirements still applied.

Submitting an application was not enough. Qualification through the relevant international federation was necessary, but did not itself establish a right to enter the Games. The construct created a dedicated eligibility review panel to conduct final assessments and issue individual invitations. Those invitations then had to be accepted under the construct's conditions of participation.

Paris ultimately had a small group of Individual Neutral Athletes: 32 participants, 15 with Russian and 17 with Belarusian sporting-nationality backgrounds, rather than a replacement Russian or Belarusian Olympic team. We must distinguish carefully between the construct's lists of eligible or invited athletes and those who ultimately accepted invitations and competed. These are different counts and must not be used interchangeably.

The category’s temporary character became particularly visible in 2026. Broadly the Paris arrangements continued into Milano Cortina 2026, with neutral medals still outside NOC tables. This was a situation-specific Executive Board arrangement, not a fixed identity in the Olympic Charter. During 2026, the recommendations concerning Belarusian and Russian participation were withdrawn separately, and the ROC was provisionally reinstated. That did not simultaneously abolish every flag-and-anthem restriction or federation policy. The changes show precisely how categories can be adjusted through particular decisions rather than constituting one uniform, permanent status.

## 6. The refugee team is different

The neutral and independent arrangements discussed so far remove an existing national representation. The Refugee Olympic Team is different. It addresses a situation in which national representation itself may be unavailable.

The refugee mechanism began in response to the displacement crisis of 2015. In March 2016, the construct formally established a Refugee Olympic Team for Rio. At those Games, it comprised ten athletes in three sports: athletics, swimming, and judo. They appeared neither as a recognized NOC nor as ten unrelated neutral individuals, but as the first Refugee Olympic Team.

The team subsequently grew. Tokyo had 29 athletes in twelve sports. Paris ultimately had 37, hosted by fifteen NOCs, originating from eleven countries, and competing in twelve sports. Paris also produced the refugee team's first Olympic medal, Ngamba's boxing bronze, giving its competitive identity a dimension beyond ceremony or participation alone. World Athletics likewise created its own Refugee Team in 2016.

The refugee-team category underwent a particularly notable evolution, and it was not simply an evolution toward more deletion.

From the start, the refugee team used the Olympic flag and anthem in place of unavailable national symbols. But it was never merely a neutral placeholder. In 2016 it already carried an affirmative message of hope for refugees worldwide and visibility for displaced people. Substituting for national symbols and forming a positive collective identity were not mutually exclusive stages: both were present at its inception.

By Paris 2024 that identity had acquired a more distinctive visual expression: the Refugee Olympic Team had an emblem of its own, rather than relying only on generic Olympic symbols. This still matters analytically. It was not a sudden passage from no content to content. An existing collective identity became more recognizable and more available for athletes actively to represent.

A category created for people who do not fit the original classification can therefore grow. It addresses an absence of national representation while giving people in different circumstances a shared name under which to appear. Emblems, performances and relationships can give that name greater substance. The refugee team and neutral individuals thus develop differently, not because one began with no meaning, but because the institution organizes and develops collective representation differently.

The difference deserves clarity. The formal neutral-individual category chiefly removes a particular entitlement to national representation; it does not make the person devoid of identity, values or things to express. The refugee team additionally organizes a shared team name and collective symbols through which athletes can actively represent a common situation. One arrangement focuses on limiting a form of representation; the other also adds a collective form. Neither exhausts the person, and neither can be judged inclusive or exclusionary from its label alone. We must examine what the institution permits and what it asks athletes to relinquish.

## 7. What the category removes, and what it establishes

To understand the two faces, we must take the new category apart and see exactly what it changes and what it retains.

First, what it removes.

The national flag is removed at the level of competitive representation. A neutral athlete cannot appear as the representative of the suspended country. The anthem is removed and usually replaced, not simply silenced. The gold medal in Rio's independent category was accompanied by the Olympic anthem, not Kuwait's. Neutral athletes in Paris used a specially approved neutral musical piece rather than the Russian or Belarusian anthem. Opening-ceremony delegations are curtailed or removed under the Individual Neutral Athlete system: athletes appear as individuals rather than as a collective delegation. Medal-table attribution is deliberately detached from the sanctioned NOC. The construct explicitly provides that neutral athletes' medals do not appear in the NOC medal table.

Now, what it retains.

The sporting performance itself is not deleted. The athlete's name, placing, time, distance, height, and competition history all remain. The substitute code merely marks competitive affiliation. In World Athletics' archives, biographical nationality data may remain alongside it.

Once this inventory of removal and retention is clear, the two faces emerge. What has the construct retained? The athlete's performance, a participation opportunity for those meeting the particular conditions, and the fact of nationality. What has it removed? The entire set of public symbols through which the athlete represents a country.

This is the essay's core, and the point on which it offers no ruling. The same action, retaining performance and removing representation, can be read in two completely opposite ways.

## 8. Two faces

The same rules support two descriptions that both stand on the facts. This essay places them side by side without choosing.

The first description is this: when normal national participation becomes impossible, the construct preserves an individual route.

There is ample evidence for this account. Athletes from East Timor competed through the category before they had an NOC. Athletes under suspended NOCs did not lose the chance to compete because of institutional wrongdoing. The logic of exceptional eligibility for neutral athletes preserved a route for individuals even when an entire federation was suspended. Marial's example is particularly powerful. At London 2012, this category allowed him to compete without representing Sudan, the country he had fled. For him, the Olympic flag was not merely an identity taken away. It was also a mechanism through which he could refuse an unwanted national representation and still compete. In this description, the new category is inclusive. It releases the individual from collective punishment and unwanted representation.

The second description is this: the construct admits the individual only after suppressing normal national representation.

There is equally ample evidence for this account. Flag and anthem are removed. Medals do not enter the NOC medal table. Individual Neutral Athletes are not permitted a team identity. Substitute Olympic or neutral symbols are used. The construct still knows the national identity, which remains legally relevant and even triggers review, yet it cannot be publicly represented in the normal manner. In this description, the new category is conditional. It accepts a person on condition that their normal mode of representation first be removed.

These are not a true description and a false one. They describe the same rules at different analytical levels, and both hold. The first asks what the athlete receives: a route into competition. The second asks the price of that route: the removal of normal representation. The construct's action contains both at once. We cannot isolate either and declare it the sole truth.

To understand why they cannot be separated, imagine insisting on only one side. If we retain only the first, saying this is purely the preservation of an individual route, we must explain why that route must cost the flag and anthem. Truly unconditional preservation could admit people with their national representation intact. If we retain only the second, saying it is purely suppression of normal representation, we must explain why the construct does not simply keep these people outside. An institution interested only in suppression could exclude them directly, as it once excluded South Africa. In fact, the construct does neither. It chooses the middle route: let them enter, but without national representation. This middle route has both sides at once. It offers something less than unconditional admission and something more than total exclusion. The “less” and the “more” cannot be separated, because they are two aspects of the same compromise.

For the refugee team, the same two faces appear in another form.

Supporters say this is recognition, not neutrality. The construct presents the refugee team as evidence that displaced athletes remain part of the Olympic community. The UN High Commissioner for Refugees called the Paris team a beacon, saying it showed what becomes possible when displaced talent is given opportunities to train and compete.

Critics give their strongest version of the argument the name “the illusion of inclusion.” The criticism is that the Olympic system can showcase a small group of exceptional refugees while leaving intact a global sporting structure overwhelmingly organized around nation-states, wealthy sporting systems, and corporate interests. On this account, the exceptional category both crosses the boundary and displays it. A related objection is that a separate Refugee Olympic Team can promote inclusion while marking refugees as other, outside the supposedly normal connection between athlete, citizenship, and state.

Recent empirical research explicitly supports neither simple celebration nor simple condemnation. It finds that the Refugee Olympic Team can provide meaningful belonging and psychosocial resources while also constructing athletes as exceptional model migrants. Their acceptance is selective, conditional on exemplary achievement, resilience, and public symbolism.

## 9. What the athletes themselves say

One natural way to judge whether this category offers inclusion or incorporation is to listen to the athletes within it. Their statements are divided, and that division is the clearest evidence of why this essay cannot deliver a ruling.

Marial offers unusually clear testimony for the individual-route side. For him, the Olympic flag was a mechanism for refusing an unwanted national representation while still competing. He had his own reasons not to use the nationality otherwise available to him.

Dahouk offers an affirmative account of the refugee team. Before Paris, the Syrian-born judoka publicly described her aim as representing refugees around the world and proving that refugees could become athletes, students, and anything else they chose to become. This is an affirmative use of refugee status as a representative identity, not a label to escape.

Lasitskene's response to the exclusion of Russian athletics was more ambivalent. She said she intended to continue competing under a neutral flag while criticizing Russian sports leaders for leaving athletes isolated in their struggles. Her position supports neither a straightforward boycott of neutrality nor an enthusiastic declaration that national identity is irrelevant. Neutral status was an available route for her, but one created by circumstances she regarded as harmful and badly handled.

Mardini's example shows that even positive refugee participation does not necessarily mean an athlete wants the label “refugee” to swallow the label “athlete.” Media representations of her frequently foreground her escape story and heroism while giving relatively less attention to her swimming performances.

These four people occupy four different positions. One point must be stated honestly: this testimony is highly selective.

An athlete who accepted an exceptional invitation and reached the Olympic arena is much easier to interview than one who refused the status, could not obtain it, or never applied. Nor should a federation's statement opposing the Individual Neutral Athlete system automatically be attributed to every athlete it represents. The available testimony therefore cannot yield a clean quantitative answer to whether neutral or refugee athletes themselves approve of these arrangements. Those most likely to object, or those entirely shut out by the category, are precisely the people least likely to be heard. Their silence cannot be read as consent.

## 10. A category that is both door and wall

Let us bring the essay together, still without resolving it into an answer.

The construct has a nationality gate. The overwhelming majority of athletes enter through it, representing their countries. Yet there are always those who cannot: athletes whose countries are sanctioned, whose NOCs are suspended, or who have fled their countries. Their bodies are qualified, their performances sufficient, but the gate keeps them outside. They are the remainder the construct, functioning normally, cannot accommodate.

Faced with this remainder, the construct does something not previously seen in the series. It does not respond to all these situations solely as it once did to South Africa; it leaves some people conditional routes of entry. Nor does it pretend they do not exist. It invents a new category. Independent Olympic Athletes, neutral athletes, the Refugee Olympic Team: these are new compartments the construct builds for people it cannot otherwise fit.

This is the essay's remainder, and its form differs from all those preceding it.

Earlier remainders were handled like this: what could not fit stayed outside the ledger; what could not be calculated prompted a retreat to results; what could not be reached prompted a retreat to burdens of proof; where closure was unwanted, a space was reserved; differences not given separate record categories remained outside the number; what was pushed beyond the line entered another ledger. Here the response is to invent a category. It expands its classification system, creating another route through which some people unable to pass the original gate can compete under new conditions. The new compartment still has thresholds; it does not accommodate everyone in similar circumstances.

This new compartment has two faces. It is both door and wall.

It is a door because it genuinely lets people in. An athlete whose country is sanctioned, or who has fled their homeland, might otherwise remain permanently outside the nationality gate. The new category gives them a route inside. Marial entered. The independent Olympic athletes entered. The refugee athletes entered. They stood in the Olympic arena, and their performances entered the construct's records. This is a real door.

It is also a wall because entry has conditions. Paris 2024’s Individual Neutral Athletes could not use national flags or anthems, enter medals in national tables or compete as national teams. But that inventory cannot be transferred to every independent or refugee category: the refugee team is itself a team, and arrangements permit different collective identities. Thresholds are real; what each leaves outside must be examined separately.

Door and wall are two sides of the same thing. The category the construct builds for its remainder is not half door and half wall. It is the same action, seen as a door from one side and a wall from the other. In these conditional arrangements, preserving individual participation and limiting or replacing a form of national representation are often two aspects of the same decision. Preservation and removal are inseparable sides of one act.

That is why this essay leaves the final verdict open. Calling an arrangement inclusive must not erase its conditions; calling it exclusionary must not erase the people who genuinely entered through it. There is preservation and there is cost, in different proportions across categories. Whether a compromise is good or fair remains a question that can be argued through evidence and reasons. This essay keeps both sides visible so that readers can form their own judgment.

The construct can create a new compartment for someone who cannot pass through its gate, put them on the field, and preserve their performance in the ledger. It cannot make that compartment identical to the normal nationality gate. The compartment's very existence marks a difference: others enter through the normal gate, while this person enters through a place built specially for people like them, a place that is both door and wall. The construct builds the compartment and places the person inside, but the act of placing them there draws a line between them and those who entered normally. The construct must draw this line to accommodate them, yet the line in turn marks them as someone requiring special accommodation.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
