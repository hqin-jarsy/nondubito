# One Distance, Two Ledgers

## 1. A Correctly Measured Course, and a Record-Eligible Course

The Boston Marathon course is an officially certified 42.195 kilometres.

That statement is true. The Boston Athletic Association explicitly states that its point-to-point course from Hopkinton to Boston is certified under World Athletics and USA Track & Field rules. Its distance is 26 miles 385 yards, or 42.195 kilometres. It has been accurately measured. It is a marathon of the standard length.

Yet Mutai's 2:03:02 on that course in 2011, then the fastest marathon performance ever recorded, could not be ratified as a world record.

It was not declared invalid, and no one said Mutai had done anything wrong. The Boston Athletic Association preserves 2:03:02 in the race’s history: it was the course record set in 2011, finally surpassed by John Korir’s 2:01:52 in 2026. Mutai’s performance remains valid; it simply was not a world record.

The distance between those two statements is the subject of this essay.

A correctly measured course and a course eligible for world records are two different things. World Athletics does not treat a correctly measured marathon of 42.195 kilometres and a marathon eligible to produce a world record as the same proposition. A course can be accurately measured and officially certified, yet fail the additional geometrical tests. Performances on it then cannot enter the world-record column.

Boston fails two geometrical tests. First, its net descent is 140 metres, approximately 3.32 metres per kilometre, while the limit for record courses is one metre per kilometre. Second, the straight-line distance between its start and finish is approximately 91 percent of the full race distance, while the limit for record courses is 50 percent. Boston exceeds both limits by a wide margin.

Boston's distance therefore qualifies; its geometry does not. The category of world record requires both.

Essay 18 considered two systematically different kinds of race sharing a name and a record table. This essay considers the reverse: the same distance, equally correctly measured, assigned to two different ledgers because the geometry differs.

We should first clarify what two ledgers means here, because it takes several forms in this essay. The most immediate is Boston: one ledger records a performance as a course record, while another excludes it from the world records. Another, more literal version is the women's marathon: the same distance has one record for mixed races and another for women-only races. Another is concealed in timing: one clock begins at the gun, another when you cross the line yourself. These paired ledgers take different forms, but share a structure. Something is assigned to separate accounts because of a condition judged relevant to comparability. This essay asks what that separation accomplishes, and what it leaves behind.

## 2. A Bicycle That Does Not Trust Its Own Wheels

To understand the geometrical boundary, we first need to understand how the construct produces a comparable length. The method itself is rather elegant.

A marathon course is measured with a calibrated bicycle. The crucial point about that bicycle is that it does not assume it knows the circumference of its wheel.

A mechanical counter attached to the wheel registers small increments of rotation. The classic Jones Counter registers twenty counts per revolution. But the measurer does not calculate distance directly from the wheel's nominal diameter. First, the measurer repeatedly rides the bicycle over a calibration course already measured precisely with a steel tape. Repeated rides establish empirically how many counts correspond to each unit of distance under the particular conditions of this measurement.

That step is the essence of the method. Tyre pressure, the load on the bicycle, road conditions and changes in temperature can all slightly alter the wheel's effective circumference. The bicycle does not pretend these changes do not exist, nor trust a nominal size printed on the tyre. It absorbs all these variations into that calibration. The result is not the bicycle's permanent constant. It is the constant for this occasion, under these conditions.

After the entire course has been measured, the bicycle must be calibrated again. Over dozens of kilometres, the wheel's effective circumference may have changed again: the tyre may have worn or its pressure fallen. Recalibration after measurement detects that change and allows it to be incorporated using the prescribed calculation.

The method does not treat a wheel’s nominal diameter, or one calibration, as an answer valid for all time. It starts from a measured calibration course and repeatedly checks the relation between counts and distance under local conditions. A comparable length depends not on a ruler that never needs checking, but on connecting a shared length standard with empirical calibration in present conditions. It is a concrete instance of this series’ recurring theme: comparability is not simply given. It is continually calibrated.

An equally important rule concerns the shortest possible route. What is measured is neither the road's centre line nor the route a typical runner actually follows, but the shortest route an athlete can legally run. At bends and places where corners may be cut, the measuring line runs approximately 0.30 metres from the kerb on the running side, taking every lawful tangent. This prevents a course from measuring 42.195 kilometres along a convenient line on a map while becoming shorter when an athlete cuts every legal corner. The construct measures the route that nobody can legally shorten any further.

Behind the shortest-possible-route rule lies the same caution as in the measurement method as a whole. It assumes an athlete who takes every lawful advantage, rather than one who dutifully runs down the middle of the road. Why measure for the worst case? Because the record must guard against precisely the runner who takes every advantage. If a course measures 42.195 kilometres along the road's centre, a runner who cuts every corner runs less than 42.195 kilometres, and the record rests on an insufficient distance. The construct therefore measures for the runner who can make the route shortest, not for the average runner. It measures the least distance anyone could run, not the distance most people will run. This caution points in the same direction as the short-course prevention factor: both reduce known risks of a short course in advance; neither guarantees that every possible error has been eliminated.

## 3. Better a Little Long Than a Little Short

Course measurement is not error-free. The IAAF road-running manual requires measurement uncertainty in the relevant competitions not to exceed one part in a thousand, about 42 metres over a marathon. That is a requirement for measurement quality, not a claim that every calibrated-bicycle measurement necessarily has exactly that uncertainty or that this is an irreducible natural limit.

If that error falls on the short side, there is a serious problem. A course advertised as 42.195 kilometres may actually be shorter, and performances on it will have been achieved over an insufficient distance.

The construct addresses this with the short-course prevention factor. The logic of that solution deserves attention.

The factor is 1.001. The measurer deliberately increases the calibration constant by one part in a thousand, so that the measured line includes the count equivalent of 1,001 metres for each nominal kilometre. For a marathon, that amounts to deliberately allowing a buffer of 42.195 metres.

This does not assert that every certified marathon is physically exactly 42.237195 kilometres long. It is a metrological safety factor, deliberately leaving a small margin on the long side to reduce the risk of a short course being found on remeasurement. One part in a thousand is the prevention factor used here, not a fixed error necessarily present in every measurement. The trade-off is clear. Extra distance makes athletes run farther and is not without effect; insufficient distance, however, deprives a record of the qualifying course on which it depends. A small margin is preferred to the risk of a record set over an insufficient distance. The procedure does not abolish error. It chooses how to guard against it.

Embedded in this design is a choice familiar from the series. Faced with measurement error it cannot eliminate, the construct neither pretends the error is absent nor waits for a more precise method. It acknowledges the error and makes a value judgement: if it must err, in which direction should it err? It chooses the long side because that error is harmless to record validity, while the short-side error is fatal. This is the same logic as the choice in Essay 6 to round electronic times towards the slower result. When error is unavoidable, the construct chooses to bear it in the direction safest for the credibility of records.

A numerical coincidence must be pointed out here, because it easily causes confusion. The short-course prevention factor is one part in a thousand, and the permitted net descent for a record course is also one part in a thousand: one metre per kilometre. These identical ratios belong to entirely different regulatory problems. They have no connection. The first prevents measurement error from producing a short course. The second limits the assistance a course's slope gives a performance. The original sources give entirely different reasons for the rules. It is wrong to treat them as the same thing, or to suppose that one can be derived from the other.

## 4. The Geometrical Boundary Has No Physiological Derivation

Now consider the two geometrical boundaries themselves: start-to-finish separation no greater than 50 percent, and net descent no greater than one metre per kilometre.

Their purpose is clear and documented. They prevent a course from being excessively downhill or designed so that a tailwind can improve performances unduly. The separation limit does not claim that every point-to-point course has a tailwind. It limits the possibility that a prevailing wind could systematically assist runners over most of a journey in one direction. Similarly, the net-descent limit prevents systematic assistance from gravity.

A clear purpose, however, does not give the particular number a physiological derivation.

No original technical derivation can be found demonstrating that 50.0 percent, or 1.000 metres per kilometre, is a physiological breakpoint. Modern biomechanics does establish that gradients, turns and aerodynamic assistance measurably affect performance. It does not reveal a natural cliff located exactly at those regulatory numbers. There is no evidence of an essential difference between separations of 49 and 51 percent, or of a sudden physiological change between descents of 0.9 and 1.1 metres per kilometre.

These two lines resemble boundaries discussed earlier in the series. In Essay 7, the scoring material located did not supply a complete derivation of every coefficient. In Essay 12, 40 millimetres had public policy reasons, but no quantitative procedure uniquely deriving that number was found. Essay 14’s eligibility conditions and Essay 16’s 0.100-second warning threshold likewise cannot simply be treated as physiological constants applying to every body. The 50-percent and one-metre-per-kilometre limits are also explainable, defensible rules adopted for comparability, not constants read directly from human physiology.

Together, these lines show how this kind of comparability rule works. Slope and start–finish separation are continuous quantities. Data can help establish the size of differences, but do not by themselves announce which performances should share a column. A threshold must also be chosen in relation to the purpose of comparison, acceptable differences and practical enforcement. It can be explained and defended. No procedure was found here that uniquely derives 50 percent or one metre per kilometre from physiology alone. The construct does not draw a line without reasons. It connects evidence about differences with a judgement about how to compare.

Road running and sprinting take different approaches to the same problem. Sprint-record eligibility uses a direct wind reading and a clear line at +2.0 metres per second; there is no physiological cliff between 2.0 and 2.1. Wind can of course be measured along a road course too. But one brief reading cannot represent changing directions and strengths across 42 kilometres and an entire race. Road-record rules therefore do not copy the sprint procedure. They use course geometry as a proxy restriction, reducing the opportunity for sustained assistance along one long line. This does not remove the effects of weather. Sprinting mainly uses wind measured over a specified interval; road running partly relies on geometry. What a construct regulates is also shaped by conditions of measurement and implementation.

## 5. Boston Is on the Other Side, and Is No Smaller for It

Return now to Boston. It is the best-known example on the other side of this boundary, and its position shows exactly what the boundary is and is not.

The key is to separate two judgements: whether Boston is accurately measured, and whether it can qualify for world records.

Boston is accurately measured. Its certified 42.195 kilometres are not in dispute. Geometry, not distance, excludes it from world records. These are separate judgements. A course can qualify entirely under the first and fail under the second. Boston does precisely that.

The IAAF's response when Mutai ran his time in 2011 deserves close attention. It did not say the course was short or that there was anything wrong with the performance. It explicitly acknowledged that this was the fastest marathon run up to that moment. It simply explained that Boston had both excessive net descent and excessive start-to-finish separation, so the performance could not be ratified as a world record.

That wording is crucial. The ruling was that the performance fell outside the record category, not that it was invalid. Those statements are entirely different. The first says the performance is excellent but does not enter this particular category. The second says something is wrong with the performance itself. The IAAF said the first.

World Athletics has used even clearer wording about another example, the Great North Run. This is a certified half-marathon course that is not record-eligible, with net descent of approximately 1.45 metres per kilometre and start-to-finish separation exceeding 75 percent. When performances there have bettered the official world record of the time, World Athletics has explained why they cannot replace it while explicitly stressing that this takes nothing away from the performances.

Takes nothing away: that is the most important phrase in this essay. Exclusion from the world-record column does not make a performance smaller or its athlete less accomplished. It simply means that the performance is not in this ledger.

Athletes themselves see it this way. Ryan Hall, fourth at Boston in 2011 in 2:04:58, publicly rejected the suggestion that his performance was tainted because it could not become an American record. Former marathon world-record holder De Castella was more direct: every course is different, he said, emphasising that such variation is part of the marathon's mystique rather than a defect that can be standardised away completely.

Several popular misconceptions about Boston also need dismantling.

First: Boston is not a legitimate marathon because it is not record-eligible. This reverses the structure of the rules. Boston's course is officially measured and certified at 42.195 kilometres. Its geometry, not the legitimacy of its distance, prevents world-record ratification.

Second: Boston is always faster because it runs downhill. Its net descent of 140 metres is real, but the profile includes substantial climbs, including the famous Newton hills. Weather can reverse the apparent geometrical advantage, as years with headwinds, heat or rain demonstrate. A study covering more than 380,000 Boston performance observations from 1897 to 2018 found substantial meteorological effects. Net descent is a regulatory proxy for comparability, not a prediction that every Boston race will be faster than Berlin, London or Chicago.

Third: Mutai's 2:03:02 was declared invalid. The ruling was much narrower: it could not be ratified as a world record. The IAAF acknowledged its unprecedented speed, the Boston Athletic Association preserves it as the course record achieved in 2011 and a valid winning performance, and there was no finding of misconduct against Mutai.

## 6. One Distance, Quite Literally Two Ledgers

Boston offers one version of two ledgers: one is called course records, the other world records, and its course belongs only in the first. Women's marathon records offer a more literal version.

In 2011, the IAAF Congress in Daegu adopted a change: a women's road-running world record would have to be achieved in a women-only race. Women's performances in mixed races would be assigned to a separate world-best category.

The rationale was that women running with men could use male pacing groups and receive shelter from the wind or pacing assistance unavailable in a genuinely women-only race. The IAAF therefore regarded mixed and women-only conditions as materially different for record comparison.

One point must immediately be made clear: this rule change did not accuse Radcliffe of cheating.

Radcliffe's 2:15:25 in London in 2003 was run under mixed-race conditions, in a race entirely legal under the rules then in force. The change did not say she had broken a rule. It concerned how the institution would classify such performances retrospectively and in future. Policy rationale and a ruling against an athlete are different things and must remain separate. Under the original proposal, her 2:15:25 would have lost its classification as a world record and become a world best, while her 2:17:42 in London's women-only race in 2005 would have become the women-only world record.

Opposition followed. World Marathon Majors and the Association of International Marathons and Distance Races publicly opposed the change in September 2011. Their argument had two parts: most mass-participation road races are mixed, so a sole women-only record would not statistically represent the sport; and removing record status from the fastest lawful historical performances would be confusing and unfair. They advocated two categories, mixed and women-only.

The IAAF subsequently adjusted its position in two stages. In November 2011, its Monaco Council retained existing women’s road world records, including those achieved in mixed races, but still allowed new records only in women-only races. Radcliffe’s 2:15:25 was therefore not permanently downgraded. Separate recognition of world records achieved in mixed and women-only races took effect on 1 November 2013, establishing the parallel structure used today.

Today, World Athletics maintains two world-record designations for the women's marathon: mixed race and women-only race. This is the clearest literal instance of one distance and two ledgers. Both performances are world records for 42.195 kilometres. The suffix records a race condition considered relevant to comparability.

The dispute has a structure resembling the shoe debate. Drafting can reduce air resistance and improve running economy, but that physical point does not settle how the difference should enter the record book. Existing models do not by themselves establish a definite women-only counterfactual time for Radcliffe in 2003. The IAAF’s initial proposal—women-only world records plus mixed-race world bests—and the opposing proposal of two world-record categories both distinguished the conditions. They disagreed about the status mixed-race performances should receive, and whether future performances could continue to establish world records in that column.

The outcome is also instructive. The IAAF initially wanted one ledger, recognising only women-only records; its opponents wanted two, recording mixed and women-only races separately. Two ledgers prevailed. This echoes the choice in the shoe essay, but in the opposite direction. There, confronted with old and new equipment, the construct chose to retain one ledger without separation. Here, confronted with mixed and women-only races, it eventually chose two. Faced with a difference in conditions, it chose merger on one occasion and separation on another. The choice was not without objective grounds. Evidence of bodily differences did not, by itself, prescribe which structure the record book should adopt. The parties’ reasons, negotiations and judgements about what best represents the sport also entered the decision. Such choices are never the single answer compelled by the evidence. They select one among several defensible options.

## 7. What the Rules Choose Not to Regulate

On the day a marathon is run, its course may be windy or hot. World Athletics' road-record rules choose not to regulate either condition directly.

There is no direct road-racing wind-speed ceiling. Sprints and the long jump have a +2.0-metres-per-second limit beyond which performances cannot count as records. Road racing has no equivalent race-day wind reading. Instead, the 50-percent start-to-finish separation rule partly substitutes for one. Rather than reading a wind speed over forty-two kilometres, it limits the ability of a prevailing local wind to provide systematic assistance by restricting the separation of the start and finish.

There is no temperature threshold either. The road-record criteria contain no maximum or minimum air temperature, humidity limit or comparable meteorological qualification. Hot or cold conditions can make a race faster or slower without altering its formal record status, provided the other rules are satisfied.

A gap in the evidence must honestly be marked here. No original World Athletics decision can be found explaining why temperature was excluded from ratification criteria. Attributing a particular philosophical or physiological rationale to the institution would therefore be speculation. The demonstrable fact is simply that the rules do not regulate it.

This outcome can be read alongside Essay 5, without supplying the institution with a motive statement that has not been found. Officials cannot change altitude or the weather on race day, but they can still classify, annotate or determine the eligibility of performances under those conditions. The Boston research associates temperature and wind direction with results. Record rules directly restrict course geometry without copying sprinting’s wind ceiling or setting a road-record temperature threshold. Geometry is more readily standardised at design and certification. That offers one possible interpretation of the rules, not a demonstrated exclusive reason for the decision.

World Athletics thus uses two different strategies of standardisation. For short events, it uses a measured, momentary, local wind variable. For long road races, it uses course geometry as the principal structural constraint against tailwind assistance. Weather variation itself remains part of road racing. The construct has not standardised it away; through geometry, it has only limited the possibility of designing courses to exploit it systematically.

## 8. The Gun's Clock, and Your Own

Another ledger is concealed in timing, and is frequently misunderstood.

After finishing a mass-participation marathon with tens of thousands of entrants, a runner may see two different times. One begins at the starting gun, the other when the runner crosses the start line. In a wave-start race, the runner may cross several minutes after the gun, so the two times may differ by several minutes.

The most tempting misunderstanding is to think the distinction is whether a chip was used. It is not.

A transponder chip is a timing technology. Gun time and net time are different definitions of when the clock starts. One chip system can produce both. Chip timing therefore does not mean net timing for record purposes.

World Athletics' official record clock measures elapsed time from the starting gun until the athlete reaches the finish line. The interval from an individual runner's own crossing of the start line to the finish may be displayed, but it is not the clock used for record ratification. Net time begins when that individual crosses the timing mat at the start. For a mass-participation runner, it is often the fairest representation of how long they personally ran. It is extremely useful for mass-race scoring, but is not interchangeable with official gun time for World Athletics world records.

Boston illustrates the distinction. The Boston Athletic Association records that in 2006, as part of its wave-start system, it began using net time for mass-race scoring. That operational change did not redefine the timing rules for world records. The same chip hardware captures official gun-referenced finish and split data for elite runners and provides each mass-participation entrant with a personal net time.

Here, then, are two more ledgers. One follows the gun's clock: the record clock, using the common starting-signal reference for that race, rather than restarting when each runner crosses the line. The other follows your own clock, beginning when you cross the start line: the clock fairest to you personally. The two ledgers use the same hardware but measure different things. Records recognise only the first.

This distinction corrects a natural intuition. A mass-participation runner may ask why waiting should count when they cross the start minutes later. Net time more closely represents their own passage from start to finish, but does not remove congestion or extra weaving at the back. Record timing uses the gun so that performances in the same race share a starting-signal reference, rather than each runner opening a separate clock on crossing. This can be understood as a trade-off: an individual’s time covering the course and their performance on a common race clock are not quite the same object of comparison. Choosing the latter does not make the former meaningless, nor would net timing make every starting position equivalent.

## 9. World Best, and World Record

The division between these ledgers has left a clear historical trace in the terms world best and world record.

For most of the twentieth century, the authoritative term in road running was world best, not an IAAF-ratified world record. The Boston Athletic Association's own historical account describes Benoit's 2:22:43 at Boston in 1983 as a world best.

This was not merely journalistic modesty; it also reflected changing institutional status. Road-running world bests through most of the twentieth century were not equivalent to later IAAF-ratified world records, and road courses were not interchangeable like standard tracks. But the words world best do not imply an absence of course-geometry conditions. The IAAF’s official world bests from 2003 already had explicit measurement and geometrical requirements.

That changed only in 2003, when the IAAF approved an official world-record framework for standard road-running distances, retaining the measurement, geometrical and other conditions already used for its official world bests. The change took effect on 1 January 2004. Existing eligible world bests could become the new system's initial world records, rather than history restarting from zero that day. A performance achieved in 2003 could therefore be called a world best at the time and later become one of the first official road-running world records.

This history adds a temporal layer to the two ledgers, but two changes must be distinguished. World best had long been used in historical records and by different organisations. From 2003, officially recognised IAAF world bests already required certified measurement, start–finish separation within 50 percent and net descent within one metre per kilometre. In 2004, eligible performances acquired formal world-record status under the same conditions. Geometry was not suddenly invented that day. Existing comparability conditions were carried into a new record status. Boston’s race and point-to-point tradition predate this modern framework, and its historical significance does not disappear because it falls outside that column.

## 10. A Line Drawn for Comparability Always Leaves Something Outside

Let us bring the essay together.

Ratifiable road world records require conditions of comparability. The new status effective in 2004 carried forward conditions already used for official world bests. A course must be properly measured and geometrically eligible, with timing referenced to the starting signal. These requirements make comparisons between races more reproducible. Descent and start–finish separation are directly restricted; geometry also indirectly reduces the opportunity for sustained tailwind assistance. It does not eliminate every difference in weather.

But drawing the boundary inevitably leaves something outside.

Boston fell outside. It is accurately measured, but the geometry of its point-to-point course from Hopkinton to Boston fails the 50-percent and one-metre-per-kilometre tests. The race began in 1897 with a start in Ashland; the start moved to Hopkinton in 1924. This long-established point-to-point arrangement still predates the world-record framework effective in 2004 by decades. It was not designed to evade those later standards. The record conditions subsequently placed the old race outside that column.

This is the remainder of this essay, and again its form is different.

Earlier remainders were things the construct could not contain, calculate or reach; things it deliberately reserved or refused to name. Here the remainder is what a line drawn for comparability necessarily pushes outside as it is drawn. Boston is one such thing. Entirely legitimate and correctly measured, it existed before the boundary. Drawing the boundary excluded it from the world-record column.

This remainder has a property the earlier ones did not: being pushed outside did not make it smaller.

That is the essay's essential point. Placing a performance outside world records does not diminish it. Mutai's 2:03:02 remained the fastest marathon run up to that moment. Its ineligibility for a world record does not change that fact. World Athletics itself says that exclusion takes nothing away from the achievement. The Boston Athletic Association preserves Boston in another ledger: course records, champions, continuity, difficulty, history. It has not redesigned the legendary route to gain entry to the world-record column. It has preserved the route, the course record Mutai set at the time and its status as the world's oldest annual marathon together in another set of accounts.

This, finally, is what two ledgers means here. World-record rules define a kind of comparability: a bounded comparability necessary for comparing performances on different courses. They do not define the whole value of the sport. A course excluded from the world-record ledger can belong in the ledgers of course records, championships and history. These accounts measure a race by different things, and can all be valid at once.

The construct can draw a boundary excluding courses whose geometry offers excessive assistance, making performances within the world-record column comparable to one another. It cannot use that boundary to measure Boston's entire weight. Part of Boston's significance cannot be measured in the world-record ledger. It lies in another ledger, in the longstanding route from Hopkinton to Boston and its century-old tradition, in the minds of everyone who knows how difficult that route is. The construct's boundary is real and useful. It makes one kind of comparison possible. What it cannot enclose is also real. Those things simply belong to another ledger.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
