# The Cao Quan Stele: What Holds an Extended Stroke in Place?

Close Readings of Chinese Calligraphy 11 · The Palace Museum’s early post-excavation Ming rubbing

On the same album opening, 孝, “filial devotion,” and 季, “youngest,” both carry the component 子, “child.” At the top of the middle column on the left-hand leaf, the upper part of 孝 spreads horizontally while its 子 is kept very short. In 季, the fourth character down the far-left column, the lower part descends and turns left in a long curve. The shared lower component, not a shared meaning, is what we are comparing. Related structures have not been made into identical shapes: one is low and broad; the other turns breadth into a downward journey.

Now look beside them. Below 孝 is 之, a grammatical connective, extending diagonally down to the right; below that, the verticals in 性, “disposition,” narrow the form. Above 季 is 養, “to care for,” with its stacked horizontals; below it comes 祖, “ancestor.” These related components are not presenting their two gestures in isolation. They also make the distances between neighboring characters perceptibly different.

*Figure 1. An opening from the Palace Museum’s early post-excavation Ming rubbing. The middle column on the left leaf reads 孝之性根生於, “the disposition of filial devotion is rooted in”; the far-left column reads 心收養季祖母, “the heart; [he] cared for the senior relative called jizumu.” 季, the fourth character in the latter column, has 禾, “grain,” above it. After noticing related outlines, return to the strokes and the sentence to identify them.* [Official high-resolution viewer](https://www.dpm.org.cn/bigimg/32/11)

The passage 孝之性，根生于心，收养季祖母 says that filial devotion sprang from Cao Quan’s heart and records his care for the elderly family member described as 季祖母. Both 孝 and 季 are organized around 子 below. Against the dark rubbing ink, their pale incised forms first make their related upper-and-lower organization conspicuous. The extended curve belongs to this particular writing of 季.

Look at the two columns together. 之 sends its long stroke towards the lower right, while the curve at the bottom of 季 turns back to the left. The long strokes do not all fly in the same direction: one moves diagonally outwards, another descends and returns. On the same album opening, related components and their neighboring characters offer different paths. The Cao Quan Stele’s expansiveness lies in these differences of direction, extension and return.

## First distinguish the Han stone from the Ming album

The original stele was erected in the tenth month of 185, the second Zhongping year of the Eastern Han. It records the ancestry and official achievements of Cao Quan, magistrate of Heyang (郃陽). The stone is now in Xi’an’s Beilin Museum. Our object is the Palace Museum’s rubbing, accession 新00045355: an early impression taken after the stone’s excavation in the Ming dynasty, before the stone broke. The rubbing was cut and mounted as an album and later belonged to Wang Hongzhuan. The dates of carving, rubbing, mounting, and photography must not be collapsed into one. [Palace Museum collection record](https://www.dpm.org.cn/collection/impres/228534.html)

The writing is pale and the ground black. This is not a manuscript that Cao Quan or a calligrapher wrote in white ink on black paper. Rubbing translates the recessed characters and the surrounding stone surface into this relationship of light and dark. Paper, ink, stone, and carving all mediate our approach to the earlier writing. When discussing thickness, turns, and opening or closing forms, we should not treat every broken patch as the brush’s original dry-brush texture.

Each illustration shows a complete album opening: two leaves with three columns each, read down each column and from right to left. The visible joins, edges of narrow strips, and gap between the leaves belong to the cut-and-mounted object. Characters now placed side by side can be compared, but they were not necessarily neighboring long columns on the stone. Nor can a turn after six characters be attributed to a deliberate pause by the Han calligrapher. We begin with the actual rubbing before us, while keeping this distinction when discussing the stone’s original layout.

## Four instances of 或, without becoming a stamped pattern

![The passage on the clan’s distribution, with four instances of 或 on the right leaf and 枝分葉布 on the left](images/page_23.jpg)

*Figure 2. On the right leaf, 或 occurs fifth in the rightmost column, third in the middle column, and first and fifth in the leftmost column. The right column of the left leaf reads 敦煌枝分葉布: “Dunhuang; branches divide and leaves spread.” A reduced photograph of a complete opening in the same Palace Museum album.* [Official high-resolution viewer](https://www.dpm.org.cn/bigimg/32/23)

The passage reads: 或在安定，或处武都，或居陇西，或家敦煌。枝分叶布，所在为雄. “Some were in Anding, some settled in Wudu, some lived in Longxi, and some made their homes in Dunhuang. They spread like branches and leaves, becoming prominent wherever they were.” This is a celebration of the Cao clan’s ancestry. It explains the repeated 或, “some,” while reminding us that the characters carry a purposeful public narrative.

All four 或 extend horizontally, yet none simply fills a ready-made frame. In the topmost example in the right leaf’s left column, the small central 口, “mouth” or enclosure, is clear; the diagonal reaching right establishes another layer above and beside it. Lower down that same column, the balance between these parts has shifted slightly. In the rightmost column, 或 follows 風, “wind”: its upper horizontal and diagonal spread outward while 口 stays within a tighter center. The same identifying features recur, but the dark spaces between them are different.

Look first only at 口, then at its distance from the far-right tip. That small space is not waste ground left over after the character was completed. The compact 口 makes the outward-reaching stroke seem longer. If the interior were stretched just as far, the character might grow larger without retaining this sensation of release from a tight center.

The left leaf presents another contrast. 葉, “leaves,” has many strokes, its horizontals crossing and gathering around a central vertical. The following 布, “to spread,” has fewer strokes: its top extends to either side, while the lower part exposes larger dark intervals. The two have not been given the same density.

## Two instances of 于 give the horizontal stroke different room

![An album opening with 于 at the lower right and upper left, retaining neighboring 曹國 and 王室 columns](images/page_21.jpg)

*Figure 3. 于 appears at the bottom of the right leaf’s rightmost column and at the top of the left leaf’s far-left column. Beneath 國, “state,” in the right leaf’s middle column, there is a large damaged and patched area with a red seal. It is not part of a normally formed character. Palace Museum, Beijing.* [Official high-resolution viewer](https://www.dpm.org.cn/bigimg/32/21)

These two 于, a preposition meaning “in,” “at,” or “to,” use fewer strokes than 或. In the example at the lower right, a shorter upper horizontal and an extended lower horizontal create distinct levels; the vertical bends toward the lower left. In the second 于, at the upper left, the balance between horizontal extension and the returning curve changes again. Both establish a definite space with very few lines.

Set beside them is 曹, the clan name, at the top of the right leaf’s middle column. Its upper and lower parts stack in layers, enclosing several small spaces. The following 國 gathers its interior within an outer frame. The breadth of sparse characters and the compactness of complex ones coexist on this opening. To describe the Cao Quan Stele merely as “flattened” would miss these different ways of organizing a character.

The traditional clerical-script term 波磔, bōzhé, refers here to a stroke’s movement from a narrower section toward a wider one and its lifted ending. Look at the bottom horizontal of 王, “king,” on the left leaf: it is not a uniform ruler-straight line. In the lower part of 土, “earth,” one can also find differences between the middle and the ends. Stability does not require every section to be level. Local rises and falls belong to a character that remains settled as a whole.

The familiar phrase 蚕头燕尾, “silkworm head and swallow tail,” can help a beginner find differences between a stroke’s beginning and end. But an eye busy searching for a swallow tail in every character may notice only its most conspicuous extremity. Here we also need to see how much dark ground remains beneath the horizontal, where a vertical crosses it, and how shorter strokes avoid competing with the principal stroke for the same space. Expansion means not only that one stroke is long, but that the others have not all filled the surrounding area.

## A very small 七 does not look empty

![A passage on relief and medicine, retaining the full column 合七首藥神明 on the right leaf](images/page_3.jpg)

*Figure 4. The left column of the right leaf reads 合七首藥神明; the second character is transcribed as 七, “seven,” in the museum record. 神明 continues with 膏 at the top of the right column on the left leaf, forming the medicinal name 神明膏, Shenming ointment. 親, “personally,” is second in that column, followed by 至, “arrive”; 王, the surname Wang, is second in the neighboring middle column. 大女桃 begins a reference to an adult woman; 桃 is the beginning of her recorded name, not necessarily the whole name. Palace Museum, Beijing.* [Official high-resolution viewer](https://www.dpm.org.cn/bigimg/32/3)

七 uses very few lines. Its horizontal reaches far to the right, while the other stroke turns to define a low corner. Above it, 合, “combine,” gathers a covering shape over a small 口; beneath it, 首 places several horizontals within a more vertical structure. Although 七 has so many fewer strokes, it does not leave a hole demanding to be filled. Its endings, turn, and surrounding space already form a sufficiently distinct relationship.

This passage concerns assistance to the population and the preparation of medicines. 神明 continues across the column break into 膏, forming the name Shenming ointment; it is not a separate phrase praising efficacy. 七首 is retained from the museum transcription without settling its numerical interpretation or alternative readings. The rubbing lets us see how 七, 首, and 藥, “medicine,” hold their places at different levels of complexity.

親 on the left leaf carries a different visual weight. Its numerous strokes have not squeezed it into a pale lump; dark intervals remain between its left and right sections. In the neighboring column, 王 is defined by several horizontals and a central vertical, contrasting with 親’s lateral division. A sparse character need not be filled up to become complete, and a dense one need not be compressed into a mass to become compact. The traditional impression of refined, supple grace becomes concrete when lines can turn and spaces remain distinguishable, rather than merely when a photograph looks soft.

Sun Chengze praised the stele as 字法遒秀，逸致翩翩: “Its character forms are vigorous and refined, its untrammeled grace lightly poised.” To understand 秀, “refined,” simply as prettiness would be too quick. 遒, “vigorous,” reminds us that rounded and extended lines still have an organizing framework. The old judgment remains useful because it puts together two sensations that can seem opposed. The tight centers and outward release of the four 或, and the few but cohering lines of 七, offer concrete occasions to reconsider it. [Sun Chengze’s appraisal quoted by the Palace Museum](https://www.dpm.org.cn/collection/impres/228534.html)

## Return to the first two characters

Return to Figure 1 and look separately at the upper and lower parts of 孝 and 季. After identifying them, comparison can continue: how does each upper part cover the lower, how does 子 contract or extend downward, and what distances do these choices establish beside 之, 養, and 祖? Recognizing meaning has not canceled formal discovery. It gives that discovery a firmer footing.

The Cao Quan Stele is often used as an introduction to clerical script. Becoming familiar with it need not begin by memorizing a set of stylistic adjectives. Start with something smaller: identify one character, keep its neighbors in view, then find another related structure. Breadth does not mean lengthening every stroke; order does not mean copying identical forms. The stele gives each character its own sense of scale while allowing these differences to inhabit the same luminous, restrained order.

## Optional reading: After recognizing a name, can the eye stay a little longer?

In [*As the Light Fades, the Garden Appears Differently*](https://nondubito.net/essays/aesthetics/en/ray08.html?lang=en), I wrote that knowing a name can help us look, but can also bring looking to a premature end. Figure 3 offers a concrete exercise. First find the long horizontal in 于; then look at the small spaces inside 曹. Having just returned from breadth, does the compact area seem tighter? Reverse the journey, from 曹 to 于: do the sparse character’s open areas acquire more weight?

The characters have not changed; the order in which we compare them has. In discussing perception, the SAE framework distinguishes beauty, pleasure, and understanding. Here it is useful simply as a way to let looking continue its own work after recognition. 于 does not acquire its long horizontal because we judge it refined, and 曹 does not lose its internal intervals when we know it is a surname. By letting the eye move back and forth, we can bring the extending lines and contained spaces into one judgment. [*SAE Judgment and Aesthetics*](https://self-as-an-end.net/papers/sae-judgment-aesthetics.html)
