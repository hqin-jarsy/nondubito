Chapter 29 One richly blessed prays still for further blessings; A girl deep in love weighs love more deeply still

Chapter end

Bao-yu was still staring vacantly when Dai-yu flung her handkerchief at him. It struck him squarely in the eye and gave him quite a fright. He asked who had done it.

‘Oh, I wouldn’t dare!’ said Dai-yu, shaking her head and smiling. ‘My hand slipped. Cousin Bao wanted to see a silly goose, so I was pointing one out to her. I didn’t mean to let go.’

Bao-yu rubbed his eye. He wanted to say something, but could find nothing he could very well say.

Presently Xi-feng arrived. Speaking of the offering service to be held at the Temple of Pure Emptiness on the first day of the month, she invited Bao-chai, Bao-yu, Dai-yu and the others to go and see the plays.

‘No, no, it will be dreadfully hot,’ said Bao-chai with a smile. ‘What play could they put on that we haven’t seen? I shan’t go.’

‘It’s cool there,’ said Xi-feng, ‘and there are galleries on either side. If we’re going, I’ll send people over a few days beforehand, turn all those Taoists out, have the galleries swept clean and the curtains hung, and see that not a single idler is let into the temple. Then it will be pleasant. I’ve already told Her Ladyship. If none of you go, I’m going. I’ve been shut up till I’m sick of it these last few days. Whenever there’s a play at home, I never get to sit and watch it in peace.’

Grandmother Jia heard this and smiled.

‘In that case, I’ll go with you.’

‘If you come too, Grandmother, that will be splendid!’ said Xi-feng, laughing. ‘Only I shan’t have my ease after all.’

‘Tomorrow I shall sit in the gallery facing the stage, and you can sit in one at the side. You needn’t come over to me and stand on ceremony. Will that do?’

‘Now that is kind of you, Grandmother!’ said Xi-feng with a smile.

Grandmother Jia turned to Bao-chai.

‘You must come too, and bring your mother. These long days, you do nothing but sleep if you stay at home.’

Bao-chai could only agree.

Grandmother Jia sent someone to invite Aunt Xue and to tell Lady Wang on the way that she meant to take the girls with her. Lady Wang had already said she would not go, partly because she was unwell and partly because she was expecting someone to come from Yuan-chun. When she heard what Grandmother Jia now proposed, she smiled.

‘She’s as fond of an outing as ever.’

She sent word into the Garden: ‘Anyone who would like an outing may go with Her Old Ladyship on the first.’

When the news spread, the others took it calmly enough, but the maids, who never got beyond the threshold from one day to the next, all longed to go. Even where a mistress was disinclined, her maids used every argument to persuade her, until Li Gong-cai and the rest had all said they were coming. Grandmother Jia was more delighted than ever. She had already sent people ahead to clean and make the arrangements, of which we need say no more.

On the first, carriages crowded before the Rong mansion, with men and horses clustered all about. Those responsible for the procession had learnt that the service was an act of piety on behalf of Her Highness, and that Grandmother Jia was going in person to offer incense. Besides being the first day of the month, it was the season of the Dragon Boat Festival. Every item of equipment was therefore supplied in full ceremonial style, quite unlike an ordinary outing.

Before long Grandmother Jia and the others came out. She took a great sedan chair with eight bearers. Li Wan, Xi-feng and Aunt Xue each had a four-bearer chair. Bao-chai and Dai-yu shared a carriage with a kingfisher-green canopy, pearl tassels and eight treasures; Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun rode together in another, with vermilion wheels and an ornate canopy.

After them came Grandmother Jia’s maids Faithful, Parrot, Amber and Pearl; Dai-yu’s maids Nightingale, Snowgoose and Chun-xian; Bao-chai’s maids Oriole and Wen-xing; Ying-chun’s maids Chess and Xiu-ju; Tan-chun’s maids Scribe and Ebony; Xi-chun’s maids Picture and Cai-ping; Aunt Xue’s maids Tong-xi and Tong-gui, together with Caltrop and Caltrop’s own maid Zhen-er; Li Wan’s maids Su-yun and Bi-yue; and Xi-feng’s maids Patience, Felicity and Crimson. Golden and Cai-yun, two of Lady Wang’s maids, also wanted to go with Xi-feng. The wet-nurse, holding Da-jie and bringing Qiao-jie along, rode in a separate carriage with two more maids. Add the elderly nurses and wet-nurses from all the apartments, and the married women of the household accompanying them, and their carriages filled the whole street in a solid mass.

Grandmother Jia’s chair and those of the other ladies were already far away while the people at the gate were still getting into their carriages. Here someone was saying, ‘I’m not sitting with you!’ and another, ‘You’re squashing our mistress’s bundle!’ From one carriage came ‘You’ve crushed my flowers!’ and from another, ‘You’ve snapped my fan!’ Their shrill chatter, laughter and joking never stopped.

Zhou Rui’s wife walked up and down among them.

‘Girls, you’re in the street! People will laugh at you.’

Only after she had said it twice did they grow a little quieter. The full ceremonial escort at the head of the procession had meanwhile drawn up at the Temple of Pure Emptiness. Bao-yu rode on horseback in front of Grandmother Jia’s chair. People stood on either side of the street to watch.

As they approached the temple, bells and drums rang out. Taoist Master Zhang, robed and bearing incense, was waiting by the roadside at the head of his fellow Taoists to welcome them. Just inside the main gate, Grandmother Jia looked out of her chair and saw the clay images of the great marshal guarding the gate, Thousand-li Eye, Wind-following Ear, the local Earth God and the City God of the district. She ordered the bearers to stop. Jia Zhen came up with the younger men of the family to greet her.

Knowing that Faithful and the other maids were still too far behind to help Grandmother Jia out, Xi-feng got out of her own chair and hurried forward to support her. A young Taoist of twelve or thirteen happened to be there with his snuffers and their holder, tending the candlewicks. He was trying to find a chance to slip away and hide when he ran straight into Xi-feng. Up went her hand. She struck him across the face so hard that he tumbled over.

‘You fucking wild-bull bastard! Where are you blundering?’

The young Taoist scrambled up and tried to run again, without stopping to pick up the snuffers. Just then Bao-chai and the others were getting out of their carriage, surrounded by an impenetrable throng of serving-women. Seeing a little Taoist come tumbling out, they all shouted, ‘Catch him, catch him, catch him!

‘Hit him, hit him, hit him!’

‘What is it?’ Grandmother Jia asked at once. Jia Zhen hurried out to see. Xi-feng came up and took Grandmother Jia’s arm.

‘A little Taoist who trims the candles. He didn’t get out of the way in time, and now he’s darting about among us.’

‘Bring the child here at once,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Don’t frighten him. Children from humble homes are always petted and spoilt. How should he ever have seen such a great procession? Poor little thing, if you frighten him, think how his mother and father would feel.’

She told Jia Zhen to fetch him gently. Jia Zhen had no choice but to bring the boy over. Still holding the snuffers in one hand, the child knelt on the ground, trembling all over. Grandmother Jia told Jia Zhen to help him up and reassure him, and asked him his age. The child could not get a word out.

‘Poor little thing!’ she said again. ‘Zhen, take him away now. Give him some money to buy fruit, and don’t let anyone ill-treat him.’

Jia Zhen assented and led him away.

Grandmother Jia now took the party through the successive courts, worshipping and looking about her as they went. Outside, the pages had just seen the ladies pass through the second gate when Jia Zhen appeared with the little Taoist. He called for someone to take the child away, give him a few hundred cash and see that no one ill-treated him. One of the servants hurried up and led him off.

Standing on the steps, Jia Zhen asked, ‘Where’s the steward?’

The pages below shouted together, ‘Fetch the steward!’

Lin Zhi-xiao came running up, straightening his hat with one hand.

‘There’s plenty of room here,’ said Jia Zhen, ‘but I hadn’t expected so many people today. Take the men you need into your courtyard, and send the ones you don’t need into the other one. Pick out a few more of the young pages and station them at this second gate and the two side gates to fetch things and carry messages. Do you understand? All the young ladies and married ladies are here today. Not a single person with no business here is to get through.’

Lin Zhi-xiao hastened to say that he understood, and added several respectful yeses.

‘Off you go,’ said Jia Zhen. ‘And why haven’t I seen Rong?’

He had scarcely finished speaking when Jia Rong came running out of the bell tower.

‘Look at him! I haven’t even dared complain of the heat, and he’s already gone off to cool himself!’

He shouted to the servants to spit on him.

The pages knew Jia Zhen’s temper too well to disobey. One came forward and spat in Jia Rong’s face.

‘Ask him!’ said Jia Zhen.

‘The Master doesn’t mind the heat yet,’ the page said to Jia Rong. ‘What makes you think you can go off to cool yourself first?’

Jia Rong stood with his hands lowered, not daring to utter a word. When Jia Yun, Jia Ping, Jia Qin and the others heard this, they were not the only ones thrown into alarm: even Jia Huang, Jia Bin and Jia Qiong bestirred themselves, edging forward one by one from the foot of the wall.

‘What are you standing there for?’ Jia Zhen said to his son. ‘Get on a horse and go home. Tell your mother and your wife that Her Old Ladyship and the young ladies are all here, and they’re to come at once to attend them.’

Jia Rong hurried out, calling over and over for a horse.

‘What were they all doing earlier?’ he grumbled. ‘Now he has to pick on me!’

He turned on the pages. ‘Are your hands tied? Can’t you even bring a horse?’

He was tempted to send a page on the errand, but feared that questions afterwards would expose him. There was nothing for it but to ride home himself. We need not follow him.

Jia Zhen was about to go back inside when he noticed Taoist Master Zhang waiting beside him with a deferential smile.

‘By rights I ought to be inside attending Her Old Ladyship. I’m hardly an outsider. But the weather is hot and all the young ladies have come out today. I would not venture in without your instructions, sir. I thought I would wait here, in case Her Old Ladyship should ask for me or wish to visit any part of the temple.’

Jia Zhen knew that Zhang had originally entered religion as a substitute for the Duke of Rong; that the late emperor had himself addressed him as ‘Immortal of the Great Illusion’; that he now held the seal of the Taoist Registry; and that the present emperor had bestowed on him the title ‘Adept of Completion’. Princes, dukes and provincial commanders all called him ‘the Immortal’. He was not a man Jia Zhen would dare slight. Besides, he was a frequent visitor to both mansions and had met all the ladies and girls. Hearing this speech, Jia Zhen smiled.

‘Why talk like that among ourselves? Another word and I’ll pull that beard of yours out! Come along in with me.’

Master Zhang laughed heartily and followed him in. Jia Zhen bent respectfully towards Grandmother Jia, smiling.

‘Grandfather Zhang has come to pay his respects.’

‘Help him over,’ she said at once.

Jia Zhen hurried back to support him. Master Zhang began with a great laugh.

‘Buddha of Boundless Life! Has our venerable lady been enjoying blessings, long life, peace and health? And all the ladies and young ladies, have you been enjoying good fortune? It’s some time since I came to pay my respects, and Your Old Ladyship looks better than ever.’

‘And how are you, old immortal?’ Grandmother Jia asked with a smile.

‘By the blessings of Your Old Ladyship’s boundless good fortune and long life, this humble Taoist is still quite well. But it is the young master I have been thinking about. Has he been keeping well? On the twenty-sixth of the fourth month, not long ago, we celebrated the sacred birthday of the Great King Who Covers the Sky here. There were few visitors and everything was very clean. I thought of inviting him over to look about, but they said he wasn’t at home. How was that?’

‘He really wasn’t at home,’ said Grandmother Jia, turning to call Bao-yu.

He had just come back from relieving himself and hurried forward.

‘Are you well, Grandfather Zhang?’

Master Zhang embraced him, returned his greeting and smiled at Grandmother Jia.

‘The young master is filling out splendidly.’

‘He looks well enough outside,’ said Grandmother Jia, ‘but he’s weak within. And his father keeps driving him at his books. He’s positively driven the child into an illness.’

‘Just lately I’ve seen the young master’s calligraphy and poems in several houses,’ said Master Zhang, ‘and they were remarkably good. Why does His Lordship still complain that he isn’t fond enough of studying? To my humble mind, that ought to be enough.’

Then he sighed.

‘When I look at the young master’s face and figure, the way he speaks and moves, it is as though the old Duke had been cast in the very same mould!’

Tears ran from his eyes. Grandmother Jia too could not keep the tears from streaming down her face.

‘That’s just it. Of all these sons and grandsons I’ve brought up, not one looks like the old Duke. Only my Yu takes after his grandfather.’

Master Zhang turned to Jia Zhen.

‘The gentlemen of your generation never saw the old Duke, of course. I dare say even the Elder Master and the Second Master can scarcely remember what he looked like.’

Then he gave another hearty laugh.

‘I saw a young lady in someone’s house the other day. She is fifteen this year, and a very fine-looking girl. It occurred to me that it must be time to look for a match for the young master. In looks, intelligence, family standing and means, I should say she would suit. But I don’t know Your Old Ladyship’s wishes, and would not presume. Only when I have your instructions would I venture to speak to the family.’

‘A monk told us some time ago that the child’s destiny forbids an early marriage,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Let him grow a little older before we decide. You may keep an eye out meanwhile. Never mind the family’s standing or wealth. If you find a girl whose looks are good enough for him, let me know. If her family is poor, all we need do is give them a few taels of silver.

‘It’s good looks and a good disposition that are hard to find.’

When she had finished, Xi-feng spoke up with a smile.

‘Grandfather Zhang, you haven’t renewed our little girl’s amulet of religious dedication. Yet the other day you had the face to send and ask me for some gosling-yellow satin! I was afraid your old face wouldn’t stand it if I refused.’

Master Zhang burst out laughing.

‘There now! My eyes are going. I hadn’t noticed you were here, ma’am, and never thanked you. The amulet has been ready for some time. I meant to send it over the other day, but then Her Highness’s service came unexpectedly and drove it clean out of my head. It’s still being kept before the Buddha. I’ll fetch it.’

He hurried to the great hall and soon came back carrying the amulet on a tea tray draped with a scripture cloth of scarlet satin patterned with pythons. Da-jie’s wet-nurse took the amulet. Master Zhang was about to take Da-jie in his arms when Xi-feng said, laughing:

‘You might just have brought it in your hand. What did you want a tray for?’

‘Hands aren’t always clean. How could I carry it in my hand?

‘A tray is cleaner.’

‘Well, you gave me quite a fright, bringing out that tray,’ said Xi-feng. ‘I didn’t think you were bringing us an amulet. I thought you’d come to collect alms!’

Everyone burst out laughing; even Jia Zhen could not contain himself.

‘You monkey, you monkey!’ Grandmother Jia said, turning to her. ‘Aren’t you afraid of going down to the hell where they cut out your tongue?’

‘He and I don’t mind,’ said Xi-feng, laughing. ‘Isn’t he always telling me I ought to store up hidden merit, or I’ll die young if I leave it too late?’

Master Zhang laughed too.

‘My tray is to serve two purposes, but neither is collecting alms. I should like to ask the young master to take off his jade, so that I may carry it out on the tray for the brethren who have come from far away, and my disciples and their disciples, to admire.’

‘If that’s all,’ said Grandmother Jia, ‘why should you run about at your age? Take him along to show them, then send him back. Wouldn’t that save trouble?’

‘Your Old Ladyship doesn’t realise. I may be over eighty, but thanks to your blessings I’m still quite strong. Besides, there are a great many people outside and the smell is unpleasant. In this hot weather the young master would not be used to it. If he were upset by the foul air, that would be paying far too dear for it.’

Grandmother Jia told Bao-yu to take off the Spiritual Jade and put it on the tray. With the greatest care Master Zhang laid it on the python-patterned cloth and carried it out. Grandmother Jia and the others looked about the temple a little longer, then went towards the upper galleries. Jia Zhen came to report that Grandfather Zhang had brought the jade back. Even as he spoke, Master Zhang approached with the tray, smiling.

‘Thanks to me, they have all had a sight of the young master’s jade. It truly is a rarity. They had no gifts worthy of the occasion, but these are the ritual objects they use in transmitting the Way, and each was eager to offer his as a token of respect and good wishes. Even if the young master thinks little of them, he can keep them in his room to play with, or give them away as rewards.’

Grandmother Jia looked into the tray. There were gold arc-shaped pendants and open-ring jade pendants, pieces with auspicious designs for ‘May all things go as you wish’ or ‘Peace year after year’, strung with pearls and gems, carved in jade or worked in gold. There must have been thirty to fifty of them altogether.

‘Now you’re being foolish too,’ she said. ‘Where do people who have renounced the world find the means for such things? There’s no need for this. We can’t accept them.’

‘They only wish to show their respect,’ Master Zhang said with a smile. ‘I could hardly stop them. If Your Old Ladyship refuses, they will think how little account you make of me, as though I had never belonged to your household.’

At this, Grandmother Jia told someone to receive the gifts.

‘Grandmother,’ said Bao-yu with a smile, ‘after what Grandfather Zhang has said, we can’t refuse. But I have no use for them. Why not have the pages carry them out with me, and I’ll give them to poor people?’

‘That’s a good idea,’ said Grandmother Jia, smiling.

Master Zhang hastened to stop him.

‘Your wish to do good is admirable, young master, but even if these things aren’t great rarities, they are objects of some value. Giving them to beggars would do the beggars no good, and would only waste the things themselves. If you want to give alms to the poor, why not give them money?’

Bao-yu accordingly told the servants to put the gifts away, and decided to distribute money that evening instead.

Master Zhang now withdrew. Grandmother Jia and the others went upstairs and settled in the gallery facing the stage. Xi-feng and her party took the eastern gallery, while the maids occupied the western one and took turns attending their mistresses. Presently Jia Zhen came to report.

‘Lots have been drawn before the deity to choose the plays. The first is The Tale of the White Serpent.’

‘What happens in The Tale of the White Serpent?’ Grandmother Jia asked.

‘The story of Han Gaozu slaying the serpent at the beginning of his rise. The second is A Bedful of Official Tablets.’

Grandmother Jia smiled.

‘That comes second, does it? Very well. If the gods and Buddhas wish it so, we must let it be.’

She asked what the third was.

‘A Dream of the Southern Bough,’ said Jia Zhen.

Grandmother Jia said nothing. Jia Zhen withdrew to make the arrangements outside for submitting the ritual memorials and petitions, burning paper money and provisions, and beginning the plays. We need say no more of that.

Up in the gallery, Bao-yu sat beside Grandmother Jia. He had a little maid bring the tray of gifts, put his own jade back on, and sifted through the other things, picking them out one by one to show her. Catching sight of a qilin of pure gold with kingfisher-feather inlay, Grandmother Jia took it up.

‘That’s like one I’ve seen some child wearing. Whose was it?’

‘Cousin Shi has one,’ said Bao-chai with a smile. ‘Only hers is smaller.’

‘Of course, our little Yun has one.’

‘She so often comes to stay with us,’ said Bao-yu, ‘yet I’ve never noticed it.’

‘Cousin Bao is observant,’ said Tan-chun, laughing. ‘She remembers everything.’

Dai-yu gave a cold little laugh.

‘With other things there are limits. It’s only the things people wear that she notices so particularly.’

Bao-chai turned away, pretending not to have heard. On learning that Shi Xiang-yun had such an ornament, Bao-yu quickly took the qilin and tucked it inside his robe.

Then it occurred to him that someone might notice how he had kept the thing as soon as he heard Xiang-yun had one. His hand still inside his robe, he glanced about him. No one seemed to be paying much attention, except Dai-yu, who was watching him and nodding as though in admiration. Feeling embarrassed, he brought it out again and smiled at her.

‘It’s rather a pretty little thing. I’ll keep it for you, and when we get home I’ll thread a cord through it for you to wear.’

Dai-yu turned her head away.

‘I don’t want it.’

‘If you really don’t want it,’ said Bao-yu with a smile, ‘I shall have to keep it myself.’

He put it away again. He was just about to speak when the wives of Jia Zhen and Jia Rong arrived, mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. After they had exchanged greetings with everyone, Grandmother Jia said:

‘What have you come for? I only came out for a look about because I had nothing to do.’

Before she had finished, someone announced that people had come from General Feng’s household. Feng Zi-ying’s family, hearing that the Jias were holding an offering service at the temple, had hastily prepared pigs and sheep, incense and candles, money for tea and other gifts, and sent them over. Xi-feng hurried from her gallery into Grandmother Jia’s and clapped her hands.

‘Oh dear! I never thought of this. I thought we women were just coming out for an outing, and now people think we’ve set up some grand altar of fasting and offerings, and are sending presents. It’s all Grandmother’s doing! Now we shall have to get packets of reward money ready as well.’

She had scarcely spoken when two stewards’ wives from the Feng household came upstairs. Before they had gone, gifts arrived from Vice-Minister Zhao. One household followed another. Hearing that the Jias were holding an offering service and their womenfolk were at the temple, relatives near and far, close friends and families of longstanding acquaintance all sent presents. Grandmother Jia began to regret having come.

‘It’s not as though we were holding some important observance. We only came out for an outing. I never thought about presents. We’ve put everyone to trouble for nothing.’

Though they spent the day watching plays, they went home in the afternoon, and the next day she felt disinclined to go again.

‘Even building a wall means disturbing the earth,’ said Xi-feng. ‘We’ve stirred everyone up already. We may as well go out again today and enjoy ourselves.’

But there were two things on Grandmother Jia’s mind. First, ever since Master Zhang had raised the question of finding Bao-yu a wife the day before, the boy had been unhappy. On coming home he had lost his temper and complained that Zhang had proposed a match for him, saying over and over that he would never see him again. No one else knew why. Second, Dai-yu had come home suffering from the heat. For these two reasons, Grandmother Jia firmly refused to go.

Finding that she would not go, Xi-feng took a party of her own, and we need not follow her. Bao-yu, meanwhile, was so worried to find Dai-yu ill again that he had no inclination even to go and eat. He kept coming to ask how she was. Dai-yu in turn was afraid that something might happen to him.

‘Go and watch your plays,’ she said. ‘What are you staying at home for?’

Bao-yu had been deeply troubled by Master Zhang’s talk of a match the day before. Now, hearing Dai-yu say this, he thought: ‘I could forgive anyone else for not knowing what is in my heart. But now even she is making fun of me.’ His distress grew a hundred times worse than usual. Before anyone else he could never have flown into such a rage. But from Dai-yu these words were altogether different from the same words spoken by another. His face darkened at once.

‘All the time I’ve known you has been wasted. Enough, enough!’

Dai-yu gave two cold little laughs.

‘I know it’s been wasted. How could I compare with someone who has something to match yours?’

Bao-yu came right up to her and demanded, ‘Are you deliberately wishing Heaven to punish me and Earth to destroy me?’

For a moment Dai-yu could make no sense of this.

‘Only yesterday I swore it several times over this very thing,’ he went on. ‘And today you condemn me to it again.

‘If Heaven punishes me and Earth destroys me, what good will that do you?’

Only now did Dai-yu remember what he had said the day before. She had indeed spoken wrongly this time. Agitated and ashamed, she answered in a trembling voice:

‘If I deliberately wished any such thing on you, may Heaven punish me and Earth destroy me too! Why must you do this? I know what it is. When Master Zhang proposed that match yesterday, you were afraid it would spoil your splendid marriage. You’re angry about it, and you’ve come to take it out on me.’

Now Bao-yu had been born with a certain disreputable weakness: he was a fool for love. He and Dai-yu had grown up in daily intimacy, their feelings answering each other’s. As he began to understand something of the world, and read those improper books and unorthodox tales, he found that none of the accomplished girls he saw in the homes of relatives or friends could begin to compare with Dai-yu. He had therefore long cherished a feeling he could not put into words. In his happiness or his anger, he tried every possible indirect means of sounding her out. Dai-yu, as it happened, had something of the same foolishness, and she too used feigned feelings to test his. You hide your true heart and show me something false; I hide my true heart and show you something false. When two such pretences meet, something real must come of it in the end. In all the little complications between them, a quarrel now and then was scarcely to be avoided.

At this very moment, Bao-yu was thinking: ‘I can forgive others for not knowing my heart. But can you really not see that in my heart and in my eyes there is only you? Instead of sharing my unhappiness, you make fun of me and shut my mouth with such words.

‘All the time you are in my heart, it counts for nothing. There is no place for me in yours.’

That was what he felt, but he could not say it. Dai-yu was thinking: ‘Of course there is a place for me in your heart. People may talk about “gold matching jade”, but surely you wouldn’t value that absurd tale more than me? When I keep bringing up “gold and jade”, you need only remain perfectly calm, as though you hadn’t heard. Then I would know you valued me and had no thought of that at all. Why do you get so agitated the moment I mention it? It must be because “gold and jade” is always in your mind. When I bring it up, you’re afraid I will begin to suspect, so you make a show of being upset, deliberately trying to deceive me.’

Thus the two of them, who had begun with one heart, put out so many branches and leaves that they made two hearts of it instead. Bao-yu went on thinking: ‘I don’t mind what happens to me, so long as you have your wish. I would willingly die for you this instant. Whether you know it or not makes no difference. Only let me follow my own heart. That would show you were close to me, not far away.’

Dai-yu meanwhile was thinking: ‘You need only look after yourself. If you are well, I shall be well too. Why should you let yourself suffer for my sake? Don’t you realise that when you suffer, I suffer? You are the one who won’t let me come close. You are deliberately making me keep my distance.’

Both wished for closeness, yet each made it seem that distance was intended. These thoughts were part of the two young people’s habitual secret feelings, and it would be difficult to recount them all. Let us return to what could be seen.

Dai-yu’s words ‘splendid marriage’ were more than Bao-yu could bear. He choked on his anger and could not get a word out. In a fit of temper he tore the Spiritual Jade from his neck, clenched his teeth and hurled it to the ground with all his strength.

‘Wretched bit of rubbish! I’ll smash you and have done with it!’

But the jade was extraordinarily hard. The throw had not done it the slightest harm. Seeing it unbroken, Bao-yu turned to look for something to smash it with. Dai-yu had already begun to cry.

‘Why must you do this? Throwing and smashing a dumb thing that can’t answer! If you want to smash something, you’d better smash me.’

As the quarrel went on, Nightingale, Snowgoose and the others hurried over to calm them.

Then, seeing Bao-yu hammering at the jade with all his might, they tried to snatch it away, but could not. This was worse than any of their previous quarrels. They had no choice but to fetch Aroma, who came in haste and finally managed to take it from him.

‘I’m smashing my own things,’ Bao-yu said with a cold laugh. ‘What has it to do with any of you?’

His face had turned yellow with rage, his whole expression distorted. Aroma had never seen him so angry. She took his hand and spoke with a smile.

‘You may have quarrelled with your cousin, but there’s no need to smash this. If you broke it, how could she bear the feeling of it, or face anyone afterwards?’

As Dai-yu wept, she heard Aroma put into words exactly what was in her own heart. So Bao-yu did not understand her as well as Aroma did! Her grief redoubled, and she sobbed more bitterly than ever. In her distress she could no longer keep down the xiangru decoction she had just taken to relieve the effects of the heat. With a retch she brought it all up. Nightingale hurried forward to catch it in a handkerchief, which Dai-yu soaked through in mouthful after mouthful. Snowgoose came to pat her back.

‘However upset you are, miss, you must take some care of yourself,’ said Nightingale. ‘You had only just taken your medicine and begun to feel better. Now you’ve quarrelled with Master Bao and brought it all up again. If it brings on an illness, how could Master Bao bear it?’

Nightingale had put into words exactly what was in Bao-yu’s heart. So Dai-yu did not understand him as well as Nightingale did!

He saw Dai-yu, her face flushed and her head throbbing, gasping as she cried, tears and sweat running together. She was terribly frail. Now he regretted arguing with her. Look at the state she was in, and he could not suffer it in her place. At the thought, his own tears began to fall.

Aroma, standing beside him as they both wept, felt her heart ache too. His hand was icy in hers. She wanted to tell him not to cry, but feared that some grievance might remain pent up in him; and she also feared slighting Dai-yu. Better that they should all have a cry and let it go. She began to weep as well. Nightingale cleaned up the vomited medicine, then took a fan and gently fanned Dai-yu. Seeing the other three utterly silent, each weeping alone, she too was overcome and wiped her eyes with her handkerchief. The four of them wept facing one another without a word.

After a while Aroma forced a smile and said to Bao-yu:

‘If nothing else, one look at the tassel on this jade ought to stop you quarrelling with Miss Lin.’

At these words Dai-yu rushed over, heedless of her illness, and snatched it away. She snatched up a pair of scissors and began to cut the tassel. Before Aroma and Nightingale could take it from her, she had cut it into several pieces.

‘All my work was wasted too,’ she cried. ‘He doesn’t want it. There’ll be someone else to make him a better one.’

Aroma hastily took the jade.

‘Why do this? It was my fault for saying too much.’

‘Go on cutting,’ Bao-yu said to Dai-yu. ‘I shan’t wear it anyway. It doesn’t matter.’

While all this was going on inside, the old serving-women had seen Dai-yu weeping and vomiting, and Bao-yu smashing his jade. They did not know where it would end. Afraid they might be held responsible, they went together to the front of the house to tell Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang, and so clear themselves of blame. Seeing them make such an urgent business of their report, the two ladies feared that some great calamity had occurred, and hurried into the Garden to see the young cousins.

In her alarm, Aroma blamed Nightingale for troubling Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship. Nightingale, supposing Aroma had gone to tell them, blamed her in turn. When Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang came in, neither Bao-yu nor Dai-yu had anything to say; questioned, neither could give a reason for the quarrel. The blame was therefore shifted to Aroma and Nightingale.

‘Why haven’t you taken proper care of them? Here they are making all this trouble, and neither of you doing anything!’

The two maids received a thorough scolding and lecture.

Neither could say a word in her defence. They could only listen. At last Grandmother Jia took Bao-yu away, and peace was restored.

A day passed, and on the third, Xue Pan’s birthday, his family gave a feast with plays and invited the Jias. Bao-yu had not seen Dai-yu since offending her, and was full of remorse. Listless and dispirited, he had no heart for plays. He pleaded illness and did not go. Dai-yu had merely suffered a little from the sultry heat two days before, and was not seriously ill. Hearing that he was not going, she thought: ‘He loves wine and plays, yet today he won’t go. It must be because he was so angry yesterday. Or perhaps, seeing that I’m not going, he has no heart for it either. But whatever happened yesterday, I should never, never have cut the tassel on his jade. He certainly won’t wear it again now, until I make him another.’

She deeply regretted what she had done.

Grandmother Jia, seeing how angry they both were, had thought that if they met at the plays that day, it would all blow over. Instead, neither would go. Distracted, the old lady began to complain.

‘What sins must this old wretch have committed in another life, to be saddled with two such thoughtless little tormentors! Not a day passes but they give me something to worry about. It’s just as the saying goes: “Those who owe each other grief are bound to meet.” Once I’ve closed my eyes and breathed my last, these two tormentors can turn heaven upside down for all I care. What I don’t see won’t trouble me. But no, this last breath won’t leave me!’1

As she complained, she too began to weep. Her words reached Bao-yu and Dai-yu. Neither of them, as it happened, had ever heard the saying ‘Those who owe each other grief are bound to meet’. Now the words had suddenly come to them. Each lowered their head and pondered their meaning as though in Chan meditation; each found tears falling unawares. Though they had not met, one shed tears into the breeze at the Naiad’s House while the other sighed long before the moon at the House of Green Delights. Were they not two people apart, whose feelings sprang from a single heart?

Aroma tried to reason with Bao-yu.

‘No matter how you look at it, the fault is all yours. When the pages at home quarrel with their sisters, or a husband and wife fall out, you hear about it and scold the pages for being fools who can’t understand a girl’s feelings. Now you’re doing the very same thing. Tomorrow is the fifth, the great festival. If you two still behave like enemies, Her Old Ladyship will be angrier than ever, and no one will have any peace. Take my advice. Swallow your pride and make a proper apology, so that everyone can be as they were before. It would be better in every way.’

Whether Bao-yu would follow this advice or not, we cannot yet say. To learn the particulars, listen to the next chapter.
