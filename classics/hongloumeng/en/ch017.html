Chapter 17 In Prospect Garden, talent is tested in couplets and inscriptions; At the Rong mansion, a homecoming celebrates the Lantern Festival

Chapter end

A poem says:

Splendour may well be envied, But parting is hard to bear. An empty name is won; Who knows the bitter and the sweet?

Now that Qin Zhong was dead, Bao-yu wept inconsolably. Only after a long struggle did Li Gui and the others persuade him to stop, and he was still stricken with grief on the way home. Grandmother Jia contributed several dozen taels of silver and provided funeral offerings besides. Bao-yu went to mourn and burn paper for the dead. Seven days later the coffin was taken out and buried. There is nothing further to relate, except that Bao-yu remembered his friend and mourned him every day, though there was nothing he could do.

An uncertain number of days had passed when Jia Zhen and the others came to report to Jia Zheng. ‘All the work in the garden is finished. The Elder Master has inspected it. We are only waiting for you to look it over, sir, so that anything unsuitable can be altered. Then the plaques and couplets can be inscribed.’ Jia Zheng considered this for a while. ‘The plaques and couplets will be a difficult matter. By rights we should ask Her Highness to bestow the inscriptions. But until she has seen the garden herself, she will hardly be willing to compose them at hazard. On the other hand, if we wait until after her visit to ask, all this scenery, all these pavilions and terraces, will look rather bare and dull without any words to distinguish them. Flowers and willows, hills and water alone cannot give them their full lustre.’ The literary dependants standing beside him answered with smiles. ‘You are quite right, sir. We have a humble suggestion. The plaques and couplets are indispensable, but they must not settle the names once and for all.

‘For the present, why not devise phrases of two, three or four characters, appropriate to the scenery, that merely suggest its meaning? They can be made into illuminated plaques and couplets and hung up temporarily. When Her Highness visits, she can be asked to decide the names. Would that not meet both difficulties?’ ‘An excellent suggestion,’ said Jia Zheng and the others. ‘Let us go and look today,’ he continued, ‘and propose whatever occurs to us. Anything suitable can be used. If it will not do, we can ask Yu-cun to come and devise something else.’ ‘Whatever you devise today is certain to be excellent, sir,’ the guests said with a laugh. ‘Why wait for Yu-cun?’ ‘You do not understand,’ Jia Zheng answered, smiling. ‘Even as a boy I was never much good at verses about flowers and birds or hills and streams. Now I am getting on in years, and official papers keep me so busy that I have grown still more unfamiliar with writing for pleasure. Whatever I produced would be pedantic and stiff. Instead of lending charm to the garden, it might be quite out of keeping and spoil the effect.’ ‘That need not matter,’ said the literary dependants. ‘We can all look round and compose together. Each of us can contribute whatever he does best. Keep the good and discard the bad. Surely that would serve.’ ‘Very true,’ said Jia Zheng.

‘And luckily it is warm today. Let us all take a walk.’ He rose and led the party out. Jia Zhen went ahead to warn the people in the garden. As it happened, Bao-yu’s grief for Qin Zhong had lately been so unremitting that Grandmother Jia often had him taken there to amuse himself. He had only just gone in when he saw Jia Zhen coming towards him. ‘Hadn’t you better get out?’ Jia Zhen said with a smile. ‘Your father is coming.’ Bao-yu fled from the garden with his nurses and pages. No sooner had he rounded a bend, however, than he came face to face with Jia Zheng and his guests. It was too late to hide. He could only stand to one side. Jia Zheng had recently heard the schoolmaster praise Bao-yu’s particular aptitude for composing couplets. He disliked his books, but apparently possessed a kind of wayward talent. Finding this unexpected opportunity, Jia Zheng ordered him to come along. Bao-yu had no choice but to follow, without the least idea why he was wanted.

As Jia Zheng reached the garden entrance, Jia Zhen came up with a large number of attendants, who stood waiting to one side. ‘Shut all the gates for the moment,’ Jia Zheng told him. ‘We will look at the outside before we go in.’ Jia Zhen had them closed. Jia Zheng first stood squarely before the main entrance to examine it. It was five bays wide, with rounded roof-tiles and a smooth, loach-back ridge. The railings and window lattices were finely carved in fresh designs, without any red paint or other coloured finish. The surrounding walls were of evenly dressed brickwork, and the white stone platform below was carved with curling foreign foliage. To either side stretched snow-white plaster walls, built on mottled stone foundations and following the lie of the ground. Jia Zheng was pleased: here was none of the usual ostentatious splendour. He ordered the gates opened. Immediately inside, a screen of green hills blocked the view. ‘Splendid hills! Splendid!’ cried the literary dependants.

‘Without this hill,’ said Jia Zheng, ‘you would see everything the moment you came in. Where would be the pleasure in that?’ ‘Precisely. Only someone with hills and valleys in his breast could have thought of it.’ Looking ahead as they spoke, they saw jagged white rocks, some like demons, others like fierce beasts, rearing and leaning across one another. Moss mottled their surfaces, and creepers half concealed them. Between them showed a narrow, twisting path. ‘We will explore along this little path,’ said Jia Zheng, ‘and leave by the other side on our way back. Then we shall have seen everything.’

He sent Jia Zhen ahead to lead the way and, resting a hand on Bao-yu, wound his way into the opening between the hills. Looking up, he suddenly saw a slab of white stone with a surface smooth as a mirror, placed to face them and clearly intended for an inscription. ‘Gentlemen,’ he said, turning with a smile, ‘what would be a fitting title here?’ One proposed ‘Fold upon Fold of Green’, another ‘Brocade Screen’, another ‘Rival to Incense-burner Peak’, another ‘Little Zhongnan’. There were dozens of suggestions. In fact, the guests already understood that Jia Zheng meant to test Bao-yu’s progress, and were merely offering conventional phrases to oblige him. Bao-yu had guessed the intention too. Having heard the proposals, Jia Zheng turned and ordered him to devise one. ‘I have heard an old saying,’ Bao-yu replied. ‘“Better to transmit the old than compose the new; an ancient carving outshines a modern embellishment.” Besides, this is neither the principal hill nor the main scene. There is nothing here that really calls for a title. It is simply a first step towards discovering the garden. Why not inscribe the old line, “A winding path leads into seclusion”? It would have a certain breadth and dignity.’ ‘Exactly!’ they all exclaimed.

‘Young Master Bao has a remarkable natural gift and an uncommon imagination. Nothing like us, with all the life read out of us!’ ‘You must not flatter him,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘He is only a child, making one scrap of knowledge do the work of ten. We may laugh at him, that is all. We shall choose the inscription later.’

They went on into a rocky passage. Beyond it, fine trees grew in luxuriant masses and strange flowers blazed with colour. A clear stream wound out of the depths of the flowers and trees and poured beneath the clefts in the rocks. A few steps further, tending northwards, brought them into a broad, level space. On either side, upper storeys seemed to fly into the sky, their carved roof-ridges and richly worked balustrades half hidden among folds of the hills and the tops of trees. Looking down, they saw the clear brook tumble white as snow, with stone steps climbing into the clouds. A white stone railing curved round the pool. A stone bridge spanned three channels, and water spouted from the mouths of carved beasts. There was a pavilion on the bridge. Jia Zheng and his companions went up into it and sat beside the railing. ‘What would you inscribe here, gentlemen?’ he asked. ‘In his Account of the Old Drunkard’s Pavilion, Ouyang Xiu wrote, “There is a pavilion with wings outspread.” Call it “Wings Outspread”.’ ‘A fine phrase,’ said Jia Zheng with a smile, ‘but this pavilion stands over water. The inscription ought to give particular attention to that. My poor judgement would favour another phrase of Ouyang’s: “Cascading out between two peaks.” I should take the word “cascading”.’ ‘Exactly, exactly!’ said one of the guests.

‘“Cascading Jade” would be perfect.’ Jia Zheng stroked his beard thoughtfully, then looked up and saw Bao-yu attending at his side. With a smile he ordered him to offer a suggestion too. ‘What you proposed just now was certainly right, Father,’ Bao-yu replied hastily. ‘But if one examines it further, “cascading” was appropriate when Ouyang used it of Brewer’s Spring. Applied to this spring, it seems less suitable. Although this is only a country retreat for Her Highness to stay in on her home visit, the inscriptions should still follow the rules for writing by imperial command. A word of that sort seems rather coarse and inelegant. Might something more restrained and suggestive be found?’ ‘What do you make of this argument, gentlemen?’ Jia Zheng asked with a smile. ‘When they composed something new, you said we should use the old. Now we use the old, you say it is coarse and unsuitable. Let us hear your own proposal.’ ‘Instead of “Cascading Jade”, would not “Drenched Blossoms” be fresher and more elegant?’ Jia Zheng stroked his beard and nodded without speaking.

The guests hurried to follow his lead, praising Bao-yu’s exceptional talent. ‘Two characters for a plaque are easy enough,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘Now compose a couplet of seven characters to a line.’ Standing in the pavilion, Bao-yu looked about him. An idea came at once, and he recited: ‘Willows round the bank lend green to three poles’ depth of water; Flowers across the shore share fragrance with a single stream.’ Jia Zheng nodded and smiled slightly. The others were already pouring out their praise.

They left the pavilion and crossed the pool, studying every hill and rock, every flower and tree. Suddenly they looked up to see a stretch of whitewashed wall ahead. Within it stood several bays of elegant rooms, half screened by hundreds upon hundreds of green bamboo stems. ‘What a lovely spot!’ they all said.

They went in. A winding covered walk began just inside the gate, and below the steps a path was laid with pebbles. Above were two or three small rooms, with one open central room and two enclosed side rooms. The beds, small tables, chairs and desks had all been made to fit their places. A little door in one of the inner rooms led into a back courtyard, where tall pear trees in blossom stood among plantains. There were also two small rooms at the rear. An opening in the back wall admitted a stream through a channel only a foot or so wide. It wound round the steps and along the house to the front courtyard, then curved away beneath the bamboo.

‘This place is not bad,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘A man who could sit reading at this window on a moonlit night would not have lived in vain.’ He looked at Bao-yu as he spoke. Bao-yu lowered his head in alarm. The guests hastened to ease the moment with conversation. ‘The plaque here ought to have four characters,’ they said. ‘Which four?’ Jia Zheng asked, smiling. One proposed ‘The Qi River’s Enduring Grace’. ‘Commonplace,’ said Jia Zheng. Another suggested ‘Traces of Sui Garden’. ‘Commonplace too.’ ‘Let Cousin Bao devise one,’ said Jia Zhen with a smile. ‘Before he has composed a thing himself,’ said Jia Zheng, ‘he must first pronounce on everyone else’s efforts. A sure sign of a frivolous nature.’ ‘But his criticisms are quite right. What can one do?’ said the guests. ‘You must not indulge him so.’ Then he addressed Bao-yu. ‘Today you may talk all the wild nonsense you please. Give us your criticisms first, and only then will you be allowed to compose anything.

‘Were any of those proposals usable?’ ‘None seems quite suitable,’ Bao-yu answered. ‘And why not?’ Jia Zheng asked with a cold laugh. ‘This is the first place where Her Highness will make a stop. There must be some tribute to the throne. If the plaque is to have four characters, there is a ready-made phrase in the old books. Why compose a new one?’

‘Are “Qi River” and “Sui Garden” not from the old books?’ asked Jia Zheng. ‘But they are so stiff and stale. “The Phoenix Comes in State” would be better.’ A chorus of delighted approval broke out. ‘You little brute!’ said Jia Zheng, nodding. ‘Little brute! A fine instance of looking at the heavens through a tube and measuring the sea with a shell.’

‘Now give us a couplet,’ he ordered. Bao-yu recited: ‘Tea rests in the precious tripod; its steam is green still; The game ends by the quiet window; the fingers are cool yet.’ Jia Zheng shook his head. ‘I see no improvement.’ He led the others out.

Just as they were leaving, another matter occurred to him. ‘These courtyards and houses are all provided for,’ he said to Jia Zhen, ‘and so are the tables, desks and chairs. But have the curtains and hangings, the ornaments and curios, all been selected to suit each place?’

‘A great many ornaments have already been added,’ Jia Zhen replied. ‘They will naturally be arranged to suit the rooms when the time comes. As for the curtains and hangings, Cousin Lian told me yesterday that they were not all here yet. When work first began, drawings were made for each place and exact measurements taken. People were sent to have them made then. I believe half had arrived by yesterday.’ Realising from this that Jia Zhen was not responsible for the business, Jia Zheng sent for Jia Lian.

Jia Lian hurried up presently. Jia Zheng asked how many kinds there were altogether, how many had arrived and how many were still outstanding. Jia Lian hastily took a folded paper memorandum from the pocket inside the shaft of his boot. He glanced at it and replied: ‘There are a hundred and twenty sets of large and small hangings: figured python designs and raised embroidery, silk-tapestry and ink-sprinkled work, and silks and damasks of various colours. Eighty sets arrived yesterday, leaving forty outstanding. All two hundred curtains arrived yesterday too. In addition, there are two hundred felt curtains, two hundred golden-cane and red-lacquered bamboo blinds, two hundred black-lacquered bamboo blinds, and two hundred curtains knotted in five-coloured thread with coiled floral patterns. Half of each kind has arrived, and they will all be here by autumn at the latest. We also have the chair covers, table skirts, bed valances and table covers, twelve hundred of each.’

They walked on as they talked, until a green hill cut across their way. Rounding its flank, they caught sight of a yellow mud wall topped with rice straw. Several hundred apricot trees blossomed there, like bursts of flame or banks of rosy cloud. Within were a few thatched rooms. Outside, young mulberry, elm, hibiscus and cudrania trees had their fresh shoots woven into two green fences that followed the winding ground. Beyond the fence, below the hillside, stood an earthen well with a counterpoised sweep, a windlass and other tackle. Beneath it, beds and plots of fine vegetables and flowering greens seemed to stretch without end. ‘Now this has something to recommend it,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘Man-made and contrived as it is, the sight of it makes me long to retire to the land. Let us go in and rest.’ He was about to enter the wicket when he noticed a stone tablet beside the path, also awaiting an inscription. ‘Better still!’ cried the guests. ‘A plaque hung up here would destroy every trace of the farmhouse character. This stone gives it so much more charm. Nothing short of Fan Shihu’s poems of rural life could do it justice.’ ‘Then suggest an inscription, gentlemen,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘As your son said just now, “Better to transmit the old than compose the new.” The old poets have said everything about such a place already. “Apricot-blossom Village” would be perfect.’ Jia Zheng smiled and turned to Jia Zhen. ‘A timely reminder!

‘Everything here is excellent, but it still lacks a tavern flag. Have one made tomorrow, nothing elaborate, just like those in the villages outside, and hang it from a bamboo pole above the trees.’ Jia Zhen assented. ‘We must not keep any other sorts of birds here either,’ he added. ‘Geese, ducks and chickens are what we should buy. They would all be in keeping.’ ‘Better still!’ said Jia Zheng and the others. ‘“Apricot-blossom Village” is good,’ Jia Zheng told the guests, ‘but it amounts to giving the place its actual name. That must wait until Her Highness is asked to name it.’ ‘Of course,’ they agreed. ‘What words would merely suggest it for the present?’ While they were thinking, Bao-yu could wait no longer. Without being told to speak, he said: ‘There is an old line: “A tavern flag hangs above the red apricot tips.” Why not use “The Apricot Tavern Flag in Sight”?’ ‘Excellent, that “in sight”!’ they all said. ‘And it hints at “Apricot-blossom Village” too.’ Bao-yu gave a scornful laugh. ‘To call the village “Apricot-blossom” would be unbearably commonplace.

‘Another old poet wrote, “A brushwood gate beside the water, rice blossom sweet in the air.” Why not call it “Sweet-rice Village”?’ They applauded with an even louder chorus of ‘Excellent!’ Jia Zheng cut them short with a shout. ‘Ignorant little curse!

‘How many poets do you know? How many familiar verses can you remember? Yet you dare show off before these gentlemen! All that nonsense of yours was only to let us test the quality of your mind and have a little amusement. You have begun to take yourself seriously!’ He led them into the thatched hall. With its paper windows and wooden couch, it had cast off every trace of wealth and rank. Jia Zheng was secretly delighted, but he glanced at Bao-yu. ‘What do you think of this place?’ The guests quietly nudged Bao-yu, urging him to praise it. He ignored them. ‘It is nothing like as good as “The Phoenix Comes in State”,’ he answered at once. ‘Ignorant fool!’ said Jia Zheng. ‘You know nothing but red towers and painted beams. Vulgar splendour is all you care for. What can you understand of this peaceful seclusion?

‘It all comes of not reading your books!’ ‘Your rebuke is quite right, Father,’ Bao-yu answered quickly. ‘But the old writers often speak of “the natural”. What does that mean?’ Everyone was exasperated by his stubbornness. They thought the boy would never learn. At his question they hastened to explain: ‘You understand everything else. How can you not understand “the natural”? It means something that exists of itself in nature, without being made by human effort.’ ‘There you are again!’ said Bao-yu. ‘To put a farm here is plainly a forced and laboured contrivance. There is no neighbouring village in the distance, no town wall near at hand. The hill behind has no range to belong to; the water in front has no source. There is no temple tower hidden on the heights, no bridge below leading to a market. It stands all by itself, abruptly, and scarcely makes a fine prospect. How can it compare with the other place, which has the pattern and spirit of nature? Bamboo has been planted and a stream brought in, but without any sense of strain. When the old writers spoke of “a natural picture”, surely their concern was precisely that one should not force a place to be what it is not, or a hill to be what it is not. However skilfully it is done, it can never fit…’ Before he could finish, Jia Zheng shouted furiously, ‘Throw him out!’ He had barely gone when Jia Zheng shouted again, ‘Come back!’

He ordered Bao-yu to compose another couplet. ‘And if it makes no sense, you shall have your mouth slapped into the bargain!’ Bao-yu could only recite: ‘New-risen water adds its green where kudzu cloth is washed; Fair clouds shelter the cress-gatherer in fragrance.’ Jia Zheng shook his head. ‘Worse than ever.’ He led the party out, round the hillside and through flowers and willows, past rocks they brushed with their hands and springs they walked beside. They passed a rose trellis, entered a bower of fragrant climbing roses, crossed a peony pavilion and a herbaceous peony garden, went into a rose courtyard and out through a plantain hollow, twisting and turning all the way. Suddenly they heard water murmuring out of a rocky cave. Creepers hung down above it, and fallen blossoms floated below. ‘A lovely scene!’ they all said. ‘What inscription would you propose here, gentlemen?’ asked Jia Zheng.

‘There is no need to invent one. “The Springs of Wuling” fits it exactly.’ ‘Too definite again,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘And hackneyed.’ ‘Then “The Qin Refugees’ Old Home”, perhaps?’ they said, smiling.

‘That is more explicit still,’ said Bao-yu. ‘“The Qin Refugees’ Old Home” suggests escaping from disorder. How could that be suitable? Why not “Knotweed Banks and Flowering Inlets”?’ Jia Zheng condemned this as further nonsense. They were about to enter the water-cave when he remembered to ask whether there were any boats. ‘Four lotus-gathering boats and one passenger boat,’ Jia Zhen replied. ‘But they are not finished yet.’ ‘A pity we cannot go in,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘There is also a winding path over the hill,’ said Jia Zhen. He led the way, and they followed, pulling themselves along by creepers and steadying themselves against trees. The fallen blossom on the water grew ever thicker, and the water itself ever clearer. It spread and rippled, winding this way and that. Two rows of weeping willows lined the banks, interspersed with peach and apricot trees. They screened out the sky and sun; not a speck of dust could enter. Presently, in the willow shade, another wooden bridge appeared, turning at angles like a folded belt and furnished with a red balustrade. Across it, paths led in every direction. Ahead stood a cool, tiled house, its walls all of dressed brick, with grey roof-tiles and patterned walling. Spurs from the principal hill ran straight through the enclosing walls.

‘This house is very uninteresting,’ Jia Zheng said. But as he stepped through the entrance, an enormous, intricately pierced rock suddenly rose before him, seeming to thrust into the sky. Other rocks of every shape stood round it, completely hiding the buildings behind. There was not a single tree or flowering shrub. Instead, strange herbs grew everywhere. Some sent out tendrils, others trailed long runners. They hung from the summit or pierced the clefts, drooped from the eaves and coiled round pillars, twined along the masonry and curled about the steps. Some fluttered like green ribbons; others twisted like golden cords. Some had fruit like cinnabar, others flowers like golden cassia. Their fragrance was rich and pervasive, beyond anything that ordinary flowers could rival. ‘How interesting!’ Jia Zheng said with an involuntary smile. ‘But I hardly recognise any of them.’ ‘Climbing figs and creepers,’ someone suggested. ‘Those could not give out such an extraordinary scent,’ Jia Zheng said. ‘No, indeed,’ said Bao-yu. ‘There are some creepers and climbing figs among them, but the scented ones are du-ruo and heng-wu. That kind is probably chai-lan, this one probably qing-ge. Those are golden-canopy grasses, these jade-lu vines. The red ones are surely purple rue, and the green ones must be green zhi.

‘They must be the strange plants in the Li Sao and the Literary Selections and books like those. Some are called huo-na and jiang-xun, some lun-zu and zi-jiang; then there are stone-sails, water-pines, fu-liu, and something called green-yi, as well as red pepper, mi-wu and feng-lian. As the years go by, people lose the knowledge of them. They give them new names from their appearance, and gradually the names go wrong. That must sometimes happen too.’ Before he had finished, Jia Zheng snapped, ‘Who asked you?’

Bao-yu stepped back in alarm and dared say no more. Jia Zheng noticed covered walks running along both sides, and followed one in. Ahead were five bays of airy rooms joined to a rounded-roofed porch, with verandahs on all four sides. Their green windows and polished walls had a quiet elegance quite different from the places they had already seen. ‘Anyone brewing tea and playing the qin in here would have no need to burn incense,’ Jia Zheng said with a sigh. ‘This design has exceeded all my expectations. You gentlemen must devise something fine and fresh for its plaque, or we shall not have done it justice.’ ‘Nothing could fit it better than “Orchid Breezes and Fragrant Dew”,’ the guests said with smiles. ‘Those four characters will serve,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘And the couplet?’ ‘I have thought of one,’ said a guest. ‘I leave it to you all to criticise and improve.’ He recited: ‘Musk and orchid fragrance mists a courtyard in the setting sun; The scent of du-ruo drifts across a moonlit isle.’

‘Very fine,’ they said, ‘but “setting sun” will not do.’ ‘There is an old line,’ he replied. ‘“Hands full of mi-wu, she weeps in the slanting light.”’ ‘Too despondent, too despondent!’ ‘I have a couplet too,’ said another. ‘Let me have your judgement, gentlemen.’ He recited: ‘On three paths, scented breezes stir the jade-hui grass; In one courtyard, bright moonlight shines on golden orchids.’ Jia Zheng stroked his beard and pondered, intending to compose a couplet himself. Looking up, he saw Bao-yu standing beside him, afraid to speak. ‘Why are you silent now that you ought to say something?’ he demanded. ‘Must we wait upon you for your opinion?’ ‘There are no orchids and musk, no moonlight, no islands and islets here,’ Bao-yu replied. ‘If one is going to make such explicit references, two hundred couplets would not be enough.’ ‘Who is holding your head down and forcing you to use those expressions?’ asked Jia Zheng. ‘In that case, the best inscription would be “Clear Fragrance of Heng and Zhi”.

‘And for the couplet: “Cardamom verses done, the poet’s gift still glows; Sleep among roses over, dreams are fragrant still.”’ ‘You have copied “Writing on plantain leaves is done; the words are green still”,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘Nothing remarkable in that.’ ‘Li Bai’s Phoenix Terrace is modelled entirely on Yellow Crane Tower,’ the guests answered. ‘All that matters is how well it is done. If we examine this couplet closely, it is more delicate and more lively than “Writing on plantain leaves”. One might almost think that line had been modelled on this.’ ‘What an absurd idea!’ Jia Zheng said, laughing.

They went out as they spoke. Before long they came upon lofty pavilions and towering storeyed buildings, surrounded on every side by palatial halls and linked by long, winding covered galleries. Green pines brushed the eaves; magnolias clustered round the stonework. Carved beasts’ faces gleamed with gold, and dragon heads shone with colour. ‘This is the principal hall,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘Rather too magnificent.’ ‘It has to be,’ they all replied. ‘Her Highness may value thrift and have a natural preference for simplicity over elaboration, but the dignity of her present rank demands these ceremonial forms. There is nothing excessive in it.’ As they walked and talked, a jade-stone gateway appeared directly ahead, exquisitely carved with coiling dragons and guardian dragon forms. ‘What should be written here?’ asked Jia Zheng. ‘“The Immortal Realm of Penglai” would surely be best,’ they said.

Jia Zheng shook his head without answering. At the sight of this place, something stirred in Bao-yu. He thought he must have seen it somewhere before, but could not for the moment remember when. Jia Zheng again ordered him to devise an inscription. Bao-yu was so intent on recalling that earlier scene that he had no attention to spare for the present one. The guests did not understand. They assumed that the long ordeal had exhausted him, and that his talent and his words had run dry. If he were pressed with further questions, he might become so distressed that something happened to him. That would put them in an awkward position. They hurried to remonstrate with Jia Zheng. ‘Enough, enough. Let the inscription wait until tomorrow.’ Jia Zheng was also afraid that Grandmother Jia might be growing anxious. He gave a cold laugh. ‘So there are times when even you can do nothing, you little brute!

‘Very well, I will give you a day. If you still cannot do it tomorrow, I shall certainly not let you off. This is an important place. You must take particular care with it.’ He led them out and looked round again. Since entering the garden they had seen only five or six parts in ten. Just then a servant reported that someone from Yu-cun’s house had come with a message. ‘We shall have to leave the remaining places,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘But we must go out by the other side. Even if we cannot inspect them closely, we can at least glance at them.’ He led the guests on until they reached a large bridge, beneath which water rushed in like a curtain of crystal. This was the sluice connecting the garden to the river outside and admitting its water.

‘What is this sluice called?’ Jia Zheng asked. ‘It is the source of the Drenched Blossoms stream,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Call it the Drenched Blossoms Sluice.’ ‘Nonsense! I expressly refuse to use “Drenched Blossoms”.’ They went on past airy halls and thatched cottages, walls of piled stone and windows framed with woven flowers. Here a secluded Buddhist convent nestled below a hill; there a women’s Taoist retreat lay hidden in the woods. There were long corridors, twisting caves, square houses and round pavilions, but Jia Zheng had no time to enter any of them. He remarked that his legs were aching after so long without a rest. Just then another courtyard appeared ahead. ‘We must go in here and sit down,’ he said with a smile. He led them straight towards it, round flowering peach trees and through a moon-shaped opening woven into a bamboo fence and flower screen. White walls enclosed the place, with green willows drooping all round. They entered and found covered walks joined on either side of the gate.

A few rocks were set about the courtyard. On one side grew several plantains; on the other stood a Xifu crab-apple, spreading like an umbrella, green threads of foliage hanging from it and flowers opening cinnabar-red. ‘Beautiful blossoms!’ cried the guests. ‘We have seen plenty of crab-apples, but never anything as fine as this.’ ‘It is called the “Daughter’s Crab-apple”,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘A foreign variety. The common story is that it comes from the Daughters’ Kingdom, where it is said to flourish more than any other kind. A preposterous and unfounded tale, of course.’

‘Unfounded it may be,’ the guests said with a laugh, ‘but how has the name survived so long?’ ‘Probably some poet thought the flowers’ rosy colour looked like rouge,’ said Bao-yu, ‘and their frail grace like a girl weakened by illness. They suggested a young lady’s manner, so he called them “Daughter’s”. Then some vulgar fool must have heard it and put it into an unofficial history as evidence. One vulgar tale bred another, one error passed into the next, until everyone took it for fact.’

The guests swayed with admiration and applauded his explanation. Still talking, they sat down on the couches built beneath the projecting porch outside the covered walk. ‘What fresh phrase can you think of for the inscription here?’ Jia Zheng asked. ‘“Plantain and Crane” would be excellent,’ said one guest. ‘“Radiance Spilling Colour” would be better,’ said another.

‘Excellent, that “Radiance Spilling Colour”!’ said Jia Zheng and the others. ‘Wonderful,’ said Bao-yu too. Then he sighed. ‘Only it is a pity.’ ‘A pity in what way?’ they asked. ‘There are two plantings here, plantain and crab-apple, and their arrangement holds the idea of both green and red. Mention only the plantain, and the crab-apple is left out; mention only the crab-apple, and the plantain has no place. Plantain without crab-apple will not do, and crab-apple without plantain is still less possible.’ ‘What would you have, then?’ asked Jia Zheng. ‘“Red Fragrance and Green Jade” would do justice to both.’ Jia Zheng shook his head. ‘No, no. That will not do.’

He led them into the house. The rooms were arranged quite differently from any they had seen. Indeed, it was impossible to distinguish their divisions. All round them were intricately pierced wooden panels, carved with designs of flowing clouds and a hundred bats, the Three Friends of Winter, landscapes and figures, birds and flowers, assorted emblems, antique treasures, and countless blessings and long-life characters,

all carved and fretted by master craftsmen, finished in many colours with gold and inlaid gems. Recess after recess held books, a tripod, brushes and inkstones, flower vases or miniature landscapes. The openings took every shape: round heaven and square earth, mallow flowers and plantain leaves, interlocking rings and half-round panels. Everything was richly clustered, delicately pierced and open to the eye. Here an opening covered in five-coloured gauze turned out to be a little window; there, a light veil of patterned silk concealed a doorway. Moreover, the walls were full of recesses cut to the exact shapes of the curios and ornaments they held. A qin, a sword, a hanging vase or a table screen might be mounted on the wall, but its surface lay flush with it. ‘What exquisite ingenuity!’ they said. ‘How did anyone think of it?’ Yet Jia Zheng and his companions had gone through fewer than two sets of partitions before they lost track of the way they had come. To the left there seemed to be a door; to the right, a window partly barred the way. They approached, only to find a bookcase in front of them. Turning back, they saw light through a gauze window and what looked like a clear passage through a door. But on reaching it they suddenly confronted a party of people exactly like themselves coming the other way. It was their own reflection in a great glass mirror.

They went round the mirror and found more doors than ever. ‘This way, sir,’ Jia Zhen said with a smile. ‘That door leads to the back courtyard. Going out through there is actually shorter than the way we came.’ They passed round two more sets of gauze screens and brocade partitions and did indeed find a door. Outside, roses and baoxiang flowers covered the courtyard trellises. Beyond a screen of flowers, a clear stream blocked their path. ‘Where does this water come from?’ they asked in surprise. Jia Zhen pointed into the distance. ‘It starts at that sluice and runs to the cave-mouth. Then it is brought round the north-eastern fold of the hill to the village. Another channel branches off towards the south-west. The water all flows here, joins together again and goes out beneath that wall.’ ‘Marvellous!’ they all said. As they spoke, a large hill suddenly barred the way. ‘We have lost ourselves!’ ‘Follow me,’ said Jia Zhen with a smile. He went ahead once more. They followed him, and one abrupt turn at the foot of the hill brought them onto a broad, level road. There, quite suddenly, was the main gate.

‘Delightful!’ they all said. ‘Such ingenuity could steal the secrets of the spirits themselves!’ Then they all went out.
