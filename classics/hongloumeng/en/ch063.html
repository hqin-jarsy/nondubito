Chapter 63 The flowers hold a birthday feast by night at Green Delights; A golden elixir brings death, and one fair woman conducts the mourning

Chapter end

Now Bao-yu returned to his room to wash his hands and consulted Aroma.

‘We’ll have wine this evening and enjoy ourselves. There must be no standing on ceremony. What shall we eat? We ought to tell them early, so they can get it ready.’

‘Don’t you worry,’ Aroma said, smiling. ‘Skybright, Musk, Ripple and I have put in five qian of silver apiece, making two taels. Parfumée, Emerald, Swallow and Si-er have put in three qian each. We’re not counting those on leave. That makes three taels and two qian altogether. Mrs Liu has had it already, to prepare forty little dishes of things to eat. I spoke to Patience too. A jar of good Shaoxing wine has been carried over and hidden there. The eight of us are giving you a birthday party all our own.’

Bao-yu was delighted.

‘But where do the little ones get money? You shouldn’t have made them pay.’

‘They have no money?’ said Skybright. ‘And we have, I suppose! They each wanted to do it. Even if they stole it, what of it? Just be grateful for their kindness.’

‘Quite right,’ Bao-yu said, laughing.

‘You can’t get through a day without a couple of sharp words from her to put you in your place,’ Aroma said with a smile.

‘You’re getting as bad as the rest,’ Skybright said, laughing. ‘Always stirring things up and fanning the flames.’

At this they all laughed.

‘Let’s shut the courtyard gate,’ said Bao-yu.

‘No wonder they call you Busy About Nothing,’ Aroma said, laughing. ‘Shutting it now would only make people suspicious. We may as well wait a little longer.’

Bao-yu nodded.

‘I’ll take a turn outside. Si-er, fetch some water. Swallow, you come with me. Just you.’

He went out. Finding no one about, he asked her about Wu-er.

‘I’ve just told Mrs Liu. She was delighted. But Wu-er was so upset by the way she was treated that night that when she got home she fell ill again with vexation. How could she come? She’ll have to wait till she’s better.’

Bao-yu sighed deeply, full of regret.

‘Does Aroma know about it?’ he asked.

‘I haven’t told her. I don’t know whether Parfumée has.’

‘I haven’t told her either,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Never mind. I’ll tell her myself.’

He went back in and made a show of washing his hands. It was already lamplighting time. Hearing a party enter at the courtyard gate, they all peeped quietly through the window. Sure enough, Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife was coming with several women in charge of the household, preceded by someone carrying a large lantern.

‘They’re checking the night watch,’ Skybright whispered with a smile. ‘Once they’ve gone, we can shut the gate.’

All those on night duty at the House of Green Delights came out to meet them. Mrs Lin checked that no one was missing.

‘No gambling or drinking, then dropping off and sleeping till broad daylight,’ she told them. ‘If I hear of it, I shan’t let you off.’

‘Who would dare do a thing like that?’ they all said, smiling.

‘Has Master Bao gone to bed?’ she asked.

None of them could say. Aroma hurriedly nudged Bao-yu. He slipped his feet into his shoes and came out to meet her with a smile.

‘I’m not asleep yet.

‘Come in and rest a little, Mamma.’ He called, ‘Aroma, bring some tea.’

Mrs Lin hurried in.

‘Still not in bed?’ she said, smiling. ‘The days are long and the nights short now. You ought to go to bed early, so you can get up early tomorrow. Otherwise, if you get up late, people will laugh and say you’re no gentleman who studies and goes to school, but more like one of those men who carry loads for a living.’

She laughed again.

‘You’re quite right, Mamma,’ Bao-yu said quickly, smiling. ‘I go to bed early every day. Whenever you come in, I’m already asleep and know nothing about it. Today I had noodles and was afraid they might lie heavy, so I’ve stayed up a little longer.’

Mrs Lin turned with a smile to Aroma and the others.

‘You ought to brew him some Pu’er tea.’

‘We’ve made a whole pot of Daughter’s Tea,’ Aroma and Skybright hastened to assure her, smiling. ‘He’s had two bowls already. Do try a bowl yourself, Mamma. It’s all ready.’

As they spoke, Skybright poured her one.

‘Lately I hear Master Bao has changed his way of speaking,’ Mrs Lin continued with a smile. ‘He’s taken to calling these senior girls by their names.

‘They may be serving in this room, but they still belong to Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship. You ought to show them some respect in the way you speak. Calling a name once in a while is all very well. But if you do it all the time, the younger brothers and nephews may copy you, and then people will laugh at a family that has no regard for its elders.’

‘You’re right, Mamma,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘It really is only once in a while.’

‘You mustn’t do him an injustice,’ Aroma and Skybright said, laughing. ‘He still calls us Sister all the time. He may say a name once or twice when we’re playing, but in front of other people he’s just as he always was.’

‘That’s as it should be,’ said Mrs Lin, smiling. ‘That shows he has learnt his books and his manners. The humbler you are, the more people respect you. Never mind old servants whose families have been here for three or five generations, or girls transferred straight from Her Old Ladyship’s or Her Ladyship’s rooms: even their cats and dogs aren’t to be hurt on a whim. That is how a properly brought-up young gentleman behaves.’

She finished her tea.

‘Do go to bed now. We’ll be off.’

‘Rest a little longer,’ Bao-yu was still saying, but she had already led her party away to inspect elsewhere.

Skybright and the others hurriedly had the gate shut and came back in laughing.

‘Our fine lady has had a cup somewhere. What a lot of jabbering! She’s given us another display of her importance and gone.’

‘She’s not doing it out of kindness, either,’ Musk said with a smile. ‘Still, she has to keep reminding us now and then. She’s guarding against some serious lapse, too.’

They began setting out the wine and food.

‘We don’t need the table hanging,’ said Aroma. ‘Let’s put the round huali kang table up on the kang and sit there. There’ll be more room, and it’ll be easier.’

They carried it over. Musk and Si-er fetched the dishes on two large tea trays, needing four or five journeys to bring everything. Two old women crouched outside by a brazier, warming the wine.

‘It’s hot,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Why don’t we all take our outer clothes off?’

‘You can take yours off if you like,’ they said, laughing. ‘We still have to take turns giving the formal welcome at your place.’

‘And by the time you’ve all done that it’ll be the fifth watch! You know how I hate those tiresome ceremonies. In front of outsiders they can’t be helped. But to plague me with them now is too bad.’

‘Have it your way,’ they said.

Instead of sitting down, they busied themselves taking off their ornaments and loosening their clothes. Soon their formal dress was laid aside. Their hair was twisted into loose knots, and they all wore long skirts and short jackets. Bao-yu had on only a little scarlet cotton-gauze jacket and lined trousers of green damask patterned with splashes of ink, their legs left loose. He leaned against a new pillow of lined blue-green gauze, stuffed with petals shaken from peonies of different colours, and began a game of finger-guessing with Parfumée. She kept complaining of the heat. All she wore above was a little lined patchwork jacket pieced from three-colour satin, with a willow-green sash about her waist. Below were lined pink trousers scattered with flowers, their legs loose too.

A ring of little plaits ran round her forehead and joined at the crown in one thick braid, as broad as a goose’s egg, hanging down behind. In her right ear she had only a tiny jade plug, the size of a grain of rice. From the left hung one large gold-mounted red stone, the size of a ginkgo nut. They made her face look whiter than the full moon and her eyes clearer than autumn water.

‘Those two look like twin brothers,’ everyone said, laughing.

Aroma and the others poured the wine and brought it over one by one.

‘Wait a moment before you go on with your guessing. Even if we don’t give the formal welcome, at least take a sip from each of us.’

Aroma came first, holding out her cup for a sip. The others followed in turn. Only when the round was finished did they all settle in a circle. There was no room for Swallow and Si-er along the edge of the kang, so they brought two chairs and placed them close beside it. The forty dishes were all of the same pale-white Ding ware, no bigger than little tea saucers. In them were delicacies to go with wine, fruits and vegetables from south of the mountains and north of the seas, from China and foreign lands: dried and fresh, from water and earth, all that the world could offer.

‘We ought to play a drinking game,’ said Bao-yu.

‘Something quiet and polite,’ said Aroma. ‘No shouting and bawling for people to hear. And remember, we can’t read. Don’t make it one of your literary ones.’

‘Let’s get the dice and play qianghong,’ Musk suggested with a smile.

‘That’s dull. No good,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Let’s draw flower lots.’

‘Just the thing,’ said Skybright, laughing. ‘I’ve been wanting to try that for ages.’

‘It’s a good game,’ Aroma said, ‘but not much fun with so few of us.’

‘Why don’t we quietly ask Miss Bao and Miss Lin to come and play?’ Swallow said with a smile. ‘It won’t be too late to go to bed at the second watch.’

‘And make a commotion opening doors and calling people?’ said Aroma. ‘What if you meet the night patrol and they start asking questions?’

‘What’s there to be afraid of?’ said Bao-yu. ‘Our Third Miss drinks wine too. We ought to ask her as well. And Miss Qin.’

‘Leave Miss Qin out of it,’ they all said. ‘She’s staying with the eldest mistress. You’ll stir up too much.’

‘Why be frightened? Go and ask them, quickly.’

Swallow and Si-er needed no second bidding. They hurried to have the gate opened and went their separate ways to invite the guests.

‘With just those two to ask them, Miss Bao and Miss Lin may refuse to come,’ said Skybright, Musk and Aroma. ‘Some of us had better go and drag them here, whatever it takes.’

Aroma and Skybright at once told an old woman to bring a lantern and set off. Sure enough, Bao-chai pleaded the lateness of the hour, and Dai-yu said she was not feeling well. The two maids begged them again and again.

‘Do us this little honour. Only sit with us for a moment, then you can come back.’

Tan-chun, however, was delighted by the invitation. But then she thought, ‘It won’t do if Li Wan hears we haven’t asked her.’ She sent Ebony with Swallow to press Li Wan and Bao-qin to come too. They gathered together and arrived, one after another, at the House of Green Delights. Aroma also insisted on bringing Caltrop.

Another table had to be set beside the first on the kang before there was room for them all.

‘Cousin Lin feels the cold,’ Bao-yu said at once. ‘Come over here by the wooden partition.’

He fetched a back cushion to make her comfortable. Aroma and the others brought chairs and sat in attendance below the kang. Leaning back on her cushion at some distance from the table, Dai-yu smiled at Bao-chai, Li Wan and Tan-chun.

‘Every day you scold other people for getting together at night to drink and gamble. Now we’re doing it ourselves. What will you say to them after this?’

‘Where’s the harm?’ Li Wan said, laughing. ‘It’s only at birthdays and festivals, once in a way. We don’t do it every night. We needn’t worry about that.’

Skybright brought a carved bamboo cylinder filled with ivory flower lots, shook it and set it in the middle. Then she put the dice in a box and shook them. When she lifted the lid, they showed five. Counting round brought them to Bao-chai.

‘So I draw first,’ she said with a smile. ‘I wonder what I shall get.’

She shook the cylinder, put in her hand and drew a lot. Everyone looked. A spray of peony was painted on it, with the heading ‘Beauty Crowning All the Flowers’. Below, in small engraved characters, was a line of Tang verse:

Even without feeling, you can move the heart.

A note added: ‘All present drink a cup in her honour. As queen of the flowers, she may call on anyone she pleases for a poem, a song or a graceful jest to enliven the wine.’

‘How very apt,’ they all said, laughing. ‘The peony is just the flower for you.’

They drank a cup together in her honour. Bao-chai finished hers and smiled.

‘Let Parfumée sing us something.’

‘In that case, everyone must drink a cup to gain admission to the performance,’ said Parfumée.

They drank, and she began:

How fair the scene where the birthday feast is spread!

‘Back with it!’ they all cried. ‘We don’t want you singing birthday wishes now. Pick the best thing you know.’

Parfumée had to begin again, singing delicately to the tune ‘A Time for Flowers’:

Green phoenix plumes are bound into my broom; At ease I tread Heaven’s gate and sweep the fallen flowers. See the wind lift clouds of jade-bright dust. One sudden plunge beneath that bank of cloud: Beyond the gate, already the ends of earth! Draw sword no more on Yellow Dragon, going wrong by just a thread; Nor seek out poor old Dong’s wine-selling home again. Let you and me lift our eyes towards the glowing clouds. Dong-bin, when you have found your man, be quick to let me know; Delay, and you will leave someone grieving among the peach flowers.1

The song ended. Bao-yu had been holding the lot all this time, repeating ‘Even without feeling, you can move the heart’ over and over. Now he looked at Parfumée without speaking. Xiang-yun snatched it from him and tossed it to Bao-chai. Bao-chai threw sixteen, which brought them to Tan-chun.

‘I wonder what I shall get,’ said Tan-chun, smiling.

She drew a lot, glanced at it herself, then flung it on the floor. Her face reddened as she laughed.

‘This is no good. We shouldn’t play this game. It’s a game for men outside, with all sorts of nonsense written on the lots.’

The others could not understand her. Aroma and her companions hurried to pick it up. It showed a spray of apricot blossom. The heading, in red, read ‘Immortal Flower of the Jasper Pool’, and the verse ran:

Red apricot by the sun, planted against the clouds.

The note said: ‘She who draws this lot shall surely find a noble husband. Let all congratulate her with a cup and drink a cup together.’

‘So that’s all!’ they said, laughing. ‘These lots were made for girls to amuse themselves with. Apart from two or three with something like this, there’s nothing improper on any of them. What harm can it do? We already have a princess in the family. Are you going to be a princess too? Congratulations! Congratulations!’

They came forward to drink to her. Tan-chun would not drink, but three or four of them, including Xiang-yun, Caltrop and Li Wan, insisted until they had forced the wine down her. She demanded that they discard this game and try another. They would not hear of it. Xiang-yun took her hand and made her throw the dice. Nineteen came up, making it Li Wan’s turn.

Li Wan shook the cylinder and drew a lot. At the sight of it she laughed.

‘Excellent! Look at this. There’s really something in this contraption.’

They looked. An old plum branch was painted on the lot, beneath the heading ‘Cold Beauty at Frosty Dawn’. On the other side was a line of old verse:

Bamboo fence and thatched cottage content my heart.

The note read: ‘Drink one cup yourself. The next player throws the dice.’

‘How amusing,’ said Li Wan, laughing. ‘Off you go, then. I’ll drink my one cup and leave you to your fortunes, good or bad.’

She drank and passed the dice to Dai-yu. Dai-yu threw eighteen, which brought them to Xiang-yun. Laughing, Xiang-yun flourished her fists, pushed up her sleeves and reached in to draw a lot. They saw a spray of crab-apple blossom on one side, with the heading ‘Deep in Fragrant Dreams’. The verse on the other side was:

I only fear the flowers may sleep when night is deep.

‘Change “night is deep” to “stone is cool”,’ Dai-yu said with a laugh.

They all knew she was teasing Xiang-yun about her drunken sleep that afternoon, and laughed. Xiang-yun pointed out the mechanical boat to Dai-yu.

‘Be off home in that boat, and stop talking!’

Everyone laughed again. They read the note: ‘Since she is “Deep in Fragrant Dreams”, the drawer of this lot should not drink. Only the players on either side must drink one cup each.’

‘Blessed Buddha! What a splendid lot!’ Xiang-yun cried, clapping her hands.

Dai-yu happened to be on one side of her and Bao-yu on the other. Two cups were poured, and they had no choice but to drink. Bao-yu drank half his, then, when no one was looking, passed it to Parfumée. She lifted it and tossed her head back. Dai-yu kept talking to someone and tipped the whole of hers into the rinsing bowl.

Xiang-yun picked up the dice and threw nine. Counting round brought them to Musk, who drew a lot. On one side was a tumi blossom, with the heading ‘Spring’s Glory at Its Height’. On the other was a line of old verse:

When tumi blooms, the season of flowers is done.2

The note said: ‘Everyone present must drink three cups to bid spring farewell.’

Musk asked what it meant. Bao-yu frowned and hastily hid the lot.

‘Let’s drink for now.’

They each took three mouthfuls to stand for the three cups. Musk threw nineteen, and it was Caltrop’s turn. She drew a lot with a double blossom on a single stem, headed ‘Spring Joined, Good Fortune Twined’. On the other side was the verse:

Flowers open on the branches grown as one.

The note read: ‘The drawer drinks three cups in celebration; everyone accompanies her with one.’

Caltrop then threw six, which made it Dai-yu’s turn. ‘I wonder if there is still something good for me to draw,’ Dai-yu thought silently. She reached out and took a lot. It bore a hibiscus flower under the heading ‘Pure Sorrow in Wind and Dew’. On the other side was a line of old verse:

Blame not the east wind; sigh for yourself instead.

The note said: ‘Drink one cup yourself. Peony drinks one in company.’

‘That’s perfect,’ everyone said, laughing. ‘Who but she could be the hibiscus?’

Dai-yu smiled too. She drank, then threw twenty, bringing the turn to Aroma. Aroma put out her hand and took a lot. It showed a spray of peach blossom, with the heading ‘Another World at Wuling’. On the other side the old verse ran:

Peach blossom reddens: spring has come another year.

The note read: ‘Apricot Blossom accompanies her with one cup; anyone present of the same age, one cup; anyone with the same birthday, one cup; anyone with the same surname, one cup.’

‘Now this will be lively!’ they all said, laughing.

They reckoned it up. Caltrop, Skybright and Bao-chai were all Aroma’s age, and Dai-yu shared her birthday. Only someone with the same surname was wanting.

‘My surname’s Hua too,’ Parfumée put in quickly. ‘I’ll drink a cup with her.’

The wine was poured. Dai-yu turned with a smile to Tan-chun.

‘You who are destined for a noble husband, you’re Apricot Blossom. Drink up, so we can drink.’

‘What is she like!’ Tan-chun said, laughing. ‘Sister-in-law, give her a smack while you’re about it.’

‘No noble husband for her, and a beating into the bargain?’ Li Wan said with a smile. ‘I haven’t the heart.’

Everyone laughed.

Aroma was just about to throw when someone called at the gate. An old woman hurried out to ask who it was. Aunt Xue had sent someone to fetch Dai-yu.

‘What watch is it?’ they asked.

‘Past the second. The clock has struck eleven.’

Bao-yu would not believe it until he had his watch brought and looked for himself. It was already ten fen into the first quarter of the early zi hour. Dai-yu rose.

‘I can’t keep going any longer. I still have my medicine to take when I get back.’

‘We ought all to be leaving,’ the others said.

Aroma and Bao-yu tried to keep them, but Li Wan and Bao-chai would not agree.

‘It’s too late. This won’t do. We’ve gone far beyond the rules already.’

‘In that case,’ said Aroma, ‘one more cup each before you go.’

Skybright and the others had already filled the cups. Everyone drank, then called for lights. Aroma and her companions saw them as far as the other side of the stream by Drenched Blossoms Pavilion before returning.

They shut the gate and began their drinking games again. Aroma and the others filled several large cups and arranged a tray of assorted fruit and dishes for the old nurses who were sitting below the kang. By now they all had a little wine in them. They played finger-guessing, the winners making the losers sing little songs.

It was the fourth watch. Between what the old nurses drank openly and what they stole on the sly, the wine jar was empty. The others were astonished to hear it. Only then did they clear away, wash and prepare for bed. Parfumée’s cheeks were rouge-red with drink, her brows and eyes lovelier still. Unable to hold herself up, she lay against Aroma.

‘Dear Sister, my heart’s beating so fast.’

‘Who told you to pour it down as hard as you could?’ Aroma said, laughing.

Swallow and Si-er had been unable to keep upright either and were already asleep. Skybright was still calling out.

‘Don’t call any more,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Let’s lie down as best we can and get a little rest.’

He put his head on the red, fragrant pillow, leaned over and was asleep himself. Seeing how drunk Parfumée was, Aroma feared that moving her about would make her vomit wine. She could only rise gently, ease the girl down beside Bao-yu and leave her to sleep. She herself lay down on the couch opposite.

They all sank into deep, sweet sleep, knowing nothing more. At daybreak Aroma opened her eyes to find the light already clear and bright.

‘We’re late!’

She looked across at the bed. Parfumée lay with her head on the edge of the kang, still sound asleep. Aroma hurried up to wake her. Bao-yu had already turned over and woken.

‘We’re late!’ he said with a smile, pushing Parfumée to rouse her.

She sat up, dazed, rubbing her eyes.

‘Have you no shame?’ Aroma said, laughing. ‘Getting drunk and stretching yourself out anywhere, without looking where you were!’

Parfumée looked round and only then realised that she had been on Bao-yu’s bed. Laughing, she scrambled down.

‘How could I have drunk till I knew nothing about it?’

‘I knew nothing about it either,’ Bao-yu said with a laugh. ‘If I had, I’d have smeared your face with ink.’

The maids came in to help him wash and dress.

‘Thank you for entertaining me yesterday,’ he said, smiling. ‘Tonight I’ll give you a feast in return.’

‘Enough, enough,’ Aroma said with a laugh. ‘No more today. If we do it again, people will start talking.’

‘What’s there to be afraid of? It’ll only be twice.

‘We can certainly call ourselves drinkers now. How did we get through that whole jar? Just when we were enjoying ourselves, it was gone.’

‘That’s what made it enjoyable,’ Aroma said, laughing. ‘If we’d gone on till we’d had enough, there’d have been nothing left to savour. Everyone was in such spirits yesterday. Skybright even forgot to be shy. I remember her singing a song.’

‘You’ve forgotten, Sister,’ Si-er said with a laugh. ‘You sang one too. Was there anyone there who didn’t?’

They all blushed, covered their faces with both hands and laughed helplessly. Just then Patience came in beaming. She had come herself to invite everyone who had been at yesterday’s feast.

‘Today it’s my turn to be hostess. Not one of you is to be missing.’

They hurried to offer her a seat and tea.

‘What a pity she wasn’t here last night,’ Skybright said, laughing.

‘What were you doing last night?’ Patience asked at once.

‘We can’t tell you,’ said Aroma. ‘We had the liveliest time. Even when Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship had us all together for a party, it was never as much fun as last night. We finished a whole jar of wine. Everyone drank their shyness away and, before we knew it, we were all singing. It was well into the fourth watch before we dropped off anyhow, sprawled all over the place.’

‘Very nice!’ Patience said, laughing. ‘You wheedle wine out of me, don’t invite me, and then tell me all about it to make me cross.’

‘He’s giving us a feast in return today,’ said Skybright. ‘You’ll be invited, never fear.’

‘Who is “he”? “He” who?’ Patience asked, laughing.

Skybright ran after her to give her a laughing slap.

‘Trust your sharp ears to hear every word!’

‘I’ve things to do now. I can’t stop and talk,’ Patience said with a smile. ‘I’m off to my work. I’ll send someone later to fetch you. If any one of you fails to come, I’ll come and beat down your door.’

Bao-yu and the others tried to keep her, but she had gone.

Bao-yu had finished washing and dressing and was drinking tea when he caught sight of a sheet of paper trapped beneath the inkstone.

‘It won’t do, leaving things anyhow under there,’ he said.

‘What is it now?’ Aroma and Skybright asked at once. ‘Who’s done something wrong this time?’

‘What’s that under the inkstone?’ he said, pointing. ‘Someone’s pattern left lying about again, I suppose.’

Skybright quickly lifted the inkstone and pulled it out. It was a written card. She handed it to Bao-yu. On the pink notepaper was written:

‘Adamantina, one outside the threshold, reverently bows from afar in honour of your birthday.’

Bao-yu read it and sprang to his feet.

‘Who took this in? Why wasn’t I told?’

Seeing his agitation, Aroma and Skybright supposed it must be a card from someone of great importance.

‘Who took in a card yesterday?’ they all demanded.

Si-er came running in.

‘Adamantina didn’t come herself yesterday,’ she said, smiling. ‘She only sent an old woman with it.

‘I put it there, and who would have thought a drinking party would drive it clean out of my mind?’

‘And we wondered who it was, to make such a fuss!’ the others said. ‘Hardly worth all this.’

‘Quick, bring paper,’ Bao-yu ordered.

Paper was brought and ink ground. But looking at the words ‘one outside the threshold’, he could not think of a suitable self-description to use in reply. He held his brush and stared, unable to decide even after a long time. ‘If I ask Bao-chai,’ he thought, ‘she’ll only criticise it as eccentric nonsense. Better ask Cousin Lin.’

He tucked the card into his sleeve and went straight to find Dai-yu. He had just passed Drenched Blossoms Pavilion when he saw Xiu-yan coming towards him with swaying, delicate steps.

‘Where are you going, Cousin?’ he asked at once.

‘To talk to Adamantina,’ Xiu-yan said with a smile.

Bao-yu was astonished.

‘She’s so solitary and eccentric, so out of step with everyone. Not one person in ten thousand is good enough for her. If she thinks so highly of you, she must know you’re not one of us common mortals.’

‘She may not really think so highly of me,’ Xiu-yan said, smiling. ‘But we were neighbours for ten years, with only a wall between us. She was practising her devotions at Panxiang Temple. My family were poor, and we rented rooms belonging to the temple. We lived there for ten years. Whenever I had nothing to do, I went over to keep her company.

‘Whatever characters I know, I owe to her teaching. We were friends in poverty, and she was half a teacher to me as well. Then we went away to stay with relations. I heard that she was so out of step with the times that the powerful people there would not tolerate her, and that she had taken refuge here. Now Heaven has brought us together again, and our old affection is unchanged. If anything, she treats me with even greater kindness than before.’

The news struck Bao-yu like a clap of thunder. Delighted, he laughed.

‘No wonder there’s something so free about the way you speak and move, like a wild crane or an idle cloud. So that is where it comes from! I was just puzzling over something to do with her and going to ask someone’s advice. Meeting you is a heaven-sent chance. Do help me.’

He took out the card and showed it to Xiu-yan.

‘She’ll never change,’ Xiu-yan said, laughing. ‘She was born to be extravagant and odd. Whoever heard of signing a visiting card with a private literary name? Just as the saying goes: “Neither nun nor laywoman, neither woman nor man.” What sense does it make?’

‘You don’t understand, Cousin,’ Bao-yu said quickly, smiling. ‘She doesn’t belong to any of those categories. She’s someone beyond the world’s imagining.

‘It is because she credits me with a little understanding that she has sent me this card. But I can’t think how to answer it. I was just going to ask Cousin Lin when I happened to meet you.’

Xiu-yan looked him up and down, studying him for some time before she smiled.

‘No wonder they say hearing of someone is not the same as meeting him. And no wonder Adamantina sent you this card, or gave you all that plum blossom last year. Since even she is like this with you, I must tell you the reason. She is always saying, “Of all the ancients, from the Han and Jin, the Five Dynasties, the Tang and the Song onwards, none wrote good poetry. Only two lines are good:

Your iron threshold may endure a thousand years; At last one little mound of earth must cover you.”

‘That is why she calls herself “one outside the threshold”. She also keeps praising Zhuang-zi’s prose, so at other times she calls herself “one apart from the world”. If she had signed the card that way, you could have answered as “one in the world”. She means that she is a person standing apart; you humbly describe yourself as one caught in the world’s bustle. That would please her.

‘Now she calls herself “one outside the threshold”, meaning that she has stepped beyond the iron threshold. So you need only sign yourself “one within the threshold”, and she will be quite satisfied.’

Bao-yu felt as though wisdom had been poured over his head in a stream of purest ghee.

‘Oh!’ he exclaimed, then laughed. ‘No wonder our family temple is called the Temple of the Iron Threshold. So that is what lies behind it.

‘Do go on, Cousin. I must write my answer.’

Xiu-yan went on towards Green Bower Hermitage. Bao-yu returned to his room and wrote a card bearing only these words:

‘Bao-yu, one within the threshold, having bathed and purified himself with incense, respectfully bows.’

He took it to Green Bower Hermitage himself, slipped it through the crack in the gate and came home.

He found Parfumée had combed her hair, twisted it into a knot and put in flowers and kingfisher ornaments. At once he told her to change her appearance. He had the short hairs round the sides shaved away to show the blue-green scalp, leaving the long hair arranged at the crown.

‘In winter you can wear a big sable headband,’ he said, ‘and little military boots in five colours, with tiger heads and coiling clouds. Or leave your trouser legs loose and just wear plain stockings and thick-soled shoes with an edging.’

Then he added, ‘Parfumée is no good as a name. A boy’s name would be much more distinctive.’

He renamed her ‘Xiong-nu’, Male Slave. She was delighted.

‘In that case, take me with you when you go out. If anyone asks, just say I’m a page like Tealeaf.’

‘They’d still be able to tell,’ Bao-yu said, laughing.

‘You have no imagination,’ Parfumée said with a laugh. ‘There are several families of border tribespeople in our own household. Say I’m a little tribal servant. Besides, everyone says I look well with my hair in paired plaits. Isn’t that a splendid idea?’

Bao-yu was delighted beyond expectation.

‘Excellent!’ he said eagerly, laughing. ‘I’ve often seen officials with attendants of foreign stock, captives presented to them. They like them because they stand up to wind and frost and are nimble in the saddle. If that’s the plan, you ought to have a foreign name too. Let’s call you Yelü Xiong-nu. The sound of Xiong-nu is like Xiongnu, and both are names of the Dog Rong. Besides, those two peoples have been a plague to China ever since Yao and Shun. The Jin, the Tang and other dynasties suffered terribly from them.

‘Luckily for us, we were born in the present age. Our ruler is the true descendant of the great Shun. His holy virtue, merit, benevolence and filial piety, like Shun’s of Yu, shine up to Heaven and shall endure with heaven, earth, sun and moon for countless ages. So all the petty villains who ran riot in earlier dynasties have now been made by Heaven itself to come from far away with folded hands and bowed heads to submit, without our using a single shield or spear. We ought to humiliate them, to add lustre to our sovereign and father.’

‘If that’s so,’ Parfumée said, laughing, ‘you ought to practise archery and riding, learn some martial arts and go out boldly to capture a few rebels. Wouldn’t that be serving him loyally? Why use us? You flap your lips and wag your tongue, making a game of it for your own amusement, then call it praising his achievements and virtues.’

‘That’s where you don’t understand,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Now the four seas acknowledge our rule and all eight quarters are at peace. There’ll be no need for armed preparations for hundreds and thousands of years. Even in our games and jokes we ought to offer praise, so we’re not ungrateful for the peace we enjoy without lifting a finger.’

Parfumée thought this reasonable. The two of them considered their arrangement thoroughly fitting and proper, and Bao-yu began calling her ‘Yelü Xiong-nu’.

In fact, both Jia mansions did possess prisoners captured by their ancestors in earlier years and bestowed on them as slaves. They were employed only in tending horses and were of little use for anything more important. Xiang-yun had always been extraordinarily fond of boisterous play. She loved military dress and often put on a bell-hung belt and turned-back sleeves herself. Seeing that Bao-yu had dressed Parfumée as a boy, she dressed Kui-guan as one too. Kui-guan habitually kept her hair shaven short to make it easier to apply stage paint and make-up to her face. She was nimble too, and dressing her up was all the easier. Li Wan and Tan-chun liked what they saw and had Bao-qin’s Dou-guan dressed as a little boy as well: two forked tufts of hair, a short jacket and red shoes. All she lacked was a painted face to be the very image of a lute-boy on the stage.

Xiang-yun changed Kui-guan’s name to Da-ying. Since her surname was Wei, this made her Wei Da-ying, which suited Xiang-yun exactly, with its hidden echo of wei da ying-xiong, ‘Only a great hero can show his true colours’. Why should a boy need rouge and powder? Dou-guan was extremely small and young, as well as wonderfully quick and mischievous; hence her name, Dou-guan, Little Bean.

Some in the garden called her A-dou, and others Fried Beans. Bao-qin thought names like Lute-boy and Book-boy far too common. The word dou, bean, was more distinctive, so she renamed her Dou-tong, Bean-boy.

After the meal, Patience gave her return feast. Saying that Red Fragrance Court was too hot, she had several tables of fresh wine and fine dishes laid in Elm-shade Hall instead.

As luck would have it, You-shi had brought Jia Zhen’s two concubines, Pei-feng and Xie-yuan, over for a visit. They were both young, pretty, playful women and seldom came there. Now they were in the garden with Xiang-yun, Caltrop, Parfumée, Rui-guan and the other girls. The sayings ‘Like draws to like’ and ‘Things gather according to their kind’ were proved true enough: there was no end to their chatter and laughter. They paid no heed to where You-shi might be, leaving the maids to attend her while they went about enjoying themselves with the others.

Presently they reached the House of Green Delights and heard Bao-yu call ‘Yelü Xiong-nu’. Pei-feng, Xie-yuan and Caltrop collapsed together in laughter and asked what it meant. Everyone tried calling the name, mispronouncing it or forgetting part, until some even turned Yelü into ‘Wild Ass’. Whoever heard it anywhere in the garden was helpless with laughter.

Bao-yu saw that everyone was making fun of her and feared that she was being humiliated. He hastily spoke again.

‘In Fu-lang-si-ya, west of the seas, I hear they have a precious stone called gold-flecked glass. In their own foreign language they call gold-flecked glass “Wen-du-li-na”.

‘Shall I compare you to that and change your name to Wen-du-li-na?’

Parfumée was more delighted still.

‘Yes, let’s do that.’

So that became her next name. But the others found it too much of a tongue-twister. They translated it back into Chinese and simply called her ‘Glass’.

Enough of these digressions. Everyone was now at Elm-shade Hall, using wine as an excuse for laughter and play. They told the woman entertainer to beat the drum. Patience picked a peony, and the company, some twenty of them, passed the flower round for a drinking game. They made merry for a while.

Then word came that two women from the Zhen family had arrived with gifts. Tan-chun, Li Wan and You-shi went out to meet them in the council hall. The others came outside to stroll about. Pei-feng and Xie-yuan went to play on the swing.

‘You two get on,’ said Bao-yu. ‘I’ll push you.’

‘No, no!’ Pei-feng said in alarm. ‘Don’t get us into trouble. We can ask Wild Ass to give us a push.’

‘Dear sisters, do stop joking,’ Bao-yu said quickly, smiling. ‘People will copy you and start calling her names.’

‘We’re weak with laughter. How can we swing?’ said Xie-yuan. ‘You’ll fall off and knock your innards out!’

Pei-feng ran after her to hit her.

Their laughter was still going on when several people from the eastern mansion came running up in a panic.

‘The Master has ascended to Heaven!’

Everyone started in horror.

‘He was perfectly well. He wasn’t ill. How can he be dead?’

‘The Master practised his devotions every day,’ the servants said. ‘His merit must have reached fulfilment, and he has risen to join the immortals.’

You-shi heard the news. With Jia Zhen, his son and Jia Lian all away, there was not a man close to her whom she could turn to, and for a moment she was at a loss. She could only hurry to take off her ornaments and send orders to Xuan-zhen Abbey that all the Daoists be locked up until her husband came home to question them. Then she hastily took a carriage out of the city, bringing Lai Sheng and a party of household men and their wives. Imperial physicians were summoned too, to discover what illness it had been.

The doctors found him already dead. How could they take his pulse now? They had long known that Jia Jing’s breathing practices were empty nonsense. His worship of the stars and the Dipper, his vigils on gengshen nights and his swallowing of cinnabar had been foolish, futile exertions. In their view, he had so exhausted his mind and strength that he had destroyed his life. Even in death his belly was hard as iron, and the skin of his face and lips was burnt purple-red, shrivelled and cracked.

They reported to his daughter-in-law:

‘He died from the burning and swelling caused by swallowing gold and cinnabar in the Daoist manner.’

The Daoists were alarmed.

‘It was the cinnabar elixir the Master had newly prepared by his secret method that did the harm,’ they said. ‘We did warn him that his merit had not yet reached the point where he could take it. We never expected him to swallow it secretly during last night’s gengshen vigil. Then he rose to immortality. Perhaps his devotion has won him the Way, and he has left the sea of suffering, cast off the shell of his flesh and gone free.’

You-shi paid no attention. She merely ordered that they be kept locked up for Jia Zhen to deal with and sent a mounted messenger at full speed to bring him the news. Meanwhile she saw that the place was too cramped for the body to lie there. In any case it could not be taken into the city. She had it dressed at once and carried in a soft litter to the Temple of the Iron Threshold to lie in state. Counting on her fingers, she reckoned that Jia Zhen could not arrive in less than half a month. In the present heat, waiting for him was out of the question. She therefore took charge herself and had an astrologer choose a day for placing the body in its coffin. The coffin had been prepared years before and deposited at this temple, which was a great convenience.

Three days later the mourning formally began and mourning clothes were put on. Services for the dead were started while they awaited Jia Zhen. At the Rong mansion Xi-feng could not come out, Li Wan was looking after the girls, and Bao-yu had no understanding of such affairs. Outside business therefore had to be entrusted for the time being to several of the household’s second-rank stewards. Jia Bin, Jia Guang, Jia Heng, Jia Ying, Jia Chang and Jia Ling each had duties assigned to them. Unable to return home herself, You-shi had her stepmother brought to keep house at the Ning mansion.

Her stepmother could only bring her two unmarried younger daughters with her. She would not feel easy unless they were all living together.

When Jia Zhen received the news, he immediately applied for leave, as did Jia Rong, who also held an official appointment. The Board of Rites knew how highly the present emperor esteemed filial piety and brotherly duty. Not daring to decide the matter themselves, they submitted a memorial requesting his command. The emperor’s benevolence and filial piety were indeed beyond measure, and he placed particular value on the descendants of meritorious servants. On seeing the memorial, he at once asked what office Jia Jing held. The Board replied:

‘He attained the jinshi degree. The hereditary office of his ancestors has already passed to his son Jia Zhen. Being advanced in years and often ill, Jia Jing had long lived in quiet retirement at Xuan-zhen Abbey outside the capital. He has now died there of illness. His son Zhen and grandson Rong are here in attendance on Your Majesty for the state mourning, and therefore beg leave to return for the coffining.’

On hearing this, the emperor at once issued an edict of exceptional favour:

‘Though Jia Jing held no official appointment and rendered no service to the state, in consideration of the services of his forebears We grant him posthumous office of the fifth rank. Let his son and grandson escort his coffin into the capital by the gate on the northern side, and take it to their private residence for the funeral observances. They are permitted, on completing the full mourning rites, to escort the coffin to their native place. In addition, the Court of Imperial Entertainments shall provide a sacrificial offering according to the highest precedent.

‘All at court, from princes and dukes downwards, are permitted to offer sacrifice and condolences. Respect this.’

When the edict was issued, not only did the Jia family give thanks for the imperial favour, but all the ministers at court acclaimed the emperor’s long life and praised him without end. Jia Zhen and his son rode home at speed through the night. Halfway, they met Jia Bin and Jia Guang galloping towards them at the head of a party of household men. At the sight of Jia Zhen, both rolled from their saddles and offered their respects.

‘What are you doing here?’ Jia Zhen asked at once.

‘Sister-in-law was afraid that once you and my nephew had left, there would be no one on the road to look after Her Old Ladyship,’ Jia Bin replied. ‘She sent the two of us to escort her.’

Jia Zhen was full of praise. He asked how things had been managed at home. Jia Bin and his companion explained how the Daoists had been detained and the body moved to the family temple, and how, fearing there would be no one left in the house, You-shi had asked her mother and the two aunts to stay in the main apartments. Jia Rong had dismounted too. At the news that the two aunts were there, he exchanged a smile with Jia Zhen.

‘Very good, very good,’ Jia Zhen said quickly, and spurred his horse on. They did not stop at inns, but changed horses through the night and galloped on. At last they reached the capital’s gates and went straight to the Temple of the Iron Threshold. It was already the fourth watch. As soon as the night watchmen heard, they shouted to wake everyone.

Jia Zhen dismounted. He and Jia Rong broke into loud weeping and, dropping to their knees outside the main gate, crawled all the way in. Before the coffin they struck their foreheads on the ground and wept as though their tears were blood. They did not stop until daylight, when their voices had grown hoarse. You-shi and the others all came to meet them. Father and son hastened to change into the mourning garments prescribed by ritual and prostrated themselves before the coffin. But business had to be attended to. They could not remain blind to everything, deaf to every sound. They had to restrain their grief a little to direct the household. Jia Zhen recounted the gracious edict in full to their assembled relatives and friends, then sent Jia Rong home ahead of him to prepare for the coffin’s arrival.

Jia Rong needed no second bidding. He galloped home and immediately ordered the tables and chairs removed from the front hall, the partition screens taken down and mourning curtains hung. A shelter for the musicians and a ceremonial archway were to be erected at the gate, with the other necessary arrangements. Then he hurried inside to see his grandmother and his two aunts.

Old Mrs You, being elderly and fond of sleep, was usually reclining. His Second and Third Aunts were doing needlework with the maids. When he arrived they offered their condolences. But Jia Rong grinned at his Second Aunt.

‘Second Aunt, so you’ve come again. Father’s been longing for you.’

You Er-jie blushed and scolded him.

‘You little Rong! You can’t bear to go two days without a scolding from me. You’ve less and less idea of proper behaviour. And you a young gentleman from a great family, studying books and manners every day! You’re worse than a lout from some common household.’

She snatched up an iron and aimed a blow at his head. Alarmed, Jia Rong covered his head with his hands and tumbled into her lap, begging for mercy. You San-jie came up to tear at his mouth.

‘Wait till our elder sister comes home. We’ll tell her.’

Laughing, Jia Rong knelt on the kang and begged them to forgive him. The two sisters laughed too.

Jia Rong next tried to snatch the sharen seeds his Second Aunt was eating. You Er-jie chewed a mouthful to a pulp and spat it all over his face. He licked it off with his tongue and ate it. The maids could not stand the sight.

‘You’re in fresh mourning,’ they said, laughing, ‘and your grandmother has only just gone to sleep. Those two may be young, but they’re still your aunts. You have no respect for your mother at all. When we tell the Master, you’ll have more trouble than you can carry away.’

Jia Rong left his aunts and began embracing and kissing the maids.

‘My darlings, you’re right. Let’s make those two jealous.’

They hurried to push him away, scolding in disgust.

‘You little devil, may you die young! You have a wife and maids of your own, but you’re always pestering us. Those who know us will say it’s play. Those who don’t, especially some rotten-hearted busybody who likes gossiping and meddling, will make such a noise that everyone in the other mansion will hear. Who isn’t already whispering behind our backs about the filthy goings-on here?’

‘Separate doors, separate households,’ Jia Rong said, laughing. ‘Who’s to mind anyone else’s business? They’ve all got enough of their own.

‘From ancient times to now, even of the Han and Tang dynasties people say “Filthy Tang, stinking Han”. What do you expect of a family like ours? What household has no love affairs? Don’t make me spell them out. The Elder Master over there is fearsome enough, yet Uncle Lian has been carrying on with the old man's young concubine. Aunt Feng is as stern as they come, yet Uncle Rui had designs on her. What have they ever kept from me?’

Jia Rong was still running on with whatever nonsense came into his head when his grandmother woke. He offered his respects and asked after her health.

‘We’ve put you to so much trouble, Grandmother, and made things so uncomfortable for our two aunts. My father and I can never thank you enough.

‘When all this is over, every one of our family, old and young, must come to your house and kowtow to you.’

Old Mrs You nodded.

‘My child, you do know how to say the right thing. Relations ought to help one another.’

Then she asked, ‘Is your father well? When did you get the news and arrive?’

‘We’ve only just arrived,’ Jia Rong answered with a smile. ‘He sent me ahead to see you.

‘You really must stay till everything is over.’

As he spoke he winked at his Second Aunt. You Er-jie smiled through clenched teeth and scolded him.

‘You chattering little monkey! Are you keeping us here to be mothers to your father?’

Jia Rong teased his grandmother again.

‘Don’t worry. Father frets about our two aunts every day. He wants to find us two uncles with good family connections, rich, young and handsome, so he can marry our aunts to them. He’s been looking all these years without finding anyone. But just the other day, on the road, he happened to spot one who would do.’

Old Mrs You took him at his word and eagerly asked whose family the man belonged to. Both sisters dropped their needlework and ran after him, laughing and trying to hit him.

‘Mother, don’t believe that thunder-struck wretch!’

Even the maids joined in.

‘Heaven has eyes. Mind a thunderbolt doesn’t find you!’

Just then someone came to report, ‘Everything is ready. Will you come out and inspect it, sir, so you can take word back to the Master?’

Only then did Jia Rong go out, still grinning.
