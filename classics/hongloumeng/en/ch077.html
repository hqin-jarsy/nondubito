Chapter 77 A lovely maid, wronged, dies before her time; A pretty actress severs love and turns to Water-Moon

Chapter end

After the Mid-Autumn Festival, Lady Wang found that Xi-feng’s illness had abated. Though far from well, she could now get about again. The doctor was still required to examine her pulse every day, and she continued to take medicine. He had also written out a prescription for Menstrual-regulating and Blood-nourishing Pills to be made up.

The prescription called for two taels of the best ginseng. Lady Wang went to get it, but after a long search could find only a few roots in a small box, each about as thick as the shank of a hairpin. She thought them poor and ordered another search. This produced a large packet of rootlets and powder.

‘There’s plenty when we have no use for it,’ she said irritably, ‘and the moment it’s wanted, none can be found. I’m forever telling you to go through things and put them all together. You never listen. You just toss them down wherever you happen to be. You don’t understand how valuable it is. When we need it, we may pay any amount and still not get what will serve.’

‘Perhaps there isn’t any left, ma’am,’ said Cai-yun. ‘There’s only this.

‘Her Ladyship from the other side came to ask for some last time, and you gave it all to her.’

‘Nonsense. Look again, properly.’

Cai-yun had to search once more. She brought several packets of medicinal ingredients.

‘We don’t recognise these, ma’am. Will you look yourself? Apart from these, there really is nothing.’

Lady Wang opened them, but she too had forgotten what they were. Not one contained a root of ginseng. She sent to ask whether Xi-feng had any. Xi-feng came to say that she had only some ginseng extract, root crowns and rootlets.

‘There are a few whole roots, but they aren’t of the best, and I still need them for the medicine I have boiled every day.’

Lady Wang could only ask at Lady Xing’s. Lady Xing replied that it was because her own supply had run out that she had asked Lady Wang for some before, and that had long since been used up too. At a loss, Lady Wang went herself to ask Grandmother Jia. Grandmother promptly told Faithful to bring out what remained of her old supply. There was still a large packet, all roots as thick as a finger. She had two taels weighed out for Lady Wang.

On leaving, Lady Wang gave it to Zhou Rui’s wife to have a page take to the doctor’s house. The unidentified packets were to go too: he was to identify them and label each one before sending them back. Presently Zhou Rui’s wife returned with the packets.

‘These have all been wrapped up separately and labelled. As for this packet of ginseng, he says it certainly is of the very best. You couldn’t get its like nowadays even at thirty times its weight in silver. But it’s far too old. Ginseng isn’t like other things: however good it may be, after a hundred years it crumbles to dust of itself. This hasn’t turned to dust yet, but it’s become rotten, decaying wood, with no strength left in it. He asks you to put it away and get some fresh, whether thick roots or thin.’

Lady Wang lowered her head and said nothing. At last she spoke.

‘Then there’s nothing to be done. We shall have to buy two taels.’

She had no heart to look through the other packets, but merely told them to put everything away.

‘Go and tell the people outside to choose some good ginseng and buy two taels,’ she told Zhou Rui’s wife.

‘If the Old Lady happens to ask, just say we used hers. There’s no need to tell her any more.’

Zhou Rui’s wife was about to leave when Bao-chai, who was sitting there, spoke with a smile.

‘Wait a moment, Aunt. There’s no good ginseng to be had in the shops now. Even if they have a whole root, they cut it into two or three pieces, fit on crowns, spongy pieces and rootlets, and mix it all together to sell. You can’t judge it by its thickness. Our shops often deal with the ginseng merchants. I’ll go and tell Mother, and have my brother ask one of the clerks to explain to them what we want: two taels of good, untouched roots. We can afford a few more taels of silver if it means getting the good sort.’

‘You do understand these things,’ Lady Wang said, smiling. ‘If you would be kind enough to go yourself, that would be better still.’

Bao-chai went, and after a while returned.

‘Someone has been sent. We should hear back this evening. It won’t be too late to have the pills made up first thing tomorrow.’

Lady Wang was naturally pleased.

‘“The oil-seller’s wife dresses her hair with water!” We’ve always had good ginseng in the house. Heaven knows how much we’ve given away.

‘Now we want some ourselves, we have to go begging everywhere.’ She sighed deeply.

‘Valuable though it is, it’s only medicine, after all,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘It ought to be given out to help people. We’re not like some family that has never seen anything of the world, locking it up and hoarding it the moment they get any.’

‘That is quite true,’ Lady Wang said, nodding.

After Bao-chai had gone, Lady Wang found there was no one else in the room. She called Zhou Rui’s wife and asked whether the search in the garden the other day had led to anything. Zhou Rui’s wife had already settled the matter in consultation with Xi-feng and the others. She gave Lady Wang the whole account, concealing nothing. Lady Wang was shocked and angry, but also at a loss. Chess belonged to Ying-chun: they were all people of the other branch of the household. She therefore ordered someone to report to Lady Xing.

‘The other day,’ Zhou Rui’s wife told her, ‘Her Ladyship was so angry with Wang Shan-bao’s wife for making trouble that she slapped her several times. Now the woman is pretending to be ill at home and won’t show herself. Besides, the girl’s her own daughter’s child. She’s slapped her own face, as you might say. All she can do is pretend to have forgotten it until things have settled down. If we go over to report it now, Her Ladyship may take it the wrong way and think we’re the ones making trouble. Wouldn’t it be simpler to take Chess straight over, with the things found on her as evidence, and let Her Ladyship see for herself? The girl will only be beaten and married off, and another maid chosen in her place.

‘If we simply send word, Her Ladyship may raise one difficulty after another, and then say, “If that’s how it is, your mistress should have dealt with it. Why tell me?” We shall only have wasted time. Suppose the girl finds a chance to kill herself? That would be a fine business. They’ve been watching her for two or three days now, but everyone slackens off sometimes. One moment with nobody there, and we might have a real calamity on our hands.’

Lady Wang considered.

‘That’s true, too. Get this one settled quickly. Then we can deal with the little demons in our own household.’

Zhou Rui’s wife assembled the other serving-women and went first to Ying-chun’s rooms.

‘Their Ladyships say Chess is grown up now,’ she told Ying-chun. ‘Her mother has been petitioning these last few days, and Her Ladyship has granted her the girl, to marry off. She is to leave today. Another good maid will be chosen for you.’

She told Chess to get ready to go. Ying-chun’s eyes filled with tears; she seemed reluctant to part with her. But she had heard the reason from the other maids whispering the night before last. Hard as it was to break the affection of years, this was a matter of morals, and nothing could be done. Chess had begged her too, fully hoping that Ying-chun would stand firm for her and secure her pardon. But Ying-chun was slow of speech, easily persuaded, irresolute: she could decide nothing for herself. Seeing how matters stood, Chess knew she could not escape.

‘How hard-hearted you are, miss!’ she cried. ‘You’ve kept me hoping these two days, and now you haven’t even a word to say?’

‘Do you still expect your mistress to keep you?’ said Zhou Rui’s wife and the others. ‘Even if she did, how could you face the people in the garden?

‘Take our advice. Stop making this scene and slip away quietly, without anyone knowing. It will look better for everybody.’

‘I know what a terrible wrong you’ve done,’ Ying-chun said through her tears. ‘If I still pleaded desperately to keep you, wouldn’t I be ruined too? Look at Picture. She had been here for years as well, yet she had to go the moment she was told. Of course it won’t be just you two. I expect all the older girls in the garden will have to leave. To my mind, since we must all part in the end, you might as well go your separate ways now.’

‘There!’ said Zhou Rui’s wife. ‘Miss understands, as you’d expect. There are others to be sent away tomorrow. You needn’t worry about that.’

Chess had no choice. In tears she kowtowed to Ying-chun and said goodbye to her fellow maids. Then she whispered in Ying-chun’s ear:

‘If you hear they’re going to punish me, please say a word for me. For the sake of the time I’ve served you!’

‘Don’t worry,’ Ying-chun promised, also weeping.

Zhou Rui’s wife and the others led Chess out of the courtyard, telling two old women to carry all her belongings. They had gone only a few steps when Xiu-ju came hurrying after them, wiping her eyes. She handed Chess a bundle wrapped in silk.

‘This is from Miss. You’ve served her all this time, and now you’re suddenly to be parted. Keep this to remember her by.’

Chess took it and began to cry still harder. She and Xiu-ju wept together for a while, but Zhou Rui’s wife was impatient and kept hurrying them on. They had to part.

‘Aunties, please show me a little kindness,’ Chess begged through her tears. ‘Let me stop for a moment and say goodbye to the girls I’m fond of. We’ve been friends all these years.’

Zhou Rui’s wife and the others all had their own work to do. They had undertaken this business only because they had to; besides, they deeply resented the airs these girls ordinarily gave themselves. How could they have time to listen to her now?

‘You’d better come along,’ they said with a sneer. ‘Stop dragging it out.

‘We’ve proper work to do. Which of them came out of the same womb as you? What do you want to say goodbye for? They can’t get enough of laughing at you as it is. You’re only trying to put it off from one moment to the next. Do you think that will make it all go away? Come along, I say.’

They talked without once stopping, taking her straight towards the rear side gate. There was nothing Chess could do. Not daring to speak again, she followed them out.

Just then Bao-yu happened to be coming in. He saw Chess being taken out, with her belongings carried behind her, and guessed that once she went she could never come back. He had heard of the affair of the night watch. Skybright’s illness, too, had grown worse since that day, but however closely he questioned her, she would not tell him why. The other day he had seen Picture go, and now Chess was leaving too. He felt as though his soul had been torn away. He hurried to stop them.

‘Where are you going?’

Zhou Rui’s wife and the rest knew his usual ways. Afraid he would hold them up with endless talk, they answered with a smile:

‘Nothing to do with you. Run along and read your books.’

‘My good sisters, do stop a moment,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘I have something to say.’

‘Her Ladyship won’t allow a moment’s delay,’ said Zhou Rui’s wife. ‘What can there be to say? We only know we have to obey her. We can’t concern ourselves with everything.’

Seeing Bao-yu, Chess clutched at him and cried:

‘They can’t decide anything. Please, please go and beg Her Ladyship!’

Bao-yu could not help grieving too.

‘I don’t know what terrible thing you’ve done,’ he said with tears in his eyes. ‘Skybright is ill, and now you’re going.

‘If everyone goes, what are we to do?’

Zhou Rui’s wife turned on Chess in exasperation.

‘You’re not a little deputy mistress now! If you won’t do as you’re told, I can beat you. Don’t imagine it’s like the old days, with your mistress protecting you while you ran wild. The more we say, the less willing you are to move! Clinging to young gentlemen now, too. What sort of behaviour is that?’

The serving-women gave her no chance to answer, but dragged her away.

Bao-yu was afraid they would go and tell tales about him. All he could do was glare at them in fury. When they were well out of reach, he pointed after them.

‘It’s extraordinary! Extraordinary! The moment these creatures marry and catch the smell of men, how can they become such beasts? They deserve killing even more than men do!’

The old women at the garden gate could not help laughing.

‘Then you mean every unmarried girl is good, and every married woman is bad?’ they asked.

‘Exactly! Exactly!’ Bao-yu nodded.

‘There’s one more thing we’re too stupid to understand,’ the women said, laughing. ‘We should like to ask you about it.’

They were about to speak when several other old women came up.

‘Mind what you’re about! Get everyone together and stand ready. Her Ladyship has come into the garden herself. She’s inspecting the servants over there, and she may come this way too. She’s also ordered Miss Skybright’s cousin and his wife to be called. They’re to wait here to take her away from the House of Green Delights.’

The speaker laughed.

‘Blessed Buddha! Heaven has opened its eyes today. Once that pest of a little demon is sent packing, we shall all have some peace.’

But Bao-yu had heard nothing of this last expression of satisfaction. The moment he learnt that Lady Wang was in the garden inspecting the servants, he knew Skybright could not be saved either, and had already flown off.

When he reached the House of Green Delights, there was a crowd of people there. Lady Wang sat inside, her face full of anger. She took no notice of him. Skybright, who had touched neither food nor water for four or five days, was scarcely breathing. They had just pulled her off the kang; with hair dishevelled and face unwashed, she was being carried away between two women. Lady Wang ordered that only her underclothes were to be thrown out after her. The rest of her good clothes were to be kept for well-behaved maids to wear. Then she had every maid in the place called before her for inspection.

Ever since the day Lady Wang had first been angry, Wang Shan-bao’s wife had seized the opportunity to bring Skybright down with her accusations. People in Lady Wang’s own quarters who were on bad terms with those in the garden had also taken their chances to drop a word. Lady Wang had remembered everything. The festival had kept her occupied, so she had held her anger in for a couple of days. Now she had come expressly to inspect them herself. Skybright alone would have been one thing. But someone had actually made Bao-yu the reason for complaint: he was grown up now, they said, and already understood the ways of men and women, all because the worthless maids in his rooms had taught him their bad habits.

This was far more serious than the case of Skybright alone. Lady Wang personally looked over every girl, from Aroma down to the youngest little maid who did the rough work.

‘Which one has the same birthday as Bao-yu?’ she asked.

The girl herself dared not answer. An old nurse pointed her out.

‘This is Hui-xiang, also called Si-er. She has the same birthday as Master Bao.’

Lady Wang examined her closely. Though not half as pretty as Skybright, she had a certain fresh charm. Her cleverness was plainly apparent in every movement, and her dress, too, was different from the rest.

‘Another shameless one,’ Lady Wang said with a cold laugh. ‘Behind my back she says people with the same birthday are husband and wife. Did you say that? You think I’m too far away to know anything, do you? Let me tell you: though I may not come here often in person, my mind, my ears and all my attention are here all the time. I have only this one Bao-yu. Do you suppose I shall leave him to you without a thought and let you entice him into wickedness?’

Si-er heard Lady Wang repeat the private things she had said to Bao-yu. Her face reddened; she lowered her head and wept.

Lady Wang ordered her family to be summoned at once and told to take her away and marry her off.

‘And which is Yelü Xiong-nu?’

The nurses pointed to Parfumée.

‘An actress! A little fox-spirit, naturally,’ Lady Wang said. ‘We let you go last time, but you were too lazy to leave. If you stayed, you should at least have behaved yourselves.

‘Instead you play your devilish tricks, egging Bao-yu on to every sort of mischief.’

‘I wouldn’t dare egg him on to anything,’ Parfumée protested with a smile.

‘Still answering back!’ Lady Wang smiled too. ‘Let me ask you: the year before last, when we went to the imperial tombs, who put Bao-yu up to asking for the Liu woman’s daughter, Wu-er? Luckily the girl’s life was short and she died. If she’d come in, you’d have formed another gang to plague this garden. You’ve even bullied your own godmother into submission, never mind anyone else!’

She raised her voice in command.

‘Call her godmother to take her away. Give her leave to find a husband outside. Let her have all her things.’

She also ordered that none of the young actresses allotted to the girls the previous year should remain in the garden. Their godmothers were to take them out and arrange their marriages themselves. As soon as the order went round, the godmothers were delighted beyond measure: it was everything they could wish for. They assembled to kowtow to Lady Wang in gratitude and take the girls away.

Lady Wang then searched Bao-yu’s belongings throughout the room. Anything that seemed at all unfamiliar was packed up or rolled up as appropriate and carried off to her own rooms.

‘Now the place is clean,’ she said. ‘We shall be spared people’s gossip.’

She warned Aroma, Musk and the others:

‘Take care! If there is the slightest thing out of order again, I shan’t spare any of you. I’ve had inquiries made, and this is not a suitable year for moving house. We shall get through this year as best we can. Next year you will all move back out again, and I shall have some peace of mind.’

Without even taking tea, she led her followers away to inspect the servants elsewhere. We shall not pursue that for the moment.

Bao-yu had supposed that Lady Wang was only coming to search the rooms, and that nothing very serious would happen. He had never dreamt she would descend on them in such thunderous wrath. Every charge she made repeated things they had actually said, without a single word astray. He knew there was no hope of reversing her decision.

In his heart he longed for death. But while Lady Wang was so angry, he dared not say an extra word or take an extra step. He followed her all the way to Drenched Blossoms Pavilion.

‘Go back and read those books properly,’ she ordered. ‘Mind yourself when your father questions you tomorrow. He’s just made some very fierce threats.’

Only then did Bao-yu turn back. All the way he wondered: ‘Who has been carrying tales like this? No one knows what goes on here. How could she have got it all so exactly?’

Still thinking, he entered his rooms and found Aroma in tears. With the best of them gone, how could he not be heartbroken? He threw himself on the bed and cried too. Aroma knew that the other things troubled him comparatively little: Skybright was the great sorrow. She gave him a push and tried to comfort him.

‘Crying won’t help now. Sit up and listen. Skybright’s better already. Now she’s gone home, she can rest quietly for a few days. If you really can’t bear to lose her, wait till Her Ladyship’s anger has passed, and then ask the Old Lady. It won’t be hard to have her brought back in time.

‘Her Ladyship happened to believe someone’s slander, that’s all. She was angry for the moment and acted on it.’

‘But what monstrous crime has Skybright committed?’ Bao-yu cried. ‘I still don’t know!’

‘Her Ladyship only dislikes her for being too good-looking,’ said Aroma. ‘There’s something flighty about it. In her view, a girl as beautiful as that is bound to be restless. That’s why she hates the sight of her. It’s better to be plain and clumsy, like us.’

‘Leave that aside. How did she know our private jokes? There was no outsider to let them get about. It’s very strange.’

‘What do you ever hold back? Once you’re in good spirits, you don’t care who’s there and who isn’t. I’ve given you looks and secret signs often enough. Other people already knew what was going on, while you noticed nothing.’

‘How is it she knows everyone’s faults, yet finds nothing to pick on in you, Musk or Ripple?’

The question gave Aroma a start. She lowered her head and could find no answer for some time. At last she smiled.

‘Yes, indeed.

‘We’ve had our careless jokes and rash moments too. How could Her Ladyship have forgotten? Perhaps she has other business to finish before she deals with us. Who knows?’

‘You’re the foremost of the famously good and virtuous,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘And those two have been brought up and educated by you. How could any of you have done anything rash enough to deserve punishment? Parfumée is young, though, and rather too clever. She has no doubt used her position to get the better of people and made herself disliked. Si-er’s troubles are my fault. Ever since the day I quarrelled with you and called her up to do the finer work, she’s been taking someone else’s place. That’s what has brought her to this. But Skybright came from the Old Lady’s rooms when she was little, just as you did. Though she was prettier than other people, it never did anyone any harm. She may be outspoken and sharp-tongued, but she has never really done any of you an injury. I suppose she was too beautiful after all, and her beauty has ruined her.’

He began to cry again.

Aroma weighed his words. He seemed to suspect her, and she hardly knew how to comfort him further.

‘Heaven knows,’ she sighed. ‘We can’t discover who it was now. Crying for a while will do no good. Better save your strength, wait till the Old Lady is pleased about something, and explain it all to her. Asking for Skybright back is the sensible thing to do.’

Bao-yu gave a bitter laugh.

‘Don’t try to comfort me with empty words. By the time Mother has calmed down and we can see whether it’s safe to ask, who knows whether Skybright’s illness will have waited? She’s been tenderly brought up ever since she was little. When has she ever had to bear a day’s ill-treatment? Even I, who understand her temper, have often upset her. Sending her down there is like taking a pot of orchids just putting out tender shoots and throwing it into a pigsty. And she’s desperately ill, with all that misery shut up inside her. She has no loving father or mother, only a drunken mudfish of a cousin. She’ll never be able to settle there all at once. How can she wait several days? I don’t even know whether I’ll see her once or twice again!’

He grew still more distressed as he spoke.

‘So the magistrate may set fires, but the people mustn’t light a lamp!’ Aroma said, smiling. ‘If one of us happens to say anything the least bit unfortunate, you call it unlucky talk. Yet now you can calmly curse her yourself, and that’s all right? She may have been more pampered than other girls, but she won’t come to that.’

‘I’m not carelessly cursing her. There was a sign this spring.’

Aroma quickly asked what sign.

‘That perfectly healthy begonia below the steps suddenly died on one side for no reason at all. I knew something strange must happen. And now it has come true in her.’

Aroma laughed again.

‘I try not to say it, but I can’t help myself. You do fuss like an old woman! Is that the sort of thing an educated man should say? What have plants to do with people? If you’re not an old woman, you really must be a fool.’

Bao-yu sighed.

‘What do you know about it? Not only plants: everything in the world has feeling and a reason for being, just as people do. Once it finds someone who understands it, it can show the most wonderful signs. To take great examples, there are the junipers before Confucius’s temple, the milfoil by his grave, the cypresses at Zhuge Liang’s shrine, and the pines at the tomb of Yue Wu-mu. All those noble things share in the upright spirit of the men, and endure through the ages. In times of disorder they wither; in times of peace they flourish. Through hundreds and thousands of years they have withered and come to life again, how many times! Aren’t those signs? Or take lesser examples: Yang Tai-zhen’s tree peonies at the Aloeswood Pavilion, the love-tree by Upright Tower, the grass on Wang Zhao-jun’s tomb. Haven’t those shown their powers too? In the same way, the begonia knew that she was going to die, and so half of it died first.’

Aroma found this foolish speech both laughable and saddening.

‘Really, the more you talk, the angrier I get,’ she said, smiling. ‘What is Skybright, that you should spend all this thought comparing her with such important people?

‘And another thing. However good she may be, she can’t come before me. Even if the begonia is a sign, you ought to compare it with me first. It isn’t her turn yet. I suppose I must be going to die.’

Bao-yu quickly put his hand over her mouth.

‘Why must you do that? One thing isn’t settled, and now you begin! Enough. Don’t mention it again. We’ve lost three. Don’t make it four.’

Aroma was secretly pleased. ‘Nothing else would have brought you to a stop,’ she thought.

‘We won’t speak of it again,’ Bao-yu said. ‘We’ll just think of all three as dead. That’s all there is to it. Others have died before, and you haven’t seen anything happen to me. It’s the same thing.

‘But for the present, let’s keep it from those above us without hiding it from those below. Have someone quietly take her things out to her. And if we have any money we’ve saved, send a few strings of cash for her to get well on. You’ve been sisters all this time, after all.’

‘You do think us mean and heartless!’ Aroma said, laughing. ‘Did I have to wait for you to tell me? I’ve already packed all the clothes she used to wear, and every last thing that belongs to her. They’re all over there. There are too many people about in the daytime, too many eyes, and I’m afraid of making more trouble. I’m waiting till night, when we can quietly have Mamma Song take them out. I’ve a few strings of cash saved too. She can have those.’

Bao-yu could not thank her enough.

‘Why, I’ve been famous for my virtue for years,’ Aroma said with a smile. ‘Surely I know how to buy myself this little bit of a good name!’

Recognising the echo of his earlier words, Bao-yu hastily smiled and soothed her for a while. That evening Mamma Song was indeed sent out in secret with the things.

Bao-yu settled everyone’s mind, then slipped out alone through the rear side gate when he had the chance. He begged an old woman to take him to see Skybright at her cousin’s home. At first she refused in every possible way, saying she was afraid someone would find out.

‘If it gets back to Her Ladyship, how am I to earn my bread?’

But Bao-yu begged as though his life depended on it and promised her money. At last she took him.

Skybright had originally been bought by Lai Da’s household. She was only ten then and had not yet begun to let her hair grow. She often came in with Nanny Lai, and Grandmother Jia took a great fancy to her cleverness and pretty looks. Nanny Lai therefore made a respectful gift of her to Grandmother, to serve her. That was how she had eventually come to Bao-yu’s rooms.

When she entered the household, Skybright could no longer remember her home or her parents. She knew only that she had an older cousin, skilled at butchery and cooking, who was also adrift in the world. She begged the Lais to buy him too and give him food and wages. The Lais saw that, for all her cleverness, sharp tongue and fierce temper, Skybright had not forgotten her old ties now she was with Grandmother Jia. They bought her cousin and married one of their own servant-girls to him. Once he had a household of his own and a secure life, however, he forgot the days when he had wandered destitute. He drank himself senseless whenever he pleased and neglected his family. As it happened, his wife was a passionate beauty. Seeing him care nothing for his life, understand nothing of love, and do nothing but drink, she could hardly help lamenting that a jewel such as herself should be paired with a common reed, and mourning the loneliness of her beauty.

She also found her husband broad-minded, without a trace of jealousy over quilt or pillow. So the wife gave free rein to her desires, recruiting heroes and taking in men of talent throughout the household. High and low, a good half of them had sat her examination. If you ask this couple’s names, they were none other than Duo the Dullard and Mrs Duo, whom Jia Lian had entertained on an earlier occasion. At present these were Skybright’s only relatives, so it was to their home that she had gone when sent out.

Duo the Dullard was out just then. Mrs Duo had eaten and gone visiting, leaving Skybright alone, lying helpless in the outer room. Bao-yu told the old woman to keep watch at the courtyard gate. He lifted the straw curtain and went in by himself. At once he saw Skybright asleep on an earthen kang covered with a reed mat. Fortunately she still had her old quilt and bedding. He scarcely knew what to do with himself. Going up to her with tears in his eyes, he took her gently by the hand and called her softly twice.

Exposure to the wind and cruel words from her cousin and his wife had made Skybright’s illness worse. She had coughed all day and had only just drifted into a doze. Hearing someone call, she forced open her starry eyes. At the sight of Bao-yu, surprise, joy, grief and pain swept over her. She seized his hand and gripped it tightly. For a long time she could only sob. At last half a sentence came:

‘I thought I’d never see you…’

Then she began coughing without a pause. Bao-yu too could only choke back sobs.

‘Blessed Buddha, I’m glad you’ve come,’ Skybright said. ‘Pour me half a bowl of that tea. I’ve been thirsty for so long, and I can’t get a single soul to answer.’

Bao-yu hurriedly wiped his tears.

‘Where is it?’

‘There, on the stove.’

He looked. There was a black earthenware pot, but it was nothing like a teapot. He had to fetch a bowl from the table. It was large and coarse, nothing like a tea-bowl either. Before he even picked it up, he could smell grease and rank animal fat. There was no alternative. He washed it twice with a little water, rinsed it again, then lifted the earthenware pot and poured half a bowl. The liquid was dark red, hardly like tea at all.

Skybright propped herself against the pillow.

‘Quick, let me have a mouthful! That is the tea. How could it be like ours?’

Bao-yu first tasted it himself. There was no delicate fragrance, no flavour of tea, only a harsh bitterness with the faintest suggestion of it. Then he gave it to Skybright. She drained it at once, as though it were nectar.

‘How often she found fault even with the fine tea we used to have,’ he thought. ‘And now this! I see the old sayings were right: “When full, we feast on slaughtered meats; when hungry, dregs and husks suffice.” And, “A belly full of rice makes a plaything of gruel.” There’s truth in both.’

Thinking this, he wept and asked:

‘Is there anything you want to say? Tell me while no one’s here.’

‘What is there to say?’ she sobbed.

‘I get through one moment, and then the next; one day, and then another. I know there can’t be more than three or five days left before I go back. There’s only one thing I shan’t accept, even when I’m dead. I may be a little prettier than the others, but I never tried to entice you with secret love or private promises. Why do they insist I’m a fox-spirit? I can’t bear the injustice! Now I’ve been given the name for nothing, and I’m dying besides… I’m not saying this just to voice my regrets, but if I’d known it would end like this, I’d have done things differently. Instead I was foolish and trusted my heart, thinking we were all going to stay together, whatever happened.

‘I never dreamt this story would come out of nowhere. And there’s no one to hear my wrongs.’

She began to cry again. Bao-yu held her hand: it felt as thin as dry sticks. There were still four silver bracelets on her wrists.

‘Let’s take these off,’ he said, weeping. ‘You can wear them again when you’re well.’

He removed them and tucked them under her pillow.

‘What a pity about those two nails. It took so long to grow them two cun long. By the time you’re over this illness, they’ll have suffered terribly.’

Skybright wiped her tears and reached for a pair of scissors. She cut the two nails on her left hand, long as slender onion tubes, right down to the quick. Then, reaching under the quilt, she took off the old red damask jacket she was wearing next to her skin. She gave it to Bao-yu with the nails.

‘Keep these. Afterwards it will be as though you were seeing me. Quick, take off your jacket and let me wear it. When I’m lying alone in my coffin, it will be as though I were still in the House of Green Delights. I know I ought not to do this. But I’ve been given the name for nothing, and there’s nothing I can do.’

Bao-yu hurriedly took off his jacket, gave it to her and put on hers, then put the nails away.

‘When you get back, they’ll see it and ask,’ Skybright said, crying again. ‘Don’t make up a lie. Just say it’s mine. Since we’ve got the name, we may as well go this far. They can’t do more than they’ve done.’

She had not finished when her cousin’s wife lifted the curtain and came in, all smiles.

‘Well, well! I’ve heard everything you two have been saying.’

She turned to Bao-yu.

‘What’s a young master doing in the servants’ quarters? Have you come to make love to me because I’m young and pretty?’

Bao-yu was frightened. He hastily put on a pleading smile.

‘Please, my good sister, don’t speak so loudly. She served me all this time. I’ve only come secretly to see her.’

Mrs Duo caught him by the hand and pulled him into the inner room.

‘It’s easy enough to keep me quiet,’ she said, smiling. ‘You only have to do one thing for me.’

She sat on the edge of the kang and clasped him tightly in her arms. Bao-yu had never encountered anything like this. His heart pounded; his whole face flushed with distress. Ashamed and frightened, he could only say:

‘Please, sister, don’t!’

Mrs Duo glanced sideways at him with drunken eyes and laughed.

‘Pah! I’m forever hearing what a practised hand you are at love. Why so bashful today?’

Bao-yu blushed and forced a smile.

‘Let me go, sister. We can talk properly. There’s an old woman outside. What will she think if she hears?’

‘I came in some time ago. I sent the old woman to wait by the garden gate. I’ve been waiting for you heaven knows how long, and today I’ve got you at last. Seeing is better than hearing, they say. Such good looks, and nothing behind them! You’re a firecracker without a fuse, only fit for show. You’re even more bashful and shy than I am. It just proves you can’t believe what people say. When our girl was sent down here today, for instance, I was certain you two had been up to your little tricks together.

‘I came back a while ago and listened carefully under the window. There were only the two of you inside. If there had been any hanky-panky, how could you have failed to mention it? But it turns out you’ve left each other alone all along. There must be plenty of injustice in this world. Now I’m sorry I misjudged you. Well, if that’s how it is, set your mind at rest. Come whenever you like. I shan’t pester you.’

Only then did Bao-yu feel easy. He got up, straightened his clothes and begged her:

‘My good sister, please, please look after her for the next couple of days. I must go now.’

He went out and told Skybright he was leaving.

Neither could bear to part, but part they must. Skybright knew how hard he found it to go. She pulled the quilt over her head and would not respond to him at all. Only then did Bao-yu leave.

He wanted to visit Parfumée and Si-er too. But it was dark, and he had been out a long time. He feared that the people inside would look for him, fail to find him and cause more trouble. He went back into the garden instead, leaving further plans till tomorrow. At the rear side gate, the pages were just bringing their bedding and the nurses inside were checking that everyone was in. One moment later, and the gate would have been shut.

To his relief, no one knew he had been out. Back in his room, he merely told Aroma that he had been at Aunt Xue’s, and that was that. When the beds were made, Aroma had to ask what the sleeping arrangements were to be that night.

‘It doesn’t matter,’ Bao-yu said.

In the past year or two, since Lady Wang had begun to think so highly of her, Aroma had been increasingly careful of her own dignity. Whether alone with Bao-yu or at night, she would no longer indulge in intimacies with him. She was, in fact, more distant than when they had been younger. Though she had no great affairs to manage, all the needlework and the money, clothes, shoes and belongings coming and going for Bao-yu and the younger maids made a great many small demands on her. Besides, though she had recovered from the old illness in which she had spat blood, overwork or exposure to wind and cold would still bring blood up with her cough. Lately, therefore, she had not slept in Bao-yu’s room at all.

Bao-yu often woke in the night. He was also extremely timid and always called for someone when he woke. Skybright slept lightly and moved about easily, so all the night duties, bringing tea, helping him up or answering his calls, had been entrusted to her alone. It was she who slept on his outer bed.

Now she was gone, Aroma had to ask: in her view, these duties mattered more than the daytime ones. Since Bao-yu had said he did not mind how it was arranged, she could only go back to the practice of former years. She brought her own bedding over and laid it out on the outer bed.

Bao-yu sat in a daze all evening. When at last they urged him to bed and Aroma and the others had lain down too, he could still be heard sighing deeply into his pillow and tossing from side to side, long past the third watch. At last he grew quiet and began to snore a little. Aroma’s mind was easier, and she too drifted into sleep. Not half the time it would take to drink a cup of tea had passed when she heard him call:

‘Skybright!’

Aroma opened her eyes and answered at once, asking what he wanted. He wanted tea. She got down, dipped her hands in the basin and poured half a cup from the insulated pot for him to drink.

‘I’ve got so used to calling her lately,’ Bao-yu said with a smile, ‘I forgot it was you.’

‘When she first came, you kept calling me in your sleep,’ Aroma said, smiling. ‘It took half a year to change. Skybright may have gone, but I know those two syllables will be harder to send away.’

They lay down again. Bao-yu tossed for another watch. Only in the fifth watch, when he finally fell asleep, did he see Skybright come in from outside, looking just as she always had. She smiled at him.

‘Live well, all of you. I’m leaving you now.’

With that she turned and went.

Bao-yu called after her and woke Aroma again. At first she thought he was simply calling the familiar name at random. Then she saw that he was crying.

‘Skybright’s dead,’ he said.

‘What nonsense!’ Aroma said with a smile. ‘You do carry on. What would people think if they heard you?’

Bao-yu would not listen. He longed for daylight so that he could send someone to ask for news at once.

At daybreak, a little maid from Lady Wang’s rooms was already waiting at the front side gate, calling for it to be opened so that she could deliver her mistress’s orders.

‘Wake Master Bao this instant! He must wash his face, change his clothes and come at once. Someone has invited His Lordship to enjoy the autumn and the osmanthus today. His Lordship was pleased with the poems Master Bao made the other day and wants to take the boys with him. Those are all Her Ladyship’s words. Don’t get one of them wrong. Run and tell him. Say he must come this minute. His Lordship is still waiting in the main rooms to have cereal tea with him. Master Huan is here already. Run, run! Send someone else to call Master Lan, and tell him exactly the same.’

The old women inside answered each sentence as they fastened their buttons and opened the gate. Already two or three others were hurrying off in different directions, still buttoning their clothes as they went. Hearing the knock at the courtyard gate, Aroma knew something was afoot. She sent someone to ask, and was herself already getting up. At the message she hurried the maids to bring warm water and urged Bao-yu out of bed to wash.

She went to fetch his clothes. Since he was going out with Jia Zheng, she would not choose anything too new or striking, but selected his second-best things. Bao-yu had no choice now. He hurried over and found Jia Zheng taking tea in excellent spirits. Bao-yu promptly made his morning obeisance. Jia Huan and Jia Lan also greeted him. Jia Zheng told him to sit and drink, then addressed Huan and Lan.

‘Bao-yu is not your equal at his books. But in the wit needed to devise inscribed couplets and answer poems, neither of you can match him. There’s sure to be some insistence on your writing poems today. Bao-yu, you must be ready to help them both.’

Lady Wang and the others had never heard him pronounce such a judgement. Their delight was wholly unexpected.

Once father, son and the other boys had left, Lady Wang was about to go to Grandmother Jia’s when the godmothers of Parfumée and two of the other girls arrived.

‘Ever since you were kind enough to let Parfumée go the other day, ma’am, she’s been like a mad thing,’ they reported. ‘She won’t take tea or eat a mouthful. She’s drawn Ou-guan and Rui-guan in with her, and all three are threatening to kill themselves unless they can cut off their hair and become nuns. I thought they were only children, unused to being out of the household, and would settle down in a couple of days. But they’ve got worse and worse. Neither beating nor scolding frightens them. We really don’t know what to do, so we’ve come to ask you. Either let them become nuns, or give them a talking-to and bestow them on someone else as daughters. We haven’t the good fortune to keep them.’

‘Nonsense!’ said Lady Wang. ‘How can they be allowed their own way like that? Is the Buddhist life something anyone can enter on a whim? Give each of them a beating and see whether they still make a fuss!’

Now, on the fifteenth of the eighth month, offerings had been sent to the various temples. It was customary for their nuns to bring back choice portions of the offerings, and on the fifteenth Lady Wang had kept Zhi-tong of Water-Moon Hermitage and Yuan-xin of Kshitigarbha Convent for a stay of two days. They had still not left. On hearing the news, both were eager to lure away another girl or two to work for them. They told Lady Wang:

‘This really is a charitable household.

‘Your devotion to good works has inspired these young girls to follow your example. It is true that the Buddhist life is not lightly entered, but remember that the Buddha’s law makes no distinctions. Our Buddha vowed to deliver all living beings, even chickens and dogs. It is only the deluded who will not awaken. Those with roots of goodness who can awaken may escape the round of rebirth. The scriptures give many examples of tigers, wolves, snakes and insects attaining the Way. These two or three girls have neither father nor mother, and their homes are far away. They have seen all this wealth and splendour, and they remember how their unhappy lot sent them into the entertainers’ trade as children. Who can tell what the rest of their lives may bring? Now they turn back from the sea of suffering and wish to leave the world, to cultivate a better life to come. That is a noble intention. You should not place bounds on their good desires, ma’am.’

Lady Wang was indeed devoted to good works. When she first heard the women’s report, she had refused to let the girls have their way because she thought they were only children acting on a moment’s disappointment. She feared they would later prove unable to endure the quiet life and incur sin instead.

But what the two traffickers said now sounded very reasonable. There had also been many troubles in the family lately. Lady Xing had sent word that Ying-chun would be fetched home the next day for a two-day stay, so that a prospective husband’s family could see her; an official matchmaker had also come about Tan-chun. With all these things vexing her, Lady Wang had little attention to spare for such trifles.

‘Since that is what you both think,’ she answered with a smile, ‘why don’t you take them as your disciples?’

The two nuns invoked the Buddha.

‘Blessed indeed! Blessed indeed! That would earn you no small store of hidden merit, ma’am.’

They bowed their heads and thanked her.

‘Then go and ask them,’ Lady Wang said. ‘If they truly mean it, let them come and acknowledge you as their teachers here before me.’

The three godmothers went out and duly brought the girls in. Lady Wang questioned them repeatedly, but all three had made up their minds. They kowtowed to the two nuns, then made their farewells to Lady Wang. Seeing how firm they were, Lady Wang knew she could not force them. Her feelings turned instead to sorrow and pity. She quickly had gifts fetched for them, and gave presents to the two nuns as well.

From that time Parfumée followed Zhi-tong of Water-Moon Hermitage, while Rui-guan and Ou-guan followed Yuan-xin of Kshitigarbha Convent. Each left the world to enter religious life.
