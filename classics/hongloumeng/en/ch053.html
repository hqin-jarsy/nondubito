Chapter 53 At Ning mansion, New Year’s Eve brings worship in the ancestral shrine; At Rong mansion, the Lantern Festival brings a night banquet

Chapter end

Now Bao-yu saw that, with the peacock-gold cloak at last mended, Skybright had spent all her strength and was perilously weak. He hurriedly called the little maids to take over patting and massaging her. They worked at this for a while, then settled down to rest. Before there had been time to eat a meal, it was broad daylight. For the moment he would not go out, but ordered them to fetch the doctor at once. Doctor Wang soon arrived. After feeling her pulse, he looked puzzled.

‘She was a little better yesterday. Why is the pulse now so empty and faint, floating and contracting? Has she perhaps eaten too much? If not, she must have overtaxed her mind. The external chill has cleared, but to neglect her recovery after sweating is no trifling matter.’

Still talking, he went out to write a prescription and sent it in. Bao-yu saw that he had reduced the dispersing medicines that drove out the harmful influence, adding instead fuling, dihuang, danggui and other remedies to strengthen the spirit and nourish the blood. He promptly ordered the medicine to be boiled.

‘What are we to do?’ he sighed. ‘If anything should happen to her, it will all be my sin.’

‘Oh, my good master!’ Skybright exclaimed from her pillow. ‘Go and attend to your own affairs!

‘It’s not as though I’ve suddenly got consumption.’

Bao-yu could do nothing but go. That afternoon, however, he said he felt unwell and came home again. Severe though Skybright’s illness was, she was fortunate in having always been one to expend her strength rather than fret her mind. Her food, too, was habitually plain, and she had harmed herself neither by going hungry nor by overeating. The Jia household had a secret remedy of its own: at the slightest cold or cough, whether the sufferer was high or low, complete fasting came first, with medicine and nursing second. Thus, when she had first fallen ill a few days before, she had eaten nothing for two or three days and had been careful to take her medicine and look after herself. Now, after overexerting herself, she was nursed with redoubled care for several days and gradually recovered. As the girls in the Garden were now all eating in their own rooms, cooking and meals were more conveniently managed. Bao-yu could devise all manner of soups and broths to suit her. There is no need to describe them one by one.

Aroma had returned after accompanying her mother’s funeral procession, and Musk had also reported to Bao-yu, point by point, what Patience had said about Mother Song and Zhui-er, and how Skybright had driven the girl out. Aroma said nothing else about it, only that it had all been a little too hasty.

Li Wan, however, had also caught a seasonal cold. Lady Xing was suffering from inflamed eyes, and Ying-chun and Xiu-yan had gone over to attend to her medicines morning and evening. Aunt Li’s younger brother had taken Aunt Li, Li Wen and Li Qi to stay at his home for a few days. Bao-yu, for his part, saw Aroma often grieving for her mother, while Skybright was still far from well. On the days appointed for the poetry club, therefore, no one took the lead, and several meetings were missed.

It was now the twelfth month, and New Year was drawing near. Lady Wang and Xi-feng were busy with the preparations. Wang Zi-teng had been promoted to Inspector-General of Nine Provinces, and Jia Yu-cun appointed Minister of War, assisting in military affairs and advising on the business of the court. Of this we shall say no more.

At Jia Zhen’s, meanwhile, the ancestral shrine had been opened. He had people clean it, put the sacrificial vessels in order and bring out the spirit tablets. The principal rooms were cleaned too, ready for the ancestral portraits to be hung for worship. High and low, inside and out, everyone in the Rong and Ning mansions was busy. One day You-shi had just risen and was arranging with Jia Rong’s wife the needlework gifts to be sent over to Grandmother Jia, when a maid came in carrying a tea tray of little gold ingots for New Year.

‘Joker reports, ma’am, that the bag of broken gold sent out the other day weighed one hundred and fifty-three taels, six qian and seven fen altogether. It was of varying fineness, and they have cast a total of two hundred and twenty little ingots from it.’

As she spoke, she handed up the tray. You-shi looked them over. Some were shaped like plum blossom, some like crab-apple blossom; others combined a writing-brush, an ingot and a ruyi, or the Eight Treasures linked with spring.

‘Put these away,’ she said, ‘and tell him to send in the silver ingots as quickly as he can.’

The maid assented and went.

Presently Jia Zhen came in to eat, and Jia Rong’s wife withdrew.

‘Have we collected the imperial grant for our spring sacrifices?’ he asked You-shi.

‘I sent Rong to collect it today.’

‘Our family may not be waiting for these few taels to spend, but they are the Emperor’s heavenly bounty, however much or little they amount to.

‘We should collect it early, show it to Grandmother over there, and buy the offerings for our ancestors. We receive the Emperor’s favour from above and enjoy our ancestors’ blessing below. Even if we spent ten thousand taels on offerings, they would not confer the same honour, the same favour and blessing. Apart from one or two families like ours, what would those poor hereditary officials use to make their offerings and keep New Year if they didn’t have this money? Truly, the Emperor’s bounty is vast. He thinks of everything.’

‘Just so,’ said You-shi.

They were still talking when someone announced that the young master had arrived. Jia Zhen ordered him to be shown in. Jia Rong entered carrying a small yellow cloth bag.

‘Why have you been gone all day?’ Jia Zhen asked.

Jia Rong smiled respectfully as he answered.

‘It wasn’t being issued at the Board of Rites today. The payment had been assigned to the treasury of the Court of Imperial Entertainments, so I had to go there before I could collect it. The officials there all asked after you, Father. They said they hadn’t seen you for a long time and missed you very much.’

‘It’s not me they miss,’ Jia Zhen said with a laugh. ‘New Year’s here again. If it isn’t my presents they’re thinking of, it’s my plays and wine.’

As he spoke he examined the yellow cloth bag. Printed on it in four large characters were the words ‘Imperial Favour Bestowed for Ever’. On the other side was the seal of the Sacrificial Bureau of the Board of Rites, together with a line of small writing:

‘Two shares in all of the perpetual imperial grant for spring sacrifices to Jia Yan, Duke of Ning, and Jia Yuan, Duke of Rong, commuted to a net sum of so many taels of silver. Received in full at this office on such-and-such a day, month and year by Jia Rong, Dragon Guard and candidate for appointment as an imperial guard. Deputy of the Court on duty for the year: so-and-so.’

Below was a flourish of signature in red ink.

After his meal, Jia Zhen washed his hands and rinsed his mouth, changed his boots and hat, and ordered Jia Rong to follow him carrying the silver. They reported to Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang, then went over to Jia She and Lady Xing and reported to them before returning home. Jia Zhen took out the silver and ordered the bag to be burnt in the great brazier in the ancestral shrine.

‘Go and ask your Aunt Lian,’ he told Jia Rong, ‘whether she has settled the dates for the New Year feasts in the first month. If she has, have the study make out a clear list. When we send our invitations, we mustn’t clash with theirs. Last year we were careless and invited several of the same families on the same days. Instead of putting it down to carelessness, people might think the two houses had arranged it between them: empty courtesies to save ourselves trouble.’

Jia Rong assented and went over. Soon he returned with the list of feast dates. Jia Zhen read it and ordered it to be given to Lai Sheng, who was to avoid those days when inviting their guests. He was in the hall watching the pages carry folding screens and wipe the tables and the gold and silver sacrificial vessels when a page came up with a formal greeting and a statement of accounts.

‘Steward Wu from Heishan Village has arrived.’

‘So the old gallows-bird has only just got here!’ said Jia Zhen.

Jia Rong took the greeting and accounts, quickly unfolded them and held them up. Jia Zhen stood with his hands behind his back and read the red greeting sheet in his son’s hands:

‘Your servant, estate steward Wu Jin-xiao, bows his head and wishes the Master and Mistress ten thousand blessings and precious peace, and the young gentlemen and ladies precious peace also. May the New Spring bring great joy and great blessings, honour, distinction and peace, higher office and greater emoluments, and may all things be as you wish.’

‘Country people have some quaint ideas,’ Jia Zhen said, laughing.

‘Never mind the composition,’ Jia Rong hastened to say with a smile. ‘It’s only meant to bring good luck.’

He quickly opened the list and looked through it. It read:

‘Thirty large deer; fifty zhang deer; fifty roe deer; twenty xian pigs; twenty tang pigs; twenty longzhu pigs; twenty wild boars; twenty cured domestic pigs; twenty wild sheep; twenty qing sheep; twenty domestic tang sheep; twenty wind-dried domestic sheep; two xunhuang fish; two hundred catties of assorted fish; two hundred each of live chickens, ducks and geese; two hundred wind-dried chickens, ducks and geese in all; two hundred pairs each of pheasants and rabbits; twenty pairs of bear paws; twenty catties of deer sinews; fifty catties of sea cucumbers; fifty deer tongues; fifty ox tongues; twenty catties of dried razor clams; two sacks each of hazelnuts, pine nuts, walnut kernels and apricot kernels; fifty pairs of large prawns; two hundred catties of dried shrimps; one thousand catties of selected best-grade silver-frost charcoal and two thousand catties of medium grade; thirty thousand catties of firewood and charcoal; two shi of rouge rice from the imperial fields; fifty hu of green glutinous rice; fifty hu of white glutinous rice; fifty hu of fenjing rice; fifty hu of each of the assorted grains; one thousand shi of ordinary rice for the lower ranks of the household; one cartload of assorted dried vegetables; proceeds from the sale of grain, livestock and other items, a total of two thousand five hundred taels of silver.

‘In addition, your servant respectfully offers the young masters and misses the following to amuse them: two pairs of live deer; four pairs of live white rabbits; four pairs of black rabbits; two pairs of live ornamental pheasants; two pairs of foreign ducks.’

Jia Zhen ordered the man to be brought in. Wu Jin-xiao soon appeared and, remaining in the courtyard, kowtowed and offered his respects.

Jia Zhen told someone to help him up.

‘Still hale and hearty, I see,’ he said with a smile.

‘Thanks to your blessing, sir, I can still get about,’ Wu Jin-xiao answered, smiling.

‘Your son must be grown up now. You ought to let him make the journey.’

‘To tell you the truth, sir, we’re used to coming. We’d feel cooped up if we didn’t. As for the young ones, don’t they all want to come and see the world at the Emperor’s feet? But they’re still young, after all. I’m afraid something might happen on the road. In a few years I shall feel easier about it.’

‘How many days did it take you?’

‘Well, sir, the snow has been heavy this year. Out there it’s four or five chi deep everywhere. Then the other day it suddenly turned warm and thawed, and the roads became terribly hard going. That held us up a few days. We’ve been travelling a month and two days, but with so little time left, I was afraid you’d be anxious, so we hurried on.’

‘That explains why you’ve only just arrived. I’ve been looking at your list. You old rogue, you’ve come to do battle with me again this year.’

Wu Jin-xiao hurried two paces forward.

‘If you please, sir, the harvest really was bad this year.

‘The rain began in the third month and went on and on until the eighth. There weren’t five fine days together in all that time. In the ninth month we had hailstones as big as bowls. Over an expanse of nearly thirteen hundred li, people, houses, livestock and crops were struck and damaged by the thousands and tens of thousands. That’s why things are as they are. I wouldn’t dare tell you a lie.’

Jia Zhen frowned.

‘I’d reckoned on your bringing at least five thousand taels of silver. What use is this? You’ve only eight or nine estates left between you now, and this year two have reported drought or flood. Then you come to do battle with me as well! You really mean us to go without a New Year.’

‘Your land has done well enough, sir, compared with some. My brother is only a little over a hundred li from me, yet who would have thought things could be so different? He manages eight estates for the other mansion, several times as much land as you have here. This year he’s only brought this much produce too, and no more than two or three thousand taels of silver extra. They’re short as well.’

‘Exactly,’ said Jia Zhen. ‘I can manage here. We’ve no great exceptional expenses any more, only the cost of keeping the place for a year.

‘I can put up with less and save a little. Then there are the annual presents and invitations. If I’m a little more thick-skinned, I can save something there too, and that will do. But it’s different in the other mansion. These past few years they’ve had all sorts of new expenses, things they simply can’t avoid paying for, without a single addition to their income or property. They’ve run up a considerable deficit in the last year or two. If we don’t ask you for the money, who are we to ask?’

‘There may be more expenses in that mansion now,’ Wu Jin-xiao said, smiling, ‘but money comes in as well as going out. Surely Her Highness and His Majesty give them rewards?’

Jia Zhen laughed and turned to Jia Rong and the others.

‘Listen to him! Isn’t that a fine thing to say?’

They hastened to laugh.

‘How would you people tucked away in the hills and along the coast know about such things?’ Jia Rong said. ‘Do you suppose Her Highness can hand the Emperor’s treasury over to us?

‘Even if she wished to, she has no say in the matter. Of course there are gifts. At the festivals and appointed seasons they amount to some coloured satins, antiques and curios. Even a gift of money is no more than a hundred taels of gold, worth only a thousand taels of silver. How much of a year’s expenses will that cover? In each of these last two years they’ve had to find several thousand taels more. Think what that first family visit cost, with the Garden to build as well. Work out that one sum, and you’ll see. If there’s another visit in a couple of years, I’m afraid it will leave them penniless.’

‘That’s the trouble with honest country folk,’ Jia Zhen said with a laugh. ‘They see the brightness outside and know nothing of the darkness within. A chime-hammer made of huangbai wood: a fine show outside, bitterness within.’

Jia Rong turned to him with another laugh.

‘But the other mansion really is poor. The other day I heard Aunt Feng and Faithful talking in secret about slipping some of Grandmother’s things out to pawn.’

‘Another of your Aunt Feng’s tricks,’ Jia Zhen said, laughing. ‘They can’t be as poor as that.

‘She must have seen how much is going out and what a heavy loss they’re making. She’s planning to cut some item of expenditure, no doubt, and is preparing the way by letting people hear they’ve become that poor. I’ve an abacus in my own head. They haven’t come to such a pass yet.’

He then ordered Wu Jin-xiao to be taken out and well looked after; of him we need say no more. Jia Zhen had the offerings for the ancestors set aside from the things just delivered. He took some of each kind and told Jia Rong to send them over to Rong mansion. After keeping what his own household needed, he divided the remainder into shares according to rank and had them piled separately below the terrace. He sent for the younger men of the clan to receive them. Soon Rong mansion too sent over a quantity of sacrificial provisions, as well as gifts for Jia Zhen. After seeing the sacrificial vessels put in order, he slipped his feet into his shoes, threw a thick coat of lynx fur over his shoulders and had a large wolfskin mattress spread in the sun on the stone ledge below a pillar of the hall. With the sun on his back, he idly watched the younger men come to collect their New Year supplies. When he saw Jia Qin among them, he called him over.

‘What are you doing here? Who told you to come?’

Jia Qin stood with his hands at his sides.

‘I heard that you were calling us over to collect things, Uncle, so I came without waiting to be sent for.’

‘These things are meant for those young uncles and cousins of yours who’ve nothing to do and no income.

‘When you had nothing to do a year or two ago, I gave you some too. Now you have a post in the other mansion, looking after the monks and Taoists at the family temple. You have your own monthly allowance, and all the monks’ allowance money passes through your hands as well. To come for this on top of it is altogether too greedy. Look at yourself. Do you look like a man entrusted with money and business? We used to say you had no prospects. What’s become of you now? You look worse than before.’

‘There are a great many mouths at home,’ Jia Qin said, ‘and the expenses are heavy.’

Jia Zhen gave a cold laugh.

‘Still trying to put me off! Do you imagine I don’t know what you get up to at the family temple? Once you’re there, of course, you’re the master. No one dares cross you. You’ve money in your hands, and you’re far enough away from us, so you set yourself up as king and lord it over everyone. Every night you gather a pack of rogues to gamble, and keep women and boys. Now you’ve spent yourself into this state, and you dare come here for provisions! You’ll get no provisions. A thrashing with a water-carrier’s pole is what you’ll get.

‘Once New Year’s over, I shall speak to your Uncle Lian and have you replaced.’

Jia Qin flushed and dared not answer. Someone announced that Prince Shui of the Northern Palace had sent calligraphic couplets and purses. Jia Zhen immediately told Jia Rong to go out and receive the messengers.

‘Just say I’m not at home.’

Jia Rong went. Jia Zhen watched the last of the gifts being collected, then returned to his rooms and ate supper with You-shi. Nothing further happened that night. The next day was busier still; there is no need to describe it all.

By the twenty-ninth of the twelfth month, everything was ready. In both mansions the door-gods, couplets and hanging plaques had been replaced, and the peachwood charms freshly oiled. All was bright and new. In Ning mansion every central doorway stood open along the whole route: the main gate, the ceremonial gate, the great hall, the heated chamber, the inner hall, the inner third gate, the inner ceremonial gate and the inner screen-gate, through to the principal hall. Below the steps on either side stood tall vermilion lanterns, all alike, their lights running in two lines like golden dragons. The next day Grandmother Jia and all the women with imperial titles put on court dress according to their ranks. Grandmother Jia took the lead in a great sedan carried by eight men, and they went with their attendants to offer congratulations at court. When the ceremonies and banquet were over, they returned and alighted at the heated chamber in Ning mansion. The younger men who had not accompanied them to court were drawn up before the gate to receive them and led them into the ancestral shrine.

This was Bao-qin’s first visit, and she looked about her with great attention. The shrine occupied a separate courtyard west of Ning mansion. Within a black-painted railing stood a five-bay gateway. Above it hung a plaque bearing the four characters ‘Ancestral Shrine of the Jia Family’, with the inscription beside them: ‘Written by Kong Ji-zong, Duke for the Transmission of Sagehood.’

A long couplet flanked the gateway:

Their brains and blood bestrewed the earth; the myriad people owe them nurture and protection;

Their merit and renown reach heaven; a hundred generations revere the splendour of their sacrifices.

This too was written by the Duke for the Transmission of Sagehood. Within the courtyard a white stone path ran between dark pines and green cypresses. On the terrace stood tripods and other ritual vessels of green bronze and antique bronze. High above the portico hung a gilded plaque with nine dragons, inscribed: ‘The Stars Shine upon the Sovereign’s Helpers’.

It was in the late Emperor’s own hand. A couplet flanking it read:

The glory of their service shines beside the sun and moon;

Their merit and renown pass without break to sons and grandsons.

This too was in the imperial hand. Before the five-bay main sanctuary hung a plaque of twisting dragons on a blue ground: ‘Honour the Departed, Remember the Distant Ancestors’. Beside it was another couplet:

The sons and grandsons yet to come inherit blessing and virtue;

To this day the common people remember Rong and Ning.

All were in the imperial hand. Inside, incense and candles glowed among brocade hangings and embroidered curtains. The spirit tablets were arranged there, but could not be clearly seen. The members of the Jia family took their places in generational order. Jia Jing presided over the sacrifice, with Jia She assisting. Jia Zhen presented the wine-cup; Jia Lian and Jia Cong presented the silk; Bao-yu carried the incense; Jia Chang and Jia Ling spread the kneeling mats and attended the burning-pit. The blue-robed musicians began to play. There were three offerings of wine, with prostrations and risings, then the sacrificial silk was burnt and wine poured in libation. When the rite was finished, the music ceased and they withdrew.

They crowded round Grandmother Jia and accompanied her to the principal hall. Brocade curtains were raised high before the portraits, painted screens stood about them, and incense and candles shone. High in the centre hung the portraits of the two founding ancestors, the Dukes of Ning and Rong, both in python-pattern robes with jade belts. Several scroll portraits of other ancestors hung on either side. Jia Xing, Jia Zhi and the others formed a line from the inner ceremonial gate to the verandah of the principal hall.

Jia Jing and Jia She stood outside the threshold, the women inside it. The menservants and pages all remained beyond the ceremonial gate. As each dish arrived there, Jia Xing, Jia Zhi and the others received it and passed it along the line to Jia Jing on the steps. Jia Rong, as the eldest grandson of the senior branch, alone stood inside the threshold with the women. Whenever Jia Jing brought up a dish, he handed it to Jia Rong, who passed it to his wife. It went on through Xi-feng, You-shi and the others until it reached Lady Wang before the offering-table. She handed it to Grandmother Jia, who then placed it on the table. Lady Xing stood to the west of the table, facing east, and helped Grandmother Jia arrange the offerings. Only when all the dishes, rice, soups, pastries, wine and accompaniments had been passed in did Jia Rong withdraw, descend the steps and take his place at the head of Jia Qin’s generation.

Of those whose names contained the ‘wen’ element, Jia Jing stood first; of the next generation, whose names contained ‘jade’, Jia Zhen stood first; and of the next, with the ‘grass’ radical, Jia Rong. The generations were ranged left and right in their prescribed order, the men to the east and the women to the west. They waited until Grandmother Jia had taken up the incense and bowed, then all knelt together. The five-bay hall, the three-bay portico, the verandahs within and without, the steps and the two courtyards below were so packed with bright, flower-like figures that no space remained. Not a bird’s peep could be heard: only the faint chime and tinkle of gold bells and jade pendants swaying, and the rustle and shuffle of boots and shoes as they rose and knelt. When the ceremony was over, Jia Jing, Jia She and the others hurried out to Rong mansion to await Grandmother Jia and pay their respects.

The floor of You-shi’s principal room had already been covered from end to end with red felt. In the middle stood a great gilt-enamel brazier with three elephant-trunk feet and a rounded, loach-back rim. The kang facing the entrance had a new scarlet felt covering, with back cushions and bolsters of bright red embroidered in colours with dragons amid clouds upholding the character for longevity. Black foxskin covers were laid over these, and a great white foxskin mattress provided the seat. Grandmother Jia was invited to sit there. Fur mattresses were laid on either side for two or three sisters-in-law of her generation. Lady Xing and the others were seated on another fur-covered little kang at right angles to it, behind a standing screen. Twelve carved-lacquer chairs faced one another in two rows on the floor below, each with matching grey-squirrel covers and cushions and a large copper foot-warmer underneath. Bao-qin and the other girls were given these seats. You-shi herself carried a tray of tea to Grandmother Jia, while Jia Rong’s wife served the other old ladies. You-shi then served Lady Xing and the others, and Jia Rong’s wife the girls. Xi-feng, Li Wan and the rest stood below to attend them. When they had finished their tea, Lady Xing and the others rose first to wait on Grandmother Jia.

After her tea and a few words with her old sisters-in-law, Grandmother Jia called for her sedan. Xi-feng hurried forward to help her rise.

‘Your dinner is already prepared, Grandmother,’ You-shi said with a smile. ‘Every year you refuse us the honour of staying to eat before you go home. Are we really so much worse than Feng?’

Xi-feng supported Grandmother Jia on her arm and laughed.

‘Come along, Grandmother. Let’s go home to eat. Never mind her.’

‘You have the ancestors to attend to here,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘You’re busy enough without a fuss over me as well. Besides, every year I don’t stay, you send the dinner over all the same. You might as well send it again. What I can’t eat I can keep for tomorrow. Won’t I get more that way?’

This made everyone laugh. She added to You-shi:

‘Be sure to put trustworthy people in charge of the incense and lights at night. There must be no carelessness about that.’

You-shi assented. Grandmother Jia went out and took her seat in the sedan before the heated chamber. Only after You-shi and the other women had stepped behind the screen did the pages bring in the bearers, who carried the sedan out through the main gate. You-shi also went with Lady Xing and the others to Rong mansion.

As the sedan emerged, the whole eastern side of the street was lined with the Duke of Ning’s ceremonial insignia, attendants and musical instruments; the western side was similarly lined with those of the Duke of Rong. Passers-by had all been kept back, and no one was allowed through. Soon they reached Rong mansion, where the doors too stood open all the way from the main gate through the principal halls. This time Grandmother Jia did not alight at the heated chamber. They passed the great hall and turned west, taking her to the entrance hall of her own quarters. Everyone escorted her into her principal room. Here too the brocade cushions and embroidered screens were all fresh and new. Pine-and-cypress incense and baihe herb burned in the brazier in the middle of the room.

When Grandmother Jia had seated herself, an old nurse announced that the other old ladies had come to pay their respects. She hastily rose to welcome them, but her two or three old sisters-in-law were already entering. They took one another’s arms, laughed and exchanged courtesies. After tea, when the visitors left, Grandmother Jia saw them only as far as the inner ceremonial gate before returning to the chief seat. Jia Jing and Jia She led in the younger men.

‘You have enough trouble all year round,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘There’s no need for bows.’

As she spoke, however, men and women came forward in separate groups, one after another, and all performed their salutations. Folding chairs were set to right and left, and the others took their seats in order of age to receive the greetings of those below them. Then the men and women servants, pages and maids of both mansions paid their respects, arranged by the upper, middle and lower grades of their duties. New Year money, purses and little gold and silver ingots were distributed, and a reunion feast was brought in. Men sat to the east and women to the west. Tusu wine, reunion soup, lucky fruits and wish-fulfilling cakes were served. Grandmother Jia then rose and went into the inner room to change, and only then did the others disperse.

That night incense and offerings were set before the Kitchen God and in every Buddhist shrine. Paper images of Heaven and Earth, with incense and offerings, stood in the courtyard of Lady Wang’s principal rooms. Great transparent horn lanterns were hung high over the main gate of Prospect Garden, with two rows of tall lanterns and lights along all the paths.

High and low, everyone was dressed as brightly as a mass of flowers. All night the voices, talk and laughter rang out, and firecrackers and fireworks followed one another without a break. At the fifth watch next morning Grandmother Jia and the others again put on full dress according to rank and went to court with their complete ceremonial retinue, to offer New Year congratulations and celebrate Yuan-chun’s birthday. After the banquet they returned to worship the ancestors once more at Ning mansion, then came home to receive the family’s greetings before changing their clothes and resting. Grandmother Jia saw none of the friends and relations who came with festival greetings. She took her ease, talking only with Aunt Xue and Aunt Li, or playing the counter game and cards with Bao-yu, Bao-qin, Bao-chai, Dai-yu and the other girls. Lady Wang and Xi-feng, meanwhile, were busy every day entertaining New Year guests. The halls and courtyards on their side were full of plays and feasting, with friends and relations coming in an endless stream. Seven or eight busy days passed before it was over.

Almost at once the Lantern Festival was upon them, and both Ning and Rong mansions were hung with lanterns and coloured decorations. On the eleventh Jia She entertained Grandmother Jia and the others; on the following day Jia Zhen did the same. Grandmother Jia went to both and spent half a day enjoying herself informally. Lady Wang and Xi-feng had invitations to New Year feasts day after day, too many to record.

On the evening of the fifteenth, Grandmother Jia ordered several tables of wine and food to be laid in the great reception hall, engaged a troupe of young players and had fine lanterns of every kind hung throughout it. Sons, nephews, grandsons and granddaughters-in-law from both mansions gathered with her for a family feast. Jia Jing never drank wine, so no invitation was sent to him. On the seventeenth, once the ancestral observances were over, he would return outside the city to pursue his cultivation. Even during these few days at home he stayed silently in a quiet room, cut off from all that went on. We need say no more of him. Jia She partook briefly of his mother’s hospitality, then took his leave. Grandmother Jia knew that neither he nor the others would be at ease while he stayed, and let him go. At home he admired the lanterns and drank with his retainers, his ears filled with music and song, his eyes with brocade and embroidery. His unfettered pleasures were naturally rather different from those here.

In Grandmother Jia’s reception hall there were ten or so tables. Beside each stood a small stand with a three-piece incense set, burning baihe palace incense bestowed by the Emperor. There were also miniature landscapes, some eight cun long, four or five wide and two or three high, with little rocks dotted among the moss and fresh flowers growing in them. Small trays of foreign lacquer held old-kiln teacups and tiny teapots of assorted designs, in which the finest teas were brewing. The ornamental screens were all of open-carved zitan wood, inset with scarlet gauze bearing openwork embroidery of flowers and poems in cursive script. The woman who had embroidered these screens came from Suzhou. Her name was Hui-niang.

She belonged to a scholarly official family and was herself accomplished in painting and calligraphy. Now and then she did a piece or two of needlework for amusement; it was never made for sale. The flower sprays on these screens were all copied from the paintings of famous masters of the Tang, Song, Yuan and Ming dynasties. Their composition and colouring were therefore refined, quite unlike the mere gaudy brilliance of craftsmen’s work. Beside every spray she embroidered in black floss an old poet’s lines on that flower, drawn variously from poems, lyrics, songs or rhapsodies. The hooks and turns of the cursive writing, its light and heavy strokes, its joins and breaks, were indistinguishable from brushwork, nothing like the stiff, disagreeable lettering of commercial embroidery. She did not use her skill for profit. Though her work was known throughout the land, very few possessed any, and many hereditary official and wealthy families had none at all. It had come to be called ‘Hui embroidery’. Lately there had even been common profiteers imitating her stitches and making money from the gullible. But Hui-niang had died young, at eighteen, and now there could never be another piece.

Even families that possessed a piece or two treasured them and never put them to use. A band of those gentlemen of the Hanlin Academy who were possessed by the literary demon cherished ‘Hui embroidery’ so deeply that they declared the word ‘embroidery’ inadequate to its wonders. To speak of writing like this as mere embroidery was almost an affront. They discussed it together and decided to suppress the word ‘embroidery’ and substitute ‘patterns’. So now everyone called it ‘Hui patterns’. A genuine example of ‘Hui patterns’ was beyond price.

For all the splendour of the Jia family, they had possessed only two or three pieces. Two had been presented to the Emperor the previous year; now only this set of ornamental screens remained, sixteen panels in all. Grandmother Jia valued them as jewels. They were not included among the furnishings brought out for guests, but kept in her own quarters for her pleasure when she was in the mood for a wine party. There were also small old-kiln vases of various kinds, filled with fresh flowers and greenery in arrangements such as ‘Three Friends of Winter’ and ‘Wealth and Honour in the Jade Hall’.

The two highest tables were for Aunt Li and Aunt Xue. On the east side Grandmother Jia had a short, low-footed couch with a protective screen open-carved in a kui-dragon pattern, complete with back cushions, bolsters and a fur mattress. On the couch, at one end, stood an exceptionally light and dainty little table of foreign lacquer traced with gold. On it were a teapot, teacup, rinsing-bowl, foreign towel and other such things, as well as a spectacle-case. Grandmother Jia reclined on the couch, laughing and talking with the others, and now and then took up her spectacles to look towards the stage. She smiled at Aunt Xue and Aunt Li.

‘Forgive an old woman. My bones ache. You must allow me to keep you company lying down, though it’s very rude of me.’

She told Amber to sit on the couch and pat her legs with a padded massage club. No dining table had been laid immediately below the couch, only a tall stand bearing an ornamental screen, vases, an incense-burner and the like. A separate, finely made little high table held her wine-cup, spoon and chopsticks. Her dining table was set beside the couch, and she had Bao-qin, Xiang-yun, Dai-yu and Bao-yu sit at it. Every dish and plate of fruit was first held up for her to see. If it pleased her, it was left on the little table for her to taste, then taken away and set before the four young people. They counted simply as sharing Grandmother Jia’s table.

Only below them, therefore, came the places of Lady Xing and Lady Wang, followed by You-shi, Li Wan, Xi-feng and Jia Rong’s wife. Along the west side sat Bao-chai, Li Wen, Li Qi, Xiu-yan, Ying-chun and the other girls.

From the great beams on either side hung a matching pair of glass lotus lanterns with coloured tassels, linked in threes and clustered in fives. Before each table stood a lacquered stem with a drooping lotus leaf, fitted with a socket holding a coloured candle. These leaves were of chased enamel and turned on movable fittings. They had all been turned outwards, screening the light and directing it towards the stage, so that the play could be seen with particular clarity. Window frames and doors had all been removed, and palace lanterns of every kind with coloured tassels hung in their place. Inside and outside the verandah eaves and under the covered walkways on both sides hung lanterns of horn, glass, embroidered gauze and spun glass; embroidered, painted, built up in relief or cut out; of silk or paper, in every variety. The tables on the verandah were occupied by Jia Zhen, Jia Lian, Jia Huan, Jia Cong, Jia Rong, Jia Qin, Jia Yun, Jia Ling, Jia Chang and the others.

Grandmother Jia had also sent invitations to the men and women of the wider clan. Some, however, were too old to care for a noisy gathering; some could not conveniently come because no one would be left at home; some were confined by illness and could not come, much as they wished to. Others stayed away out of envy of the rich and shame at their own poverty. There were even some who disliked and feared Xi-feng so much that they refused to come out of spite; and some were shy and awkward, unused to company, and dared not come. Thus, for all the numbers of the clan, the only woman guest was Jia Jun’s mother Lou-shi, who brought Jia Jun with her. Of the men, only Jia Qin, Jia Yun, Jia Chang and Jia Ling came, all four currently working under Xi-feng. Though they were far from all being present, for a small family party the company was quite a lively one.

Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife now arrived with six women servants carrying three kang tables, two women to each table. Every table was covered with a strip of red felt, on which lay new copper cash fresh from the mint, carefully selected for uniform size and threaded on bright red cords.

Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife directed them to place two of the tables below Aunt Xue’s and Aunt Li’s seats, and bring the third to Grandmother Jia’s couch.

‘Put it in the middle,’ Grandmother Jia said.

The women knew the procedure well. They set down the tables, untied all the strings of cash, drew out the coloured cords and left the coins in loose heaps. On stage, ‘Meeting in the Tower’, a scene from The Western Tower, was nearing its end. Yu Shu-ye had gone off in a huff, and Wen Bao broke into a comic aside.

‘Off you go in a temper! It so happens that today’s the fifteenth of the first month, and Her Old Ladyship is giving a family feast at Rong mansion. I’ll get on this horse and hurry in to beg some fruit. That’s the important thing!’

This set Grandmother Jia and the others laughing.

‘What a little imp!’ Aunt Xue and the others said. ‘The dear little thing!’

‘He’s only nine,’ said Xi-feng.

‘How cleverly he put that,’ Grandmother Jia said with a laugh. Then she uttered the single word:

‘Reward!’

Three women had already made ready their winnowing baskets. At the word ‘Reward’, they stepped up to the heaps of loose cash and each scooped up a basketful. They came out and called towards the stage:

‘Her Old Ladyship, Aunt Xue and Aunt Li reward Wen Bao with money to buy fruit!’
