Chapter 79 Xue Wen-long regrets marrying a lioness of Hedong; Jia Ying-chun is mismatched with a wolf of Zhongshan1

Chapter end

Bao-yu had just finished his offering to Skybright when a voice from among the flower-shadows gave him a fright. He stepped out to look more closely. It was none other than Lin Dai-yu, smiling all over her face.

‘What an original funeral tribute!

‘It ought to be handed down alongside the Cao E inscription.’

Bao-yu blushed in spite of himself.

‘I thought all the funeral tributes in the world had worn the old phrases threadbare,’ he answered, smiling, ‘so I tried a new pattern. It was only something I did for amusement on the spur of the moment. Who could have guessed you would catch me at it again? If anything is very much out of place, why not cut it about and improve it?’

‘Where is the draft? I should like to read it carefully. It went on so long I don’t know what it was about. I only caught two phrases somewhere in the middle: something like “Within the red gauze curtains, a young gentleman full of love; beneath the mound of yellow earth, a maiden ill-starred.” The thought in that couplet is good, but “within the red gauze curtains” is rather threadbare itself. Why not use the real thing lying ready to hand?’

‘What real thing?’ Bao-yu asked at once.

‘All our window-lattices are covered with Rosy-cloud Gauze now,’ said Dai-yu, smiling. ‘Why not say, “Beneath the madder-red gauze window, a young gentleman full of love”?’

Bao-yu could not help stamping his foot and laughing.

‘Excellent! Exactly right! Only you could think of it and put it into words. It shows how many beautiful sights and wonderful things have always been lying ready to hand, if only fools and dunces could think of them and express them. There’s just one difficulty. Wonderful as the change is, it would be fitting if you were the one beneath that window. I really cannot claim such an honour.’

He went on repeating ‘I cannot’ ten or twenty times.

‘What does it matter?’ Dai-yu said with a laugh. ‘My window can just as well be yours. Why draw such distinctions, as though we were strangers? In olden times men of different surnames, who had begun as strangers, would share their fine horses and light fur coats, and not mind if they wore them out. How much more should we!’

‘When it comes to friendship,’ said Bao-yu, smiling, ‘it isn’t merely a question of fine horses and light furs. Even gold and white jade are nothing to count out to the last fraction. But taking liberties with a young lady’s chamber is quite another matter. That would never do. I had better change “young gentleman” and “maiden” altogether, and make it an elegy you have written for her. You were always very kind to her, after all. I would rather give up this whole great composition than lose that new phrase about the madder-red gauze. Why not make it: “Beneath the madder-red gauze window, a young lady full of love; beneath the mound of yellow earth, a maid ill-starred”? Even if it leaves me out altogether, I shall be content.’

‘She wasn’t my maid,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘Why should I say that? Besides, “young lady” and “maid” aren’t very elegant. When my Nightingale dies, it will be time enough for me to say it.’

‘Why go and wish her dead?’ Bao-yu said hastily, laughing.

‘You were the one who wanted to wish it on her. It wasn’t my idea.’

‘I’ve thought of something else,’ said Bao-yu. ‘This change will put it right.

‘Why not say: “Beneath the madder-red gauze window, no bond was destined for me; beneath the mound of yellow earth, why were you so ill-starred?”’

Dai-yu’s face changed with a sudden pang. Countless uneasy conjectures crowded into her mind, but she would not let them show. Instead she hastily smiled and nodded her approval.

‘That is a good change. Don’t go altering it at random any more. Run along and do something useful. Your mother sent someone just now to say you must go over to your elder aunt’s early tomorrow morning. A proposal for your Second Sister has been accepted. I suppose the family is coming tomorrow to pay its respects and acknowledge the consent, and that is why you are all wanted there.’

Bao-yu clapped his hands.

‘Why such a hurry? I’m not feeling very well either. I may not be able to go tomorrow.’

‘There you go again! I do wish you would mend your ways. A year older and two years younger…’

She began coughing as she spoke.

‘The wind is cold here,’ Bao-yu said quickly. ‘We’ve been foolish to stand about in it all this time. Let’s go back.’

‘I’m going home to rest too. I’ll see you tomorrow.’

With that she took her own path away.

Bao-yu had no choice but to turn back dispiritedly. Then he suddenly remembered that Dai-yu had no one with her, and hurriedly told a little maid to follow and see her home. When he reached the House of Green Delights, sure enough, an old nanny sent by Lady Wang was there with instructions that he was to go over to Jia She’s early next morning, exactly as Dai-yu had said.

Jia She had, in fact, promised Ying-chun to the Sun family. The Suns came from Datong prefecture. Their forebears had been military officers and protégés of the Ning and Rong houses, so the connection could be reckoned a friendship of long standing. Only one member of the Sun family was now in the capital, a man who had inherited the rank of commandant. His name was Sun Shao-zu. He was imposing in appearance and powerfully built, skilled with horse and bow, and adept at handling people and adapting to circumstances. Not yet thirty, he possessed a substantial fortune and was waiting at the Board of War to be nominated for promotion when a vacancy arose. He was unmarried. To Jia She he was the descendant of an old family connection, a suitable match in both personal qualities and property, and Jia She had accordingly singled him out for the honour of becoming his son-in-law.

He had also informed Grandmother Jia. She was not particularly pleased, but supposed that if she tried to prevent it he might not listen. Marriages had their own heavenly purposes and causes from a former existence. Besides, it was the girl’s own father who was deciding: why should she step forward and make trouble? She therefore said only, ‘I see,’ and did not pursue the matter.

Jia Zheng, on the other hand, thoroughly disliked the Suns. Old family friends they might be, but their ancestor had merely coveted the influence of the Rong and Ning houses, and had placed himself under their patronage because he had troubles he could not settle. They were not the descendants of a distinguished family schooled in poetry and ritual. Jia Zheng had therefore tried twice to dissuade his brother. Jia She would not listen, and he too had had to let the matter drop.

Bao-yu had never so much as met Sun Shao-zu. Next day he had to go over merely to discharge his duty. He heard that the wedding was to take place very soon: the bride was to enter her husband’s household before the year was out. Then Lady Xing and the others informed Grandmother Jia that they were taking Ying-chun out of Prospect Garden. This and all the other arrangements extinguished what little spirit he had left. Day after day he wandered about in a daze, unable to find anything to occupy him. When he heard that four maids were to go with her, he stamped his foot again and sighed.

‘From now on there will be five fewer unspoilt people in the world.’

Every day he went to linger about Amaryllis Eyot, looking this way and that. The windows stood lonely; the screens and curtains hung desolate. There were only a few old women whose turn it was to keep watch at night. Even the knotweed flowers and reed leaves on the bank, the green floating-heart and fragrant water-caltrops in the pond, seemed to droop and dwindle, as though remembering someone who had gone. How unlike their usual rivalry of colour and beauty! Taking in this bleak and sorrowful scene, he could no longer contain his feelings, and a song came to him as he spoke:

One night a cold autumn wind crossed the pond, Scattering the red-jade shadows of caltrop and lotus.

Knotweed flowers and caltrop leaves cannot bear their grief; Heavy dew and thick frost weigh down their slender stems.

All the long day, no click of gaming-stones is heard; Flecks of the swallows’ mud have soiled the board.

The ancients grieved to part from those they called their friends; How much more I, who part from my own flesh and blood!

He had scarcely finished when someone behind him said with a laugh, ‘What are you mooning about now?’

Bao-yu turned quickly to see who it was. It was Caltrop.

‘My dear sister,’ he asked, turning towards her with a smile, ‘what brings you running here just now? You haven’t been in for a stroll for days.’

Caltrop clapped her hands, beaming.

‘It isn’t that I don’t come. But your cousin is home now: how could I be as free as I was before? Just now our mistress sent someone to look for your Cousin Feng, but they couldn’t find her. They said she had come into the garden. When I heard that, I asked to be sent in to find her. I met her maid, who told me she was at Sweet-rice Village. I was on my way there when I happened to run into you. Tell me, has Sister Aroma been well these last few days?

‘And how did we lose Sister Skybright so suddenly? What illness was it, after all? Miss Ying-chun moved out so quickly too. Look how empty this place is.’

Bao-yu could hardly answer fast enough. He invited her to come to the House of Green Delights for tea.

‘I really can’t just now,’ said Caltrop. ‘I’ll come when I’ve found Mrs Lian and finished my business.’

‘What business is in such a hurry?’

‘Getting your cousin a wife. That’s why it’s urgent.’

‘Of course. Whose daughter have they finally decided on? For the last six months I’ve heard nothing but talk. Today it’s the Zhang girl who’s so good; tomorrow they want the Li girl; the day after, they’re discussing the Wang girl. What sins can all those daughters have committed, to be talked over like that when they’ve done nothing to anyone?’

‘It’s settled now,’ said Caltrop. ‘There’ll be no need to drag in any more families.’

‘Whose is it?’ Bao-yu asked eagerly.

‘The last time your cousin went away on business, he called on some relations who lived along his route.

‘They’ve been related to us for a long time, and, like us, they are registered as merchants with the Board of Revenue. They’re one of the very foremost families. When we were talking about them the other day, it turned out that both your houses knew of them too. All over Chang’an, from princes and noblemen down to shopkeepers, people call them the “Osmanthus Xias”.’

‘Why the “Osmanthus Xias”?’ Bao-yu asked, smiling.

‘Their family name is Xia,’ said Caltrop, ‘and they’re enormously rich and grand. Never mind all their other land: they have several dozen qing planted with nothing but osmanthus. Every osmanthus establishment inside and outside Chang’an belongs to them. They even supply all the ornamental potted plants for the palace as tribute. That’s how they got the nickname. The old master is dead now, and there’s only the old mistress living with her own daughter. There isn’t a son or a brother. It’s a pity: the whole family line is dying out.’

‘Never mind whether their line dies out or not,’ Bao-yu said quickly. ‘What is the girl like? How did your master come to take a fancy to her?’

Caltrop laughed.

‘For one thing, it was a match made in heaven. For another, “every lover sees a Xi Shi”. The families used to visit one another, and the two of them played together from childhood. They’re cousins, the children of a brother and sister, so there was nothing to be shy about. They had been apart for several years, though. When he arrived at their house the other day, Mrs Xia, who has no son, saw how your cousin had grown up and cried and laughed by turns. She made even more of him than if he had been a son of her own.

‘Then she had the two cousins meet. Who would have thought the girl had grown into such a flower! And she studies and writes at home, too. Your cousin made up his mind about her there and then. He and the whole crowd from the pawnshop, the old appraiser and the assistants, imposed on them for three or four days. They still wanted them to stay longer. It took a great deal of persuasion to get away. The moment your cousin came home, he began whispering and coaxing our mistress to ask for the girl’s hand. Our mistress had seen her before, and the families are well matched, so she agreed. She talked it over with her sister here and Miss Feng, and sent someone to make the proposal. It was accepted at once. Only the wedding is so soon that we hardly know which way to turn. I can’t wait for her to arrive: we shall have another person to write poems with.’

Bao-yu gave a cold little laugh.

‘You may say so, but somehow, hearing all this, I can’t help worrying about what will become of you.’

Caltrop blushed. Her face grew serious.

‘What do you mean by that?

‘We’ve always treated each other with respect. What do you mean by suddenly bringing up things like that today? No wonder everyone says you’re not a person one can be friendly with.’

As she spoke she turned and walked away.

Her response left Bao-yu forlorn, as though something had slipped out of his grasp. He stood in a stupor for a long time, turning things over in his mind, until tears began to fall without his knowing it. At last he dragged himself back to the House of Green Delights. He had no peaceful sleep that night. Even in his dreams he called for Skybright, or was terrified by nightmares, and was restless in every way. Next day he had no appetite, and his body grew feverish. The recent search of Prospect Garden, the expulsion of Chess, the parting from Ying-chun and his grief for Skybright had subjected him to humiliation, alarm and sorrow. These, together with an invasion of wind and cold from without, brought on an illness that confined him to bed. When Grandmother Jia heard of it, she came to see him herself every day.

In her heart Lady Wang regretted having pressed him too harshly over Skybright, though she allowed none of this to appear in her face. She merely told the nurses and the others to tend him and watch him carefully, and had a doctor brought in twice a day to feel his pulse and prescribe. Only after a month did he gradually recover. Grandmother Jia ordered that he must be carefully nursed back to health. A hundred days must pass before he was allowed meat, fish, rich flour-based foods and the like, or permitted to go out. During those hundred days he was not even to approach the courtyard gate, but must amuse himself indoors. After forty or fifty days this confinement had him all but throwing off sparks. How could he endure it? Yet whatever devices he tried, Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang would not yield, and he had to submit. He consequently went to every length with the maids, teasing and playing just as he pleased.

Then he heard that Xue Pan had held a splendid wedding feast, with plays and a great deal of merrymaking, and had brought his bride home. The young Miss Xia was said to be very pretty, and to have some knowledge of literature. Bao-yu longed to go over and see her at once. A little later he heard that Ying-chun too had been married.

He thought of the days when he and the girls had all lived together, always at one another’s side. Once parted, even if they met again, they could never be as close as before. And he could not so much as go and see her now. His distress and longing were almost unbearable. There was nothing for it but to school himself to patience, and for the time being to banish his gloom by romping with the maids. At least he was spared the ordeal of Jia Zheng’s rebukes and demands that he study. In those hundred days he and the girls stopped at nothing short of pulling down the House of Green Delights. They were a law unto themselves, inventing games such as the world had never seen. But there is no need to describe them all here.

To return to Caltrop: after giving Bao-yu that rebuff, she had decided that he had deliberately taken a liberty with her.

‘No wonder Miss Bao-chai won’t venture to be friendly with him. I’m a long way behind her, that’s plain. And no wonder Miss Lin quarrels with him so often and cries so bitterly: he must take liberties with her too. I had better keep well out of his way from now on.’

After that she rarely even entered Prospect Garden. She was busy from morning to night. Once Xue Pan had a wife, she imagined she would have gained a protecting talisman: some of the responsibility would be lifted from her own shoulders, and life would be quieter than it was now. Besides, she had heard that the bride possessed both beauty and talent, so naturally she would be refined and gentle. For these reasons she awaited the wedding day ten times more eagerly than Xue Pan. At last the longed-for day came and the bride was brought home. Caltrop attended her with the utmost care and diligence.

This Miss Xia was only seventeen. She was indeed quite good-looking, and could read a few characters. In the hidden workings and intricate designs of her mind, she followed close in Xi-feng’s footsteps. There was only one disadvantage. Her father had died when she was very young, and she had no brothers. Her widowed mother had this one daughter to herself and cherished and indulged her as something more precious than a jewel. Whatever the daughter did, her mother gave way to her. Such excessive pampering had finally produced the temper of Robber Zhi.

She revered herself as a bodhisattva and regarded other people as filth. Outwardly she had the grace of flowers and willows; inwardly she was wind and thunder. At home she was forever flying into rages with her maids, scolding them at the mildest, beating them at the worst. Now that she was married, she considered herself a mistress of a household. She could no longer be shy and gentle as a daughter was expected to be; she must display her authority if she was to keep people under control. Moreover, Xue Pan was hard in temper and arrogant and extravagant in his behaviour. Unless she took advantage of the hot stove to cook him thoroughly through at once, she would never be able to plant her own standard. The presence in his rooms of a favourite concubine as talented and beautiful as Caltrop only strengthened her resolve to emulate ‘Emperor Taizu of Song destroying the Southern Tang’. ‘How can I allow someone else to sleep soundly beside my bed?’ was the spirit of her enterprise.

Because her family owned so much osmanthus, her childhood name was Jin-gui, ‘Golden Osmanthus’. At home she would not let anyone utter the two syllables of her name. If someone inadvertently let slip so much as one of them, she insisted on a severe beating or heavy punishment before she was satisfied. Yet she realised that it was impossible to prohibit the words for the flower itself: it would have to be given another name. Remembering the association of osmanthus with Chang-e in the Palace of Broad Cold, she renamed it ‘Chang-e flowers’, thereby intimating that she herself was just such an exalted being.

Xue Pan had always been one to dote on the new and discard the old. He had courage when wine was in him, but little backbone when it was only rice. Having acquired such a wife, and being still in the first flush of novelty, he naturally gave way to her in most things. Xia Jin-gui saw how matters stood and tried pressing him a little harder at every step. For the first month they were still evenly matched. After two months, however, Xue Pan’s proud bearing began visibly to subside.

One day, after drinking, he wanted to do something or other and consulted Jin-gui first. She flatly refused. Unable to contain himself, he spoke a few angry words and went off to do it on his own. Jin-gui flew into such a rage that she wept like a drunken person, refused even tea or broth, and took to feigning illness. A doctor was called in to treat her.

‘The vital energy and the blood are running counter to one another,’ he pronounced. ‘She needs a remedy to ease the chest and restore the flow of energy.’

Aunt Xue scolded Xue Pan in her vexation.

‘You’re married now, and you’ll soon have a son in your arms, yet still you carry on like this! Her mother brought up this one daughter as carefully as a phoenix’s egg, a creature more delicate than a flower. She thought you were someone worth having and gave her to you as your wife.

‘Instead of settling down and behaving yourself, and putting all your mind to living happily together, you go on with your nonsense. You pour that yellow swill down your throat and torment the girl. Now there’s money to spend on medicine and all this needless worry.’

Her words filled Xue Pan with remorse, and he went to comfort Jin-gui. Seeing her mother-in-law scold her husband in this fashion delighted Jin-gui. She put on still more airs and refused to take any notice of him. He could think of nothing to do but reproach himself. Only after ten days or half a month did he finally manage to coax her back into a good humour. From then on he was twice as careful, and his proud bearing dwindled by another half.

Seeing her husband’s banners droop and her mother-in-law so kindly disposed, Jin-gui began to take up her spear and try her charger. At first she had merely brought Xue Pan under her thumb. Later she traded on her charms and indulged in petulant airs, extending her campaign to Aunt Xue, and then to Xue Bao-chai.

Bao-chai had long been watching her unruly designs. She met each turn of events as it came, quietly using words to hold Jin-gui’s ambitions in check. Jin-gui knew she was not to be trifled with. Again and again she wanted to find an opening, but there was none, and she had to bend herself to being agreeable.

One day, with nothing to do, she fell into conversation with Caltrop and asked about her native place and her parents. Caltrop said she had forgotten them all. Jin-gui was displeased and accused her of deliberately keeping things from her.

She then asked who had given her the name ‘Caltrop’.

‘Miss Bao-chai,’ Caltrop replied.

Jin-gui gave a cold laugh.

‘Everyone says how learned she is. That name alone shows that she isn’t.’

‘Oh, ma’am, you don’t know!’ Caltrop said hastily, smiling. ‘Even our mistress’s brother-in-law is always praising her learning.’
