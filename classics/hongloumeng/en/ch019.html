Chapter 19 On a tender night, a flower finds words for her heart; In a quiet day’s lingering affection, jade gives forth fragrance

Chapter end

After Consort Jia returned to the palace, she had an audience with the Emperor the following day to thank him for his favour and report on her visit home. His Majesty was greatly pleased, and made further gifts of coloured satins, gold, silver and other articles from the imperial privy treasury to Jia Zheng, the other imperial consorts’ families and those concerned. We need not give the particulars.

For days the two mansions, Rong and Ning, had strained every nerve. Now everyone was exhausted in body and mind, and putting away all the furnishings and other things used in the garden took another two or three days. Xi-feng, above all, had a multitude of duties and a heavy burden to carry. Others might steal a little ease or find a quiet corner to hide in; she alone could not get away. Besides, she was too proud by nature to give anyone grounds for criticism, and struggled to look as though she had had nothing to do.

Bao-yu, by contrast, had less to do and more leisure than anyone. As it happened, Aroma’s mother came early that morning, obtained Grandmother Jia’s permission and took her home for New Year tea. She would not be back until evening. Bao-yu therefore stayed with the other maids, playing a dice game with Go counters. He was finding their games indoors rather dull when some maids came to announce, ‘Master Zhen from the East Mansion invites you over for the plays and the lanterns.’

Bao-yu told them to change his clothes. Just as he was leaving, a gift of sweet steamed milk curd arrived from Consort Jia. Remembering how much Aroma had enjoyed it the last time, he ordered it to be kept for her.

He himself reported to Grandmother Jia and went over to see the plays. Jia Zhen’s programme, however, consisted of Ding Lang Acknowledges His Father, Huang Bo-yang Marshals the Ghostly Host, Pilgrim Sun Wreaks Havoc in Heaven, Jiang Zi-ya Beheads the Generals and Invests the Gods, and other pieces of that kind. Gods and ghosts suddenly swarmed out, only to give way to a full display of monsters and demons. There were even processions with banners, invocations of the Buddha and offerings of incense; gongs, drums and shouts could be heard beyond the lane. Everyone in the street declared, ‘What a lively show! You wouldn’t see anything like this at another house.’

Bao-yu found all this magnificent commotion quite unbearable. He sat through very little of it before wandering off to amuse himself elsewhere. First he went into the inner quarters and spent a while laughing and talking with You-shi, the maids and the concubines, then came out through the second gate. You-shi and the others supposed he had gone back to the plays and thought no more about him. Jia Zhen, Jia Lian, Xue Pan and their companions were too busy with guessing games, drinking forfeits and every sort of merriment to pay him any attention. If they noticed his empty seat, they merely assumed he had gone inside, and did not ask. As for Bao-yu’s pages, the older ones knew that once he had arrived here he would not leave until evening. Some slipped away to gamble, others to take New Year tea with friends or relations, and others again to visit brothels or go drinking. They dispersed in secret, intending to return that evening. The younger boys had all squeezed into the actors’ dressing room to watch the excitement.

Finding himself quite alone, Bao-yu thought, ‘There’s that little study here, called [text missing], where they used to hang a scroll of a beautiful woman, painted so wonderfully true to life. With all this bustle today, I suppose the place must naturally [text missing], and the beauty is bound to be lonely too. I must go and see her, to cheer her up.’

With this in mind he made for the study. As he reached the window, he heard a sound of moaning within. It gave him quite a fright. Could the beauty have come to life? Summoning his courage, he licked a hole in the paper window and peeped inside. The painted beauty had not come to life at all. It was Tealeaf, holding a girl beneath him, engaged in what Disenchantment had taught.

‘Good heavens!’ Bao-yu cried involuntarily. He kicked the door open, startling the two apart; they trembled as they shook their clothes into place.1

Seeing that it was Bao-yu, Tealeaf dropped to his knees and pleaded over and over again.

‘In broad daylight! What do you mean by it?’ Bao-yu said. ‘If Master Zhen knew, would you get out of it alive?’

Meanwhile he looked at the girl. Though not a beauty, she had a clear, fair complexion and something rather pleasing about her. Her face and ears scarlet with shame, she stood with lowered head and said nothing.

Bao-yu stamped his foot. ‘Why don’t you run?’

That brought her to her senses, and she fled as fast as she could. Bao-yu hurried out after her, calling, ‘Don’t be frightened! I shan’t tell anyone!’

‘My lord and master!’ Tealeaf cried behind him in dismay. ‘You’re telling everyone as it is!’

Bao-yu then asked, ‘How old is that girl? In her teens?’

‘Sixteen or seventeen at most,’ said Tealeaf.

‘You haven’t even asked her age or birth-year animal. Naturally you’ll know still less about anything else. A fine thing for her to have known you!

‘Poor girl, poor girl!’ He went on, ‘What’s her name?’

Tealeaf burst out laughing. ‘That’s a long story. Such an extraordinary tale, you couldn’t put it in a book! She says that when her mother had her, she dreamed she’d been given a length of brocade with a five-coloured pattern of wealth and honour and an unbroken wan motif. So they called her Wan-er, after the wan in the pattern.’

‘That really is unusual,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘Perhaps she has some good fortune ahead of her.’ He fell silent for a while, thinking.

Tealeaf asked, ‘Why aren’t you watching such fine plays, Master Bao?’

‘I watched for ages and got dreadfully bored. I came out for a stroll and found the two of you. What shall we do now?’

Tealeaf grinned. ‘No one knows where we are. I could quietly take you outside the city for a look round, and we’d come back here in a little while. They’d never know.’

‘No. We might get carried off by beggars. And if they found out, there would be an awful fuss. We’d better go somewhere familiar and close by, where we can get back at once.’

‘Somewhere familiar and close by? Whose house could we go to? That’s a difficult one.’

‘I know,’ said Bao-yu, smiling. ‘Let’s go and find your big sister Hua, and see what she’s doing at home.’

‘Yes, yes!’ Tealeaf laughed. ‘I’d forgotten about her house. But if they find out and say I took you roaming about, won’t they beat me?’

‘You’ve got me,’ Bao-yu said with a smile.

At that Tealeaf brought the horses, and the two went out by the back gate.

Fortunately Aroma’s home was close by, no more than half a li away. In a moment they were at the door. Tealeaf went in first and called for her elder brother, Hua Zi-fang. Aroma’s mother had brought her home along with several nieces, some her sisters’ daughters and some her brothers’, and they were taking tea and fruit.

Hearing someone outside call ‘Brother Hua!’, Hua Zi-fang hurried out. The sight of master and servant left him speechless with surprise and apprehension. He quickly lifted Bao-yu from his horse and called into the courtyard, ‘Master Bao is here!’

The news might mean little to the others, but Aroma had no idea what could have brought him. She ran out to meet him, caught him by the arm and asked, ‘What have you come for?’

‘I was so bored. I came to see what you were doing.’

Only then did she feel reassured. With a sigh and a smile she said, ‘What a way to behave! Whatever did you want to come here for?’ Then she asked Tealeaf, ‘Who else came with you?’

‘Nobody knows,’ Tealeaf said, grinning. ‘It’s just the two of us.’

Aroma became alarmed again. ‘This is too much! Suppose you’d met someone, or run into the master? The streets are crowded with people, carts bumping into one another, horses and chairs everywhere. What if there had been an accident? Is that something to play about with? You’ve got more nerve than sense, the pair of you. Tealeaf put you up to it. I shall certainly tell the mammas to beat you when I get back.’

Tealeaf stuck out his lips. ‘Master Bao ordered me to bring him, with threats and blows, and now it’s all my fault. I said we shouldn’t come. We’d better go away again.’

‘Never mind,’ Hua Zi-fang interposed. ‘You’re here now. There’s no need to say any more. Only this poor little place of ours is so cramped and dirty. Where can the young master possibly sit?’

Aroma’s mother had already come out to welcome him. Aroma led Bao-yu inside, where three or five girls lowered their heads in shy embarrassment as he entered. Hua Zi-fang and his mother were terribly afraid that he would be cold. They urged him to sit on the kang, hurried to set out a fresh table of fruit and bustled about pouring good tea.

‘Don’t put yourselves to all that trouble,’ Aroma said, smiling. ‘I know what to do. There’s no need for the fruit either. We can’t just give him anything to eat.’

As she spoke, she took her own cushion and spread it on a kang for Bao-yu to sit on, setting her own foot-warmer beneath his feet. She took two plum-blossom incense cakes from her purse, lifted the lid of her hand-warmer and put them in to burn. Then she replaced the lid and put the warmer in his arms. Finally she filled her own teacup and handed it to him.

Meanwhile her mother and brother had busily arranged a separate table of fruit, all very neat. Aroma could see nothing suitable for him to eat.

‘Now you’ve come,’ she said with a smile, ‘we can’t let you go without something. You must at least taste a little, to mark your visit to my home.’

She picked out a few pine kernels, blew away the thin skins and offered them to Bao-yu on her handkerchief.

Bao-yu noticed that Aroma’s eyes were a little red, her powdered face soft and smooth. He asked her quietly, ‘Why have you been crying? What’s wrong?’

‘Crying? I haven’t. I got something in my eye and rubbed it,’ she said with a smile, covering the matter up.

Bao-yu was wearing a scarlet narrow-sleeved robe with golden python designs, lined with fox-underarm fur, and over it a dark blue sable-fur jacket with rows of tassels.

‘You even changed into new clothes to come here?’ Aroma asked. ‘Didn’t they ask where you were going?’

‘I changed to go and see the plays at Cousin Zhen’s.’

Aroma nodded. ‘Stay a moment, then go back. This isn’t a place for you to come to.’

‘It would be much better if you came home now,’ said Bao-yu, smiling. ‘I’ve saved you something nice.’

‘Hush!’ she said with a quiet smile. ‘What will they think if they hear you?’

She reached up and took the Spiritual Jade from his neck.

‘Here, have a look,’ she said to her cousins. ‘Whenever it’s mentioned, you all think it’s so wonderful and long to see it. Now you can look to your hearts’ content. However rare a thing may be, when you actually see it, it’s only a thing like this.’

She handed it round, then hung it on Bao-yu’s neck again. She told her brother to hire either a small sedan-chair or a little carriage to take him back.

‘If I go with him, he’ll be quite all right on horseback,’ Hua Zi-fang said.

‘It’s not a question of being all right. It’s meeting people.’

Hua Zi-fang hurried away and hired a small chair. No one dared press Bao-yu to stay; they could only see him out.

Aroma gave Tealeaf a handful of fruit and some money to buy fireworks, warning him, ‘Don’t tell anyone. You’re to blame too, remember.’

She accompanied Bao-yu right to the door, watched him into the chair and lowered its curtain. Hua Zi-fang and Tealeaf followed, leading the horses. When they reached the street outside the Ning mansion, Tealeaf had the chair stopped.

‘Master Bao and I must go back into the East Mansion and mingle with the others a little before he goes home,’ he told Hua Zi-fang. ‘Otherwise people will suspect something.’

Hua Zi-fang agreed. He lifted Bao-yu out of the chair and helped him on to his horse.

‘I’ve put you to a lot of trouble,’ Bao-yu said, smiling.

They went in again by the back gate. Of this we need say no more.

Once Bao-yu was out, the maids in his room had given themselves over to still freer merriment. Some played the dice game with Go counters, others threw dice or played cards, and melon-seed shells littered the floor. His wet-nurse, Nurse Li, happened to come in leaning on her stick, to pay her respects and see Bao-yu. Finding him out and the maids absorbed in their games, she thoroughly disapproved.

‘Since I left and stopped coming in so often,’ she sighed, ‘you’ve grown more and more unruly. The other mammas scarcely dare say a word to you now. That Bao-yu is a lampstand a zhang and eight chi tall: lights up other people, but can’t see what’s beneath him. Always complaining how dirty other people are, yet here’s his own room and he lets you turn it into a pigsty. There’s less decency here every day.’

The maids knew very well that Bao-yu did not mind such things. Besides, Nurse Li had retired from her duties and no longer had any authority over them. They went on playing and took no notice. Nurse Li persisted with her questions: how much rice did Bao-yu eat at a meal now, what time did he go to bed, and so on.

The maids gave whatever answers came into their heads. One said, ‘What a tiresome old thing!’

‘Is that milk curd in the covered bowl?’ Nurse Li asked. ‘Why wasn’t it sent to me? I’ll eat it now.’ She took a spoon and began.

‘Don’t touch that!’ one of the maids said. ‘He specifically told us to save it for Aroma. There’ll be another row when he comes back. You’ll have to own up yourself, mind, and not get us into trouble.’

Nurse Li was both angry and ashamed. ‘I don’t believe he’s become as bad as that. A bowl of milk! I’d have every right to it if it cost a great deal more. Does he think more of Aroma than of me? Doesn’t he remember how he grew up? My blood turned to milk, and he drank it to grow as big as he is. Now he’ll be angry because I eat one bowl of his milk? I’ll eat it, and we’ll see what happens! You think Aroma’s someone wonderful. She’s a slip of a girl I trained with my own hands. What is she, after all?’

Talking all the while, she defiantly finished the curd.

Another maid laughed. ‘They don’t know how to speak to you. No wonder you’re angry. Master Bao is always sending you things to show his respect. Why should he be upset about this?’

‘You needn’t put on your wheedling ways to coax me,’ said Nurse Li. ‘Do you think I don’t know about Snowpink being driven out over that tea last time? When there’s trouble tomorrow, I’ll come and take the blame!’

She went off in a huff. A little later Bao-yu returned and sent to fetch Aroma. Seeing Skybright lying motionless on the bed, he asked, ‘Are you ill? Or did you lose?’

‘She’d been winning,’ Ripple said. ‘Then old Nurse Li came in and interfered, and she lost. She was so cross she went to bed.’

‘Don’t take her so seriously,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Just let her be.’

By now Aroma had arrived, and they greeted one another. She asked Bao-yu where he had eaten and what time he had come back, and passed on her mother’s and cousins’ greetings to her fellow maids. Then she changed her clothes and took off her finery.

Bao-yu asked for the curd. ‘Nurse Li ate it,’ the maids told him.

Before he could speak, Aroma said quickly, smiling, ‘So that was what you saved me! How thoughtful of you. It tasted lovely when I had it the other day, but afterwards I got such a stomach-ache. I wasn’t better until I’d been sick. It’s just as well she ate it. Left here, it would only have been wasted. What I really want is dried chestnuts. Peel some for me while I make up the bed.’

Bao-yu believed her. He forgot the curd, fetched some chestnuts and sat picking them over and peeling them by the lamp. Seeing that the others were out of the room, he asked Aroma with a smile, ‘Who was that girl in red today? How is she related to you?’

‘She’s my mother’s sister’s daughter, my younger cousin.’

Bao-yu gave two murmurs of admiration.

‘What are you sighing about?’ Aroma asked. ‘I know what you’re thinking. You suppose she isn’t fit to wear red.’

‘No, no!’ he said, smiling. ‘If someone like her can’t wear red, who dares? I thought she was so lovely. If only we could have her here with us somehow!’

Aroma gave a cold laugh. ‘It’s enough for me to have a servant’s fate. Must all my relations have one too? Only the very prettiest girls are to be brought into your house, are they?’

‘Now you’re taking it the wrong way again,’ Bao-yu said hastily, with a smile. ‘Must coming to our house mean being a servant? Couldn’t she come as a relation?’

‘We’re not a fit match for that either,’ Aroma said.

Bao-yu would say no more, and went on peeling chestnuts.

‘Why so silent?’ she asked, smiling. ‘Have I been too forward and offended you? You can spend a few taels tomorrow and buy them in, just to spite me.’

‘What answer can I make when you talk like that?’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘I was only admiring her. She’s just the sort who ought to have been born in a great house like this. Instead, it’s filthy creatures like us who are born here.’

‘She hasn’t had that good fortune,’ Aroma said, ‘but she’s been brought up as carefully as any young lady. She’s the apple of my aunt and uncle’s eye. She’s seventeen now, and they’ve got every bit of her dowry ready. She’s to be married next year.’

The word ‘married’ drew another two sighs from Bao-yu. While he was still feeling unhappy, he heard Aroma sigh as well.

‘All these years since I came here, we cousins haven’t been able to be together.

‘Now I’m going home, they’ll all be gone.’

Hearing something behind these words, Bao-yu started. He dropped the chestnuts and asked urgently, ‘What do you mean, you’re going home?’

‘I heard my mother and brother discussing it today. They want me to put up with another year, and next year they’ll come and buy my release.’

Bao-yu was still more bewildered. ‘Why do they want to buy your release?’

‘What a question! I’m not a servant born into the household. My whole family is elsewhere, and I’m the only one here. How can that go on for ever?’

‘But you could hardly go if I wouldn’t let you.’

‘There’s no such rule,’ said Aroma. ‘Even in the imperial palace they have fixed regulations: a selection every so many years, an intake every so many years. Even there they have no right to keep people for ever, let alone you!’

Bao-yu thought this over. It did seem reasonable.

‘But if Grandmother wouldn’t let you go?’

‘Why wouldn’t she? If I were really someone quite out of the ordinary, I might have moved Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship so much that they’d insist on keeping me. Perhaps they’d give my family a few more taels and retain me. That could happen. But I’m only an ordinary person. There are plenty and plenty better than me. I came here as a little girl to attend Her Old Ladyship, served Miss Shi for several years, and now I’ve served you for several more. If my family comes to buy me back, it’s only right to let me go. They might even be kind enough to send me off without asking for my purchase money. As for keeping me because I’ve looked after you well, that’s out of the question.

‘Looking after you properly is my duty, not some extraordinary achievement. When I leave, someone else just as good will come. Things won’t fall apart without me.’

To Bao-yu it sounded as though there was every reason for her to go and none for her to stay. He grew more anxious.

‘Even so, if I’m determined to keep you, Grandmother will certainly speak to your mother. She could give her plenty of silver, and then your mother would hardly have the face to take you back.’

‘Of course my mother wouldn’t dare insist,’ Aroma said. ‘Never mind speaking nicely to her and giving her more silver. Even if they weren’t nice at all and gave her nothing, if they were set on keeping me by force, she wouldn’t dare refuse. But our family has never bullied people with its rank and power like that. I’m not some object they can get for you because you fancy it, at ten times its value. The seller loses nothing by that, so it can be done. But to keep me here for no reason does you no good, and separates us from our own flesh and blood. Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship would never do such a thing.’

Bao-yu pondered for a long while. ‘Then, by your account, you’re definitely going?’

‘Definitely.’

‘Who would have thought someone like her could be so cold and unfeeling?’ he thought. Then he sighed. ‘If I’d known you were all going away, I should never have had you brought here. In the end I’ll be left all by myself, a lonely ghost.’

He went off to bed in a sulk.

In fact, when Aroma had heard her mother and brother propose buying her back, she had told them that she would never come home, not to her dying day.

‘You sold me because you had nothing to eat,’ she said. ‘I was the only thing left worth a few taels. If I hadn’t let you sell me, was I to stand by and watch my own parents starve? Luckily I was sold here, where I eat and dress like the masters and mistresses, and I’m not beaten in the morning and cursed at night. Father may be dead now, but you’ve put the household and your livelihood in order and got back on your feet. If you were still in difficulties and wanted to buy me back to squeeze a little more money out of me, that would be another matter. But you’re not in difficulties. What do you want to buy me back for now? Think of me as dead, and never entertain the idea again!’

There had been a bout of tears and protest. Her mother and brother saw how determined she was; plainly she would not come out. Besides, she had originally been sold under a deed of outright sale. They had been counting on the Jia family’s kindness and generosity: they need only make a plea, and quite possibly even the purchase money would be waived as a gift.

Moreover, the Jia household had never ill-treated its servants, showing far more kindness than severity. The personal maids who attended its old and young members were treated quite differently even from the other servants. Young ladies in ordinary poor families would not have been accorded so much consideration. Mother and son accordingly gave up the idea of buying her back. Then Bao-yu unexpectedly arrived. Seeing how the two behaved together, mother and son understood still more. Their last fears fell away; here was something beyond their expectations. Each felt reassured, and neither thought any more of buying her release.

Now Aroma had known Bao-yu since childhood and had seen how unusual his disposition was. His mischief, his foolish playfulness, went far beyond that of other children. There were also several quite extraordinary habits she could scarcely bring herself to speak of.

Recently, with his grandmother’s indulgence to rely on, he had become harder even for his parents to restrain. He was growing more unruly and unbridled, following every whim and impulse, and disliked nothing so much as attending to proper business. Aroma had often wanted to advise him, but reckoned he would not listen. Today’s talk of buying her release had given her the chance: first she would use a pretence to sound out his feelings and take down his pride, and then she could lay down some rules. Seeing him go silently to bed, she knew he could not bear to lose her, and that his spirit was subdued.

She had never wanted the chestnuts. Her only fear was that the curd would cause another incident like the trouble over Snowpink’s tea. Chestnuts had been an excuse to divert Bao-yu and let the matter drop. She now told the little maids to take them away and eat them, and went to nudge Bao-yu. His face was covered with tear-stains.

‘What is there to be so miserable about?’ she asked with a smile. ‘If you really want to keep me, of course I won’t go.’

Sensing a condition behind this, Bao-yu said, ‘Then tell me how else I’m to keep you. I can’t think of anything more.’

‘We needn’t talk about how fond we’ve always been of each other,’ Aroma said, smiling.

‘But if you’re really set on keeping me now, that’s not what matters. There are three other things I want to ask of you. If you agree to them, I’ll know you truly mean it, and I won’t leave even with a knife at my throat.’

Bao-yu smiled at once. ‘Tell me. What are they? I’ll agree to anything. Dear sister, my own dear sister, never mind two or three things: I’d agree to two or three hundred. All I ask is that you all stay and watch over me, and keep me company until the day I turn to flying ash. No, not ash: ash still has shape and leaves a trace; it still knows something.

‘Wait until I’ve turned to a wisp of smoke that scatters at a breath of wind. Then you’ll have no hold on me, and I shan’t be able to think of you. Let me go then, and I’ll let you go wherever you please.’

Before he had finished, Aroma hastily clapped a hand over his mouth.

‘There now! That’s just what I wanted to stop you saying, and here you are saying worse.’

‘I won’t say it again,’ Bao-yu promised quickly.

‘That’s the first thing you must change.’

‘Changed! If I say it again, pinch my mouth.

‘What else?’

‘Second, whether you really like studying or only pretend to, don’t keep criticising and sneering at it in front of your father or other people. At least make a show of liking it. That will spare your father some anger and give him something to boast of when he’s with people. He thinks, “Every generation of our family has studied, and now there’s this boy. I never expected him to dislike books.” That’s enough to make him angry and upset already. Then you talk all that nonsense, to people’s faces and behind their backs, calling everyone who studies to get on a “salary-grub”. You say there are no books worth the name beyond “Making Bright Virtue Shine”; the rest were cobbled together by people in earlier times who couldn’t understand the sages’ books and invented notions of their own. When you say things like that, how can you blame your father for being angry and beating you so often? What are other people to think of you?’

‘I won’t say it again,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘That was when I was little and didn’t know how high the sky was or how deep the earth. I said whatever came into my head. I wouldn’t dare say it now.

‘What else?’

‘No more abusing Buddhist monks and slandering Taoist priests, or meddling with rouge and powder. And one thing matters still more: no more eating the rouge from people’s lips, or this mania of yours for red.’

‘I’ll change it all, all of it. What else? Tell me quickly.’

‘Nothing else,’ she said, smiling. ‘Only be a little more careful in everything, instead of following every whim and fancy. If you really do all that, even an eight-bearer sedan-chair won’t carry me out of here.’

‘Stay here long enough,’ Bao-yu said with a smile, ‘and you needn’t fear you’ll have no eight-bearer chair to ride in.’

Aroma gave a cold laugh. ‘That’s nothing I hanker after. Even if I had the good fortune, it wouldn’t be proper. And if I did ride in one, what pleasure would there be in it?’

While they were talking, Ripple came in.

‘It’s almost the third watch. Time for bed. Her Old Ladyship sent a mamma to ask just now, and I said you were asleep.’

Bao-yu asked for the clock. Sure enough, its hand already pointed to the middle of the hai hour. They washed again, undressed and settled for the night. There is no more to tell of that.

Early next morning, Aroma rose feeling heavy all over, with a headache, aching eyes and burning limbs. At first she struggled on, but soon she could no longer bear it and wanted only to sleep. She lay down on the kang without undressing. Bao-yu hurried to tell Grandmother Jia, and a physician was summoned to examine her.

‘Only a slight attack of wind-cold,’ he said. ‘One or two doses to disperse it will put her right.’

After he had written a prescription and gone, the medicine was fetched and boiled. As soon as Aroma had taken it, she was told to cover herself with a quilt and sweat. Bao-yu went off to visit Dai-yu.

Dai-yu was resting on her bed after the midday meal. Her maids had all gone out to attend to their own affairs, and the room was perfectly still. Bao-yu lifted the soft embroidered curtain and went into the inner room. Seeing her asleep, he hurried over and nudged her.

‘Dear cousin, you’ve only just eaten, and now you’re asleep again!’

He woke her up.

Seeing Bao-yu, Dai-yu said, ‘Go out for a stroll. I was up all night the other day, and I still haven’t recovered. I ache all over.’

‘Aches are a small matter. Making yourself ill by sleeping is much worse. I’ll amuse you until the drowsiness passes, and then you’ll be all right.’

With her eyes still shut, Dai-yu said, ‘I’m not drowsy. I only want a little rest. Go and make a nuisance of yourself somewhere else, then come back.’

Bao-yu nudged her. ‘Where am I to go? I get so tired of seeing anyone else.’

Dai-yu gave a little snort of laughter. ‘If you must stay, sit over there and behave yourself, and we’ll talk.’

‘I want to lie down too.’

‘Then lie down.’

‘There’s no pillow. Let’s share yours.’

‘Balls! Aren’t there pillows outside? Go and get one.’

Bao-yu went into the outer room, looked round and came back smiling.

‘I don’t want that one. Who knows which dirty old woman it belongs to?’

Dai-yu opened her eyes and sat up, laughing. ‘You really are the demon star fated to plague me! Kindly use this one.’

She pushed her own pillow towards him, then got up and fetched another of hers for herself. They lay down facing one another.

Dai-yu noticed a blood-red mark the size of a button on Bao-yu’s left cheek. She raised herself, leaned closer and touched it, examining it carefully.

‘Whose fingernail scratched you this time?’

Bao-yu turned aside to evade her, smiling. ‘It’s not a scratch. I was rinsing and straining rouge paste for them just now. I must have rubbed some on myself.’

He began looking for a handkerchief to wipe it off. Dai-yu used her own to clean his cheek.

‘You’ve been at it again! Doing it is one thing, but must you hang out a sign to advertise it? Even if my uncle doesn’t see you, someone else will. They’ll make a fine new story of it and carry tales to curry favour. It will reach my uncle’s ears, and then everyone will be in trouble and there’ll be another fuss.’

Bao-yu heard none of this. A delicate fragrance came from Dai-yu’s sleeve, intoxicating him, making his very bones melt. He caught hold of her sleeve to see what she had concealed inside.

‘Who wears perfume in the winter cold of the tenth month?’ Dai-yu asked, smiling.

‘Then where does this fragrance come from?’

‘I don’t know myself. Perhaps it’s the scent in the chest, and the clothes have picked it up.’

Bao-yu shook his head. ‘I don’t think so. This is an unusual fragrance, nothing like incense cakes or scent-balls or sachets.’

Dai-yu gave a cold laugh. ‘Do you suppose some “arhat” or “perfected immortal” has given me a special fragrance too?

‘Even if I had some marvellous perfume, I haven’t a dear elder or younger brother to gather flowers and blossoms, frost and snow, and prepare it for me. I’ve only ordinary, vulgar scents!’

‘Every time I say one thing,’ Bao-yu said, smiling, ‘you drag in all that. You won’t learn until I give you what you deserve. I’m not sparing you today.’

He rolled over and sat up, breathed on his hands twice, then darted them under Dai-yu’s arms and tickled her sides. She had always been helplessly ticklish. As his hands ran over her sides, she laughed until she could scarcely breathe.

‘Bao-yu! If you go on, I shall be angry.’

Only then did he stop. ‘Will you say that sort of thing again?’

‘I wouldn’t dare.’ Smoothing her hair, she smiled. ‘I have a special fragrance. Have you any “Warm Fragrance”?’

Bao-yu could make nothing of this. ‘What “Warm Fragrance”?’

Dai-yu nodded, sighing and laughing. ‘You fool, you fool!

‘You have jade, and someone else has gold to match you. Someone else has “Cold Fragrance”, so haven’t you any “Warm Fragrance” to make the pair?’

At last Bao-yu understood.

‘You were begging for mercy a moment ago, and now you’re worse than ever,’ he said, smiling, and reached for her again.

‘Dear cousin, I daren’t!’ Dai-yu cried, laughing.

‘I’ll let you off if you let me smell your sleeve.’

He pulled it over his face and kept breathing in the fragrance. Dai-yu snatched her hand away.

‘Now you really must go.’

‘Go? I can’t. Let’s lie here and talk nicely.’

He lay back down. Dai-yu did the same and covered her face with her handkerchief. Bao-yu talked nonsense on and off; she paid no attention. He asked how old she had been when she came to the capital, what scenery and ancient sites she had seen on the journey, what old remains and stories there were in Yangzhou, and what the people and their customs were like. Dai-yu would not answer.

Afraid that sleeping would make her ill, Bao-yu tried to coax her out of it.

‘Oh! There’s a great story about your Yangzhou yamen. Have you heard it?’

He spoke so solemnly, with such a stern, serious face, that Dai-yu took him at his word.

‘What story?’

At this Bao-yu suppressed a smile and began inventing whatever came into his head.

‘There’s a mountain in Yangzhou called Dai Mountain, and on it there’s a cave called Lin’s Woodland Cave.’

‘You’re lying already,’ Dai-yu said, laughing. ‘I’ve never heard of any such mountain.’

‘There are plenty of mountains and rivers in the world. How could you know them all? Wait till I’ve finished before you criticise.’

‘Go on, then.’

Bao-yu continued his invention. ‘Now, a colony of mouse spirits lived in Lin’s Woodland Cave. One year, on the seventh day of the twelfth month, the old mouse took his seat to hold council. “Tomorrow is Laba, the eighth,” he said, “when people everywhere make laba porridge. We’re short of fruit and nuts in our cave. This would be an excellent chance to raid a few supplies.” So he drew a command-arrow and sent a capable young mouse to scout about.

‘Presently the young mouse returned to report. “I’ve searched and made enquiries everywhere. The temple at the foot of the mountain has by far the most fruit and grain.”

‘“How many sorts of grain? How many kinds of fruit?” asked the old mouse.

‘“There are whole granaries of rice and beans, too much to count. There are five kinds of fruit and nuts: first, red dates; second, chestnuts; third, peanuts; fourth, water-chestnuts; fifth, fragrant taro.”

‘The old mouse was delighted and began choosing mice to send at once. He drew a command-arrow. “Who will steal the rice?”

‘One mouse accepted the order and went to steal rice. The old mouse drew another arrow. “Who will steal the beans?” Another mouse accepted that order and went off for beans. One by one, they all took their orders and left. Only the fragrant taro remained. The old mouse drew another arrow and asked, “Who will steal the fragrant taro?”

‘A very tiny, very feeble little mouse piped up, “I’m willing to steal the fragrant taro.”

‘The old mouse and all the others looked at him. They were afraid he lacked experience, besides being timid and weak, and none of them would let him go.

‘“I may be young and small,” the little mouse said, “but my magic has no limits, my tongue is nimble and my schemes are deep. I guarantee I can steal more cleverly than any of them.”

‘“How can you be cleverer than they are?” the other mice asked eagerly.

‘“I shan’t steal outright like them. I’ll shake myself and turn into a piece of fragrant taro, then roll into the pile of taro. No one will see or hear a thing. Secretly I’ll use my magic of dividing into many selves to carry it off, little by little, until the lot is gone. Isn’t that cleverer than simply stealing and grabbing?”

‘“Very clever indeed,” they all said. “But we don’t know how you do this transformation. Change for us first, and let us see.”

‘The little mouse laughed. “Nothing easier. Watch me.”

‘He shook himself and said, “Change!” And there he was, transformed into a most exquisite, beautiful young lady.

‘“Wrong, wrong!” the other mice cried, laughing. “You said you’d turn into something to eat. How have you turned into a young lady?”

‘The little mouse resumed his own shape and laughed. “I told you you hadn’t seen the world. You only know fragrant taro, xiangyu, the food. You don’t know that the daughter of Mr Lin, the Salt Inspector, is the real fragrant jade, xiangyu!”’

Dai-yu rolled over, scrambled up and pinned Bao-yu down, laughing.

‘May your wicked mouth rot! I knew you were making it up about me.’

She pinched him until he begged her again and again, ‘Dear cousin, spare me! I’ll never dare do it again. Your fragrance suddenly reminded me of that old tale.’

‘You insult me, and then call it an old tale!’ Dai-yu said, laughing.

Before she had finished, Bao-chai came in.

‘Who’s talking about old tales?’ she asked with a smile. ‘Let me hear one too.’

Dai-yu quickly invited her to sit down. ‘Who do you think? He insults me, then says it’s an old tale.’

‘Oh, Cousin Bao,’ Bao-chai said, smiling. ‘You can’t blame him. He has a bellyful of old tales and allusions. The pity is that whenever he needs one, he forgets it. If he’d remembered the other night as well as he does today, he should have remembered one for his plantain poem. He couldn’t think of what was right under his nose. Someone else was so cold, and he was in such a fluster that he did nothing but sweat. Now, suddenly, his memory works again.’

‘Amitabha!’ Dai-yu laughed. ‘You really are my dear good sister. So you’ve met your match too! It just shows that every deed gets its return, without the slightest mistake.’

At that very moment, a clamour broke out in Bao-yu’s room. Truly:

A teasing master banters with his maid;

Sisters in accord find joy in kinship.
