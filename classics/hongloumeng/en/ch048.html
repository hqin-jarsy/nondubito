Chapter 48 An indiscriminate lover, thwarted in love, thinks of travelling to learn a trade; A girl who longs for refinement labours over verse among the poets

Chapter end

Only after hearing this did Xue Pan’s anger begin to subside. Three or five days later the pain was gone, but the marks remained. Ashamed to face his friends and relations, he stayed at home pretending to be ill. Before long it was the tenth month. Some of the men from the shops were settling their annual accounts before going home, and the family naturally gave a farewell dinner for them. Among them was Zhang De-hui, a man over sixty who had worked in the Xue family’s pawnshop from his youth and managed its business. He had two or three thousand taels of his own to live on. He too was going home that year and would not return until the following spring.

‘Paper goods and aromatics have been scarce this year,’ he remarked. ‘They’re sure to be dear next year. I’ll send my eldest boy up first to look after the pawnshop. Then, on my way back before the Dragon Boat Festival, I’ll buy some paper goods, incense and fans to sell here. Even after duties and expenses, there should be several times the outlay in profit.’

Xue Pan considered this. ‘Since that beating I can hardly face anyone. I’ve been wanting to hide for six months or a year, but there’s nowhere to go. I can’t keep pretending to be ill for ever. Besides, I’ve grown to this age without learning either letters or arms. They say I’m in business, but I’ve never so much as handled a silver balance or an abacus. I know nothing of places or customs, roads or distances. Why not get a little capital together and spend a year travelling with Zhang De-hui?

‘Whether I make money or not, at least I’ll be out of the way of this disgrace. And seeing some mountains and rivers would be pleasant too.’

Having made up his mind, he spoke to Zhang De-hui after dinner and told him to wait a day or two so that they could leave together.

That evening Xue Pan told his mother. Aunt Xue was pleased, but she was afraid he would get into trouble away from home; losing the capital was the least of her worries. She would not let him go.

‘At least if you stay with me I can feel a little easier. There’s no need for you to do this business. We aren’t waiting for those few hundred taels to live on. Keeping out of mischief at home would be worth more than the money.’

But Xue Pan had decided, and would not listen.

‘Every day you say I know nothing of the world. I don’t understand this, I won’t learn that. Now I’ve made up my mind to give up all those things that don’t matter, become a man, do something and learn business, you won’t let me! What am I supposed to do? I’m not a girl. How long are you going to keep me shut up at home? Besides, Zhang De-hui is a respectable old man. Our families have known each other for generations. What can go wrong if I go with him? Even if I do go astray for a moment, he’ll speak to me and put me right.

‘He knows what things cost and how the market stands. I can ask him about everything. Nothing could be more convenient, yet you won’t let me go. One of these days I’ll pack up secretly and leave without telling anyone. When I come home rich next year, then you’ll see!’

With that, he went off to bed in a sulk.

After hearing him out, Aunt Xue consulted Bao-chai.

‘If he really wants to get some experience of proper business,’ Bao-chai said with a smile, ‘that is a good thing. Of course, he talks very well at home. Once he’s away, his old habits may return, and then it will be harder still to restrain him. But we can’t worry about everything. If he truly changes, it will be a blessing for the rest of his life. If he doesn’t, Mother, there’s nothing else you can do. We must do our part and leave the rest to Heaven. He’s a grown man. If we’re always afraid he doesn’t know the ways of the world and can’t go anywhere or do anything, we can keep him shut up this year, but next year he’ll be just the same. Since what he proposes is perfectly proper, reckon on losing eight hundred or a thousand taels, and let him have a try. There will be the shop men to help him, after all. They would hardly have the face to cheat him. Besides, once he’s away there will be no one to egg him on and no one for him to fall back on. Out there, why should anybody be afraid of anybody else? If he has money, he eats; if he hasn’t, he goes hungry. With nobody to turn to, he may even give less trouble than he does at home.’

Aunt Xue thought for a while.

‘You’re right. If a little money will teach him some sense, it will be well spent.’

With that settled, the night passed without further incident. Next day Aunt Xue sent for Zhang De-hui and had Xue Pan entertain him with food and wine in the study. She herself stood in the rear gallery, speaking through the window and entrusting her son to his care with a thousand injunctions. Zhang De-hui promised everything. After the meal, as he took his leave, he added:

‘The fourteenth is an excellent day for starting a journey. If the young master packs at once and hires the mules, we can set off early that morning.’

Xue Pan was delighted and told his mother. For the next several days Aunt Xue, Bao-chai, Caltrop and two elderly nurses were busy with his travelling things. To go with him they chose an old servant who was his wet-nurse’s husband, two longstanding servants familiar with the world, and the two pages who usually attended him: six people in all, master and servants. Three large carts were hired solely for their luggage and equipment, and four mules for the long journey.

Xue Pan himself was to ride a large iron-grey pacing mule kept by the family, with a saddle-horse in reserve. Once everything was ready, Aunt Xue and Bao-chai spent night after night advising and cautioning him; we need not set down all they said. On the thirteenth he went first to take leave of his uncle, then called on everyone at the Jia mansion. Jia Zhen and the others naturally insisted on farewell entertainments, which need not detain us either. Early on the fourteenth Aunt Xue, Bao-chai and the others went with him right out beyond the ceremonial gate. Mother and daughter watched him go, four eyes full of tears, before turning back.

Aunt Xue had brought only four or five servant families to the capital, with two or three old nurses and young maids. Now that Xue Pan had taken his party away, only one or two men remained in the outer quarters. That very day she went to the study and had all its ornaments, curios, curtains and hangings brought into the inner rooms and put away. The wives of two of the men who had gone were told to sleep in the inner quarters too. She also told Caltrop to put her own room thoroughly in order.

‘Lock the door and come and sleep in my room at night.’

‘Since you have all these people to keep you company, Mother,’ said Bao-chai, ‘why not let Caltrop come and keep me company? It’s so empty in the Garden, and the nights are getting long. I do needlework every evening. Surely another person would make it all the better.’

‘Of course!’ Aunt Xue said, smiling. ‘I’d forgotten. She ought to go with you. I was only saying to your brother the other day that Wen-xing is too young and can’t get anything straight, and Oriole isn’t enough to look after you alone. I meant to buy another maid for you.’

‘You never know what you’re getting when you buy one,’ said Bao-chai. ‘If we make a bad choice, the money is the least of it. We shall only have made trouble for ourselves. Better inquire at our leisure. If we find someone whose background we know, then we can buy her.’

As she spoke, she told Caltrop to pack her bedding and toilet-box, and had an old nurse and Zhen-er take them to Allspice Court. Then she and Caltrop went back into the Garden together.

‘I was going to ask your mother myself,’ said Caltrop, ‘whether I might keep you company while the master was away. But I was afraid she might take it amiss and think I only wanted to play in the Garden. I never expected you to ask!’

‘I know you’ve been longing to be here for more than a day or two,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘You simply haven’t had the leisure. Even when you came every day, you were always in such a hurry that it was no pleasure. So I thought we might take the opportunity and let you stay a whole year. I shall have another companion, and you’ll have your wish.’

‘Dear miss,’ Caltrop said, smiling, ‘while I have the chance, do teach me to write poems.’

‘You’ve won Long and now you want Shu!1’ Bao-chai laughed. ‘On your first day here, I advise you to go out by the eastern side gate and call on everyone, beginning with Grandmother. Pay your respects all round. There’s no need to make a special announcement that you’ve moved into the Garden. If anyone asks, just mention that I brought you in to keep me company. That will do.

‘When you come back, call at each of the girls’ rooms in the Garden too.’

Caltrop agreed and was just setting out when Patience came hurrying up. Caltrop greeted her at once, and Patience had to pause and return the greeting with a smile.

‘I’ve brought her in to keep me company today,’ Bao-chai told Patience, smiling. ‘I was just going to let your mistress know.’

‘Oh, miss, what are you saying?’ Patience laughed. ‘I don’t know how to answer you.’

‘It’s only proper. An inn has its proprietor and a temple its abbot. It’s a small matter, but someone ought to be told. Even the people who keep watch at night should know there are two more here, so that they can attend to the doors and gates. Tell her when you go back, will you? Then I needn’t send anyone.’

Patience agreed, and turned to Caltrop with a smile.

‘Now you’re here, aren’t you going to call on your neighbours?’

‘I was just sending her,’ said Bao-chai.

‘You needn’t come to our place yet,’ Patience told her. ‘The Second Master is ill at home.’

Caltrop assented and left, beginning her round at Grandmother Jia’s. We need say no more of that.

As soon as Caltrop had gone, Patience caught Bao-chai by the arm.

‘Have you heard our news, miss?’

‘No. These last few days I’ve been busy getting my brother off. I know nothing of what’s happened here. I haven’t even seen the girls for the past two days.’

‘The Elder Master’s beaten the Second Master till he can’t move,’ said Patience with a little laugh. ‘Surely you’ve heard that?’

‘I thought I heard something this morning, but I wasn’t sure. I was just going to see your mistress when you came. What was he beaten for this time?’

Patience cursed through clenched teeth.

‘It’s all that Jia Yu-cun, Rain-village or Wind-village or whatever he calls himself! A stray bastard picked up Heaven knows where, and too tough to starve. We haven’t known him ten years, and look at all the trouble he’s made! This spring the Elder Master saw some old fans somewhere. When he came home, all the fine fans in his own collection were no good any more. He immediately had people searching everywhere.

‘And there was this wretched man who didn’t know what was good for him, nicknamed Stony. He was too poor to buy food, but he had twenty old fans, and he’d sooner die than let one of them go outside his door. The Second Master called in no end of favours before he managed to meet him. After a great deal of persuasion the man invited him to his house, sat him down and let him have a quick look at them. The Second Master said they were beyond anything: mottled Xiangfei bamboo, palm bamboo, milu2 and jade bamboo, all with genuine paintings and calligraphy by the old masters. He told the Elder Master, who ordered him to buy them, whatever the price. But that Stony said, “I’ll starve or freeze to death first. I wouldn’t sell them for a thousand taels apiece!” There was nothing the Elder Master could do, so he cursed the Second Master every day for being useless. The man had already been offered five hundred taels, the silver to be weighed out before the fans were handed over. Still he wouldn’t sell. “If you want my fans,” he said, “you must take my life first!” Tell me, miss, what could anyone do?

‘Then that conscienceless Yu-cun heard of it and thought up a scheme. He falsely charged the man with owing money to the government, had him taken to the yamen, and said his property must be sold to make up the arrears. He confiscated the fans, put an official valuation on them and sent them here. Whether Stony is alive or dead now, I don’t know. The Elder Master took the fans and asked the Second Master, “How did he manage to get them, then?” All the Second Master said was, “Ruining a man and his household over such a little thing is no great feat!”

‘That made the Elder Master angry. He said the Second Master was trying to silence him with an insolent answer. That was the main thing. There have been a few smaller things these last few days too, but I can’t remember them clearly. They all came together, and he started beating him. He wasn’t held down and beaten with a board or a stick. He was standing up, and I don’t know what the Elder Master hit him with, just struck out at him anyhow. There are two cuts on his face. We heard your mother had some pills to put on injuries from a beating. Do find me one quickly, miss.’

Bao-chai at once sent Oriole to ask for one and gave it to Patience.

‘In that case, give them my regards. I shan’t go over.’

Patience promised and went away. We may leave her there.

After Caltrop had called on everyone and eaten her supper, Bao-chai and the others went to Grandmother Jia’s. Caltrop herself went to the Naiad’s House. Dai-yu was now very much better and was naturally pleased to hear that Caltrop too had come to live in the Garden.

‘Now that I’m here and have some time to myself,’ Caltrop said with a smile, ‘you must teach me to write poems. It would be such good fortune for me!’

‘If you want to write poems, you must take me as your teacher,’ Dai-yu smiled. ‘I don’t know much, but I can probably teach you the rudiments.’

‘If you mean it, I shall. Only you mustn’t get tired of me!’

‘What’s so difficult that you need to study it? There’s an opening, a development, a turn and a conclusion. The middle sections, development and turn, make two pairs of matching lines. Level tones answer oblique tones, grammatical words answer substantive words, and substantive words grammatical ones. If you really have an extraordinary line, you can even disregard the matching of tones and words.’

‘No wonder!’ Caltrop said, smiling. ‘I often get hold of an old book of poems and steal a little time to read one or two. Some lines match perfectly, and some don’t. Then I’ve heard the rule, “One, three, five may go free; two, four, six must be clear.3” In old poems some keep to it, but others go wrong even at two, four and six. It has puzzled me every day.

‘Now you’ve explained it, I see that these rules of form are only minor things. What matters most is fresh, original wording.’

‘Exactly,’ said Dai-yu. ‘Though wording too is a minor thing in the end. The governing idea comes first.

‘If the feeling and thought are true, it will be good of itself, even without ornament in the words. That is what is meant by “not letting words harm the meaning”.’

‘I love those lines by Lu Fang-weng,’ Caltrop said, smiling. ‘“Thick curtains left unrolled keep fragrance long; / The old inkstone’s slight hollow holds more ink.” There’s such pleasure in them!’

‘You must never take poems like that as your model. Because you don’t yet understand poetry, you like what is obvious and easy. Once you fall into that manner, you’ll never learn your way out. Now listen to me. If you truly want to learn, I have The Collected Works of Wang Mo-jie. Read a hundred of his five-character regulated poems first. Study them carefully until you know them through and through. Then read a hundred or two of old Du’s seven-character regulated poems, and after that a hundred or two of Li Qing-lian’s seven-character quatrains. Once those three have given you a foundation, you can look at Tao Yuan-ming, Ying Yang, Xie, Ruan, Yu, Bao and the others. You’re so intelligent and quick that in less than a year you’ll be an old hand at poetry, never fear!’

‘In that case, dear miss, do get me the book,’ Caltrop said with a smile. ‘Even if I only take it back and read a few poems tonight, that will be something.’

Dai-yu told Nightingale to bring Wang You-cheng’s five-character regulated poems and handed them to Caltrop.

‘The ones with red circles are my selections. Read every one of them.4

‘Ask your mistress about anything you don’t understand. Or ask me when we meet, and I’ll explain it.’

Caltrop took the poems back to Allspice Court. Forgetting everything else, she sat beneath the lamp reading them one by one. Bao-chai told her several times to go to bed, but she would not. Seeing how earnestly she was working, Bao-chai could only let her be.

One day, just as Dai-yu had finished washing and dressing, Caltrop appeared with a beaming smile, returning the book and asking to exchange it for Du’s regulated poems.

‘How many can you remember?’ Dai-yu asked with a smile.

‘I’ve read all the ones marked with red circles.’

‘Have you begun to get a taste of them?’

‘A little, though I don’t know whether I’m right. Let me tell you.’

‘It’s only by discussing and examining them that you can improve,’ Dai-yu smiled. ‘Go on. I’m listening.’

‘It seems to me,’ Caltrop said, smiling, ‘that what is good in poetry is something you can’t put into words, yet when you think about it, it is perfectly true to life. There are things that seem unreasonable, but when you think them over they have both sense and feeling.’

‘There’s something in that. But where did you find it?’

‘In his “On the Frontier”, there’s the couplet: “Across the great desert, solitary smoke is straight; / Above the long river, the setting sun is round.” How can smoke be straight? And of course the sun is round. “Straight” seems unreasonable, and “round” too ordinary. But if I close the book and think, it’s as though I can see the scene. And if I try to find two other words to replace them, I can’t.

‘Then there’s “The sun sets: rivers and lakes are white; / The tide comes: earth and sky are blue-green.” “White” and “blue-green” seem unreasonable too. Yet when I think about it, only those two words can describe it fully. Saying them feels like having an olive weighing thousands of catties in my mouth. And “At the ferry landing, sunset remains; / From the village, solitary smoke rises.” “Remains” and “rises”: how did he ever think of them? The year we came to the capital, we moored one evening. There was nobody on the bank, only a few trees. Some families far away were cooking supper, and the smoke was a deep blue-green, going straight up to join the clouds. When I read those two lines last night, I seemed to be back there again.’

While she was speaking, Bao-yu and Tan-chun arrived and sat down to listen to her discussion of poetry.

‘If that’s how it is,’ Bao-yu said with a smile, ‘you needn’t read any more poems. Understanding doesn’t depend on quantity. From what you’ve said about those two lines, you’ve already found the heart of it.’

‘You like his “solitary smoke rises”,’ said Dai-yu, smiling, ‘but you don’t know that he adapted that line from an earlier poet. Let me show you. This one is even plainer and more effortless.’

She found Tao Yuan-ming’s lines, ‘Dim, the distant village; / Lingering, the smoke above its houses,’ and passed them to Caltrop. Caltrop read them, nodding and sighing in admiration.

‘So “rises” was developed out of those two characters for “lingering”!’ she said with a smile.

Bao-yu laughed aloud.

‘You’ve got it! No more explanations. More study will only muddle you. Start writing. It’s sure to be good.’

‘Tomorrow I’ll send you an invitation to join the society,’ Tan-chun said, smiling.

‘Why tease me, miss? I only envy you all and want to learn for fun.’

‘Who isn’t doing it for fun?’ Tan-chun and Dai-yu both laughed. ‘Do you think we’re serious poets? If we set up as proper poets outside this Garden, people would laugh their teeth out!’

‘That’s giving yourselves up as hopeless,’ said Bao-yu. ‘The other day I was discussing the painting with the gentlemen outside. When they heard we’d formed a poetry society, they begged me to show them our work. I wrote out a few poems for them, and every one of them was sincerely impressed. They’ve copied them out and had them printed.’

‘Is that really true?’ Tan-chun and Dai-yu asked at once.

‘If I’m lying, I’m that parrot on its perch,’ Bao-yu said with a smile.

‘You’re quite impossible!’ they both said. ‘Never mind whether they’re any good as poems. Even if they were, you shouldn’t let our writing circulate outside.’

‘What is there to be afraid of? If the women of earlier times hadn’t let their writing circulate, nobody would know of them now.’

Just then Xi-chun’s maid Picture arrived to fetch him, and Bao-yu left. Caltrop again pressed Dai-yu to exchange the book for Du’s regulated poems, and begged her and Tan-chun:

‘Give me a subject to try my hand at. When I’ve cobbled something together, you can correct it.’

‘The moon was beautiful last night,’ Dai-yu said. ‘I meant to try a poem myself but never managed it. You write one instead. Use whichever rhymes you like from the fourteenth rhyme group, han.’

Delighted, Caltrop took the poems home. She would think hard and write a couple of lines, then, unable to leave Du’s poetry alone, read another two poems. She could think of neither tea nor food, and could neither sit still nor lie at ease.

‘Why make trouble for yourself?’ said Bao-chai. ‘This is all Pin-pin’s doing. I’ll settle with her. You were absent-minded enough already; add this, and you’ll be a complete simpleton.’

‘Dear miss, don’t put me off,’ Caltrop said with a smile.

As she spoke, she finished a poem and showed it first to Bao-chai.

‘This won’t do,’ Bao-chai said, smiling after she had read it. ‘That isn’t the way to go about it. Don’t be shy. Take it to her and see what she says.’

Caltrop took the poem to Dai-yu, who read:

The moon hangs in mid-heaven; cold the night,

Its light so clear and bright, its shape so round.

Poets look often, seeking inspiration;

Lonely wanderers cannot bear to look, for added grief.

Beside the emerald tower a jade mirror hangs;

Beyond the pearl curtain an ice disk is suspended.

On this fine night, what need to burn silver candles?

Its cloudless splendour lights the painted rail.

‘There is an idea,’ Dai-yu said with a smile, ‘but the wording lacks grace. You’ve read too little poetry and are caught in what you have read. Put this one aside and write another. Be bold. Let yourself go.’

Caltrop went back in silence. This time she did not even go indoors, but stayed beneath the trees by the pond, now sitting on a rock in a trance, now squatting to scratch at the earth. People passing by were astonished. Li Wan, Bao-chai, Tan-chun, Bao-yu and the others heard about it and stood some way off on the hillside watching her. She frowned for a while, then smiled to herself.

‘She’ll certainly go mad,’ Bao-chai said, laughing. ‘Last night she kept muttering and mumbling until the fifth watch before she went to sleep. It was light again in less time than it takes to eat a meal. I heard her get up, hurry through her hairdressing and go off to Pin-pin. Then she came back and spent the whole day in a daze. She wrote one poem and it wasn’t good, so now of course she’s writing another.’

‘This is just what they mean by “a place of fine spirit produces people of talent”,’ Bao-yu smiled. ‘Heaven never gives anyone feeling and intelligence for nothing. We’re always lamenting that someone like her should have become so commonplace. Who would have thought this day would come? It shows how just Heaven and earth are.’

‘If you worked as hard as she does,’ Bao-chai said, smiling, ‘what could you fail to learn?’

Bao-yu made no reply. Caltrop was now hurrying eagerly towards Dai-yu’s again.

‘Let’s follow and see whether she’s found something,’ Tan-chun said with a smile.

They all went to the Naiad’s House. Dai-yu was already holding the poem and discussing it with Caltrop. They asked how she had done.

‘It’s certainly a credit to her effort, but it still isn’t good. This one is too contrived. She must write another.’

They asked to see it. It read:

Not silver and not water, cold against the window;

Look how the clear sky shelters its jade disk.

Pale plum blossoms would steep it in their fragrance;

From threadlike willow ribbons dew has just dried.

One might think leftover powder smeared the golden steps,

Or a thin frost had brushed the jade balustrade.

Waking from dreams in the west tower, no one there;

Its lingering face can still be seen beyond the curtain.

‘This scarcely seems a poem on the moon,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘Add “light” after “moon” and it might do. Look: every line is about moonlight. Never mind. Poetry begins in nonsense. Give it a few more days and you’ll improve.’

Caltrop had thought this poem wonderful. Their comments dispirited her, but she would not give up and began thinking again.

While the girls chatted and laughed, she went out to stroll beneath the bamboo in front of the steps, racking her heart and mind. She heard nothing else, saw nothing else. Presently Tan-chun called through the window with a laugh:

‘Miss Caltrop, take a little leisure!’

Caltrop answered in a daze, ‘“Leisure”, xian, belongs to the fifteenth rhyme group, shan. You’ve used the wrong rhyme.’

They all burst out laughing.5

‘She really is possessed by poetry,’ said Bao-chai. ‘And it’s all Pin-pin’s doing!’

‘The Sage says, “Never tire of teaching others”,’ Dai-yu replied, smiling. ‘She came and asked me. How could I refuse to tell her?’

‘Let’s pull her over to Fourth Sister’s,’ Li Wan said with a smile. ‘Get her to look at the painting and bring her back to her senses.’

They did indeed come out and pull her along, past Lotus Pavilion to Warm Fragrance Retreat. Xi-chun was tired and lay on her bed having an afternoon nap. The painting silk stood against the wall, covered with gauze. They woke her and lifted the covering to look. Only three tenths of the painting was done. Caltrop noticed several beautiful women in it and pointed, smiling.

‘This one is my mistress, and that one is Miss Lin.’

‘Everyone who can write poetry is in the picture,’ Tan-chun said with a smile. ‘Hurry up and learn!’

They stayed there joking for a while.

After they had all dispersed, Caltrop’s mind was still full of poetry. That evening she sat staring at the lamp. After the third watch she lay down, but her eyes stayed wide open; only at the fifth watch did she drift off to sleep. When daylight came, Bao-chai woke and listened. Caltrop was sleeping quietly.

‘She’s been tossing about all night,’ she thought. ‘I wonder whether she managed a poem. She’s tired now. I won’t wake her.’

Just then Caltrop laughed in her sleep.

‘Now I have it! Surely this one must be good?’

Bao-chai did not know whether to sigh or laugh. She quickly woke her.

‘What have you found? Such devotion could reach the immortals. Before you learn to write poetry, you’ll have made yourself ill.’

As she spoke, Bao-chai washed and dressed, then joined the other girls to go to Grandmother Jia’s. Caltrop’s determination to learn poetry had gathered all her vital energies into one purpose. What she could not compose by day had suddenly come to her in eight lines in a dream. As soon as she had washed and dressed, she hurried to write them down. Unable to judge whether they were good or bad, she took them off to Dai-yu again.

She had just reached Drenched Blossoms Pavilion when she saw Li Wan and the girls returning from Lady Wang’s. Bao-chai was telling them how Caltrop had composed in her sleep and talked in her dreams. They were laughing when they looked up and saw her. All clamoured to see the poem.

Translator’s notes

  1. Long and Shu are regions: the saying describes wanting another conquest as soon as one has been won. ↩
  2. Milu is retained as the source’s name for a fan material; its precise identity is uncertain. ↩
  3. The numbers refer to character positions in Chinese lines. Level and oblique are traditional tone categories, not English stresses. ↩
  4. Wang Mo-jie and Wang You-cheng are names for Wang Wei; old Du is Du Fu; Li Qing-lian is Li Bai; Lu Fang-weng is Lu You. ↩
  5. Han and shan are the fourteenth and fifteenth groups in the traditional rhyme tables. Xian, “leisure”, belongs to shan: Caltrop has heard the invitation to rest as a suggested rhyme. ↩
