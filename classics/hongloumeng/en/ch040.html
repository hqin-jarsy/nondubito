Chapter 40 Old Lady Shi gives two feasts in Prospect Garden; Faithful Jin calls three tiles in a drinking game

Chapter end

Bao-yu hurried in on hearing this. Amber was standing by the screen.

‘Do go at once. They’re waiting to speak to you.’

He went to the principal rooms and found Grandmother Jia discussing a return party for Xiang-yun with Lady Wang and the girls.

‘I have an idea,’ he said. ‘Since there won’t be any outside guests, why have a set number of dishes? Choose a few of the things each of us likes best and have those made. And instead of laying tables for a banquet, put a tall little table in front of each person, with one or two of their favourite dishes, a compartmented box of assorted things, and a wine pot to pour for themselves. Wouldn’t that make a change?’

Grandmother Jia approved and sent word to the kitchen at once.

‘Tomorrow, just make the things we like best. Pack a box for each person as well. We’ll have breakfast in the garden too.’

While they were still discussing it, the lamps were lit. Nothing more need be said of that night.

To their delight, next morning dawned clear and bright. Li Wan was up before daybreak, supervising the older women and maids as they swept away fallen leaves, wiped tables and chairs, and set out the tea and wine services. Felicity came in with Grannie Liu and Ban-er.

‘How busy you are, Mrs Zhu!’

‘I told you yesterday you wouldn’t get away,’ Li Wan said to Grannie Liu with a smile, ‘but you were in such a hurry to go.’

‘Her Old Ladyship kept me,’ Grannie Liu said, laughing. ‘She said I was to have a day’s fun too.’

Felicity produced several keys of different sizes.

‘Our mistress says there may not be enough tall tables out here. She thought we might open the upper rooms and bring down the ones stored there, just for the day. She ought to have come herself, but she’s talking to Her Ladyship. Would you open up and have them carried down, ma’am?’

Li Wan told Su-yun to take the keys and sent an older woman to fetch some of the pages from the inner gate. Standing below Prospect Tower, she looked up while someone went to open Brocade-adorned Pavilion, and the tables were carried down one by one. Pages, older women and maids all lent a hand. More than twenty were brought down.

‘Take care,’ said Li Wan. ‘Don’t rush about as though devils were after you. Mind you don’t knock the carved edges.’

She turned to Grannie Liu with a smile. ‘Why don’t you go up and have a look too, Grannie?’

Grannie Liu had been longing for the invitation. Taking Ban-er with her, she climbed the stairs and went inside. Screens, tables, chairs and lanterns of every size were stacked there in a dense mass. She did not know much about them, but their colours dazzled her, and each had something wonderful about it. With several invocations of the Buddha, she came back to the others. The doors were locked, and they all went down.

‘In case Her Old Ladyship feels like it,’ said Li Wan, ‘we had better bring down all the paddles, poles, oars and sun-awnings for the boats as well, and have them ready.’

They agreed, opened the rooms again and brought down everything she had named. The pages were told to send for the boatwomen and have two boats brought out of the boathouse.

They were still in the bustle of arranging things when Grandmother Jia came in with a crowd of people. Li Wan hurried to meet her.

‘You’re in good spirits this morning, Grandmother, coming in already! I thought you would still be having your hair done. I’ve just picked some chrysanthemums to send you.’

As she spoke, Bi-yue brought forward a large green-jade platter shaped like a lotus leaf, full of chrysanthemum sprays in different colours. Grandmother Jia chose a large red flower and put it in her hair. Turning, she caught sight of Grannie Liu.

‘Come and have a flower too!’

Before she had finished, Xi-feng caught hold of Grannie Liu.

‘Let me dress you up.’

She stuck the whole platterful of flowers all over her head, this way and that. Grandmother Jia and the others laughed uncontrollably.

‘I wonder what blessings this old head has earned,’ Grannie Liu said, laughing, ‘to be made so grand today.’

‘Why don’t you pull them out and throw them in her face?’ the others laughed. ‘She’s dressed you up like an old goblin.’

‘I may be old now, but I was a lively one when I was young,’ Grannie Liu said. ‘I liked my flowers and powder. Today’s the day for an old beauty to shine.’

Talking and laughing, they reached Drenched Blossoms Pavilion. The maids brought a large brocade cushion and spread it on the bench along the balustrade. Grandmother Jia sat down against a pillar and asked Grannie Liu to sit beside her.

‘Do you like the garden?’

Grannie Liu invoked the Buddha.

‘At New Year, we country folk come into town to buy pictures to put up. When we have a spare moment, we’re always saying, “How could we ever go and walk about inside a picture?” We know it’s only a made-up thing. Where could there be a real place like that? But now I’ve come into your garden and seen it, why, it’s ten times better than the pictures. If only someone would paint this very garden for me to take home and show them! It would do me good even after I’m dead.’

Grandmother Jia pointed to Xi-chun with a smile.

‘Look at my little granddaughter here. She can paint. How would it be if I asked her to paint one for you?’

Grannie Liu was so pleased that she hurried over and took Xi-chun’s hand.

‘My dear young lady, at your age, and with such good looks, to be so clever too! You must be an immortal born into this world.’

After a short rest, Grandmother Jia naturally took Grannie Liu to see the sights. They went first to Naiad’s House. Inside the gate, bamboos stood close on either side of a narrow, winding pebble path, and green moss covered the ground. Grannie Liu left the path free for Grandmother Jia and the others and walked on the earth instead. Amber caught her arm.

‘Come up on the path, Grannie. Mind you don’t slip on that moss.’

‘Never fear. We’re used to walking on such things. You young ladies keep to the path. It would be a pity to dirty those embroidered shoes.’

Talking to them, she paid no attention to her feet, and sure enough, she slipped and came down with a thump. They all clapped their hands and laughed.

‘You little baggages!’ Grandmother Jia scolded, laughing too. ‘Help her up, instead of standing there laughing.’

But Grannie Liu had already scrambled to her feet and was laughing herself.

‘No sooner was the boast out of my mouth than I was slapped for it.’

‘Have you hurt your back?’ Grandmother Jia asked.

‘Let the girls give it a rub.’

‘As if I were such a delicate creature! There isn’t a day I don’t fall down a couple of times. If I had to have it rubbed every time, there’d be no end to it.’

Nightingale had already raised the bamboo blind, and Grandmother Jia and the others went in and sat down. Dai-yu herself brought Grandmother Jia a covered bowl of tea on a small tray.

‘We shan’t have tea,’ Lady Wang said. ‘You needn’t pour any for us, dear.’

Dai-yu told a maid to move the chair she usually sat in by the window to a lower place, and invited Lady Wang to sit there. Grannie Liu noticed the brushes and inkstone on the table beneath the window, and the shelves stacked full of books.

‘This must be one of the young gentlemen’s studies.’

‘It is my granddaughter’s room,’ Grandmother Jia said, pointing to Dai-yu. ‘My daughter’s child.’

Grannie Liu looked Dai-yu carefully up and down.

‘It’s nothing like a young lady’s chamber. It’s better than the finest study.’

‘Where has Bao-yu got to?’ Grandmother Jia asked.

‘On one of the boats in the pool,’ the maids replied.

‘Who has got boats ready?’

‘When we opened the upper rooms for the tables,’ Li Wan said quickly, ‘I thought you might like a ride, so I had them made ready.’

Grandmother Jia was about to answer when someone announced Aunt Xue. They were just rising as she came in. Taking her seat, she said with a smile:

‘You’re in fine spirits today, coming out so early.’

‘I was just saying we must fine anyone who came late,’ Grandmother Jia replied. ‘And here you are, the late one!’

After a little talk and laughter, Grandmother Jia noticed that the window gauze had faded.

‘This gauze looks pretty when it’s first put up,’ she said to Lady Wang, ‘but it loses its fresh green after a time. There are no peach or apricot trees in this courtyard, and the bamboo is green already. More green gauze on the windows is quite the wrong thing. I remember we used to have four or five colours of window gauze. Have hers changed tomorrow.’

‘When I opened the storeroom yesterday,’ Xi-feng put in, ‘I saw quite a few bolts of silvery-pink cicada-wing gauze in the big wooden chests. Some had sprays of flowers on them, some flowing clouds and ten thousand blessings, some a hundred butterflies among flowers. Such fresh colours, and the gauze so light and soft! I’d never seen anything like it. I took out two bolts. I thought they would make two lovely padded gauze quilts.’

‘Pah!’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘Everyone says there is nothing you haven’t handled or seen, and you don’t even know what this gauze is. You’ll be boasting again tomorrow, no doubt.’

Aunt Xue and the others laughed.

‘However much she has handled and seen, how could she presume to compare with you?

‘Do tell her about it, so that we can listen too.’

‘Do teach me, dear Grannie,’ Xi-feng said, laughing.

Grandmother Jia turned with a smile to Aunt Xue and the others.

‘That gauze is older than any of you.

‘No wonder she took it for cicada-wing gauze. It does look a little like it. Anyone who didn’t know would call it that. Its proper name is Soft Mist Gauze.’

‘What a pretty name,’ said Xi-feng. ‘But I’ve grown to this age and seen hundreds of kinds of gauze and silk, and I’ve never heard of it.’

‘And how long have you lived?’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘You’ve seen a few odds and ends that were lying about for want of a place to put them, and now you’re boasting. Soft Mist Gauze comes in only four colours: clear-sky blue after rain, olive-yellow, pine green, and silvery-pink. Make it into bed-curtains or cover window frames with it, and from a distance it looks like mist. That is why it is called Soft Mist Gauze. The silvery-pink is also called Rosy-cloud Gauze. Even the gauze officially supplied to the Palace nowadays is not so soft and substantial, so light and closely woven.’

‘Never mind Xi-feng not having seen it,’ said Aunt Xue with a smile. ‘I’ve never even heard of it.’

While they were talking, Xi-feng had already had a bolt brought in.

‘That is it,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘At first we used it only on window frames. Later we tried making quilts and bed-curtains with it, and it did very well. Find a few bolts tomorrow, and use the silvery-pink on her windows.’

Xi-feng agreed. Everyone looked at it and admired it. Grannie Liu kept peering at it, murmuring prayers to the Buddha.

‘We couldn’t hope to have clothes made of this, and you put it on the windows! Isn’t it a waste?’

‘It doesn’t look well as clothes,’ said Grandmother Jia.

Xi-feng pulled out the front of the padded scarlet gauze jacket she was wearing and showed it to Grandmother Jia and Aunt Xue.

‘Look at my jacket.’

‘That is very fine too,’ they both said. ‘It’s made in the Palace workshops for use at court nowadays, but it still isn’t as good as this.’

‘This thin stuff!’ said Xi-feng. ‘And they call it made in the Palace workshops for use at court. It isn’t even as good as what they used to make for officials.’

‘Look again,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘There may be some blue too.

‘If there is, bring it all out. Give our Cousin Liu two bolts, make a set of bed-curtains for me, and line what is left to make sleeveless jackets for the maids. It will only go mouldy if we leave it in storage.’

Xi-feng readily agreed and had the bolt taken away.

‘This room is small,’ Grandmother Jia said, rising with a smile. ‘Let us go and see somewhere else.’

Grannie Liu invoked the Buddha.

‘They always say great families live in great rooms. Yesterday I saw your principal rooms, with those great chests and cupboards and tables and beds. They certainly are imposing.

‘Those cupboards are bigger and taller than a whole room in our house. No wonder there’s a ladder in the back courtyard. I thought, “They don’t go on to the roof to dry things, so what do they want a ladder for?” Then I realised it must be for putting things in and out of the top cupboards. How else could anyone reach them? And now I see this little room, and it’s even more beautifully arranged than the big ones. Everything in it is so pretty, and I don’t know what any of it is called. The more I look, the less I want to leave.’

‘There are better things still,’ said Xi-feng. ‘I’ll take you to see them all.’

They left Naiad’s House.

In the distance they could see a group of people poling boats on the pool.

‘Since they’ve got the boats ready, let’s have a ride,’ said Grandmother Jia.

They set off towards Amaryllis Eyot and the knotweed shallows. Before they reached the pool, they met several older women carrying large boxes, all alike, decorated in colours, fine wirework and incised gold. Xi-feng asked Lady Wang where breakfast should be laid.

‘Ask Grandmother. Wherever she would like.’

Grandmother Jia turned round.

‘Your third sister’s would be a good place. Take the people there and set it out. We’ll come round by boat.’

Xi-feng went back with Tan-chun, Li Wan, Faithful, Amber and the people carrying the food. Taking a short cut to Autumn Studio, they arranged the tables in its Dawn-green Hall.

‘We’re always saying how the gentlemen outside have a hanger-on to amuse them when they eat and drink,’ Faithful said, laughing. ‘Today we’ve got a female hanger-on of our own.’

Li Wan, who was a kind-hearted person, did not understand her.

Xi-feng knew very well that she meant Grannie Liu.

‘Then we’ll have some fun with her today,’ she said, laughing too.

The two of them put their heads together and made their plans.

‘You never do a good deed,’ Li Wan protested with a smile. ‘You’re not children any more, and you’re still so mischievous. Mind Grandmother doesn’t scold you.’

‘It has nothing to do with you,’ said Faithful, laughing. ‘I’ll take responsibility.’

Grandmother Jia and the others arrived as they spoke, and sat down wherever they pleased. The maids first brought two trays of tea, which everyone drank. Xi-feng, holding a bundle of ebony chopsticks with three silver mounts, wrapped in a Western-cloth napkin, counted the places and laid them out.

‘Bring that little nanmu table over,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Let Cousin Liu sit close beside me.’

It was brought at once. Xi-feng gave Faithful a look, and Faithful took Grannie Liu outside. She whispered a string of instructions to her, ending:

‘That’s the rule in our household. If you get it wrong, we shall laugh at you.’

With everything settled, they returned to their seats. Aunt Xue had eaten before coming, so she sat aside drinking tea. Grandmother Jia had Bao-yu, Xiang-yun, Dai-yu and Bao-chai at her table; Lady Wang sat with Ying-chun and her two sisters; Grannie Liu had a table beside Grandmother Jia. At meals Grandmother Jia usually had little maids standing by with a rinsing bowl, fly-whisk, towels and handkerchiefs.

Faithful no longer did that work, but today she deliberately took the fly-whisk and began to use it. Knowing that she meant to play a trick on Grannie Liu, the maids withdrew and left her to it. As she stood waiting, Faithful whispered to Grannie Liu:

‘Don’t forget.’

‘You can trust me, miss.’

Once seated, Grannie Liu picked up her chopsticks. They were heavy and awkward in her hand. Xi-feng and Faithful had agreed to give her, alone of all the company, an old-fashioned square-sided pair of ivory chopsticks mounted in gold.

‘These rakes are heavier than our iron shovel,’ Grannie Liu said. ‘How am I to wrestle with them?’

Everyone laughed.

A married servant came in and stood in the middle of the room with a food box. A maid lifted the lid. There were two bowls of food inside. Li Wan set one on Grandmother Jia’s table. Xi-feng deliberately chose a bowl of pigeons’ eggs for Grannie Liu.

As Grandmother Jia invited everyone to begin, Grannie Liu stood up and called out:

‘Old Liu, old Liu, eats as much as a cow; Won’t lift her head till she’s eaten a whole old sow.’

Then she puffed out her cheeks and said nothing. At first they stared. Then the whole company, high and low, burst into laughter. Xiang-yun could not contain herself and sprayed out a mouthful of rice. Dai-yu laughed until her breath caught and she lay over the table gasping. Bao-yu rolled into Grandmother Jia’s arms, and she held him, laughing and crying, ‘My darling!’ Lady Wang pointed at Xi-feng, unable to get a word out. Aunt Xue lost control too and sprayed her tea all over Tan-chun’s skirt. Tan-chun tipped her rice bowl on to Ying-chun. Xi-chun left her seat and pulled at her nurse, begging her to rub her aching stomach.

Not one of the attendants could keep from doubling up. Some escaped outside to squat and laugh; others fought down their laughter to come and help the girls change their clothes. Only Xi-feng and Faithful kept straight faces and continued urging Grannie Liu to eat. She picked up her chopsticks, which refused to obey her.

‘Even the chickens here are pretty. And such neat little eggs they lay! Lovely little things. I’ll shove one down.’

They had only just stopped laughing, and now they began again. Tears ran down Grandmother Jia’s face while Amber patted her back.

‘That imp Xi-feng must be behind this,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘Don’t believe what she tells you.’

Grannie Liu was still admiring the little eggs and preparing to shove one down.

‘They’re a tael of silver each,’ Xi-feng told her, smiling. ‘Taste one quickly. They won’t be good once they’re cold.’

Grannie Liu reached out with her chopsticks, but could not get hold of one. After a long struggle all round the bowl, she finally managed to trap one. She had just stretched out her neck to eat it when it slipped away, rolled off the table and fell to the ground. She put down her chopsticks and was about to pick it up herself, but one of the attendants had already retrieved it and taken it away.

‘A tael of silver!’ Grannie Liu sighed. ‘Gone, and it didn’t even make a sound.’

No one had any thought of eating now; they were all watching her and laughing.

‘Why have you brought out those chopsticks?’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘We’re not entertaining guests at a grand banquet. This is all Xi-feng’s doing. Change them at once.’

The attendants had not prepared the ivory chopsticks: Xi-feng and Faithful had brought them. At Grandmother Jia’s words they hurried to take them away and substituted a silver-mounted ebony pair like everyone else’s.

‘Gold ones gone, silver ones come,’ said Grannie Liu. ‘Neither sits in the hand like ours.’

‘If there’s poison in the food,’ Xi-feng said, ‘the silver will detect it when you put them in.’

‘If there’s poison in this food, ours must be pure arsenic.

‘I’d eat every bit of it, even if it killed me.’

Amused by her and pleased with her appetite, Grandmother Jia had all her own dishes brought over for Grannie Liu to eat too. She also called an old nurse and told her to put some of each dish into Ban-er’s bowl.

When the meal was over, Grandmother Jia and the others went into Tan-chun’s bedroom to chat. The tables were cleared and another meal was laid out. Watching Li Wan and Xi-feng sit down opposite one another to eat, Grannie Liu sighed.

‘Never mind anything else, it’s the way you do things here that I admire. No wonder they say good manners come from great houses.’

‘You mustn’t take it to heart,’ Xi-feng said quickly, smiling. ‘We were only having a little fun just now.’

Before she had finished, Faithful came in.

‘Don’t be angry, Grannie,’ she said with a smile. ‘I owe you an apology.’

‘What are you saying, miss? We were coaxing Her Old Ladyship into a good laugh. What was there to be angry about? When you gave me my instructions, I understood at once. It was only to give everyone a bit of fun. If I’d been angry inside, I wouldn’t have said it.’

Faithful scolded the servants. ‘Why hasn’t anyone poured Grannie some tea?’

‘That woman brought me some just now,’ Grannie Liu said hastily. ‘I’ve had it. You ought to have your meal too, miss.’

Xi-feng caught hold of Faithful.

‘Sit down and eat with us. It will save them making a fuss again later.’

Faithful sat down.

The older women brought another bowl and pair of chopsticks, and the three finished their meal.

‘You all eat such a little bit, and then you’ve done,’ Grannie Liu said, smiling. ‘I wonder you don’t go hungry. No wonder a puff of wind would blow you over.’

‘There’s plenty left today,’ Faithful asked. ‘Where has it all gone?’

‘It hasn’t been shared out yet,’ the women told her. ‘We’re keeping it here to give them all their portions together.’

‘They can’t eat all this. Choose two dishes and send them to Patience in Mrs Lian’s rooms.’

‘She’s had her meal already,’ said Xi-feng. ‘There’s no need.’

‘Then she can feed your cat with them.’

The women quickly chose two dishes, put them in a box and sent them off.

‘Where has Su-yun gone?’ Faithful asked.

‘They’re all eating here together,’ said Li Wan. ‘What do you want her for now?’

‘That’s all right, then.’

‘Aroma isn’t here,’ Xi-feng said. ‘You might have a couple of dishes sent to her.’

Faithful did so, then asked the older women, ‘Have the compartmented boxes been filled for the drinking party later?’

‘They’ll probably be a little while yet.’

‘Hurry them up.’

The women agreed.

Xi-feng and the others went into Tan-chun’s room, where the older and younger women were talking and laughing. Tan-chun liked space and openness: the three bays of her room had never been partitioned. A large rosewood table with a marble top stood in the middle, piled with copybooks of famous calligraphers’ work, dozens of fine inkstones and brush pots of every kind. The brushes in the large brush holder stood thick as a wood. To one side was a Ru-ware flower vase as big as a dou grain measure, filled with white ‘Crystal Ball’ chrysanthemums. In the middle of the west wall hung a large painting, Misty Rain, by Mi Xiang-yang, flanked by a pair of scrolls in the hand of Yan, Duke of Lu:

A spirit at ease in mist and rosy clouds;

Springs and rocks, a life in the wilds.

A large tripod vessel stood on the table. To the left, on a red sandalwood stand, a great dish of Daguan ware held dozens of large, delicately formed, tender-yellow finger citrons. To the right, a white-jade chime carved as paired fish hung from a stand in foreign lacquer, with a little mallet beside it. Ban-er, becoming rather more at home, reached for the mallet to strike it, but the maids hurried to stop him. Then he wanted to eat a finger citron. Tan-chun chose one for him.

‘You can play with it,’ she said, ‘but you can’t eat it.’

Her couch was on the east. The bed, raised on a platform, had spring-green gauze curtains embroidered on both sides with flowers and insects. Ban-er ran over to look.

‘Here’s a bush-cricket! Here’s a grasshopper!’

Grannie Liu slapped him.

‘You nasty little wretch, making a filthy nuisance of yourself! We let you in to look, and now you’re getting above yourself.’

Ban-er began to cry, and only when everyone intervened did she stop. Grandmother Jia looked for a while through the gauze window into the back courtyard.

‘The parasol trees under the eaves of the back veranda have come on well too. Only they’re a little thin.’

As they spoke, a gust of wind brought the faint sound of drums and music.

‘Whose wedding is that?’ Grandmother Jia asked. ‘We must be quite close to the street here.’

‘We couldn’t hear anything from the street,’ Lady Wang and the others replied, smiling. ‘It’s our dozen or so girls practising their music.’

‘If they’re practising, why not have them in here?’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘They can have an outing too, and we shall enjoy it.’

Xi-feng sent for them at once and ordered long tables to be set out and covered with red felt.

‘Set them up out over the water at Lotus Pavilion,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘It will sound better over the water. Later we’ll have our wine below Brocade-adorned Pavilion. There’s plenty of room, and it’s near enough to hear.’

Everyone agreed it was a good place. Grandmother Jia smiled at Aunt Xue.

‘Let us go. These girls don’t much like people sitting about in their rooms. They’re afraid we shall make them dirty. We mustn’t be so lacking in tact. Let’s go for our boat ride, then have our wine.’

They all rose to leave.

‘What a thing to say!’ Tan-chun protested, laughing. ‘We can hardly get you and Aunt to come and sit with us, however much we beg.’

‘My third girl is very good,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘It’s only the two Jades who are such nuisances.

‘When we’ve had too much wine, we’ll go and make a disturbance in their rooms on purpose.’

They all laughed and went out together. A short walk brought them to Leafy Islet, where the boatwomen chosen from Suzhou had already brought up two tang-wood pleasure boats. Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang, Aunt Xue, Grannie Liu, Faithful and Silver were helped into the first, with Li Wan following. Xi-feng got in too and stood in the bow, determined to try her hand at poling.

‘This is no game,’ Grandmother Jia called from the cabin. ‘It may not be a river, but it’s deep enough. Come in here at once.’

‘What is there to be afraid of? Don’t worry, Grannie.’

With one push of the pole, Xi-feng sent the boat away from the bank. Out in the middle of the pool, with so many people in the little boat, Xi-feng felt it rocking alarmingly. She hastily handed the pole to a boatwoman and crouched down. Ying-chun, her sisters and the others, with Bao-yu, boarded the second boat and followed. The remaining nurses and maids walked along the bank beside them.

‘Those ragged lotus leaves are hateful,’ Bao-yu said. ‘Why haven’t they sent someone to pull them out?’

‘These last few days we haven’t given the garden a moment’s peace,’ Bao-chai said, smiling. ‘We’re out here every day. When would there be time to send anyone to tidy it?’

‘I dislike Li Yi-shan’s poetry more than anyone’s,’ Dai-yu said. ‘The only line I like is this: “Leave the withered lotus; listen to the rain.”

‘And you won’t leave even that.’

‘It is a fine line,’ Bao-yu said. ‘We won’t have them pulled out in future.’

They had reached the vine-shaded creek below Flowering Inlets. Its gloom struck cold to the bone, and the withered grass and dying water-caltrops on both banks deepened the feeling of autumn.

Grandmother Jia noticed a spacious, airy building on the bank.

‘Isn’t that Miss Xue’s place?’

‘Yes,’ they told her.

She had the boat brought to shore, and they went up the cloud-stepping stone stairway into Allspice Court. An unusual fragrance met them. The strange plants and fairy-like vines grew greener as the weather grew colder. They were all in fruit, hung with lovely clusters like little coral beads. But inside, the rooms were like a cave of snow, without a single ornament. On the table stood only a coarse Ding-ware vase containing a few sprays of chrysanthemums, two books, a tea caddy and tea cups. Plain blue gauze curtains hung round the bed, and the quilt and bedding were very simple.

‘The child is far too unassuming,’ Grandmother Jia sighed. ‘If you had no ornaments, why didn’t you ask your aunt for some? I haven’t attended to it either; it never occurred to me. Of course, you must have left your own things at home and not brought them with you.’

She told Faithful to fetch some antiques, then scolded Xi-feng.

‘You might have sent your cousin a few pretty things. How mean of you!’

‘She doesn’t want them,’ Lady Wang and Xi-feng protested, smiling. ‘We did send some, and she sent them all back.’

‘She doesn’t bother much with such things at home either,’ Aunt Xue said with a smile.

Grandmother Jia shook her head.

‘It won’t do. She may save herself some trouble, but suppose a relation calls: it wouldn’t look right. Besides, it’s unlucky for young girls to have such bare rooms. Old women like us ought to be living in the stables, then! Listen to the descriptions of young ladies’ chambers in books and plays. You can’t imagine how exquisite they are. Our girls may not presume to rival those young ladies, but they needn’t fall so far below the proper standard. When the things are there already, why not put them out? If she really likes simplicity, a few less would do. I used to be the best of anyone at arranging a room. Now I’m old, I haven’t the leisure or the inclination. These girls are learning to do it quite well. Vulgarity is the danger: even fine things can be spoilt by the way you set them out. I don’t think theirs are vulgar. Now let me arrange yours for you. I promise it will be both handsome and simple.

‘There are two things among my private possessions that I’ve kept all this time without letting Bao-yu see them. If he’d set eyes on them, they would have been gone.’

She called Faithful over and gave her instructions herself.

‘The miniature rock landscape, the little gauze table-screen, and that tripod of smoky-black translucent stone: those three on this table will be enough. Bring the white damask bed-curtains with the ink paintings and calligraphy too, and change these curtains.’

Faithful agreed, then said with a smile, ‘Those things are in one of the chests upstairs in the east building, goodness knows which. We shall have to take our time looking. Perhaps we could bring them tomorrow.’

‘Tomorrow or the day after will do. Only don’t forget.’

After sitting a little longer, Grandmother Jia came out, and they went straight to the space below Brocade-adorned Pavilion.

Wen-guan and the others came to pay their respects and asked what pieces they should rehearse.

‘Choose a few you’re not so familiar with,’ Grandmother Jia told them.

They withdrew to Lotus Pavilion, and we shall leave them there. Meanwhile Xi-feng and her helpers had finished setting everything out. At the upper end stood a couch on either side, each spread with brocade cushions and furong bamboo mats, with two carved-lacquer tables before it. There were tables shaped like begonia blossoms, plum blossoms, lotus leaves and mallow flowers, square ones and round ones, in a variety of shapes. On one table stood an incense burner, a vase and a compartmented food box; the other was left clear for the dishes each person liked. The two upper couches and their four tables were for Grandmother Jia and Aunt Xue. Below them, Lady Wang had a chair and two tables; everyone else had a chair and one table.

Grannie Liu sat to the east, with Lady Wang below her. On the west sat Xiang-yun, then Bao-chai, then Dai-yu, followed in order by Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun, with Bao-yu last. Li Wan’s and Xi-feng’s tables stood inside the third set of rails, outside the second gauze enclosure. The compartmented boxes matched the shapes of the tables.

Each person had a blackened-silver wine pot with Western chasing, to pour for themselves, and an enamel cup with assorted designs. Once everyone was seated, Grandmother Jia said with a smile:

‘Let’s have a couple of cups first. We ought to play a drinking game today. It will be more fun.’

‘You will know some good games, of course,’ Aunt Xue and the others replied, laughing. ‘How could we keep up? You mean to get us all drunk. Let us just drink a couple of extra cups and have done.’

‘You’ve become modest today too,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘You must be tired of me now I’m old.’

‘It isn’t modesty,’ said Aunt Xue. ‘I’m afraid I won’t be able to answer, and then I shall look a fool.’

‘If you can’t answer, just have an extra cup and go to sleep when you’re drunk,’ Lady Wang said quickly, smiling. ‘Who would dare laugh at us?’

Aunt Xue nodded and laughed. ‘Very well, I submit to the rules. But you must drink a cup to start the game, ma’am.’

‘Of course,’ Grandmother Jia said, and drank one.

Xi-feng hurried forward into the middle.

‘If we’re to have a game, wouldn’t it be better to ask Faithful to conduct it?’

They all knew that Grandmother Jia’s games needed Faithful to prompt her, so they agreed at once.

Xi-feng brought Faithful over.

‘If she is in the game, there can be no question of her standing,’ said Lady Wang.

She turned to a little maid. ‘Bring a chair and put it with your two mistresses.’

Faithful half protested, half accepted, thanked them for the seat and sat down. After drinking a cup of wine herself, she said with a smile:

‘The rules of a drinking game are as binding as military orders. High or low, it makes no difference. I alone am in command. Anyone who disobeys me will be punished.’

‘Certainly,’ Lady Wang and the others said, laughing. ‘Tell us quickly.’

Before Faithful could begin, Grannie Liu got up and waved her hands.

‘Don’t play such tricks on me. I’m going home.’

‘That won’t do at all,’ they all laughed.

‘Bring her back to her place!’ Faithful ordered the little maids.

Laughing too, the girls did pull her back into her seat.

‘Spare me!’ Grannie Liu kept crying.

‘Anyone who says another word will be fined a whole pot.’

Grannie Liu fell silent.

‘Now I shall call out hands of dominoes,’ said Faithful. ‘We’ll begin with Her Old Ladyship and go round in order, ending with Grannie Liu. For each hand I shall separate the three tiles, call the first one, then the second, then the third. After that I shall give the name they make together. You must answer each call with a suitable line, whether from a poem, a song, a rhapsody, a saying or a proverb, and it must rhyme. Make a mistake and you drink a cup.’

‘That’s a good game,’ they said. ‘Begin.’1

‘Here is a hand,’ said Faithful. ‘On the left, a Heaven.’

Grandmother Jia replied:

‘Above our heads is blue heaven.

’‘Good!’ they all said.

‘In the middle, a Five and Six,’ Faithful called.

Grandmother Jia replied:

‘At the Six Bridges, plum-blossom scent sinks to the bone.

’‘One tile remains, a Six and One,’ said Faithful.

Grandmother Jia replied:

‘A round red sun rises out of the clouds.

’‘Together they make a Tousle-headed Ghost,’ said Faithful.

Grandmother Jia replied:

‘This ghost clings to Zhong Kui’s leg.

’‘Excellent!’ they all laughed.

Grandmother Jia drank a cup.

‘Here is another hand,’ said Faithful. ‘On the left, a Great Long Five.’

Aunt Xue replied:2

‘Each plum blossom dances before the wind.

’

‘On the right, another Great Five Long,’ said Faithful.

Aunt Xue replied:

‘In the tenth month, plum blossom scents the ridge.

’‘In the middle, Two and Five make a Mixed Seven,’ said Faithful.

Aunt Xue replied:

‘Weaver Girl and Cowherd meet on the seventh night.

’

‘Together, Erlang Tours the Five Sacred Peaks,’ said Faithful.

Aunt Xue replied:

‘No mortal has such happiness as the immortals.

’They praised her answers, and she drank.

‘Here is a hand,’ said Faithful. ‘On the left, a Double One: two bright dots.’

Xiang-yun replied:

‘Sun and moon hang together, shining on heaven and earth.

’‘On the right, a Double One: two bright dots,’ said Faithful.

Xiang-yun replied:

‘Idle flowers fall to earth without a sound.

’‘In the middle we need a One and Four,’ said Faithful.

Xiang-yun replied, ‘Beside the sun, red apricot blossoms grow against the clouds.’

‘Together, Nine Ripe Cherries,’ said Faithful.

Xiang-yun replied:

‘Carried from the imperial garden in a bird’s beak.

’She drank a cup.

‘Here is a hand,’ said Faithful. ‘On the left, a Long Three.’

Bao-chai replied:

‘Swallows in pairs chatter among the beams.

’‘On the right, a Three Long,’ said Faithful.

Bao-chai replied:

‘Waterweed trails in the wind, a long green ribbon.

’‘In the middle, Three and Six: nine dots,’ said Faithful.

Bao-chai replied:

‘The Three Hills half sink beyond the blue sky.

’‘Together, A Lone Boat in Iron Chains,’ said Faithful.

Bao-chai replied:

‘Everywhere wind and waves, everywhere sorrow.

’She finished and drank.

‘On the left, a Heaven,’ said Faithful.

Dai-yu replied:

‘In this fine hour, before this lovely scene, what can I do?3

’Bao-chai turned to look at her. Dai-yu was too concerned about being fined to notice.

‘In the middle, a Brocade Screen, prettily coloured,’ said Faithful.

Dai-yu replied:

‘At the gauze window, no Hong-niang brings me news.

’‘There remains a Two and Six: eight dots together,’ said Faithful.

Dai-yu replied:

‘Side by side they gaze towards the jade throne, guiding the rites of court.

’‘Together, a Basket, good for gathering flowers,’ said Faithful.

Dai-yu replied:

‘An immortal’s staff bears fragrant peonies.

’Dai-yu took a sip.

‘On the left, Four and Five make a Flower Nine,’ said Faithful.

Ying-chun replied:

‘Peach blossoms rich with rain.

’‘A forfeit!’ they cried. ‘The rhyme is wrong, and it doesn’t fit either.’

Ying-chun laughed and took a sip. Xi-feng and Faithful were eager to hear Grannie Liu say something funny, so they deliberately made everyone get their answers wrong and fined them all. When Lady Wang’s turn came, Faithful supplied an answer for her. Then it was Grannie Liu’s turn.

‘We farming people often get a few neighbours together for this when we’ve a little leisure,’ she said. ‘Only we don’t make it sound so fine.

‘Still, I’ll have to try.’

‘It’s easy,’ they all said, laughing. ‘Just say whatever you like. It doesn’t matter.’

‘On the left, Four and Four make a Man,’ Faithful called with a smile.

Grannie Liu thought for a long while, then said:

‘A countryman, I suppose.

’The whole room laughed.

‘Very good,’ said Grandmother Jia, smiling. ‘That’s just how to do it.’

‘We country people have nothing but what comes naturally,’ Grannie Liu said, laughing herself. ‘Don’t laugh at me, all of you.’

‘In the middle, Three and Four: green with red,’ said Faithful.

Grannie Liu replied:

‘A big fire burnt a hairy caterpillar.

’‘That’s true enough!’ they laughed. ‘Keep on as you are.’

‘On the right, a One and Four, very pretty,’ said Faithful.

Grannie Liu replied:

‘One radish and a head of garlic.

’They laughed again.

‘Together they make A Spray of Flowers,’ said Faithful, smiling.

Grannie Liu held out both hands to show them, and said:

‘The flower falls and a big pumpkin grows.
