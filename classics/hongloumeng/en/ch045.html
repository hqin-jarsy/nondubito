Chapter 45 Kindred hearts open to each other in words of friendship; On an evening of wind and rain, sorrow makes verses of wind and rain

Chapter end

Xi-feng was still comforting Patience when the girls came in. She hurriedly offered them seats, and Patience poured the tea.

‘What a full gathering!’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Anyone would think I’d sent out invitations.’

‘We’ve come on two errands,’ Tan-chun said with a smile. ‘One for me and one for Fourth Sister, with a message from Grandmother thrown in.’

‘What can be so important?’

‘We’ve started a poetry society, and at the very first meeting someone was missing. None of us can bring ourselves to be stern, so it’s all got out of hand. I think we must have you for Censor of the Society, to be perfectly impartial and show no mercy. Then Fourth Sister needs all sorts of things for painting the garden. We told Grandmother, and she said, “There may still be some left from years ago in the rooms beneath the rear storey. Look and see. If there are, bring them out; if not, send someone to buy them.”’

‘I can’t make verses, wet or dry,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Do you want me there to do the eating?’1

‘You needn’t write any, whether you can or not,’ said Tan-chun.

‘Just keep an eye on us, and punish anyone who shirks or takes things easy as you think fit.’

‘Don’t try to take me in. I’ve guessed,’ said Xi-feng, laughing. ‘Censor of the Society, indeed! What you want is a copper merchant to keep the cash coming in. Whatever society you’ve started, you must be taking turns to entertain. Your allowances won’t stretch to it, so you’ve hit on this way of dragging me in and asking me for money. Isn’t that the plan?’

This made them all laugh.

‘Really, you’re made of glass, with a heart of crystal!’ said Li Wan.

‘And you call yourself our elder sister-in-law!’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘The girls were put in your charge to study, learn their manners and do their sewing. If they misbehave, you’re supposed to put them right. Now they start a poetry society, which can’t cost much, and you wash your hands of them? Grandmother and Lady Wang are different: they’re the old ladies of the family. You have ten taels a month, twice what we get. Then Grandmother and Lady Wang say, “Poor thing, she’s a widow with no livelihood, and a boy to bring up. That won’t be enough,” and add another full ten taels, putting you on the same footing as themselves. You’ve been given garden land too, with your own rents to collect, and when the yearly allowances are divided at New Year, yours is in the very highest class. You and your son, masters and servants all told, don’t make ten people, and your food and clothes still come out of the common household funds. Add it all up and you must have four or five hundred taels a year.

‘Even if you spent a hundred or two each year to let them enjoy themselves, how many years could it last? Once they’ve all married, surely you won’t still be paying for them? But no, you’re afraid of spending your money, so you put them up to pestering me. Well, I shall be delighted to eat your rivers and seas dry, and pretend I know nothing about it!’

‘Just listen!’ Li Wan said, laughing. ‘One word from me, and off she goes, with two cartloads of penny-pinching market talk, such as you’d hear from some muddy-legged rogue, forever weighing and reckoning to the last fraction. To think that this creature was born a young lady in a great, learned family of high officials, and married into another just as good, yet still behaves like this! If she’d been born a boy in some poor little household, there’s no saying what low tricks and cheap, nasty talk we’d have had. You’ve calculated the whole world into your pocket! And yesterday you actually hit Patience. How could you raise your hand to her? Did you pour all that yellow swill into a dog’s belly? I was so angry, I wanted to come and stand up for Patience. I thought it over for ages. It was your birthday, your precious “day a dog’s tail grows a tip”, which comes round so seldom, and I was afraid of upsetting Grandmother. That was the only reason I didn’t come. I’m still angry, and here you are provoking me again! You’re not fit to pick up Patience’s shoes. You two ought to change places.’

Everyone laughed.

‘So it’s neither poetry nor painting you’ve come about,’ Xi-feng hastily answered, laughing. ‘That fierce face is all to avenge Patience. I never dreamt she had a champion like you. If I’d known, I wouldn’t have struck her even if a devil had dragged my hand up to do it. Miss Patience, come here! Let me apologise to you before our elder sister-in-law and all the girls. Forgive me for disgracing myself in drink.’

They all laughed again.

‘Well, Patience?’ Li Wan asked her, smiling. ‘I told you I wouldn’t rest until I’d stood up for you.’

‘You have, ma’am,’ Patience said with a smile, ‘but I can’t bear all you ladies making fun of me.’

‘Why shouldn’t you bear it? You have me. Quick, get the keys and make your mistress unlock the storerooms and look for those things.’

‘Be a dear sister-in-law and take them back to the garden for now,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘I was just going to reckon up the rice accounts when Lady Xing sent for me. I don’t know what she wants this time, but I shall have to go over. And I haven’t yet arranged to have your extra clothes made for the New Year.’

‘I’m not concerned with any of that,’ said Li Wan. ‘Finish my business first, so that I can go and rest without these young ladies worrying me.’

‘Do spare me a moment, dear sister-in-law!’ Xi-feng said quickly, with a laugh. ‘You’ve always been so kind to me. Why should you stop just because of Patience? You used to tell me that however much I had to do, I must look after myself and snatch a rest whenever I could. Now you’re trying to drive me to my death. Besides, it wouldn’t matter if anyone else’s New Year clothes were late, but if theirs were, you’d be responsible. Wouldn’t Grandmother blame you for not concerning yourself with it, when a single word from you was all it needed?

‘I’d rather be blamed myself. How could I let you suffer on my account?’

‘Listen to that!’ Li Wan said, laughing. ‘Isn’t it beautifully put? What a tongue she has! Now tell me, will you take our poetry society in hand or won’t you?’

‘What a question!’ said Xi-feng. ‘If I don’t join and spend a little money, I shall be a rebel against Prospect Garden. How could I expect to get another meal here? I’ll take office first thing tomorrow, dismount and pay my respects to the official seal, and put down fifty taels to begin with. You can take your time spending it on the society’s gatherings. Then, a few days later, you’ll find I write neither poems nor essays and am no more than a common creature after all. Censor or no Censor, once you’ve got the money you’ll turn me out!’

They all laughed again.

‘I’ll open the storerooms in a little while,’ Xi-feng went on, ‘and have everything of the sort brought out for you to look over. Keep whatever you can use. If anything’s missing, I’ll send someone to buy it from your list.

‘I’ll cut out the painting silk straight away. As for the plan, Lady Wang hasn’t got it: it’s still over at Cousin Zhen’s. I’m telling you so you won’t get sent away empty-handed. I’ll have it fetched, and send it out with the silk to the painters, for them to size the silk with alum. Will that do?’

Li Wan nodded, smiling.

‘We shall be obliged to you. If you really do all that, it will do very well. Come, then, let’s go home. We can come back and pester her if she doesn’t send it.’

She rose to leave with the girls.

‘There’s only one person behind all this,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Bao-yu!’

Li Wan turned back with a laugh.

‘We came about him, and I’d quite forgotten! He was the one who missed the first meeting. We’re all too soft-hearted. How do you think we ought to punish him?’

Xi-feng considered.

‘There’s only one thing for it. Make him sweep the floor in every one of your rooms.’

‘That’s a good idea!’ they all said, laughing.

They were just going when a little maid came in supporting Nanny Lai. Xi-feng and the others hurriedly stood up.

‘Sit down, Nanny!’

They all congratulated her. Nanny Lai sat on the edge of the kang and smiled.

‘It’s a happy thing for me, and for my masters and mistresses too. Where would our good fortune have come from without your kindness? Yesterday you sent young Cai with more gifts, ma’am. My grandson kowtowed at the gate towards your rooms to thank you.’

‘When does he leave to take up his post?’ Li Wan asked with a smile.

Nanny Lai sighed.

‘What have I to do with their arrangements? Let them see to it! He came and kowtowed to me at home the other day, and I had no soft words for him. “My boy,” I said, “don’t think being an official entitles you to bully people and ride roughshod over them. You’re thirty this year. Though you were born a servant, the masters were kind enough to give you your freedom the moment you left your mother’s womb. With their good fortune sheltering you above and your parents supporting you below, you’ve learnt your letters and read your books just like a young gentleman. Maids, serving-women and nurses have carried you up to this age as though they were cradling a phoenix.

‘“You don’t even know how to write the two characters for ‘servant’! You know only how to enjoy yourself, not what your grandfather and father suffered. It took two or three generations of endurance to produce a creature like you. All those illnesses and troubles you had as a child: the money spent on you would have made a silver boy your size. When you were twenty, our masters were kind enough to let you purchase official standing. Think of all the sons of respectable families with proper claims to it who are going hungry! You’re a servant’s offspring. Take care you don’t use up your good fortune. Now you’ve enjoyed ten years of ease, and heaven knows what strings you’ve pulled, but you’ve begged the masters to help and got yourself selected for a post. A district or county magistrate may be a minor official, but his responsibilities are great. The magistrate of a district is father and mother to all its people. Unless you behave yourself, do your duty to your country and honour your masters, heaven itself may refuse to tolerate you.”’

‘You worry too much,’ Li Wan and Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘He seems quite all right to us.

‘He used to come in once or twice in earlier years, but we haven’t seen him for several years now, only his name on the lists at New Year and birthdays. The other day he came to pay his respects to Grandmother and Lady Wang, and we saw him in Grandmother’s courtyard in his new official dress. He looked quite imposing, and he’s filled out too. Now that he’s got a post, you ought to be enjoying yourself, not worrying over all this. If he misbehaves, he still has his father to deal with him. All you need do is take your ease. Whenever you’re free, get into your chair and come over for a day’s cards and a good talk with Grandmother. Who would have the face to deny you anything?

‘And at home you have your fine upper rooms and halls. Everyone treats you with respect. You’re just like an old lady of rank yourself.’

Patience brought her tea. Nanny Lai hastily stood to take it.

‘You could have asked any of the children to pour it, miss. You’re making me accept more honour than I’m fit for.’

She drank, then went on:

‘You don’t know, ma’am.

‘These young people have to be kept under strict control. Strict as we are, they still find chances to get into mischief and make their elders worry. People who know them say they’re naughty children. Those who don’t say they’re using their money and influence to bully others, and even our masters’ name suffers. It makes me so angry I don’t know what to do. I often send for his father and scold him soundly. Then things improve a little.’

She pointed at Bao-yu.

‘Don’t be offended with me, but nowadays your father only keeps you a little in order, and Grandmother is there in front of you, shielding you. Who didn’t see the beatings your father got from your grandfather when he was a boy? He never was as fearless of heaven and earth as you are. Nor was your elder uncle: naughty as he was, he was never such a confirmed little troublemaker as you. He was beaten every day too. And your Cousin Zhen’s grandfather at the Ning mansion, his temper was like oil poured on a fire! Once he was angry, he forgot all about its being his own son. You’d have thought he was trying a thief. From what I see and hear nowadays, Master Zhen does discipline his son rather as the old master used to, but there’s no rhyme or reason to it.

‘He doesn’t keep himself in order. No wonder his younger brothers and nephews aren’t afraid of him! If you understand what I mean, you’ll be glad I’ve spoken. If not, you won’t like to say anything, but heaven knows what names you’ll be calling me in your heart!’

While she was speaking, Lai Da’s wife arrived. Zhou Rui’s wife and Zhang Cai’s wife followed with business to report.

‘The daughter-in-law has come to fetch her mother-in-law,’ Xi-feng said, smiling.

‘Not to fetch her, ma’am. I’ve come to find out whether you ladies and young ladies will honour us.’

‘Dear me, I’m getting foolish!’ Nanny Lai said with a laugh. ‘Instead of saying what I came to say, I’ve been turning over all this stale grain and rotten sesame, the same old stories. As our boy has been selected for a post, our relations and friends want to congratulate him, so we must give a feast at home. I thought, if we gave just one day’s entertainment, whoever we invited, we’d be leaving someone out. Then I thought again: this is glory we never dreamt of, all through our masters’ good fortune. I’d be willing to spend every penny we had on it. So I told his father to give three days’ feasting. On the first day we’ll lay a few tables and have a play in our poor little garden, and invite Her Old Ladyship, Their Ladyships and all the ladies and girls to while away a day with us. There’ll be another play and a few tables in the outer hall, where the masters and young masters can honour us with their presence. On the second day we’ll invite our relations and friends, and on the third our fellow servants from both mansions.

‘Three days of merrymaking, to share a little reflected glory from our masters’ good fortune.’

‘What date is it?’ Li Wan and Xi-feng asked, laughing. ‘We’ll certainly come. Grandmother may even feel like coming too.’

‘We’ve chosen the fourteenth,’ Lai Da’s wife hastened to say. ‘Please come for the sake of our old mistress.’

‘I can’t answer for anyone else, but I shall certainly be there,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Only let me warn you, I’m bringing no present, and I don’t know how to give tips. I shall eat and go, so don’t laugh at me.’

‘What are you saying, ma’am?’ Lai Da’s wife laughed. ‘If you want to give us something, twenty or thirty thousand taels will do.’

‘I’ve just invited Her Old Ladyship,’ Nanny Lai said with a smile, ‘and she says she’ll come too. My old face still counts for something.’

She repeated her invitation before rising to go. Catching sight of Zhou Rui’s wife, she remembered something.

‘There’s one other thing I meant to ask you, ma’am. What has Mrs Zhou’s boy done wrong, for you to dismiss him?’

‘That’s just what I meant to tell your daughter-in-law,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘There’s so much to do that I forgot.

‘Mrs Lai, tell your husband when you get home that neither mansion is to take that boy in. He can go and fend for himself.’

Lai Da’s wife had to assent. Zhou Rui’s wife hurriedly knelt and begged for mercy.

‘Tell me what happened,’ Nanny Lai put in. ‘Let me judge.’

‘On my birthday the other day, before we’d had any wine inside, that boy was drunk already,’ said Xi-feng. ‘My mother’s household sent presents, and instead of attending to things outside, he sat there abusing people and didn’t even send the gifts in. Only when the two women had come inside did he bring them in with the young pages. The pages managed perfectly well, but he dropped the box he was carrying and scattered steamed buns all over the courtyard. When the visitors had gone, I sent Sunshine to reprimand him, and he gave Sunshine a mouthful of abuse. What should I keep a lawless little bastard like that for?’

‘And I was wondering what terrible thing it could be!’ Nanny Lai said, laughing. ‘So that’s all. Listen to me, ma’am. If he’s done wrong, beat him, scold him, make him mend his ways. But you certainly mustn’t turn him out.

‘He isn’t like one of our own household-born servants. He belongs to the servants Lady Wang brought with her when she married. If you dismiss him out of hand, it will reflect badly on Her Ladyship. I say you should teach him a lesson with a few strokes of the board, as a warning for next time, and keep him on. If not for his mother’s sake, then for Her Ladyship’s.’

‘Very well,’ Xi-feng said to Lai Da’s wife. ‘Have him given forty strokes, and forbid him to drink from now on.’

Lai Da’s wife assented. Zhou Rui’s wife kowtowed and rose, then would have kowtowed to Nanny Lai as well, but Lai Da’s wife caught hold of her and stopped her. The three of them went away, and Li Wan and the others returned to the garden.

By evening Xi-feng did indeed have a quantity of old painting equipment brought out of storage and sent into the garden. Bao-chai and the others sorted through it, but only half the things were fit for use. They made a fresh list of the other half and sent it to Xi-feng to have replacements bought. We need not dwell on this.

One day the silk came back from outside, sized with alum and with the preliminary drawing begun. Bao-yu now went to Xi-chun’s every day to help. Tan-chun, Li Wan, Ying-chun and Bao-chai often sat there too, both to watch the painting and as a convenient way of meeting.

With the weather turning cool and the nights growing longer, Bao-chai went to her mother’s rooms to discuss getting some sewing ready. During the day she paid her respects to Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang twice, and would naturally stay a little while to keep them company as they wished. She also had to find time for a chat with the girls in the garden. There was little leisure by day, so every night she worked at her needle by lamplight and did not go to bed until the third watch.

Every year after the Spring and Autumn Equinoxes Dai-yu suffered a bout of coughing. This autumn, with Grandmother Jia in such good spirits, she had gone on a couple of extra outings and tired herself too much. Her cough had returned over the last few days, and seemed worse than usual. She no longer went out, but stayed in her room to nurse herself. Sometimes she grew lonely and wished one of the girls would come and talk to her to pass the time. Yet when Bao-chai or the others came to visit, scarcely a few words had been exchanged before she felt weary of them.

They all made allowances for her illness, and knew that she was naturally frail and could bear very little distress. If she was inattentive to her guests or careless of courtesies, no one judged her harshly.

Bao-chai came to see her that day, and they began to speak of her illness.

‘The physicians who come here are all quite good,’ said Bao-chai, ‘but their medicine never seems to do you any good. Wouldn’t it be better to ask someone more skilful to see you and get you well? To go through this every spring and summer, when you’re neither old nor a little child: what sort of life is it? It can’t go on for ever.’

‘It’s no use,’ said Dai-yu. ‘I know this illness of mine will never get better. Never mind when I’m ill: you have only to look at me on my good days to see that.’

Bao-chai nodded.

‘That’s true enough. The ancients said, “Those who eat grain live.” Yet what you eat never seems to build up your strength, your qi or your blood. That isn’t good either.’

‘“Life and death are ordained; wealth and rank rest with Heaven,”’ Dai-yu said with a sigh. ‘We can’t force these things by our own efforts.

‘This year it seems worse than in former years.’

She had already coughed two or three times while they were talking.

‘I looked at your prescription yesterday,’ said Bao-chai. ‘There seems to be too much ginseng and cinnamon bark. They may replenish qi and strengthen the spirit, but they mustn’t be too heating. I should say the first thing is to calm the liver and strengthen the stomach. Once the liver fire is calmed, it can’t overcome earth; with the stomach’s qi sound, food will be able to nourish you. Every morning, take one tael of the best bird’s nests and five qian of rock sugar, and simmer them into a congee in a small silver saucepan. If you get into the habit of eating that, it will do more than medicine. There’s nothing better for nourishing yin and replenishing qi.’

‘You’ve always been very kind to people,’ Dai-yu said with a sigh, ‘but I’m so suspicious that I thought you were hiding something wicked in your heart. The other day, when you said I shouldn’t read those miscellaneous books and gave me such good advice, I was deeply grateful. I was wrong about you before, and I’ve gone on being wrong all this time. When I think of it, my mother died so early, and I have no brothers or sisters. I’m fifteen this year, yet no one has ever spoken to guide me as you did the other day. No wonder Yun says you’re good. I used to feel quite put out when she praised you, but yesterday I found out for myself. Suppose you’d been the one to say those words: I’d never have let you off so lightly. You took no offence at all, and even advised me as you did. That shows how wrong I was. If I hadn’t seen it the other day, I’d never be telling you this now. About the bird’s-nest congee you suggested: bird’s nests are easy enough to get, but I’m always unwell, and this illness comes back every year without anything very urgent about it.

‘Calling doctors, boiling medicine, ginseng and cinnamon bark: it’s already turned the place upside down. Now if I dream up some new business about cooking bird’s-nest congee, Grandmother, Lady Wang and Cousin Feng may not object, but the women and maids below them are bound to think I’m making too much trouble. Look at the people here. Because Grandmother is especially fond of Bao-yu and Feng, they watch even those two like tigers and gossip behind their backs. What must it be like for me? Besides, I’m not really one of their mistresses. I only came here because I had no one and nowhere else to turn to, and they already resent me enough. Why should I give them more reason to curse me by not knowing my place?’

‘By that reckoning, I’m in the same position,’ said Bao-chai.

‘How can you compare yourself to me? You have your mother and brother. Your family has business interests and land here, and still has a house and land at home. You’re simply staying here rent-free because you’re relations. In everything else, great or small, you don’t cost them a single cash. You can leave whenever you choose. I have nothing at all. Food, clothes, everything I use, down to a blade of grass or a scrap of paper, comes to me just as it does to the daughters of this house. How could those petty people help resenting it?’

‘In the end it can only cost them an extra dowry,’ Bao-chai said, smiling. ‘There’s no need to worry about that yet.’

Dai-yu blushed in spite of herself.

‘Here I am treating you as a serious person and telling you my troubles,’ she said with a laugh, ‘and you make fun of me!’

‘It’s a joke, but it’s true too. Don’t worry. As long as I’m here, I’ll keep you company.

‘If anything hurts or worries you, tell me. Whatever I can put right for you, I will, for as long as I can. I may have a brother, but you know what he’s like. It’s only in having a mother that I’m a little better off than you. We can sympathise with each other’s troubles. You’re an intelligent girl: why give way to “Sima Niu’s lament”? Still, what you said just now is quite right. Better to save trouble than make more. I’ll go home tomorrow and speak to Mother. I think we still have some bird’s nests, and I can send you a few taels. Then your maids can cook them each day, quite easily, without mobilising the whole household.’2

‘It’s not the gift that matters,’ Dai-yu said quickly, smiling. ‘It’s your kindness.’

‘That’s hardly worth mentioning. My only fear is that I fail to give everyone the attention I should. I’m afraid you must be tired of me now. I’ll go.’

‘Come back this evening and talk to me a little.’

Bao-chai promised and left. We may leave her there.

Dai-yu drank a mouthful or two of thin congee and lay back on her bed. Before the sun had set, the weather unexpectedly changed and rain began to patter down. The lingering autumn rain came softly, with no certainty of cloud or clearing. Evening drew on, dark and heavy beneath the clouds, and the drops striking the bamboo tips made it lonelier still. Knowing that Bao-chai would not be able to come, Dai-yu picked up a book at random by the lamp. It was Miscellaneous Yuefu Pieces, containing poems such as ‘Autumn Chamber Lament’ and ‘Lament for Parting’. She found herself moved, and could not keep her feelings from finding words. She composed a piece ‘In the Manner of a Parting Song’, following the pattern of ‘A Moonlit Night of Flowers on the Spring River’, and gave it the title ‘An Evening of Wind and Rain at the Autumn Window’. It ran:

Autumn flowers are dim, autumn grasses yellow; The autumn lamp burns wakeful through the long autumn night.

At the autumn window, autumn seems already endless; How bear the wind and rain that deepen desolation?

How swiftly wind and rain come to autumn’s aid! At the autumn window they shatter an autumn dream still green.

Holding autumn’s sorrow, I cannot bear to sleep; I move the weeping candle towards the autumn screen.

The weeping candle flickers, burning on its low stand, Drawing out grief, lighting sorrow, stirring thoughts of parting.

Whose autumn courtyard does no wind enter? At what autumn window is no rain heard?

The gauze quilt cannot withstand the autumn wind; The waning water-clock’s drip spurs on the autumn rain.

Night after night, soft rain and shivering gusts Seem to weep beside the lamp with one who is parted.

Cold mist makes the small courtyard lonelier still; By sparse bamboo and empty window, drops fall now and then.

Who knows when wind and rain will cease? Already falling tears have wet the gauze window.

Having recited her poem, she laid down her brush and was about to go to bed when a maid announced:

‘Master Bao is here.’

Before the words were out, there was Bao-yu, wearing a great bamboo-leaf hat and a woven rain cape.

‘Where has this old fisherman come from?’ Dai-yu laughed despite herself.

‘Are you better today?’ he asked at once. ‘Have you had your medicine?

‘How much have you eaten all day?’

As he spoke, he took off his hat and rain cape. Then he quickly lifted the lamp in one hand, shading its light with the other, and held it towards Dai-yu’s face. He narrowed his eyes and looked closely.

‘Your colour is a little better today,’ he said with a smile.

With the cape off, Dai-yu saw that he was wearing only a somewhat worn short jacket of red damask, tied with a green sash. Below his knees showed lustrous deep-green silk trousers scattered with flowers; beneath these were stockings of spun yarn, embroidered all over and ornamented with gold thread. On his feet he had slippers with butterflies and falling flowers.3

‘You’re afraid of rain on top, but not on those shoes and stockings below?’ she asked. ‘They’re remarkably clean.’

‘My outfit is complete,’ Bao-yu said with a laugh. ‘There’s a pair of tang-wood clogs too. I wore them over here and took them off under the eaves.’

Dai-yu looked again at the cape and hat. Unlike the ordinary sort sold in the shops, they were exquisitely made, light and delicate.

‘What sort of grass are they woven from?’ she asked. ‘No wonder you don’t look like a hedgehog in them.’

‘The Prince of Beijing gave me all three,’ said Bao-yu. ‘When he has nothing to do at home on a rainy day, he wears these too. If you like them, I’ll get a set for you.

‘The others are nothing special, but this hat is interesting. It comes apart. The crown here is removable. When it snows in winter and you have a cap on, you pull out the bamboo pin and take the crown off, leaving just the ring. Both men and women can wear it in the snow. I’ll give you one to wear when it snows this winter.’

‘I don’t want one,’ Dai-yu said, laughing.

‘If I wore it, I’d look like one of those fishermen’s wives in pictures or on the stage.’

Only after the words were out did she realise how little she had considered them. Set beside what she had just called Bao-yu, they were beyond recalling. Her face flushed scarlet with embarrassment, and she bent over the table, coughing without a pause. Bao-yu, however, had not noticed. Seeing the poem on the desk, he picked it up and read it through, then could not help exclaiming over it. Dai-yu heard him, hastily rose, snatched it from him and burnt it at the lamp.

‘I’ve already learnt it by heart,’ he said, smiling. ‘Burning it won’t matter.’

‘I’m much better now,’ said Dai-yu. ‘Thank you for coming several times a day to see me, even in the rain. It’s late, and I want to rest. Please go back for now and come again tomorrow.’

Bao-yu reached inside his clothes and brought out a gold watch the size of a walnut. He glanced at it: the hand showed that it was about the turn from the hour of the Dog to the hour of the Pig. He quickly put it away.

‘You certainly ought to be resting. I’ve kept you occupied all this time again.’

He put on his cape and hat and went out, then turned and came back.

‘If there’s anything you fancy eating, tell me. I can ask Grandmother first thing tomorrow. Won’t I explain it better than the old women?’

‘If I think of anything in the night, I’ll tell you tomorrow morning,’ Dai-yu said with a smile.

‘Listen, the rain’s getting heavier. Do hurry back. Is anyone with you?’

Two older women answered:

‘Yes, miss. There are umbrellas and a lighted lantern outside.’

‘A lighted lantern in this weather?’ Dai-yu said, smiling.

‘It’s all right,’ Bao-yu told her. ‘It has translucent panels. Rain won’t hurt it.’

Dai-yu reached back to the bookshelf and took down a globe-shaped glass lantern. She had a small candle lit in it and handed it to Bao-yu.

‘This is brighter than that one. It’s just the thing for rain.’

‘I have one like it,’ said Bao-yu. ‘I didn’t bring it because I was afraid they’d slip and fall and break it.’

‘Which is worth more, a lantern falling or a person falling? And you’re not used to walking in wooden clogs.

‘Let them carry their lantern in front. This one is light and bright, made for carrying yourself in the rain. Why don’t you take it in your own hand? You can send it back tomorrow. Even if you dropped it, it wouldn’t be such a great loss. What’s suddenly given you this taste for “cutting open your belly to hide a pearl”?’

Bao-yu hastily took it. The two older women went ahead with umbrellas and the translucent-panel lantern; two young maids followed behind with umbrellas. Bao-yu handed his lantern to one of the little maids to carry, rested a hand on her shoulder and went off with them.

Just then a woman from Allspice Court arrived, also carrying an umbrella and a lantern. She brought a large bundle of the best bird’s nests and a packet of fine white imported sugar in plum-petal flakes like snow.

‘These are better than the ones you can buy,’ she said. ‘Miss Bao-chai says you’re to use these to begin with, and she’ll send more when they’re finished.’

‘Tell her I’m obliged to her for taking such trouble,’ Dai-yu said, and asked the woman to sit outside and have some tea.

‘I won’t have tea, miss,’ the woman said with a smile. ‘I’ve something else to do.’

‘I know how busy you are,’ said Dai-yu, smiling. ‘Now the weather’s cooler and the nights are longer, all the more reason to get an evening party together and have a good long gamble.’

‘To tell you the truth, miss, I’ve done very well this year,’ the woman said with a laugh. ‘There are always a few of us on night duty in the different places, and it wouldn’t do to miss a watch. Better to get together for an evening’s play: we keep the watch and pass the time too. Tonight it’s my turn to host. Now the garden gates are shut, it’s time to begin.’

‘How good of you to come through the rain, when it keeps you from making your fortune!’ Dai-yu said with a laugh.

She told someone to give the woman a few hundred cash to buy wine and keep out the damp.

‘Now I’ve put you to the expense of treating me to wine as well, miss,’ said the woman, smiling.

She kowtowed, collected the money outside and went away under her umbrella.

Nightingale put away the bird’s nests, moved the lamp, lowered the curtain and helped Dai-yu to bed. On her pillow, Dai-yu thought gratefully of Bao-chai, then envied her for having a mother and brother. She thought too that, close as she and Bao-yu had always been, their intimacy was still open to suspicion. Outside the window the rain pattered on bamboo tips and withered leaves. A chill stole through the curtains, and tears fell again before she knew it. Only towards the end of the fourth watch did she gradually fall asleep. We may leave her there for the moment.
