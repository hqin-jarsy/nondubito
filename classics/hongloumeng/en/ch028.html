Chapter 28 Jiang Yu-han gives Qianxiang silk gauze in affection; Xue Bao-chai wears red musk-scented beads in shy confusion

Chapter end

Now Lin Dai-yu had wrongly blamed Bao-yu for Skybright’s refusal to open the door the night before. The next day happened to be the day of farewell to the flowers. Her unspent anger was joined by sorrow at the passing of spring, and she had gathered some withered flowers and fallen petals to bury. Their fate reminded her irresistibly of her own. After weeping a little, she began to recite whatever lines came to her lips. She had no idea that Bao-yu was listening on the hillside. At first he merely nodded and sighed; but when he heard such lines as “Today I bury flowers, and people call me foolish; In years to come, who knows who will bury me?” and “One day spring ends and beauty has grown old; Flowers fall, people die, neither knowing of the other!”, he collapsed on the slope in an agony of grief, scattering the fallen flowers from the front of his robe. To think that Dai-yu, with her flower-like face and moon-like beauty, would one day be nowhere to be found: how could his heart not break? And if Dai-yu must one day vanish beyond all finding, so must Bao-chai, Caltrop, Aroma and the others. When Bao-chai and the rest could no longer be found, where would he himself be?

If he could not even tell where he would be, or where he would go, whose would this place be then, this garden, these flowers and willows? One thought led to another, and that to a third; he followed them round and round, until he scarcely knew what senseless thing he wished he might become, without a vestige of awareness, beyond creation’s reach and free of the world’s net, if only he could be released from this sorrow. As the lines say: Flower-shadows never leave his side; Birdsong sounds on either hand.

Dai-yu was still grieving when she suddenly heard sobbing on the slope above her. “Everyone laughs at me for being a little foolish,” she thought. “Can there be another fool here?” She looked up and saw Bao-yu.

‘Pah! I wondered who it was. So it’s that heartless wretch who’ll die before his time…’

No sooner had she said ‘die before his time’ than she covered her mouth, gave a long sigh, turned and left.

Bao-yu wept for a while longer. Then, looking up and finding Dai-yu gone, he knew she must have seen him and slipped away. He too felt dispirited. Getting up and shaking the earth from his clothes, he went down the hill to find the path back to the House of Green Delights. As chance would have it, Dai-yu was walking just ahead. He hurried after her.

‘Wait a moment. I know you won’t speak to me. I only want to say one thing, and after that I’ll leave you alone.’

Dai-yu looked back. Seeing who it was, she would have ignored him, but there was something behind that ‘one thing’ and ‘leave you alone’. She could not help stopping.

‘If you have one thing to say, please say it.’

‘Suppose it’s two things?’ Bao-yu asked with a smile. ‘Will you listen?’

At that Dai-yu turned and walked away.

‘If it was to come to this,’ he sighed behind her, ‘why did we ever begin?’

She could not help stopping again.

‘Begin what? Come to what?’

‘When you first came here,’ he said with a sigh, ‘wasn’t I the one who played with you and made you laugh?

‘Whatever I treasured most, you only had to want it and it was yours. If I heard that you liked something I liked to eat, I’d put it carefully away, quite untouched, and keep it for you. We ate at one table and slept in one bed. I thought of things for you that the maids never thought of, for fear you might be upset. I told myself that when cousins grow up together, whether they’re fond of each other or close to each other, what makes them nearer than other people is staying on good terms to the very end. Who could have imagined that as you grew bigger, your heart would grow too grand to notice me? As for outsiders like Cousin Bao-chai and Cousin Xi-feng,

‘you hold them in your heart, while going three days without a word to me, four without seeing me. I haven’t a brother or sister of my very own. There are two, of course, but you know they have a different mother. I’m an only child, just as you are, and I thought perhaps you felt as I did. But it seems I’ve troubled myself for nothing, and now I have no one to hear my grievance!’

Tears began to fall as he spoke. Hearing his words and seeing him like this, Dai-yu felt more than half her anger die away. She too shed tears, looking down without a word. Seeing her so, Bao-yu went on:

‘I know I’m no good now. But however bad I am, I would never knowingly do anything wrong to you. If I did go a little wrong, you could explain it to me and warn me not to do it again. You could scold me, or hit me a couple of times. I wouldn’t lose heart. Instead, you simply won’t speak to me. I can’t make any sense of it. I’m quite beside myself and don’t know what to do.

‘Even if I died, I’d be a wronged ghost. No prayers of the holiest Buddhist or Taoist could deliver me. You would still have to explain the reason before I could be born again!’

At these words, last night’s affair seemed to slip far from Dai-yu’s mind.

‘If that’s how you feel, why wouldn’t you let the maids open the door when I came yesterday?’

‘Where did you get that idea?’ Bao-yu asked in astonishment. ‘If I ever did such a thing, may I drop dead this instant!’

‘Pah! Talking about death first thing in the morning, without a thought for ill luck! If you did, you did; if you didn’t, you didn’t. Why swear an oath?’

‘I truly never saw you come. Cousin Bao-chai sat for a little while, and then she left.’

Dai-yu thought a moment, then smiled.

‘Of course. Your maids were probably too lazy to stir. They can be surly enough.’

‘That must be it. When I go back I’ll find out who it was and give them a good talking-to.’

‘Those young ladies of yours could certainly do with one, though I suppose it isn’t my place to say so.

‘Offending me today is a small matter. But suppose tomorrow they offend Miss Bao, or some Miss Bei, some other precious treasure of yours: wouldn’t that be serious?’

She smiled with her lips pressed together. Bao-yu could only grit his teeth and laugh.1

While they were talking, a maid came to call them to eat, and they both went to the front. Seeing Dai-yu, Lady Wang asked:

‘My dear, has Doctor Bao’s medicine done you any good?’

‘It’s much the same. Grandmother still wants me to take Doctor Wang’s medicine.’

‘You don’t understand, Mother,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Cousin Lin’s trouble is internal. She was born delicate, so she can’t stand the least wind or cold. A couple of doses of a decoction will put that right. Once the wind and cold have been driven out, pills are better.’

‘The doctor mentioned some pills the other day,’ said Lady Wang, ‘but I’ve forgotten the name.’

‘I know what those pills are. He only wants her to take ginseng pills to nourish the blood.’

‘No, not those.’

‘Eight-Treasure Mother-Benefiting Pills? Left-Restoring? Right-Restoring? Or else Maiwei Dihuang Pills?’

‘None of those,’ said Lady Wang. ‘All I remember is that there was a “Vajra” in it somewhere.’

Bao-yu spread his hands and laughed.

‘I’ve never heard of any Vajra Pills. If there are Vajra Pills, there must be Bodhisattva Powder too!’

Everyone in the room laughed.

‘Perhaps it was Heavenly King Heart-Nourishing Pills,’ Bao-chai suggested with a small smile.

‘That was the name,’ Lady Wang said, laughing. ‘How muddled I’ve become!’

‘You’re not muddled, Mother. It’s all those Vajras and Bodhisattvas ordering you about that have muddled you.’

‘Making fun of your own mother, you shameless thing! You’re asking for another beating from your father.’

‘My father would never beat me for that,’ said Bao-yu, laughing.

‘Well, if those are the pills,’ Lady Wang continued, ‘we’ll send out for some tomorrow.’

‘None of them are any use,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Give me three hundred and sixty taels, Mother, and I’ll have a batch of pills made up for Cousin Lin. I guarantee she’ll be better before she’s finished them.’

‘Poppycock!

‘What medicine could cost so much?’

‘I mean it,’ said Bao-yu, laughing. ‘My prescription is different from other people’s. The ingredients have such strange names that I can’t explain them all at once. Human placenta from a first pregnancy, human-shaped ginseng with its leaves: three hundred and sixty taels wouldn’t cover those alone. He-shou-wu as big as a tortoise, the core of fuling from the roots of a thousand-year-old pine, things like that don’t even count as rarities. They’re just among the ordinary ingredients. As for the chief ingredient, you’d get a fright if I told you. Cousin Xue begged me for a year or two before I let him have the prescription. Then he spent another two or three years collecting the ingredients, and over a thousand taels, before he could have it made up. If you don’t believe me, ask Cousin Bao-chai.’

Bao-chai shook her hand at him, laughing.

‘I know nothing about it. I’ve never even heard of it. Don’t make Aunt ask me.’

‘That’s our Bao-chai,’ said Lady Wang with a smile. ‘A good child who doesn’t tell lies.’

Bao-yu, who had been standing in the middle of the room, spun round and clapped his hands.

‘But I’m telling the truth, and you say I’m lying!’

As he spoke, he turned again and saw Dai-yu sitting behind Bao-chai, suppressing a smile and drawing a finger down her cheek to shame him.

Xi-feng had been in the inner room watching the servants set up the table. Hearing this, she came out with a smile.

‘Cousin Bao isn’t lying. There is such a thing. The other day Cousin Xue came to me himself to ask for pearls. I asked what he wanted them for, and he said he was having medicine made up. He was grumbling too: if only he’d never started! Who could have known it would be such a business? I asked what medicine it was, and he said it was Cousin Bao’s prescription. He named any number of ingredients, but I had no time to listen. He said he could have bought a few pearls himself, only these had to have been worn on someone’s head, so he’d come to me. “If you haven’t any loose ones, cousin,” he said, “ones on a flower ornament will do. Take them off, and I’ll choose some good ones and have them threaded for you afterwards.” There was nothing for it: I took two pearl flowers apart there and then and gave him the pearls. He also took a piece of scarlet gauze for imperial use, three chi long, to sift the powder after grinding it in a mortar.’

At every sentence Bao-yu invoked the Buddha.

‘Here’s the sun shining inside the house!’

When Xi-feng had finished, he went on:

‘You see, Mother, even that was only making do.

‘Strictly according to the prescription, the pearls and precious stones ought to come from an ancient tomb: you need the ornaments rich people were buried in long ago. But who could go digging up graves for that nowadays? So ornaments worn by living people can be used instead.’

‘Amitabha!’ said Lady Wang. ‘How easy it is to squander money when you don’t keep house! Even if there were such things in a tomb, the people have been dead for hundreds of years. Digging up bodies and stealing bones now, no medicine made of that would do any good!’

‘Did you hear?’ Bao-yu said to Dai-yu. ‘Surely Cousin Xi-feng isn’t joining me in telling lies?’

His face was turned to Dai-yu, but his eyes glanced at Bao-chai.

Dai-yu caught Lady Wang’s arm.

‘Listen to him, Aunt. Cousin Bao-chai won’t back up his lie, so he takes it out on me.’

‘You do bully your cousin, Bao-yu,’ Lady Wang agreed.

‘You don’t understand, Mother,’ said Bao-yu with a smile. ‘Even when Cousin Bao-chai was living at home, she didn’t know about Cousin Xue’s affairs. Now that she’s living in the garden, naturally she knows still less.

‘Cousin Lin was making faces at me behind my back just now. She thought I was lying.’

At that moment a maid came from Grandmother Jia’s to fetch Bao-yu and Dai-yu to eat. Without calling Bao-yu, Dai-yu rose, took the maid by the hand and made to leave. The maid suggested waiting for him.

‘He isn’t eating. Let’s go. I’m going on ahead.’

And out she went.

‘I’ll eat with you today, Mother,’ said Bao-yu.

‘No, no. I’m keeping a vegetarian fast today. Go and have your own meal.’

‘I’ll keep the fast with you.’

Telling the maid to go, he ran ahead and sat down at the table.

‘You girls go on with your meal,’ Lady Wang said to Bao-chai and the others, smiling. ‘Let him be.’

‘Do go,’ said Bao-chai with a smile. ‘Whether you eat there or not, at least walk over with Miss Lin. She’s very upset.’

‘Never mind her. She’ll be all right in a little while.’

When he had finished eating, however, Bao-yu was afraid Grandmother Jia might be worrying about him. He was also worrying about Dai-yu himself. He hurriedly called for tea to rinse his mouth.

Tan-chun and Xi-chun laughed.

‘Second Brother, what keeps you in such a hurry all day? Even eating and drinking tea have to be done in a rush.’

‘Let him finish quickly and go to Cousin Lin,’ said Bao-chai with a smile. ‘What good is he doing, hanging about here?’

Bao-yu drank his tea and went out, making straight for the western courtyard. As he passed Xi-feng’s gate he saw her with one foot planted on the threshold, picking her teeth with an ear-scoop and watching ten or so pages move flowerpots.

‘Just the person I want,’ she called with a smile. ‘Come in. Come in and write a few words for me.’

Bao-yu had to follow her. Once they were inside, she called for brush, inkstone and paper.

‘Forty bolts of scarlet figured satin,’ she dictated. ‘Forty bolts of satin with python designs. One hundred bolts of gauze for imperial use, in various colours. Four gold neck-rings.’

‘What is this supposed to be?’ Bao-yu asked. ‘It isn’t an account, and it isn’t a list of presents. How am I to write it?’

‘Just put it down. So long as I understand it, that’s all that matters.’

Bao-yu could only write as he was told. As Xi-feng put the paper away, she smiled.

‘There’s something else I wanted to ask you. I don’t know whether you’ll agree. You have a maid called Crimson. I’ve been meaning to ask you for her, to work for me, but never got round to it. Seeing you today reminded me.’

‘I’ve more than enough people in my rooms. If you like any of them, cousin, take her. Why ask me?’

‘In that case I’ll send someone to fetch her.’

‘Do.’

He was already going when Xi-feng called him back.

‘Come here. There’s one more thing.’

‘Grandmother’s asking for me. Whatever it is can wait till I come back.’

He went on to Grandmother Jia’s, where he found everyone had finished eating.

‘What delicacies did you have with your mother?’ she asked.

‘No delicacies, but I ate an extra bowl of rice. Where’s Cousin Lin?’

‘In the inner room.’

He went in. One maid on the floor was blowing the coals in a pressing iron; two on the kang were snapping a chalk line. Dai-yu stood bent over, cutting something out with a pair of scissors.

‘Oh, what are you doing?’ he asked with a smile. ‘You’ve only just eaten, and here you are with your head bent down like that. You’ll have a headache again in a moment.’

Dai-yu paid no attention and went on cutting.

‘That corner of the silk still isn’t right,’ one of the maids said. ‘Give it another press.’

Dai-yu put down the scissors.

‘Never mind it. It’ll be all right in a little while.’

Bao-yu could only wonder at her. Just then Bao-chai, Tan-chun and the others arrived. After talking to Grandmother Jia for a little, Bao-chai came into the inner room too.

‘What are you making, Cousin Lin?’

Seeing Dai-yu cutting out the cloth, she smiled.

‘You grow cleverer every day. You can even cut things out now.’

‘Only telling lies to take people in,’ Dai-yu said with a smile.

‘Here’s something to make you laugh,’ said Bao-chai. ‘Just now, when I said I knew nothing about that medicine, Cousin Bao took offence.’

‘Never mind him,’ said Dai-yu. ‘He’ll be all right in a little while.’

‘Grandmother wants to play dominoes,’ Bao-yu told Bao-chai, ‘and there aren’t enough people. Why don’t you go and play?’

‘Did you think I came here to play dominoes?’ she asked with a smile, and went out.

‘You had better go,’ said Dai-yu. ‘There’s a tiger in here. Mind it doesn’t eat you!’

She went on cutting. Seeing that she would take no notice of him, Bao-yu could only try another smile.

‘Why don’t you go out for a little walk? You can do that later.’

Still she ignored him. He asked the maids who had told her to cut it out.

‘Whoever told me to,’ Dai-yu answered when she heard him questioning them, ‘it’s no concern of yours, Master Bao.’

He was about to reply when someone came in to say that a caller outside had brought an invitation. Bao-yu slipped out at once.

‘Amitabha!’ Dai-yu called after him. ‘I might as well be dead by the time you come back.’

Outside, Tealeaf told him that Mr Feng had sent to invite him. Remembering yesterday’s conversation, Bao-yu told him to fetch his going-out clothes and went to the study. Tealeaf hurried straight to the inner gate to wait for someone. An old woman came out, and he went up to her.

‘Master Bao’s in the study waiting for his going-out clothes. Would you be so kind as to take a message in?’

‘Your mother’s cunt!

‘A fine business! Master Bao lives in the garden now, and all his people are there, but you come running here to have a message taken in!’

‘Quite right to scold me,’ said Tealeaf with a laugh. ‘I’ve lost my wits.’

He went straight to the inner gate on the east. The page on duty was down by the paved walk playing ball. Tealeaf explained what was wanted. The boy ran in and, after some time, brought out a bundle and handed it to him. Back in the study Bao-yu changed his clothes, ordered his horse and set off with only four pages: Tealeaf, Chu-yao, Shuang-rui and Shuang-shou.

They went straight to Feng Zi-ying’s house. Someone announced Bao-yu, and Feng came out to welcome him in. Xue Pan had been waiting there for some time. There were also a number of boy singers, Jiang Yu-han, an actor who played young female roles, and Nuageuse, a courtesan from the Budding Grove. Once the introductions were over, they drank tea.

Raising his cup, Bao-yu said with a smile, ‘That fortunate and unfortunate business you spoke of the other day has been on my mind day and night. The moment I heard your invitation today, I came.’

‘You cousins are an honest pair,’ Feng Zi-ying said, laughing. ‘That was only a pretext. I truly wanted to have you both here for a drink, but I was afraid you’d find excuses, so I said that to tempt you. And today you came the moment I asked! Who would have thought you’d take it all as true?’

They all laughed. The wine was brought in, and they took their places.

Feng Zi-ying first called the boy singers over to press wine on the guests, then told Nuageuse to offer them some too. After three cups Xue Pan forgot all restraint. Taking her hand, he laughed.

‘Sing me one of those new songs you keep to yourself. How about it? I’ll drink a whole jar.’

Nuageuse could only take up her pipa and sing:

Two dear tormentors,

I cannot give up either;

When I think of you, I fret for him.

Both of them so handsome,

Beyond any painter’s skill.

Last night, I remember, a secret tryst at the rose trellis:

One stealing love,

One coming to catch us.

Caught, all three confronted with the case,

I had not a word in reply.

When she had finished she smiled.

‘Now drink your jar.’

‘Not worth a whole jar,’ Xue Pan said, laughing. ‘Sing me a better one.’

‘Listen to me,’ said Bao-yu with a smile. ‘Drinking like this gets us drunk without giving us any pleasure. I’ll begin by drinking a large beaker and proposing a new game. Anyone who breaks the rules must drink ten large beakers in succession, then leave his place and pour wine for the others.’

‘Very sensible,’ said Feng Zi-ying, Jiang Yu-han and the rest.

Bao-yu lifted the beaker and drank it dry in one draught.

‘We each have to give a girl’s grief, care, delight and joy, and explain the reason for each. When you’ve finished, you drink the cup required by the game. For the song round, you sing one of the latest fashionable songs. For the closing quotation, you take your cue from something on the table: it can be an old poem, an old couplet, something from the Four Books or the Five Classics, or a familiar saying.’

Before he had finished, Xue Pan stood up and interrupted.

‘I’m not playing. Leave me out. You’re only trying to make a fool of me!’

Nuageuse stood up too and pushed him back into his seat.

‘What are you afraid of?’ she asked, laughing. ‘You drink every day, and yet you can’t even do as well as I can? I’m going to have a turn too. If you get it right, well and good. If you don’t, you only have to drink a few cups. They won’t kill you. But if you break the rules now, you’ll have to drink ten large beakers and go and pour for everyone else, won’t you?’

They all clapped and applauded her.

There was no way out for Xue Pan. He sat down and listened as Bao-yu began:

A girl’s grief: youth slips away in an empty chamber.

A girl’s care: she let her husband seek a lordship, and regrets it.

A girl’s delight: her beauty in the mirror as she dresses at dawn.

A girl’s joy: upon the swing, in a light spring gown.

‘Those all make sense,’ the others said.

Only Xue Pan lifted his chin and shook his head.

‘No good. He should drink a forfeit.’

‘Why should he?’ they asked.

‘I couldn’t understand a word of it. Why shouldn’t he?’

Nuageuse gave him a pinch and laughed.

‘Keep quiet and think of your own lines. If you can’t say them when your turn comes, you’ll have to drink again.’

She took up her pipa to accompany Bao-yu, who sang:

No end to longing’s bloody tears, shed like red beans;

No end to spring willows and flowers in bloom, filling the painted chambers;

No settled sleep, with wind and rain at the gauze window after dusk;

No forgetting the new sorrows and the old;

No swallowing jade-white rice and golden water-shield: they choke my throat;

No seeing my wasted face in the flower-patterned mirror.

No smoothing of this furrowed brow,

No enduring the water-clock’s drip until dawn.

Ah, like the dim green hills that cannot be hidden,

The green waters flowing on and on without end.

When he finished, they all applauded, except Xue Pan, who said there was no beat to it. Bao-yu drank his required cup, then picked up a slice of pear and recited: ‘

Rain beats on pear blossom; the door is shut fast.

’ His turn was over.

Feng Zi-ying came next:

A girl’s grief: her husband lies sick, at the brink of death.

A girl’s care: a gale has blown down her dressing chamber.

A girl’s delight: at her first birth she has borne twins.

A girl’s joy: slipping into the garden to dig out crickets.

Having finished, he lifted his cup and sang:

You are a darling,

You are full of love,

You are a wayward, wilful, clever little imp,

You are beyond the powers of an immortal.

You won’t believe a single word I say:

Go and make quiet inquiries for yourself,

Then you’ll know whether I love you or not!

After the song he drank his required cup and recited: ‘

Cockcrow, a thatched inn, the moon.

’ His turn done, it was Nuageuse’s. She began: ‘

A girl’s grief: who can she depend on all her life?

’ ‘My darling,’ Xue Pan sighed, ‘with your Mr Xue here, what have you to fear?’

‘Don’t interrupt her!’ they all cried. ‘Let her go on.’

Nuageuse continued: ‘

A girl’s care: when will her madam stop the beatings and abuse?

’ ‘I saw your madam the other day,’ said Xue Pan. ‘I told her she wasn’t to beat you.’

‘The next person to interrupt drinks ten cups,’ the others declared.

Xue Pan promptly slapped his own cheek.

‘Never listen, do you? Not another word!’

Nuageuse went on: ‘

A girl’s delight: her lover cannot bear to go back home.

A girl’s joy: the pipes fall silent, and she plays the strings.

’ Then she sang:

Cardamom flowers on the third day of the third month;

A little insect tries to burrow in.

It burrows half the day and cannot enter,

Then climbs upon the flower and swings.

My little darling, my own heart’s flesh,

How will you get in unless I open?

She finished singing, drank her required cup and recited: ‘

How lush the peach tree!

’ Her turn was over. It was Xue Pan’s next.

‘Here I go,’ said Xue Pan. ‘A girl’s grief…’

Nothing more came, though they waited a long while.

‘Grief over what?’ Feng Zi-ying asked with a laugh. ‘Out with it!’

Xue Pan’s eyes bulged like bells in his anxiety. He stared for a long time before managing:

‘A girl’s grief…’

He coughed twice and tried again: ‘

A girl’s grief: she’s married a man who’s a cuckold.

’

They all burst out laughing.

‘What’s so funny?’ Xue Pan demanded. ‘Isn’t it true? A girl marries a man, and he turns out to be a cuckold. Why shouldn’t she be heartbroken?’

They doubled up with laughter.

‘Quite right. Go on to the next one.’

Xue Pan glared, then began again: ‘

A girl has a care…

’ Once more he fell silent.

‘What care?’ they prompted.

Xue Pan said: ‘

Out jumps a great monkey from her chamber there.

’ ‘A forfeit! A forfeit!’ they roared with laughter. ‘That’s even more absurd. We could just forgive the first one.’

They were about to pour the wine when Bao-yu said with a smile, ‘So long as it rhymes.’

‘The master of the game has allowed it,’ Xue Pan said. ‘What are you making a fuss for?’

At that they desisted.

‘The next two will be even harder,’ Nuageuse said with a laugh. ‘Let me do them for you.’

‘Nonsense! Do you really think I haven’t any good ones? Listen!’ ‘

A girl’s delight: slow to rise at dawn after the bridal candles’ night.

’ ‘How did he manage such an elegant line?’ they asked in astonishment.

Xue Pan continued: ‘

A girl’s joy: a cock jabbing in her.

’ They all turned their faces aside.

‘You wretch! You wretch, you wretch, you wretch! Get on with your song!’

Xue Pan sang: ‘

One mosquito, hum, hum, hum.

’ They stared.

‘What sort of song is that?’

Xue Pan went on singing: ‘

Two flies, buzz, buzz, buzz.

’ ‘Enough! Enough! Enough!’ they all cried.

‘Listen or don’t, as you please!

‘It’s a new song, called The Humming Tune. If you can’t be bothered to listen, let me off the closing quotation too, and I won’t sing.’

‘Let him off! Let him off! Don’t keep everyone else waiting.’

Jiang Yu-han then began:

A girl’s grief: her husband has gone and never returned.

A girl’s care: no money to buy osmanthus hair-oil.

A girl’s delight: the lamp-wick flowers in two buds, side by side.

A girl’s joy: the husband sings, the wife follows, in true accord.

Then he sang:

How lovely that you were born with a hundred tender charms,

Like a living immortal come down from the blue heavens.

In the springtime of life, so young in years,

A phoenix pair: a fitting match indeed.

Ah, see the River of Heaven high above,

Hear the watchtower drum,

Trim the silver lamp; together slip softly behind the mandarin-duck curtains.

When he had finished the song, he drank his required cup and said with a smile:

‘I know very little poetry. Luckily I saw a couplet yesterday, and this one line happens to have stayed in my mind. Luckily too, the thing it mentions is here on the table.’

He drained his cup, picked up an osmanthus blossom and recited: ‘

the flowers’ aroma steals upon one, telling of the warmth of day.

’

The company accepted it, and his turn was done. But Xue Pan jumped up again and shouted:

‘Outrageous! Outrageous! A forfeit! There’s no precious treasure on this table. What are you doing reciting about one?’

Jiang Yu-han looked blank.

‘What precious treasure?’

‘Still denying it? Say it again.’

Jiang Yu-han could only repeat the line.

‘If Aroma isn’t a precious treasure, what is she?’ Xue Pan demanded. ‘Don’t believe me? Ask him.’

He pointed to Bao-yu.

Bao-yu grew embarrassed.

‘Cousin Xue, how much should you drink for that?’

‘A forfeit, a forfeit!’ Xue Pan agreed, and lifted his cup and drained it.

Feng Zi-ying and Jiang Yu-han did not understand, so Nuageuse explained. Jiang Yu-han rose at once to apologise.

‘No blame where there was no knowledge,’ the others said.

A little later Bao-yu left the table to relieve himself, and Jiang Yu-han followed. As they stood beneath the eaves of the verandah, Jiang apologised again. Bao-yu was so taken by his gentle, enchanting manner that he could not bear to part with him. He held his hand tightly.

‘Come and see us whenever you’re free. And there’s something else I wanted to ask. There’s someone in your company called Bijou. Where is he? His fame has spread everywhere, and I alone have never had the good fortune to meet him.’

‘That’s my nickname,’ Jiang Yu-han said with a smile.

Bao-yu stamped his foot in delight and laughed.

‘What luck! What luck!

‘Your reputation is well deserved. It’s our first meeting today. What can I do to mark it?’

After thinking a moment, he took his fan from his sleeve, unfastened its open-ring jade pendant and handed it to Bijou.

‘A poor little thing, but let it stand for the friendship we’ve made today.’

Bijou accepted it with a smile.

‘How can I deserve a reward when I’ve done nothing to earn it? Well, I have a rare thing here. I only put it on this morning. It’s quite new, and may serve as a small token of my affection.’

He lifted his robe, untied the scarlet sash that fastened his underclothes and handed it to Bao-yu.

‘This sash was sent as tribute by the queen of Qianxiang. If you wear it in summer, it scents your skin and leaves no sweat-stains. The Prince of Beijing gave it to me yesterday, and I put it on for the first time today. I would never give it to anyone else. Will you take off the one you’re wearing, Master Bao, and let me have that to tie round myself?’

Overjoyed, Bao-yu accepted it at once, untied his own pine-pollen-green sash and handed it to Bijou. They had just tied them on when a great shout rang out.

‘Caught you!’

Xue Pan sprang out and seized them both.

‘Leaving all that wine to come sneaking away together! What are you two up to?

‘Quick, let me see what you’ve got.’

‘Nothing,’ they both said.

Xue Pan would not let them go. Only when Feng Zi-ying came out were they released, and they returned to their places and went on drinking. The party did not break up until evening.

Back in the garden, Bao-yu took off his outer clothes and drank some tea. Aroma noticed that the pendant was missing from his fan.

‘What happened to it?’

‘I lost it on horseback.’

At bedtime she saw the sash round his waist, scarlet as a drop of blood, and guessed most of the story.

‘Now you’ve got a fine new one to hold your trousers up, give me mine back.’

Only then did Bao-yu remember that the sash had belonged to Aroma, and that he ought not to have given it away. He regretted it, but could not bring himself to say so. He could only smile.

‘I’ll give you another.’

Aroma nodded and sighed.

‘I knew you’d been up to that sort of thing again. You had no business giving my things to those scoundrels. Really, there’s no accounting for you. You never think.’

She would have said more, but was afraid of upsetting him with so much wine in him, and had to go to sleep too. Nothing more need be said of the night.

She had only just woken at daybreak when Bao-yu said with a smile:

‘There were thieves about in the night, and you never knew. Look at your trousers.’

Aroma looked down. The sash Bao-yu had worn the day before was now tied round her own waist. Knowing he must have changed it in the night, she hastily tugged it off.

‘I don’t want the thing. Take it away this instant!’

Bao-yu had to coax and persuade her for some time. With no help for it, she tied it round her waist. But afterwards, when he had gone out, she took it off after all, flung it into an empty chest and tied on another of her own.

Bao-yu paid no attention to this. He asked whether anything had happened the day before.

‘Mrs Lian sent for Crimson,’ Aroma told him. ‘She wanted to wait until you came back, but I thought there was no need, so I took it on myself to send her.’

‘Quite right. I knew about it already. There was no need to wait for me.’

‘Yesterday Her Highness sent Eunuch Xia out with a hundred and twenty taels,’ Aroma went on. ‘She wants a three-day Taoist offering rite for peace at the Temple of Pure Emptiness, from the first to the third, with plays and offerings. Mr Zhen is to lead all the gentlemen in kneeling before the incense and worshipping the Buddha. She also sent Dragon Boat Festival gifts.’

She called a little maid to bring out the presents received the day before. There were two fine palace fans, two strings of red musk-scented beads, two lengths of phoenix-tail gauze and a furong bamboo mat. Bao-yu was delighted.

‘Did everyone else get the same?’

‘Her Old Ladyship had a scented ruyi and an agate pillow as well.

‘The Master, Her Ladyship and Aunt Xue each had just an extra ruyi. Yours are the same as Miss Bao’s. Miss Lin and the Second, Third and Fourth Misses had only fans and beads, with nothing else. The First and Second Mistresses each had two bolts of gauze, two of silk gauze, two scented sachets and two medicinal ingots.’

‘What’s the reason for that?’ Bao-yu asked, smiling. ‘Why aren’t Miss Lin’s the same as mine, instead of Cousin Bao-chai’s? Could they have been handed out wrongly?’

‘Every share had a label on it when they came out yesterday,’ said Aroma. ‘How could there be a mistake? Yours were in Her Old Ladyship’s rooms. I went and fetched them. Her Old Ladyship says you’re to go into the Palace on your own tomorrow, at the fifth watch, to give thanks.’

‘Of course I must go.’

Then he called Purple Silk.

‘Take these to Miss Lin. Tell her they came for me yesterday, and she can keep whatever she likes.’

Purple Silk took them away as she was told, but soon came back.

‘Miss Lin says she had some yesterday too, and you’re to keep these, Master Bao.’

Bao-yu told them to put the things away. He had just washed his face and come out, meaning to pay his respects to Grandmother Jia, when he met Dai-yu coming towards him. He hurried up to her, smiling.

‘I sent my things for you to choose from. Why didn’t you take anything?’

Dai-yu had long since put aside what had troubled her about Bao-yu the day before. Now her thoughts were on today’s grievance.

‘I haven’t the good fortune to bear such favours. I’m not like Miss Bao, with all that gold and jade. I’m only a creature of grass and trees.’

The words ‘gold and jade’ set Bao-yu’s heart stirring with doubt.

‘Whatever other people say about gold and jade, if I have any such thought myself, may Heaven destroy me and earth swallow me, and may I never be born human again in ten thousand lifetimes!’

Dai-yu knew from this that she had aroused his suspicions. She hastened to smile again.

‘How silly! Why swear an oath over nothing? What do I care about your gold and jade?’

‘It’s hard to tell you what’s in my heart,’ said Bao-yu. ‘One day you’ll understand. After Grandmother, Father and Mother, you come fourth. If there’s anyone fifth, I’ll swear another oath.’

‘You needn’t swear. I know very well that you have room in your heart for your younger cousin. Only when an elder cousin appears, you forget the younger one.’

‘That’s your own suspicion. I never do.’

‘Then when that girl Bao-chai wouldn’t back up your lie yesterday, why did you question me?

‘If it had been me, there’s no knowing how you’d have behaved.’

Just then they saw Bao-chai coming from the other direction and moved away. She saw them quite clearly, but pretended not to, and went past with her head lowered. She sat for a while at Lady Wang’s, then went to Grandmother Jia’s, where she found Bao-yu.

Bao-chai’s mother had once told Lady Wang and the others that her golden locket had been given by a monk, and that when the time came she must marry someone who had jade. Because of this Bao-chai always kept her distance from Bao-yu. Yesterday’s gifts from Yuan-chun, when she alone had received the same as he, had made her feel still more awkward. Fortunately Bao-yu was so bound up with Dai-yu, with his every thought fixed on her, that he paid no attention to the matter.

Now, however, he suddenly asked with a smile:

‘Cousin Bao-chai, may I see your red musk-scented beads?’

As it happened, she was wearing a string round her left wrist. Since he had asked, she had to slip them off.

Bao-chai’s flesh was full and smooth, and the beads would not come off easily. Watching that soft, snow-white arm, Bao-yu felt a stirring of desire.

“If only that arm belonged to Cousin Lin,” he thought, “I might at least get to touch it. Why must it belong to her?”

He was regretting his lack of the good fortune to touch it when the business of ‘gold and jade’ suddenly came back to him. He looked again at Bao-chai: a face like a silver basin, eyes like glistening apricots, lips red without rouge, brows dark without paint. Hers was a different kind of charm and grace from Dai-yu’s. He stared, quite lost, and even forgot to take the beads when she slipped them off and held them out.

Seeing his abstraction, Bao-chai grew embarrassed herself. She put down the beads and was turning to go when she saw Dai-yu with one foot on the threshold, biting her handkerchief and smiling.

‘You can’t stand the wind,’ Bao-chai said. ‘Why are you standing in the draught again?’

‘I was in the room,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘Then I heard a cry in the sky and came out to look. It was only a silly goose.’

‘Where’s the silly goose?

‘Let me have a look too.’

‘As soon as I came out, it went “honk!” and flew away.’

As she spoke, Dai-yu flicked the handkerchief in her hand towards Bao-yu’s face. Caught unawares, he took it squarely in the eye and cried, ‘Ow!’
