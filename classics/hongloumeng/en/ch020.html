Chapter 20 Wang Xi-feng speaks firmly against jealousy; Lin Dai-yu’s quick wit teases a sweet lisp

Chapter end

Now Bao-yu had been telling Dai-yu about the mouse spirits in her room when Bao-chai came upon them and teased him for not knowing the allusion to ‘green wax’ at the Lantern Festival. The three were still there, laughing and making fun of one another. Bao-yu had been afraid that Dai-yu would indulge in a nap after her meal and either suffer from indigestion or be unable to sleep that night: neither was any way to look after her health. Fortunately Bao-chai’s arrival had set them all talking and laughing, so that Dai-yu no longer felt like sleeping, and he could stop worrying.

Suddenly they heard an uproar in his rooms. They all listened for a moment.

‘That’s your mamma shouting at Aroma,’ Dai-yu said with a laugh. ‘Whatever Aroma may have done, if your mamma is seriously making a scene with her, she must be getting very muddle-headed in her old age.’

Bao-yu hurried to go, but Bao-chai caught his arm.

‘You mustn’t quarrel with your mamma. She’s old and confused. You ought to give way to her a little.’

‘I know,’ said Bao-yu.

He went over and found Nurse Li standing in the middle of the room, leaning on her stick and abusing Aroma.

‘You little whore! Forgotten where you came from, have you?

‘I made something of you, and now when I come here you lie on the kang as grand as you please and don’t so much as take notice of me. All you think about is playing the little vixen and bewitching Bao-yu, till he won’t have anything to do with me and listens to you lot instead. You’re nothing but a chit bought for a few stinking taels of silver. Do you think you can make mischief in this room? I’ll have you dragged out and married to one of the boys, and then we’ll see whether you can still play the witch and charm Bao-yu!’

At first Aroma thought Nurse Li was merely angry at finding her lying down. She tried to explain that she was ill, had just begun to sweat and had had her head covered, so that she had not seen the old lady come in. But when the accusations of ‘bewitching Bao-yu’ and ‘playing the vixen’ went on and on, followed by the threat of marrying her to one of the boys, she felt so ashamed and wronged that she could not help crying.

Bao-yu heard all this but could do little about it. He could only explain on Aroma’s behalf that she was ill and taking medicine.

‘If you don’t believe me, ask the other maids.’

This made Nurse Li angrier still.

‘You do nothing but protect those vixens. What am I to you now? Who am I to ask? Which of them wouldn’t take your side? Which of them hasn’t Aroma brought to heel? I know all about their doings. I’ll just have to take you before Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship and tell them. I nursed you till you grew this big, and now you have no use for my milk you push me aside and let the maids lord it over me.’

She too began to cry as she spoke. Dai-yu and Bao-chai had followed him over and tried to soothe her.

‘Mamma, you’re older and wiser. Just put up with them a little.’

At the sight of the two girls, Nurse Li seized on them to hear her grievances. On and on she rambled about the tea she had drunk that other day, Snowpink’s dismissal, yesterday’s milk curd and all the rest.

As it happened, Xi-feng had just finished settling the gambling accounts in Grandmother Jia’s rooms. Hearing the uproar at the back, she knew Nurse Li was at her old tricks again, scolding Bao-yu’s attendants. Nurse Li had lost money that day and was taking it out on someone else. Xi-feng hurried over, took her by the arm and said with a smile:

‘Now, my dear mamma, don’t be angry. It’s the holiday, and Her Old Ladyship has only just had one happy day. You’re one of our old people. If anyone else raised their voice, you’d be the one to stop them. Surely you know better than to shout here yourself and upset Her Old Ladyship? Just tell me who’s been naughty, and I’ll beat her for you. There’s a pheasant cooking at my place, piping hot. Come and have some wine with me.’

Talking all the while, she pulled Nurse Li away.

‘Felicity! Carry your Grannie Li’s stick, and her handkerchief for wiping her eyes.’

Nurse Li was swept off her feet and away with Xi-feng, still protesting:

‘I don’t care if I die! I might as well forget my manners today, make a proper scene and disgrace myself. Anything’s better than being put upon by that little whore!’

Bao-chai and Dai-yu had followed them out. Seeing how Xi-feng managed it, they clapped their hands and laughed.

‘Thank goodness for that gust of wind! It whisked the old woman away.’

Bao-yu nodded and sighed.

‘Who knows whose score she’s settling this time? She always picks on someone who won’t answer back.

‘Yesterday, too, some girl must have offended her, and Aroma got the blame for it.’

Before he had quite finished, Skybright, who was standing nearby, said with a laugh:

‘Who’d be mad enough to go and offend her? And anyone who did would have the courage to own up. There’d be no need to get anyone else into trouble!’

Still crying, Aroma caught Bao-yu’s hand.

‘You’ve offended an old lady on my account, and now you’re offending all these people for me as well. Haven’t I enough to bear already, without your dragging in everyone else?’

Seeing her so ill, with all this trouble added to it, Bao-yu hurriedly swallowed his resentment and soothed her into lying down again to sweat. She was burning hot. He stayed beside her, reclining there and urging her to think only of getting well, not to vex herself over things that did not matter.

Aroma gave a bitter laugh.

‘If I let things like that upset me, I couldn’t stay in this room for a moment. But when it goes on and on, day after day, what is one to do? I’m always telling you not to offend people for our sake. You only think of standing up for us at the time, but they remember it. When they get a chance, out it all comes, and it’s very unpleasant to hear. What good does that do any of us?’

As she spoke she could not hold back her tears. Afraid of distressing Bao-yu, however, she forced herself to check them.

Presently an old woman who did odd jobs brought in a second decoction of the medicine. Seeing that Aroma had only just begun to perspire, Bao-yu would not let her get up. He took the bowl himself and held it to her as she lay on her pillow, then told the little maids to make up the kang.

‘Whether you’re going to eat or not,’ Aroma said, ‘at least go and sit with Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship for a while, and spend a little time with the young ladies before you come back. Then I can lie here quietly too.’

Bao-yu could only do as she asked. He took off her hairpins and earrings, saw her lie down, and went to Grandmother Jia’s rooms. After they had eaten, she wanted to amuse herself at cards with the old senior serving-women, but he was thinking of Aroma and returned to his room. She had drifted into a doze. He thought of going to bed himself, but it was still too early.

Skybright, Mackerel, Ripple and Emerald had all gone off in search of amusement with Faithful, Amber and the others. Only Musk remained, laying out dominoes by lamplight in the outer room.

‘Why haven’t you gone to play with them?’ he asked, smiling.

‘I haven’t any money.’

‘There’s all that money piled under the bed. Isn’t that enough for you to lose?’

‘If everyone goes off to play, who’s to look after the room? She’s ill, too. There are lamps up above and fires down below, all over the place. Those poor old mammas, at their age, have been waiting on us all day. They ought to have a rest. And the little maids have been working all day too. If they can’t go and play now, when can they?

‘So let them all go. I’ll stay here and keep an eye on things.’

Hearing this, Bao-yu thought she was positively another Aroma.

‘I’ll sit here,’ he said with a smile. ‘Off you go, and don’t worry.’

‘Now you’re here, there’s even less reason to go. Wouldn’t it be nice for the two of us to talk and have a laugh?’

‘What shall we do, though?’ said Bao-yu. ‘It’s rather dull. Oh, I know. You said this morning your head was itching. There’s nothing to do just now. Let me comb your hair.’

‘That’s just the thing,’ said Musk.

She brought over her fitted mirror case, took off her hairpins and bracelets, and let down her hair. Bao-yu took the fine-toothed comb and worked through it, strand by strand. He had made only three to five strokes when Skybright hurried in for some money. At the sight of them she gave a scornful laugh.

‘Oh! You haven’t drunk the wedding cups yet, and already you’re dressing her hair like a bride!’

‘Come here,’ said Bao-yu, laughing. ‘I’ll comb yours too.’

‘I’m not so blessed.’

She took her money and flung the curtain down behind her as she went out.

Bao-yu was behind Musk, who sat facing the mirror, and their eyes met in the glass.

‘Of everyone in this room, she’s the only one who’s always sniping,’ he said with a smile at her reflection.

Musk hastily waved a warning hand in the mirror. Bao-yu understood. Suddenly the curtain swished, and Skybright ran in again.

‘How am I always sniping? Come on, let’s hear it.’

‘Off you go,’ said Musk, laughing. ‘Back again to question people!’

‘And you’re protecting him again!’ Skybright laughed. ‘I know all about your sneaking and your tricks. Wait till I’ve won my money back. Then we’ll talk.’

And straight out she went.

Bao-yu finished combing Musk’s hair and had her help him quietly to bed, unwilling to disturb Aroma. Nothing more need be said of that night.

By the time they rose next morning, Aroma had sweated during the night and felt somewhat easier. She took only a little rice water and rested quietly. Bao-yu, relieved, went over after breakfast to Aunt Xue’s to while away some time. It was the first month: the school was closed for the New Year, and needlework was forbidden in the women’s apartments, so everyone was at leisure.

Jia Huan had also come to play. He found Bao-chai, Caltrop and Oriole, the three of them, amusing themselves with a dice game played with Go counters, and wanted to join in. Bao-chai had always treated him just as she treated Bao-yu, without any other thought. Hearing that he wanted to play, she invited him up to sit with them. The cash lay in piles of ten. He won the first time and was delighted. But after losing several games in succession, he began to grow anxious. In the current game it was his turn to throw the dice. A seven would win it for him; with a six, Oriole would only need to throw a three on her next turn to win. He picked up the dice and flung them down hard. One settled on five; the other spun round and round.

Oriole clapped her hands and kept calling, ‘One!’ Jia Huan glared, shouting out ‘Six! Seven! Eight!’ indiscriminately. As ill luck would have it, the die came up one. In a panic he snatched it up, then reached for the money, claiming it had been a six.

‘It was a one, plain as anything!’ said Oriole.

Seeing Jia Huan’s agitation, Bao-chai gave Oriole a warning look.

‘The older you get, the worse your manners. Would a young master cheat you? Put that money down at once!’

Oriole felt deeply wronged, but after her mistress’s rebuke she dared not argue. She had to put down the money, though she muttered:

‘A young master, cheating us out of a few cash! He doesn’t even show me any respect. The other day, when I was playing with Master Bao, he lost all that money and never got cross. The little maids snatched up what he had left, and he only laughed.’

Bao-chai sharply cut her off before she had finished.

‘How can I compare with Bao-yu?’ said Jia Huan. ‘You’re afraid of him. You all like him, and you all pick on me because I wasn’t born to Her Ladyship.’

He began to cry.

‘My dear boy, don’t say things like that,’ Bao-chai hastened to soothe him. ‘People will laugh at you.’

She scolded Oriole again.

Just then Bao-yu came in. Seeing the state they were in, he asked what had happened. Jia Huan dared not make a sound. Bao-chai knew the rules of their household: younger brothers were all afraid of their elders. What she did not know was that Bao-yu wanted no one to be afraid of him. His reasoning was this: ‘My brothers all have parents to teach them. Why should I interfere and only make them shy of me? Besides, I was born to the lawful wife, and he to a concubine. People talk behind our backs as it is. What would they say if I started ordering him about?’ He also cherished another foolish notion.

What foolish notion, you ask? From earliest childhood he had grown up among girls. There were his own sisters Yuan-chun and Tan-chun, his paternal cousins Ying-chun and Xi-chun, and other relations such as Shi Xiang-yun, Lin Dai-yu and Xue Bao-chai. He had therefore concluded that, though human beings were born the most spiritual of all creatures, the purest essence of mountains and rivers, sun and moon, was bestowed on girls alone. Bearded men were merely the dregs and dirty froth.

With this foolish notion in his head, he considered all males muddy, undifferentiated creatures whom one could quite well do without. There was an exception for his father, uncles and brothers: Confucius, the foremost man of all time, had said one must not defy or slight them, and Bao-yu could only obey his injunction. So, with his brothers, he did no more than observe the broad requirements of affection and propriety. He never thought that, as a man, he ought to set an example to the younger members of the family. Jia Huan and the rest were consequently not afraid of him, though fear of Grandmother Jia made them show him a little deference.

Now Bao-chai, afraid that a lecture from Bao-yu would only make matters awkward for Jia Huan, hastily made excuses for him.

‘What are you crying for in the New Year holidays?’ said Bao-yu. ‘If you don’t like it here, go and play somewhere else. You read every day, and all your reading seems to have muddled you. Suppose this thing isn’t any good: something else is bound to be better. You leave this one and take the other. Do you think sitting here crying over it will make it any better? You came to enjoy yourself. If you can’t enjoy yourself here, go somewhere else and find some fun.

‘Can you call it enjoying yourself, sitting here crying? You’re only making yourself miserable. You’d do better to go at once.’

Jia Huan could only go home. Aunt Zhao, seeing his face, asked:

‘Who’s been using you for a kicking-block this time?’

He did not answer her first question. When she asked again, he said:

‘I was playing with Cousin Bao-chai. Oriole bullied me and cheated me out of my money, and Brother Bao-yu sent me away.’

Aunt Zhao spat in disgust.

‘Who told you to go mixing with your betters? You shameless little wretch! Couldn’t you find anywhere else to play? Who told you to go there and make a fool of yourself?’

Xi-feng happened to pass outside the window just then and heard every word. She spoke through the window:

‘What’s all this, in the middle of the New Year holidays? Huan’s only a child. If he does something a little wrong, you should teach him better. What’s the good of all this nonsense? Whatever he does, there are still Her Ladyship and His Lordship to look after him. How dare you spit abuse at him! He is one of the masters of this house. If he misbehaves, there are people to correct him. What business is it of yours? Huan, come out and play with me.’

Jia Huan had always been more afraid of Xi-feng than of Lady Wang. At her summons he hurried out, murmuring his obedience. Aunt Zhao did not dare utter a word either.

‘And you’re a spineless creature too,’ Xi-feng told Jia Huan. ‘How often have I told you? If you want something to eat or drink, or a game or a laugh, go to whichever sister, cousin, brother or sister-in-law you like.

‘Instead of listening to me, you let these people teach you their crooked, spiteful ways, all sly tricks and bullying. You have no self-respect, you’re determined to sink as low as you can, you harbour all these nasty little thoughts, and then you complain that other people are partial. How much did you lose, to get into such a state?’

Questioned, Jia Huan could only stammer an answer.

‘One or two hundred.’

‘And you call yourself a young master!’ said Xi-feng. ‘All this over one or two hundred cash!’

She turned to Felicity.

‘Go and fetch a string of cash. The girls are all playing at the back. Take him there to play.’

Then, to Jia Huan:

‘And if you behave like this again, you sly little wretch, I’ll beat you first and then send word to the school. They’ll flay you alive! Your brother’s so furious at your disgraceful ways he’s grinding his teeth. If I hadn’t stopped him, he’d have kicked your guts out.’

‘Off you go!’ she ordered.

Jia Huan meekly followed Felicity, received the money and went to play with Ying-chun and the others. Of him we need say no more.

Bao-yu was still talking and laughing with Bao-chai when someone announced, ‘Miss Shi has come.’ He sprang up to go.

‘Wait,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘Let’s go and see her together.’

She got down from the kang and went with him to Grandmother Jia’s rooms. They found Shi Xiang-yun talking and laughing at the top of her voice. When she saw the two of them, she hurried to exchange greetings. Dai-yu was there too.

‘Where have you been?’ she asked Bao-yu.

‘At Cousin Bao-chai’s.’

Dai-yu gave a cold laugh.

‘I thought as much. Lucky something kept you there, or you’d have flown here long ago.’

‘So I’m only allowed to play with you and keep you amused?’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘I go to see her once in a while, and this is what you say.’

‘What a tiresome thing to say! What do I care whether you go or not? I never asked you to keep me amused. You’re quite welcome never to speak to me again!’

With that, she went back to her room in a huff.

Bao-yu hurried after her.

‘What’s made you angry again, all of a sudden? Even if I said something wrong, you could have stayed there and talked and laughed with the others for a while. Instead you’ve come back to sit here making yourself miserable.’

‘What business is it of yours?’

‘I wouldn’t dare interfere,’ he said with a smile. ‘But I can’t just watch you ruin your health.’

‘If I ruin my health and die, what’s that to you?’

‘Why talk about living and dying in the New Year holidays?’

‘I will talk about dying! I’ll die this very minute! If you’re afraid of death, you can live to be a hundred. How’s that?’

‘Do you think I’m afraid of dying, if this is to go on and on?’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘Better to die and have done with it.’

‘Exactly!’ Dai-yu said at once. ‘If this is to go on, better to die and have done with it.’

‘I meant it would be better if I died. Don’t mishear me and then blame me for it.’

While they were talking, Bao-chai came in.

‘Cousin Xiang-yun is waiting for you.’

She pushed Bao-yu out with her. Left behind, Dai-yu felt more wretched than ever and sat by the window weeping. Before two cups of tea could have been drunk, Bao-yu was back. At the sight of him her sobs grew louder and would not stop. He knew it would be hard to put matters right, and marshalled a thousand tender, soothing things to say. But before he could open his mouth, Dai-yu spoke.

‘What have you come back for? You’ve someone to play with now who reads better than I do, and writes better poetry, and has better handwriting, and is better at talking and laughing. And she’s so afraid you’ll get angry that she pulls you away. What have you come back for? Leave me to live or die as I please!’

Bao-yu hurried up to her and spoke softly.

‘You’re so sensible. Surely you know the saying, “Nearer kin should not be displaced by remoter kin, nor earlier ties by later ones”?1

‘Muddle-headed as I am, I understand those two things. First, your mother and my father were sister and brother, whereas Cousin Bao-chai’s mother and mine are sisters. In terms of kinship, she isn’t as close to me as you are. Second, you came here first. We’ve eaten at the same table and slept in the same bed, and grown up together to this age. She’s only just come. How could I neglect you for her?’

Dai-yu gave a little snort.

‘Am I asking you to neglect her? What sort of person would that make me? It’s my heart I’m thinking of.’

‘And it’s my heart I’m thinking of. Do you know only your own heart, and not mine?’

Dai-yu lowered her head and said nothing. At last she spoke.

‘You’re always complaining that I take offence at everything you do. You never know how you torment me.

‘Just take the weather today. It’s so obviously cold, yet you’ve taken off your dark fox-flank cloak. Why?’

‘I had it on,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘But when I saw how angry you were, I got all hot and took it off.’

Dai-yu sighed.

‘You’ll catch cold, and then you’ll be kept on an empty stomach and make a fuss for something to eat again.’

As they were talking, Xiang-yun came up to them with a smile.

‘Second Cousin! Cousin Lin! You two can play together every day. I hardly ever manage to come, and now I’m here you won’t take any notice of me.’

‘You do love to talk, for someone who lisps,’ Dai-yu said, laughing. ‘You can’t even say “Second Cousin”. It’s always “Dear Cousin, Dear Cousin”!2

‘And when we play the counter game later, you’ll be at it again: “One, ai, three, four, five!”’

Bao-yu laughed.

‘Keep on copying her, and one of these days you’ll be lisping yourself.’

‘She never lets anyone off,’ said Shi Xiang-yun. ‘She does nothing but pick on people’s faults. Even if you’re better than everyone else, that’s no reason to make fun of everyone you meet. There’s one person I could name: if you dare find fault with her, I’ll admit you’re the clever one.’

Dai-yu promptly asked who it was.

‘Cousin Bao-chai. Dare to find fault with her, and I’ll give you credit. I may not be as good as you, but how is she any worse?’

Dai-yu gave a cold laugh.

‘Oh, so that’s who you meant! How would I dare find fault with her?’

Before she could finish, Bao-yu hastily turned the conversation.

‘Of course I shan’t be your equal in this lifetime,’ Xiang-yun said with a laugh. ‘All I pray for is a husband for Cousin Lin who lisps too. Then you can listen to “ai” and “e” every moment of the day. Amitabha! If only I could see that!’

Everyone laughed at this. Xiang-yun quickly turned and ran away. To learn what followed, read the next chapter.

Translator’s notes

  1. Bao-yu’s saying is translated in the sense he gives it next. Its wording can also be read as “Nearer kin should not separate remoter kin; the earlier should not overstep the later”, which pulls against his explanation. ↩
  2. The teasing turns on Xiang-yun’s saying ai (“love”) for er (“second”), hence “Dear Cousin” and the altered count. The further sound e joins the teasing at the end of the exchange. ↩
