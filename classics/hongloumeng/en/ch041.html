Chapter 41 At Green Bower Hermitage, tea is savoured with snow from plum blossom; The House of Green Delights suffers an invasion by a mother locust

Chapter end

Now Grannie Liu held out both hands to show them, and said, ‘The flower falls and a big pumpkin grows.’ The whole company roared with laughter. She drank the cup required by the game, then continued her joking.

‘To tell you the truth, I’m clumsy with my hands and feet, and I’ve had wine as well. I might let this porcelain cup slip and break it. If you have a wooden one, bring it for me. Then if I drop it on the ground, no harm will be done.’

They laughed again. Xi-feng promptly took her up.

‘If you really want a wooden one, I’ll have it fetched. But there’s one thing I must tell you first. Wooden cups aren’t like porcelain ones. They come in sets, and you have to drink your way through the whole set.’

Grannie Liu considered this. ‘I was only making a joke to amuse them. Who would have thought they really had such things? I’ve been to feasts at the big houses of country gentlemen often enough, and I’ve seen gold cups and silver cups, but I’ve never heard of wooden ones.

‘Oh, I know! They must be those little wooden bowls children use. She’s only trying to coax a couple more bowls of wine into me. Never mind. This wine tastes like honey-water, anyway. A little more won’t hurt.’

Having thought it over, she said, ‘Bring them here, and then we’ll see.’

Xi-feng told Felicity, ‘In the inner room at the front there’s a set of ten bamboo-root cups on the bookshelf. Fetch them.’

Felicity had answered and was just going when Faithful laughed.

‘I know those ten cups of yours. They’re still quite small. Besides, you said wood just now. It won’t look very well if you produce bamboo root instead. Why not fetch our big set of ten, hollowed out of solid boxwood root, and give her ten good doses?’

‘Better still!’ said Xi-feng, laughing.

Faithful did send someone for them. At the sight, Grannie Liu was both startled and delighted. What startled her was the row of ten cups, diminishing in size: the largest was quite as big as a small basin, and even the tenth and smallest was twice the size of the cup she was holding. What delighted her was the extraordinary carving, all landscapes, trees and figures, with cursive writing and seal impressions besides.

‘Just bring me the little one,’ she said hastily. ‘What do I want with so many?’

‘You can’t drink out of only one of these,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Nobody in our family can hold that much, so no one dares use them. But you asked for them, Grannie, and now we’ve gone to all this trouble to find them, you must drink from each in turn.’

‘Oh, I daren’t!’ cried Grannie Liu in alarm. ‘My dear Madam, have mercy on me!’

Grandmother Jia, Aunt Xue and Lady Wang knew that at her age she could not stand it.

‘A joke is a joke,’ they hastily interposed, smiling. ‘She mustn’t drink too much. Just this first cup will do.’

‘Blessed Buddha!’ said Grannie Liu. ‘Let me go on drinking from the little cup. Put this big one away for me, and I’ll take it home and drink it at my leisure.’

This set them all laughing again. Faithful could do nothing but have a large cup filled to the brim. Grannie Liu held it in both hands to drink.

‘Slowly,’ Grandmother Jia and Aunt Xue both warned her. ‘Don’t choke.’

Aunt Xue also told Xi-feng to give her something to eat.

‘What would you like, Grannie?’ Xi-feng asked, smiling. ‘Tell me the name and I’ll pick it up and feed you.’

‘What do I know about the names? Everything’s good.’

‘Give her some of the aubergine relish,’ said Grandmother Jia, smiling.

Xi-feng obediently picked some up and put it into Grannie Liu’s mouth.

‘You eat aubergines every day,’ she said with a laugh. ‘Now try ours and see whether we make them taste nice.’

‘Don’t you fool me!’ Grannie Liu laughed. ‘If aubergines could taste like this, we needn’t grow grain any more. We’d grow nothing but aubergines.’

‘It really is aubergine,’ they all said, laughing. ‘We aren’t fooling you now.’

‘Is it really?’ Grannie Liu asked in astonishment. ‘Then I’ve been eating all this time for nothing. Give me a little more, Madam. I’ll chew this mouthful carefully.’

Xi-feng put some more into her mouth. After chewing it for a long time, Grannie Liu laughed.

‘There’s a bit of an aubergine flavour, but it still doesn’t seem like aubergine. Tell me how you make it, and I’ll make some to eat myself.’

‘There’s nothing difficult about it,’ said Xi-feng, smiling. ‘You pare the skin off freshly picked aubergines, keeping only the flesh, cut it into little dice and fry it in chicken fat. Then take chicken breast, fragrant mushrooms, fresh bamboo shoots, ordinary mushrooms, five-spice dried bean curd and dried fruits and nuts of all kinds. Dice those too, simmer them in chicken stock until it has cooked away, finish with sesame oil and mix in some wine-lees sauce. Put it all in a porcelain jar and seal it tight. When you want to eat it, take some out and mix in stir-fried chicken pieces. That’s all.’

Grannie Liu shook her head and stuck out her tongue.

‘Merciful Buddha!

‘You’d need ten or so chickens just to go with it. No wonder it tastes like that!’

Talking and laughing, she slowly finished the wine, but went on examining the cup with great interest.

‘You haven’t had enough fun yet,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Have another cup!’

‘Oh, no!’ Grannie Liu said quickly. ‘That would be the death of me. I’m only admiring the pattern. How ever did they make it?’

‘Now you’ve finished the wine,’ Faithful asked with a smile, ‘what wood is the cup made of, after all?’

‘No wonder you don’t know, miss,’ Grannie Liu said, laughing. ‘Living behind golden gates and embroidered curtains, how would you know one wood from another? We spend our lives with trees for neighbours. When we’re sleepy, we lay our heads on them; when we’re tired, we lean against them; when the crops fail and we’re hungry, we even eat them. We see them every day, hear them every day and talk about them every day. So I can tell good from bad and true from false. Let me have a look.’

As she spoke, she studied it closely for some time.

‘A family like yours would never have any of the cheap stuff. You wouldn’t keep wood that was easy to come by. Now, judging by the weight of this cup, it certainly isn’t poplar. It must be yellow pine.’

The whole company burst out laughing.

An older serving-woman came up to Grandmother Jia.

‘The girls are all at Lotus Pavilion, ma’am. They ask whether they should begin playing or wait a little longer.’

‘Why, I’d quite forgotten them!’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘Tell them to begin.’

The woman answered and went away. Soon the lingering notes of flutes and pipes rose with the sound of mouth-organs and transverse flutes. The breeze was fresh and the air clear. Coming through the trees and over the water, the music could not but lift the spirits and open the heart. Bao-yu was the first to give way to it. He took up his wine pot, poured a cup and drained it in one mouthful. He poured another and was about to drink when Lady Wang, who also wanted some, told a servant to bring her warm wine instead. Bao-yu quickly carried his own cup over and held it to her lips. She took two sips from his hand. When the warm wine arrived, Bao-yu returned to his seat. Lady Wang took up the warmed pot and rose from the table. Everyone else rose too, including Aunt Xue. Grandmother Jia at once told Li Wan and Xi-feng to take the pot from her.

‘Let your aunt sit down. Then everyone can be comfortable.’

At this, Lady Wang handed the pot to Xi-feng and sat down again.

‘Let everyone have a couple of cups,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘This really is a pleasant day.’

She raised her cup to Aunt Xue, then turned to Xiang-yun and Bao-chai.

‘You two girls must have a cup as well. Your younger cousin may not be much of a drinker, but don’t let her off.’

As she spoke, she emptied her own cup. Xiang-yun, Bao-chai and Dai-yu all drained theirs too. Between the music and the wine, Grannie Liu was so delighted that she began waving her arms and tapping her feet. Bao-yu left his seat and went over to Dai-yu.

‘Look at Grannie Liu!’ he said, laughing.

‘In ancient times,’ Dai-yu replied with a smile, ‘when the sages’ music sounded, all the beasts danced. Today we have only one cow.’

The girls all laughed.

Presently the music stopped. Aunt Xue rose from the table.

‘I think we’ve all had enough wine,’ she said, smiling. ‘Let’s take a stroll before we sit down again.’

Grandmother Jia had been wanting a walk herself. They all rose and followed her out to enjoy the garden. Wishing to give Grannie Liu some diversion, Grandmother Jia took her by the hand and wandered for a while among the trees in front of the rockery, telling her what this tree was, what that rock was, and what those flowers were. Grannie Liu took it all in, then said to Grandmother Jia:

‘Who would have thought it? In town it isn’t just the people who are grand. Even the birds are grand. Once a bird comes here to you, it turns handsome and learns to talk as well.’

No one understood. They asked which birds had turned handsome and learnt to talk.

‘The one with green feathers and a red beak on the gold perch under the veranda is a parrot,’ said Grannie Liu. ‘I know that. But how did that black old crow in the cage grow a phoenix’s crest and learn to talk too?’

They all began to laugh.

The maids came to ask them to have some refreshments.

‘After those cups of wine I’m not hungry,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘Still, bring them here, and everyone can have a little as they please.’

The maids brought two small tables and two little food-boxes. When these were opened, each contained two kinds of food. In one were lotus-root-starch cakes with osmanthus sugar, and pine-kernel rolls made with goose fat. In the other were little dumplings about a cun across. Grandmother Jia asked what the filling was, and the serving-women quickly answered that it was crab.

‘All that grease!’ she said with a frown. ‘Who could eat such things?’

The other kind, little pastries in assorted shapes fried in butter, did not please her either. She invited Aunt Xue to help herself, but Aunt Xue took only one cake. Grandmother Jia chose a roll, took no more than a taste and handed the remaining half to a maid. Grannie Liu, struck by the delicate openwork of the little pastries, picked one in the shape of a peony.

‘The cleverest girls where we live couldn’t cut a thing like this out of paper,’ she said, laughing.

‘I want to eat it, but I can’t bear to. I ought to wrap some up and take them home for the girls to copy their patterns.’

Everyone laughed.

‘I’ll give you a jarful when you go home,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘For now, eat that one while it’s hot.’

The others chose a piece or two of whatever they liked and had done. Grannie Liu had never eaten such things before, and they were so daintily made that they scarcely seemed to make a heap on the dishes. By the time she and Ban-er had tried a little of each, half a dishful was gone. Xi-feng had the rest gathered onto two dishes and into a compartmented box, and sent to Wen-guan and the others. Just then the nurse brought Da-jie in her arms, and everyone amused the child for a while. Da-jie had been playing with a large pomelo. When she caught sight of the finger citron Ban-er was holding, she wanted that instead. A maid coaxed her, saying she would fetch it, but Da-jie could not wait and began to cry. Everyone hurried to give Ban-er the pomelo and coax the citron from him. Only when Da-jie had it did she stop. Ban-er had been playing with the citron for some time and now had both hands full of fruit to eat. The pomelo, so round and fragrant, seemed even more fun. He went off kicking it about like a ball and wanted the citron no longer. After tea, Grandmother Jia and the others took Grannie Liu on to Green Bower Hermitage.

Adamantina hurried out to welcome them. In the courtyard they found the flowers and trees flourishing.

‘You can tell these are people devoted to religious practice,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘With nothing else to do, they are always tending things. It looks even better than anywhere else.’

As she spoke, she made her way towards the eastern meditation hall. Adamantina smilingly invited them further in.

‘We’ve just been eating meat and drinking wine,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘You have the Bodhisattva in there. It would be a sin to offend him. We’ll sit here. Bring us some of your good tea, and we’ll leave after a cup.’

Adamantina hurried to brew it. Bao-yu watched closely to see how she went about things. She herself carried a little begonia-shaped tea tray of carved lacquer, picked out in gold with cloud-dragons offering long life. On it stood a small lidded cup in five-colour Chenghua ware, which she presented to Grandmother Jia.

‘I don’t drink Lu’an tea,’ said Grandmother Jia.

‘I know,’ Adamantina answered with a smile. ‘This is Old Man’s Eyebrows.’

Grandmother Jia took it and asked what water she had used.

‘Rainwater collected and kept from last year,’ Adamantina replied, smiling.

Grandmother Jia drank half the cup, then smilingly passed it to Grannie Liu.

‘Try this tea.’

Grannie Liu swallowed the rest in one gulp.

‘It’s good enough,’ she said, laughing, ‘but a little weak. It would be better if you boiled it up stronger.’

Grandmother Jia and the others all laughed.

The rest were served in matching thin-bodied white lidded bowls from the imperial kilns. Adamantina tugged at the fronts of Bao-chai’s and Dai-yu’s clothes, and the two followed her out. Bao-yu quietly came after them. She invited them into a side room, where Bao-chai sat on the couch and Dai-yu on Adamantina’s rush meditation cushion. Adamantina herself fanned the little stove until the water boiled, then made a separate pot of tea. Bao-yu came in, smiling.

‘So you’re having private tea all to yourselves!’

‘Here you are again, cadging tea,’ the two girls said, laughing. ‘There’s none for you.’

Adamantina was just going to fetch cups when the old woman who served at the hermitage came in with the cups from the other room.

‘Don’t put that Chenghua cup away,’ Adamantina told her at once. ‘Leave it outside.’

Bao-yu understood: because Grannie Liu had drunk from it, she thought it dirty and would not have it. Then he saw her bring out two other cups. One had a single handle at the side, and was engraved with the three characters for ‘Banpao cup’ in clerical script. On the back was a line of small regular characters: ‘A treasured plaything of Wang Kai of the Jin’; and another line of small characters read, ‘Seen in the imperial treasury by Su Shi of Meishan, in the fourth month of the fifth year of Yuanfeng of the Song.’1

Adamantina filled the Banpao cup and handed it to Bao-chai. The other was shaped like a small begging-bowl and likewise bore three engraved characters, this time in the drooping-pearl style of seal script: ‘Xingxi bowl’. Adamantina filled it for Dai-yu. For Bao-yu she used the green-jade cup she herself had been drinking from every day.2

‘They say that “in worldly matters all are equal”,’ Bao-yu said, laughing. ‘But those two get such rare old treasures, and I get a common vessel.’

‘A common vessel?’ said Adamantina. ‘I don’t mean to boast, but I doubt whether your whole house could produce another common vessel like this.’

‘As the saying goes, “Follow the customs of the place you’re in”,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Once we come here to you, gold, jade, pearls and jewels must naturally all be dismissed as common things.’

Adamantina was delighted at this. She found an enormous beaker carved from a single bamboo root as a coiling dragon, with nine bends, ten rings and a hundred and twenty joints.

‘This is the only one left,’ she said, laughing. ‘Could you drink this whole beaker?’

‘Yes, I could!’ Bao-yu answered eagerly, delighted.

‘Even if you could, I haven’t that much tea to waste,’ Adamantina said with a laugh. ‘Haven’t you heard? “One cup is for savouring; two make you a dolt merely quenching his thirst; three are for watering cattle and mules.” What would drinking this whole beaker make you?’

Bao-chai, Dai-yu and Bao-yu all laughed.

Adamantina took the pot and poured only about a cupful into the great beaker. Bao-yu sipped it carefully. It did indeed seem incomparably light and delicate, and he could not praise it enough.

‘You have those two to thank for the tea you’ve had today,’ Adamantina said seriously. ‘If you came by yourself, I wouldn’t give you any.’

‘I know that very well,’ Bao-yu answered with a smile. ‘I don’t owe you any thanks. I need only thank them.’

‘Then you understand,’ said Adamantina.

Dai-yu asked, ‘Is this last year’s rainwater too?’

Adamantina gave a cold laugh.

‘Someone like you, and such a philistine! You can’t even tell the water by its taste. This is snow I collected from plum blossoms five years ago, when I was living at Panxiang Temple at Xuanmu. All I gathered filled that one ghost-face-blue flower jar. I could never bear to use it, so I buried it underground and only opened it this summer. I’ve used it once before. This is the second time. How can you not taste the difference? How could rainwater kept over from last year be as light and delicate as this? How could one drink it?’

Knowing her eccentric disposition, Dai-yu thought it best neither to say much more nor to sit much longer. When she had finished her tea, she asked Bao-chai to leave with her.

Bao-yu spoke to Adamantina with a conciliatory smile.

‘That teacup may be dirty, but wouldn’t it be a pity simply to throw it away? Why not give it to the poor old woman? She could sell it and have something to live on. Would that do, do you think?’

Adamantina considered, then nodded.

‘Very well. Luckily I’ve never drunk from it myself. If I had used it, I would sooner smash it than give it to her. If you want her to have it, I shan’t interfere. I’ll give it to you. Only take it away quickly.’

‘Of course,’ said Bao-yu. ‘You couldn’t go talking to her and handing things back and forth. Then you would be dirtied too. Just leave it to me.’

Adamantina told someone to bring it and give it to him. Bao-yu took it.

‘When we’ve gone,’ he added, ‘shall I send a few of the boys to fetch some buckets of water from the stream to wash the ground?’

‘That would be better still,’ Adamantina said, smiling. ‘Only tell them to leave the water by the wall outside the hermitage gate. They mustn’t come in.’

‘Of course not.’

He slipped the cup into his sleeve, then gave it to a little maid from Grandmother Jia’s rooms to hold.

‘When Grannie Liu goes home tomorrow, give her this to take with her.’

By the time he had made this clear, Grandmother Jia had come out and was ready to leave.

Adamantina made little effort to keep them. She saw them through the hermitage gate, then turned back and shut it. We shall say no more of her. Grandmother Jia, feeling tired, told Lady Wang and Ying-chun and her sisters to keep Aunt Xue company over some more wine, while she went to rest at Sweet-rice Village. Xi-feng at once sent for the little bamboo chair. Grandmother Jia sat down in it, and two serving-women lifted it and carried her off, surrounded by Xi-feng, Li Wan and a crowd of maids and older women. We shall leave them there. Aunt Xue now took her leave too. Lady Wang dismissed Wen-guan and the others, and had the compartmented food-boxes distributed among the maids. She herself took the opportunity to rest, reclining informally on the couch where Grandmother Jia had been sitting. She told a little maid to lower the blind, then to massage her legs.

‘If there’s any word from Her Old Ladyship, call me.’

With that, she too fell asleep where she lay.

Bao-yu, Xiang-yun and the others watched the maids set the food-boxes on the rocks. Some sat on the rocks, some on the grass, some leaned against trees, and some settled by the water. It was a lively gathering. Presently Faithful came to take Grannie Liu round the garden, and everyone hurried after them to enjoy the fun. When they reached the archway bearing the words ‘Villa for the Family Visit’, Grannie Liu exclaimed:

‘Oh, my! There’s a great temple here too!’

She dropped to the ground and kowtowed. They doubled up with laughter.

‘What are you laughing at? I know all the characters on that arch. We have plenty of temples like this where I come from, with just such archways. The writing is the name of the temple.’

‘Then tell us which temple this is,’ they said, laughing.

Grannie Liu looked up and pointed to the inscription.

‘Aren’t those the four characters for “Precious Hall of the Jade Emperor”?’

They laughed, clapping their hands and stamping their feet, and were ready to tease her further when a sudden rumbling began in Grannie Liu’s belly. She hastily caught hold of a little maid, asked for two sheets of paper and started undoing her clothes. Still laughing, everyone cried out:

‘You can’t do that here!’

They quickly told an older serving-woman to take her off towards the north-east. Having shown her where to go, the woman was glad to slip away and rest.

Grannie Liu had drunk some wine, and rice wine did not agree with her constitution. Moreover, she had eaten a great deal of rich food, then drunk several bowls of tea to quench her thirst. A thorough bout of diarrhoea was the result. She had to squat for a long time before she was finished. Coming out of the privy, she felt the wine take hold in the breeze. At her age, too, rising suddenly after squatting so long made her eyes swim and her head spin. She could no longer make out the way.

She looked all round. Everywhere were trees, rocks, towers and houses, but she could not tell which way led where. All she could do was choose a pebble path and walk slowly along it. When she reached a building, she could find no door. After searching for some time, she noticed a stretch of bamboo trellis.

‘They have bean frames here too,’ she thought.

Still thinking this, she followed the flower trellis until she found a moon gate and went through. Suddenly a narrow pool lay before her, only seven or eight chi wide, with stone banks and clear green water flowing away to one side. A slab of white stone spanned it. Grannie Liu crossed the slab, followed the pebble walk round two bends and came to a door. She went in, and there, facing her, was a girl coming to meet her with a broad smile.

‘You girls left me behind,’ Grannie Liu said at once, smiling. ‘I’ve had to knock my head about finding my way here.’

The girl made no answer. Grannie Liu hurried forward to take her hand. Thump! She struck the wooden partition and gave her head a painful knock. Looking closely, she saw that it was a painting.

‘So paintings can stand right out, as alive as that!’ she thought.

She went on looking and thinking, then felt it with her hand. It was perfectly flat all over. She nodded and gave two sighs of wonder.

Only when she turned round did she find a small doorway hung with a soft spring-green curtain scattered with flowers. Grannie Liu lifted it and went in. Looking up, she saw that the walls on every side were all intricate openwork, with qin, swords, vases and incense burners set against them, brocade coverings and gauze screens, glittering gold and gleaming pearls. Even the bricks beneath her feet were green and carved with flowers. Her eyes were more dazzled than ever. She looked for a door to get out, but where was there a door? Bookshelves on her left, a screen on her right. At last she found a doorway behind the screen and turned through it, only to see her daughter’s mother-in-law coming in from outside to meet her. Grannie Liu was astonished.

‘You must have wondered why I hadn’t come home these last few days. Fancy your finding your way here! Which of the young ladies brought you in?’

Her fellow grandmother merely smiled, without answering.

‘You’ve seen so little of the world!’ Grannie Liu said with a laugh. ‘The moment you saw the lovely flowers in this garden, you couldn’t rest till you’d stuck them all over your head.’

Still the other woman said nothing. Suddenly a thought struck her.

‘I’ve often heard that very rich families have a thing called a dressing-mirror. Could that be me in the mirror?’

She put out a hand to feel it and looked more closely. So it was: the mirror was set in the middle of a purple sandalwood partition, carved in openwork on all four sides.

‘But if this blocks the way, how am I to get out?’ she said, feeling about with her hands as she spoke.

The mirror had a Western mechanism by which it could be opened and closed. As Grannie Liu groped about, she happened to press with just the right force in just the right place. The catch sprang, the mirror swung aside and a doorway appeared. Astonished and delighted, she stepped through. There before her was a bed with the most exquisite hangings. By now she was thoroughly tipsy and tired from walking. She plumped herself down on the bed, meaning only to rest for a moment. But she could not help herself: she swayed backwards and forwards, her eyes grew dim, and, rolling onto her side, she fell fast asleep on the bed.

Meanwhile the others had waited in vain for her return. Ban-er, finding his grandmother gone, began to cry in distress.

‘Surely she hasn’t fallen into the privy?’ they said, laughing. ‘Send someone to look, quickly.’

Two serving-women were sent, but came back saying she was not there. They searched everywhere without finding her. Aroma thought over the paths she might have taken.

‘She must be drunk and have lost her way, and gone down this path into our back courtyard. If she went through the flower trellis and in at the back door, she might bump her head, but at least the little maids would know. If she missed the trellis and went further south-west, it would be all right if she found a way out; if not, she could wander round for a good long time. I’d better go and see.’

Thinking this, she went back and called out as she entered the House of Green Delights. But the little maids who should have been in the rooms had slipped off to play.

Aroma went straight indoors. As she turned past the lattice partition with its varied patterns, she heard thunderous snoring. She hurried in. The room smelt of wine and farts. Looking round, she saw Grannie Liu sprawled on her back across the bed, arms and legs flung wide. Aroma was badly startled. She rushed over and shook her awake with all her might. Grannie Liu opened her eyes in alarm. At the sight of Aroma she scrambled up.

‘I’ve done wrong, miss! But I haven’t dirtied the bed or the curtains.’

As she spoke, she brushed at them with her hand. Afraid of attracting attention and letting Bao-yu hear of it, Aroma waved at her to be quiet. She quickly put three or four handfuls of Baihe incense into the tripod burner and replaced its cover. After tidying a little, relieved that there had been no vomiting, she said with a quiet smile:

‘It’s all right. I’m here. Come out with me.’

Grannie Liu followed her into the little maids’ room. Aroma told her to sit down.

‘Just say you were drunk and lay down on the rockery stones for a nap.’

Grannie Liu agreed and said she understood.

Aroma gave her two bowls of tea. Only then did she feel sober again.

‘Which young lady’s bedroom is this, so beautiful?’ she asked. ‘It was like being in a heavenly palace.’

Aroma smiled a little.

‘That? It’s Master Bao’s bedroom.’

Grannie Liu was too frightened to say a word. Aroma took her out by the front. When they met the others, she merely said she had found her asleep on the grass and brought her back. Nobody pursued the matter, and that was that.

Presently Grandmother Jia awoke, and supper was laid at Sweet-rice Village. Feeling languid, she would not eat, but returned to her own rooms to rest in the little open bamboo carrying-chair. She told Xi-feng and the others to have their supper, and only then did the women go back into the garden.
