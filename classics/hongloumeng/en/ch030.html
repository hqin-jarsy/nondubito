Chapter 30 Bao-chai seizes on a fan to aim two barbs; Ling-guan traces ‘qiang’, and her absorption draws in an outsider

Chapter end

Now Dai-yu regretted her quarrel with Bao-yu, but could hardly go and seek him out. Day and night she was miserable, as though something had gone missing. Nightingale guessed what was on her mind and tried to reason with her.

‘If you ask me, miss, you were a little too hasty the other day. Other people may not know Master Bao’s temper, but surely we do. It isn’t just once or twice that there’s been trouble over that jade.’

‘Pah!’ said Dai-yu. ‘So you’re taking his side and finding fault with me! What was hasty about me?’

‘Why did you have to cut the tassel off, when there was nothing wrong with it?’ Nightingale asked, smiling. ‘That makes him three parts in the wrong and you seven. As I see it, he’s always good to you. It’s only because you’re so easily offended, and so often put him in the wrong for nothing, that things have come to this.’

Dai-yu was about to answer when someone called at the courtyard gate. Nightingale listened for a moment and smiled.

‘That’s Master Bao’s voice. He must have come to apologise.’

‘Don’t open the gate!’ said Dai-yu.

‘There you go again, miss. It’s such a hot day, with the sun beating down like that. What if he falls ill out there in the sun?’

As she spoke she went out to open the gate. It was indeed Bao-yu. She let him in with a smile.

‘I thought Master Bao was never going to darken our door again. And here you are!’

‘You make so much of the tiniest thing,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘Why on earth shouldn’t I come? Even if I died, my ghost would come a hundred times a day. Is Cousin Lin quite well again?’

‘Her illness is better. Her temper isn’t, though.’

‘I know what’s troubling her,’ Bao-yu said with a smile.

He went in as he spoke and found Dai-yu on her bed, crying again. She had not been crying before; but when she heard he had come, her heart ached in spite of herself, and the tears began to roll down, beyond her power to stop them.

Bao-yu came smiling up to the bed.

‘Are you quite well again, cousin?’

Dai-yu went on wiping her tears, without answering. Bao-yu edged himself on to the side of the bed and smiled.

‘I know you’re not angry with me.

‘Only if I stay away, other people will think we’ve quarrelled again. And if we wait for them to come and make peace between us, won’t that make us seem like strangers? You’d better beat me or scold me now, just as you please. Only don’t refuse to speak to me.’

He called her ‘dear cousin’ tens of thousands of times. Dai-yu had meant never to have anything more to do with him. But now, when he said that letting other people know of their quarrel would make them seem like strangers, she could see once again that he felt himself closer to her than anyone else. Unable to hold back her tears, she said:

‘You needn’t coax me. From now on, I shan’t presume to be close to you any more, Master Bao. You can think of me as gone.’

‘Where would you go?’ Bao-yu asked with a smile.

‘Home.’

‘I’ll go with you,’ he said, still smiling.

‘I’ll die.’

‘If you die, I’ll become a monk!’

Dai-yu’s expression hardened at once.

‘You must have a death wish yourself, talking such nonsense! You’ve several sisters of your own, older and younger. If they all died one day, how many bodies have you got to turn into monks? I shall tell people what you’ve said and let them judge.’

Bao-yu knew he had spoken rashly. It was too late to regret it now. His face flushed deep red, and he hung his head, not daring to make a sound. Fortunately no one else was in the room. Dai-yu stared hard at him for a long while, too angry to utter a word. When she saw his face swelling purple with the strain, she clenched her teeth, jabbed him hard in the forehead with one finger and gave a little snort.

‘You, you—’ she said through her teeth.

She had scarcely got the two words out before she sighed again and took up her handkerchief to wipe her eyes. Bao-yu’s heart was already full of things he could not say, and now he had blundered and was bitterly sorry. The poke in the forehead, her attempt to speak, the words that would not come, her sighing and weeping: all this moved him too, until tears rolled down his own face. He meant to wipe them away with his handkerchief, only to find he had forgotten it again, and used his sleeve instead. Though she was crying, Dai-yu noticed at once. He was wearing a brand-new mauve gauze robe, and there he was wiping his tears on it! Still wiping her own eyes, she turned, took a fine silk handkerchief lying beside her pillow and flung it into his lap. Without a word, she covered her face and went on crying.

Bao-yu hastily caught the handkerchief and wiped his tears. He edged a little closer, took one of Dai-yu’s hands and smiled.

‘I’m torn to pieces inside, and still you cry! Come, let’s go to Grandmother together.’

Dai-yu flung his hand away.

‘Who wants you pawing at them? You’re growing older every day, and still you’re so shameless, without the least idea how to behave.’

Before she had finished, a voice called, ‘All right again!’

Both of them jumped. Turning round, they saw Xi-feng come bounding in, laughing.

‘Grandmother’s been blaming heaven and earth, insisting I come and see whether you’ve made it up. I told her there was no need. Give them three days, I said, and they’ll make it up by themselves. She scolded me and said I was lazy. Well, here I am, and it’s just as I told her. I can’t see what the two of you ever have to quarrel about. Friends for three days, enemies for two! The older you get, the more childish you become. Here you are holding hands and crying. Why were you a pair of fighting cocks yesterday? Come along to Grandmother and set the poor old lady’s mind at rest.’

She took Dai-yu by the hand and started off. Dai-yu turned to call her maids, but not one was there.

‘What do you want them for?’ said Xi-feng. ‘I’m here to wait on you.’

Talking all the while, she pulled her away.

Bao-yu followed them out through the garden gate. When they reached Grandmother Jia, Xi-feng said, laughing:

‘I told you no one need trouble about them: they’d make it up themselves. But you wouldn’t believe me, Grandmother. You insisted I go and mediate. And when I got there to make peace, what did I find but the two of them already apologising to each other! Smiling at each other, pouring out their feelings, like “a hawk with its claws round a kite’s feet”. Each had the other hooked. What need was there for me to make peace?’

Everyone in the room laughed.

Bao-chai was there too. Dai-yu sat down beside Grandmother Jia without a word. Bao-yu, having nothing to say, turned to Bao-chai with a smile.

‘It was Cousin Xue’s birthday, and I had to be ill just then. I had no other present to give him, and couldn’t even go and kowtow. He didn’t know I was ill. He must have thought I was lazy and making excuses. If he’s cross with me later, you’ll explain for me, won’t you, cousin?’

‘What a fuss!’ Bao-chai said, smiling. ‘Even if you’d wanted to come, we couldn’t have put you to the trouble, much less when you were ill. You cousins see each other every day. Making a point of such things would only put you on a formal footing.’

‘As long as you understand, cousin, that’s all right,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘Why aren’t you watching the plays?’

‘I can’t stand the heat. I watched two scenes and was terribly hot. I wanted to go, but the guests wouldn’t leave, so I had to make the excuse that I wasn’t feeling well and come away.’

Hearing this, Bao-yu could not help looking awkward. He tried to cover his embarrassment with another smiling remark.

‘No wonder they compare you to Noble Consort Yang. You’re plump and afraid of the heat too.’

Bao-chai was furious, but could hardly act on it. After a moment’s thought her face reddened. She gave two cold little laughs.

‘I may be like Noble Consort Yang, but I haven’t a fine elder or younger brother to play Yang Guo-zhong!’

Just then the little maid Indigo, who had lost her fan, came up to Bao-chai with a smile.

‘I’m sure you’ve hidden it, Miss Bao. Do be kind and give it back.’

Bao-chai pointed at her.

‘Mind what you’re saying! If I’d been playing with you, you might have cause to suspect me.

‘Go and ask those young ladies who are always laughing and joking with you.’

Indigo ran off at this. Bao-yu knew he had blundered again. With so many people present, he was even more embarrassed than he had been with Dai-yu. He quickly turned away and tried to make conversation with somebody else.

Dai-yu had been thoroughly pleased to hear Bao-yu tease Bao-chai and was just about to join in and have a laugh at her expense. But Indigo’s search for her fan had brought those two retorts from Bao-chai, and Dai-yu changed what she was going to say.

‘Cousin Bao,’ she asked, smiling, ‘which two scenes did you see?’

Bao-chai had noticed the look of satisfaction on Dai-yu’s face: Bao-yu’s teasing must have been just what she wanted to hear. Now this sudden question came. Bao-chai smiled.

‘I saw Li Kui scold Song Jiang and then apologise to him.’

‘You know everything, past and present,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘How can you not know the name of that play, instead of telling us all that? It’s called Bearing Thorns to Beg Pardon.’

‘So it’s called Bearing Thorns to Beg Pardon!’ Bao-chai said, smiling. ‘You know everything, past and present, so of course you know about bearing thorns to beg pardon. I know nothing about bearing thorns to beg pardon!’

Before she had finished, Bao-yu and Dai-yu, whose consciences were uneasy, had already blushed scarlet.

Xi-feng understood little of these matters, but a glance at the three of them was enough to tell her what was meant. She too smiled and asked the company:

‘Who’s been eating raw ginger in this sweltering weather?’

No one understood.

‘No one’s been eating raw ginger,’ they said.

Xi-feng deliberately put her hand to her cheek and looked surprised.

‘If no one’s been eating raw ginger, why is there such a sting?’

This made Bao-yu and Dai-yu feel more uncomfortable still. Bao-chai was about to say something else, but Bao-yu looked so thoroughly abashed, so altered, that she could not go on. She brought it to an end with a smile. None of the others had understood what the four of them were saying, so they let it pass.

Presently Bao-chai and Xi-feng left. Dai-yu turned to Bao-yu with a smile.

‘Now you’ve had a taste of someone fiercer than I am. Did you think everyone was as slow-witted and tongue-tied as me, letting people say whatever they please?’

Bao-yu was already feeling foolish because Bao-chai had taken his words amiss. Dai-yu’s question only made him more irritable. He wanted to retort, but was afraid that she too would take it amiss. He had to swallow his anger and went straight out, listless and dejected.

It was the height of summer, the morning meal was over, and the long day had left most of the household, masters and servants alike, drowsy and tired. Bao-yu wandered with his hands behind his back. Wherever he went, not a sound was to be heard. Leaving Grandmother Jia’s rooms, he went west through a passage hall to Xi-feng’s courtyard. When he reached the gate he found it shut. He knew her regular custom in hot weather was to rest at midday for a double-hour. It would not do to go in, so he passed through a corner gate and entered Lady Wang’s principal room. Several maids were nodding over the needlework in their hands. In the inner room Lady Wang was asleep on a summer couch, with Golden seated beside her patting her legs. Golden too was swaying drowsily, her eyes half closed.

Bao-yu went softly up to her and took off one of her dangling earrings. Golden opened her eyes and saw who it was.

‘As sleepy as all that?’ he whispered with a smile.

She smiled with her lips pressed together, waved him away and closed her eyes again. The sight of her made him unwilling to leave. He quietly craned his neck to make sure Lady Wang’s eyes were shut, then reached into the purse at his side, took out some Fragrant Snow Mouth-Moistening Pills and slipped them into Golden’s mouth. Without opening her eyes, she simply held them there. Bao-yu came closer, took her hand and whispered, smiling:

‘Tomorrow I’ll ask Mother to give you to me. Then we can be together.’

Golden did not answer.

‘Or I’ll ask as soon as Mother wakes up,’ he went on.

Golden opened her eyes, gave him a push and smiled.

‘What’s the hurry? “A golden hairpin may fall down a well, but what’s yours will still be yours.” Don’t you even understand that saying?

‘I’ll tell you something worth your while. Go to the little courtyard to the east and catch Huan and Cai-yun together.’

‘Let him do as he pleases,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘I only want to stay with you.’

Lady Wang suddenly rolled over and sat up. She struck Golden across the face, then pointed at her and scolded:

‘You filthy little whore! It’s you who teach decent young gentlemen to misbehave.’

The moment Bao-yu saw his mother sit up, he had fled like a wisp of smoke.

Golden’s cheek burned. She dared not say a word. Hearing Lady Wang awake, the other maids hurried in. Lady Wang called to Silver:

‘Fetch your mother and tell her to take your elder sister away.’

Golden dropped to her knees, weeping.

‘I’ll never dare do it again. Beat me or scold me as you please, Your Ladyship. Only don’t send me away, and I shall count it heaven’s own mercy. I’ve been with you for ten years and more. If I’m driven out now, how can I ever face anyone again?’

Lady Wang was indeed a kind, generous and compassionate woman, and had never once struck any of her maids. But now she had suddenly seen Golden behave with a shamelessness of the very kind she had most detested all her life. Unable to contain her anger, she had struck her once and scolded her. For all Golden’s pleading, she would not keep her. In the end she sent for Golden’s mother, old Mrs Bai, to take her away. Golden went out in shame and humiliation. We need say no more of her for the present.

To return to Bao-yu: when he saw Lady Wang wake, he had hurried off into Prospect Garden in discomfiture. The blazing sun was overhead; the shadows of the trees spread over the ground. Cicadas filled his ears, but no human voice disturbed the stillness. He had just reached a rose trellis when he heard someone sob. Puzzled, he stopped and listened carefully. There was indeed someone on the far side of the trellis. It was the fifth month, when the roses were thick with leaves and blossom. Bao-yu quietly peered through a gap in the fence. A girl was crouching beneath the flowers, scratching at the earth with a hairpin, the kind used to fasten up the hair, while tears ran silently down her face.

‘Can this be another foolish girl come to bury flowers like Little Pin-pin?’ Bao-yu wondered. Then he sighed to himself. ‘If she really is burying flowers, it’s Dong Shi copying a beauty’s frown. Not only is there nothing new in it: it’s positively tiresome.’

He was about to call out, ‘You needn’t copy Miss Lin!’ Fortunately, before the words had left his mouth, he looked again. The girl’s face was unfamiliar. She was not a maid, but seemed to be one of the twelve girls learning to act, though he could not tell which sort of role she played: male, female, painted-face or clown. He quickly put out his tongue and covered his mouth.

‘Luckily I didn’t blurt it out. Both times before, speaking too soon was what made Pin-pin angry and Bao-chai take offence. If I offend these girls as well, I’ll feel more foolish than ever.’1

As he thought this, he was vexed that he could not recognise her. He looked more closely. Her brows drew together like spring hills, her eyes narrowed like autumn water; her face was delicate, her waist slender, and her graceful, swaying figure was very like Dai-yu’s. Already he could not bear to leave her and simply gazed, entranced. Though she was scratching the ground with her golden hairpin, she was not digging a hole to bury flowers. She was tracing a character in the earth. Bao-yu followed the hairpin up and down, watching each upright, each cross-stroke, dot and hook. He counted: eighteen strokes. Then he used his finger to trace them on his palm in the same order, trying to make out the character. When he had finished, he thought a moment and recognised it: ‘qiang’, the character for rose. ‘She must want to write a poem or a song,’ he thought. ‘Perhaps the sight of these flowers has moved her and a couple of lines have come to her. Afraid of forgetting them in the excitement, she may be writing in the earth while she works them out. Let’s see what she writes next.’ He went on thinking and watching. The girl was still tracing away, but whatever she wrote, it was always ‘qiang’.2

He watched again. Still it was ‘qiang’. The girl inside had long since lost herself in what she was doing, writing one after another, until she had written several thousand ‘qiang’ characters. The watcher outside, without knowing it, had become just as absorbed. His eyes moved ceaselessly with the hairpin.

‘She must have some great trouble on her mind that she cannot put into words, to go on like this,’ he thought. ‘If this is what shows on the outside, what torment must there be within?

‘She looks so frail. How can her heart bear such suffering? If only I could take some of it from you!’

In the dog days the weather was changeable: a single cloud could bring rain. Suddenly a cool breeze passed, and a shower came swishing down. Bao-yu saw the water dripping from the girl’s head; her gauze clothes were soaked in an instant.

‘Rain now!’ he thought. ‘How can a girl so delicate stand the shock of a sudden downpour?’

He could not help calling out:

‘Stop writing! Look, it’s pouring. You’re wet through.’

Startled, the girl looked up. Someone outside the flowers was telling her to stop writing because it was raining hard. Bao-yu had a handsome, delicate face; moreover, the profusion of flowers and leaves hid him above and below, leaving only half his face visible. The girl took him for a maid, never imagining it was Bao-yu.

‘Thank you for reminding me, sister,’ she said with a smile. ‘But have you something to shelter you out there?’

Her words brought him to his senses. He gave a cry. Only then did he realise he was cold all over.

He looked down and saw that he too was soaked through. ‘Oh dear!’ he said, and ran all the way back to the House of Green Delights, still worrying that the girl had nowhere to shelter from the rain.

The next day was the Dragon Boat Festival. Wen-guan and the other girls, twelve in all, had been given leave from their lessons and had come to amuse themselves about the garden. Two of them, Bao-guan, who played young men, and Yu-guan, who played principal female roles, happened to be at the House of Green Delights, joking with Aroma, when the downpour trapped them there. They had all blocked the drain so that water collected in the courtyard. Some caught waterfowl and others herded them in: green-headed ducks, brightly patterned mandarin ducks, and richly coloured mandarin ducks. They stitched their wings together and let them loose in the courtyard to play, shutting the gate. Aroma and the others were laughing under the covered walk.

Finding the gate shut, Bao-yu knocked, but everyone inside was laughing too much to hear. He called for a long while and hammered on the gate till it boomed before they finally heard him. They reckoned Bao-yu could not possibly be coming back just then.

‘Who’s knocking at this hour?’ Aroma called, laughing. ‘There’s no one to let you in.’

‘It’s me!’ said Bao-yu.

‘That sounds like Miss Bao,’ said Musk.

‘Nonsense!’ said Skybright. ‘What would Miss Bao be coming here for now?’

‘Let me peep through the crack,’ said Aroma. ‘If it’s someone we can let in, I’ll open it. If not, they can stay in the rain.’

She went along the covered walk to the gate and looked out. There was Bao-yu, as bedraggled as a chicken caught in the rain. She was at once alarmed and amused. Hastily opening the gate, she doubled up with laughter and clapped her hands.

‘What are you doing running about in rain like this? How were we to know it was you, sir?’

Bao-yu was seething. He had set his heart on giving whoever opened the gate a few kicks. When it opened, he did not look properly to see who was there, still taking it for one of the little maids. He raised his foot and kicked her in the ribs.

Aroma cried out. Bao-yu went on scolding:

‘You low little creatures! I’ve let you get away with so much that you’ve grown pleased with yourselves. You’re not afraid of me at all. Now you even make a joke of me!’

As he spoke, he looked down and saw that it was Aroma who was crying. Only then did he realise he had kicked the wrong person. He hastily put on a smile.

‘Oh! It was you who came! Where did I catch you?’

Aroma had never even been harshly spoken to. Now, without warning, Bao-yu had lost his temper and kicked her, in front of so many people. Shame, anger and pain left her not knowing where to put herself. She wanted to do something, but reckoned that he probably had not meant to kick her. She had to master herself.

‘You didn’t catch me,’ she said. ‘Go and change your clothes.’

Bao-yu went into his room and began to undress, saying with a smile:

‘In all my life, this is the first time I’ve lost my temper and hit anyone. And to think it had to be you!’

Aroma bore the pain as she helped him change. She too smiled.

‘I’m always the one to begin things, so naturally I should come first, whether it’s something big or small, good or bad. Only don’t let hitting me give you a taste for it, so that you start hitting other people tomorrow.’

‘I didn’t mean it just now,’ said Bao-yu.

‘Who said you meant it? Opening and shutting the gate is usually those little maids’ business.

‘They’re so used to playing the fool, they make you grind your teeth. And they aren’t afraid of anything. You thought it was one of them. A kick might do them good, frighten them a little. It was my fault for playing about just now and telling them not to open the gate.’

By now the rain had stopped, and Bao-guan and Yu-guan had already gone. The pain under Aroma’s ribs made her feel wretched; she could scarcely eat her evening meal. When she undressed to bathe that night, she saw a bruise as big as a bowl on her side. It frightened her, but she did not like to make it known. Presently she went to bed. The pain followed her into her dreams, and she could not help moaning in her sleep.

Although Bao-yu said he had not meant it, the sight of Aroma so listless kept him from sleeping peacefully either. Hearing her cry out in the night, he knew he must have kicked her hard. He got out of bed and quietly brought a lamp to look at her. Just as he reached her bedside, she coughed twice and spat out a mouthful of phlegm. With another moan she opened her eyes. The sight of Bao-yu startled her.

‘What are you doing?’

‘You were crying out in your sleep. I must have kicked you hard. Let me have a look.’

‘My head’s swimming, and there’s a rank, sweet taste in my throat. Shine the lamp on the floor.’

Bao-yu did as she asked. On the floor lay a mouthful of fresh blood.

Bao-yu panicked. All he could say was, ‘This is terrible!’ When Aroma saw it, her own heart sank.

Translator’s notes

  1. In the proverbial story, Dong Shi tries to imitate the beautiful Xi Shi by copying her frown. The word for frowning, pin, also occurs in Dai-yu’s familiar name Pin-pin. ↩
  2. The text counts eighteen strokes for this character. Its traditional form is 薔, the first character of qiangwei, ‘rose’; the simplified form is 蔷. ↩
