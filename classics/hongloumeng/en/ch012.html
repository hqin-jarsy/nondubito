Chapter 12 Wang Xi-feng lays a cruel trap for a lovesick man; Jia Tian-xiang looks into the front of the Mirror of Love and Desire

Chapter end

Now Xi-feng was talking to Patience when someone came to announce, ‘Master Rui is here.’

‘Show him in at once,’ Xi-feng said promptly.

Jia Rui was overjoyed to find himself invited inside. He hurried in and greeted Xi-feng again and again, his face all smiles. She too made a show of eagerness, offering him a seat and tea.

The sight of Xi-feng dressed as she was made Jia Rui melt still further. With languishing eyes he asked, ‘Why hasn’t Second Brother come back yet?’

‘I don’t know what’s keeping him.’

‘Perhaps someone has detained him on the road,’ Jia Rui said with a smile, ‘and he can’t bear to come away?’

‘Perhaps. There are men who fall in love with every woman they see.’

‘You’re wrong there, Sister-in-law,’ Jia Rui said with a smile. ‘I’m not like that.’

‘How many men are there like you?’ Xi-feng asked, smiling. ‘You couldn’t pick one out of ten.’

Jia Rui scratched his ears and rubbed his cheeks in delight. ‘You must find it very dull, day after day?’

‘Indeed I do. I keep hoping someone will come and talk to me, to relieve the boredom.’

‘I’m free every day,’ he said with a smile. ‘How would it be if I came every day to keep you company?’

Xi-feng smiled. ‘You’re teasing me. As if you’d want to come here!’

‘May Heaven strike me with a thunderbolt if I tell you a single lie, Sister-in-law!

‘It was only because I had heard people say you were so formidable, and one couldn’t put a foot wrong in front of you, that I was frightened off. Now I find you so ready to laugh and talk, and so kind, how could I stay away? I’d come even if it killed me!’

‘You really are a man of understanding,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘Far better than Jia Rong and the other one. With such a pretty face, I thought he must have some sense, and the other too. But they’re a pair of fools. They haven’t the least idea what anyone feels.’

This went straight to Jia Rui’s heart. He could not help edging a little closer, peering at the purse Xi-feng wore, then asking what rings she had on.

‘Behave yourself,’ she said softly. ‘Don’t let the maids see you and laugh.’

To Jia Rui it was like hearing an imperial decree or the words of the Buddha. He hastily moved back.

‘You ought to go now,’ Xi-feng said, smiling.

‘Let me sit a little longer. How cruel you are, Sister-in-law!’

She lowered her voice again. ‘In broad daylight, with people coming and going, it’s hardly convenient for you to stay here. Go now, and come back tonight when the watches have begun. Wait quietly for me in the passage on the west side.’

Jia Rui received this like a precious treasure. ‘You won’t play me false? But so many people go through there. How can I keep out of sight?’

‘Don’t worry. I’ll give all the boys on night duty leave. Once the doors at both ends are shut, there won’t be anyone else.’

Delighted beyond measure, Jia Rui hurriedly took his leave, convinced that success was his.

At last evening came. Sure enough, he felt his way into the Rong mansion in the dark and slipped into the passage as the doors were being shut. It was indeed pitch-black and deserted. The door towards Grandmother Jia’s quarters was already bolted; only the one to the east remained open. Jia Rui strained his ears, but a long time passed and no one came. Suddenly there was a clunk: the eastern door had been bolted too. In his alarm he dared not make a sound. He could only creep out and shake the door. It was shut as tight as an iron barrel. There was no getting out now.

To north and south were the walls of great buildings, with no handhold by which he might climb over. The passage itself was bare and draughty. It was the twelfth month, the night was long, and the bitter north wind cut into his flesh and split his very bones. He all but froze to death that night.

Morning came at last. An old woman opened the eastern door and went in to call for the western one to be opened. While her back was turned, Jia Rui shot out, hugging his shoulders. Luckily it was still early and no one was up. He ran straight home by the back gate.

Jia Rui’s parents had died when he was young, and he had been brought up by his grandfather Dai-ru alone. Dai-ru was always very strict with him. He would not let him stray a step, for fear he might go drinking or gambling and neglect his studies. Now that he had stayed out all night, Dai-ru was certain he had been drinking or gambling, visiting brothels and sleeping with prostitutes. This particular adventure never entered his head, and he had been furious all night.

Jia Rui was in a cold sweat too. Once home, he had no choice but to lie. ‘I went to my mother’s brother’s house. It got dark, and they kept me for the night.’

‘You have never dared go out without telling me first,’ Dai-ru said. ‘Why did you go off on your own yesterday? That alone deserves a beating, let alone the lie!’

In his fury he gave him a full thirty or forty strokes with the board, forbade him any food, and made him kneel in the courtyard reciting his lessons. He was to make up ten days’ work before he could stop. After a whole night in the freezing cold, Jia Rui now had a savage beating, and knelt hungry in the wind reciting essays. His sufferings were beyond description. Yet his desire had not changed at all. It still never occurred to him that Xi-feng was making a fool of him.

Two days later he found a chance to call on Xi-feng again. She deliberately reproached him for breaking his word, and in his agitation he swore oath after oath. Seeing him walk straight into the net, she had to devise another scheme to make him mend his ways.

‘Don’t wait in that place tonight,’ she told him. ‘Wait in the empty room in the little passage behind my rooms. And don’t blunder about.’

‘Do you mean it?’

‘Who’s deceiving you? If you don’t believe me, don’t come.’

‘I’ll come, I’ll come, I’ll come! I’ll come if it kills me!’

‘Go along now, then.’

Certain that everything would go well that night, Jia Rui left for the time being. Xi-feng began mustering her forces and laying her trap.

Jia Rui could scarcely wait for nightfall, but some relatives happened to call at home and stayed until after supper. By the time they left, the lamps had already been lit. He had to wait again until his grandfather was in bed before he could slip into the Rong mansion and make straight for the room in the narrow passage. There he waited, turning helplessly round and round like an ant on a hot pan. He looked and looked, but saw no one; he listened and listened, but heard nothing. ‘Can she be staying away again?’ he wondered. ‘Am I to be left to freeze for another night?’

While he was tormenting himself with these guesses, a dark figure appeared. Certain that it was Xi-feng, Jia Rui did not stop to look. Like a hungry tiger, like a cat pouncing on a mouse, he seized the newcomer the moment the figure reached the door.

‘My darling sister-in-law! You’ve kept me waiting till I’m half dead!’

He carried the figure inside to the kang, kissing and tugging at trousers, babbling ‘My darling mother!’ and ‘My darling father!’ The other remained silent. Jia Rui pulled down his own trousers and, stiff with excitement, was about to thrust himself in when a light suddenly flared.

‘Who’s in there?’ called Jia Qiang, holding up a lighted taper.

The figure on the kang laughed. ‘Uncle Rui is trying to shame me.’

Jia Rui looked: it was Jia Rong. He was so mortified he wished he could sink through the floor. At a loss what to do, he turned to run, but Jia Qiang caught hold of him.

‘Stay where you are! Aunt Lian has already complained to Her Ladyship that you’ve been making advances to her without the least encouragement.

‘She only tricked you into waiting here so that she could get away. Her Ladyship was so angry she fainted, and she sent me to fetch you. Now you’ve gone and seized him as well. There’s nothing more to be said. Come and see Her Ladyship!’

Jia Rui was beside himself with terror. ‘My good nephew, just say you haven’t seen me. I’ll thank you handsomely tomorrow.’

‘If you’re going to thank me, letting you go is nothing,’ Jia Qiang said. ‘Only how much is this thanks to be?

‘Besides, your word is no proof. Put it in writing.’

‘How could I write such a thing down?’

‘No difficulty there. Write that you lost so many taels gambling with outsiders, and borrowed the money from the keeper of the gambling house.’

‘That’s easy enough. Only there’s no paper or brush here.’

‘That’s easy enough too,’ said Jia Qiang. He went out at once. Paper and brush were ready to hand, and he brought them in and made Jia Rui write. After much bargaining between the two of them, the sum was brought down to fifty taels of silver. Jia Rui put his mark to the document, and Jia Qiang took it away.

Then there was Jia Rong to deal with. At first he was adamant. ‘Tomorrow I’ll tell the clan and let them judge,’ was all he would say. Jia Rui was so desperate that he went down on his knees and kowtowed. Jia Qiang coaxed and argued until another bond for fifty taels had been written; only then was it settled.

‘If I let you go now, I’m taking the blame on myself,’ Jia Qiang said. ‘The door on Her Old Ladyship’s side has long been shut, and His Lordship is in the hall looking at the things from Nanjing. You certainly can’t go that way. There’s only the back gate. But if you run into anyone going out, I’m done for too. Wait while we go and see whether the coast is clear, and then we’ll come and take you out. You can’t hide in this room, either. They’ll be bringing things in to store here in a moment. Let me find somewhere.’

He took Jia Rui by the arm, put out the light again, and led him into the courtyard. Feeling his way to the foot of a high flight of steps, he said, ‘This hollow will do. Crouch here and don’t make a sound. Don’t move until we come.’ Then the two of them went away.

Jia Rui had no choice now but to crouch there. He was turning things over in his mind when he heard a noise above his head. Down came a whole slop-pail of urine and faeces, splashing over his head and body. He could not suppress a cry, but quickly covered his mouth, afraid to make a noise. His head, face and body were plastered with filth; he shivered with cold. Then Jia Qiang came running up.

‘Quick! Go, go!’

As though granted his life again, Jia Rui ran home by the back gate in a few bounds. It was already the third watch, and he had to call for the gate to be opened. The person who opened the door saw the state he was in and asked what had happened. He had to lie again. ‘It was dark. I missed my footing and fell into the privy.’

He went to his room to change and wash. Only now did it dawn on him that Xi-feng had been playing with him, and for a while he hated her. Then he thought of her looks and longed to have her in his arms that very instant. He did not close his eyes all night.

From then on his thoughts were full of Xi-feng, though he dared not go to the Rong mansion again. Jia Rong and his companion kept coming to demand their money, and he was afraid his grandfather would find out. His longing was hard enough to bear, and now there were debts as well; by day his studies left him little respite. He was a man in his twenties, still unmarried, and with Xi-feng so much in his thoughts, his fingers had inevitably been called upon to work themselves weary. Add to this the cold, vexation and exertion of those two nights: assailed from several sides at once, he fell ill before he knew it.

His chest felt distended, there was no taste in his mouth, his legs were like cotton, his eyes as though full of vinegar. He burned at night and was weary by day; semen passed with his urine, and he coughed blood in his phlegm. In less than a year he had acquired all these symptoms. At last he could hold out no longer and took to his bed. Even with his eyes shut he was still lost in disordered dreams, babbling wildly and frightened out of his wits. Doctors were summoned and every sort of treatment tried. He swallowed dozens of catties of such medicines as cinnamon bark, fuzi, soft-shelled turtle shell, maidong and yuzhu, without the slightest improvement.

Before long the twelfth month had ended and spring returned, and his illness grew still worse. Dai-ru was alarmed now. He called in doctors from everywhere, but nothing they did had any effect. Later Jia Rui began taking a ginseng-only decoction. How could Dai-ru afford that? He had to go to the Rong mansion to ask for some ginseng.

Lady Wang told Xi-feng to weigh out two taels for him.

‘The new supply was all used for Her Old Ladyship’s medicine the other day,’ Xi-feng replied. ‘And you said the whole roots were to be kept for Commander Yang’s wife to make up her medicine. As it happens, I sent them over yesterday.’

‘Even if we have none here, send someone to ask your mother-in-law, or try your Cousin Zhen’s household. Get some together for them. If it does him good and saves a life, that will be to your credit too.’

Xi-feng listened, but sent no one to look. Instead she scraped together a few qian of crumbs, dregs and little root fibres and had them sent over, with the message, ‘Her Ladyship sends you this. There isn’t any more.’

She then reported to Lady Wang, ‘I’ve tried everywhere. We managed to get together over two taels and sent it all.’

By now Jia Rui wanted so desperately to live that there was no medicine he would refuse. It only wasted money; nothing helped. One day a lame Taoist came begging for food, proclaiming that his special skill was to cure afflictions born of karmic wrongs. Jia Rui happened to hear him from inside and cried at the top of his voice, ‘Quick, ask that Bodhisattva in to save me!’

As he called, he knocked his forehead against his pillow. The others had to bring the Taoist in. Jia Rui seized him, crying again and again, ‘Bodhisattva, save me!’

‘Your illness is beyond medicine,’ the Taoist said with a sigh. ‘But I have a treasure for you. Look into it every day, and your life can be saved.’

From his shoulder-bag he took a mirror which reflected on both sides. Its handle bore the inscription ‘The Mirror of Love and Desire’, engraved in four Chinese characters. He handed it to Jia Rui.

‘This object comes from the Hall of Numinous Emptiness in the Land of Illusion. It was made by Fairy Disenchantment to cure the afflictions of wayward thoughts and unruly desires, and has the power to help the world and preserve life. That is why I have brought it into the world, solely for clever, gifted men and cultivated young nobles to look into.

‘Never, on any account, look into the front. Look only into the back. This is vital, vital! I shall return in three days to fetch it, and I guarantee you will be well.’

With that he went away. For all their entreaties, they could not keep him. Jia Rui took the mirror. ‘There’s something in what this Taoist says,’ he thought. ‘Why shouldn’t I try looking?’

He picked up the Mirror of Love and Desire and looked into the back. There stood a skeleton. So frightened was he that he hastily covered the mirror.

‘Damn that Taoist! Why does he want to frighten me?’

‘I’ll have a look at the front and see what’s there,’ he thought.

He turned it over and looked. Xi-feng was standing inside, beckoning to him. His heart leapt with joy. He felt himself drifting into the mirror, where he lay with Xi-feng; afterwards she saw him out again. Back on his bed he gave a cry and opened his eyes. The mirror slipped from his hand and turned over, showing the skeleton in its reverse once more. He felt himself wet with sweat; beneath him he had spilled a pool of semen. Still unsatisfied, he turned the front towards him again. Xi-feng was still beckoning, and in he went.

This happened three or four times. On this occasion, just as he was about to come out of the mirror, two men approached, looped an iron chain about him and dragged him away.

‘Let me take the mirror before I go!’ Jia Rui cried. Those were the last words he could speak.

The people attending Jia Rui at his bedside saw only that at first he kept looking into the mirror, dropping it, then opening his eyes and picking it up again. The last time the mirror fell, he did not move. They came to look: he had stopped breathing. Beneath his body lay a great cold, wet patch of semen. They hurried to dress him and move him to the bier.

Dai-ru and his wife wept until they were nearly dead themselves, cursing the Taoist. ‘What a devilish mirror! If we don’t destroy it at once, there’s no telling what harm it will do in the world.’

They ordered a fire made to burn it. But a voice inside the mirror began to weep.

‘Who told you to look into the front? You yourselves took the false for the true. Why must you burn me?’

While it was still crying, the lame Taoist came running in from outside.

‘Who would destroy the Mirror of Love and Desire? I have come to save it!’

He went straight into the central hall, snatched it up and floated away.

Dai-ru set about arranging the funeral and sent word of the death to everyone. On the third day the chanting of scriptures began; on the seventh the funeral procession set out. The coffin was left temporarily at the Temple of the Iron Threshold, to be taken back to their native place at a later date. All the Jia clan came to offer condolences. Jia She of the Rong mansion gave twenty taels of silver, Jia Zheng another twenty, and Jia Zhen of the Ning mansion twenty too. The other members of the clan gave according to their means, three taels here, five there, too many to enumerate. Contributions from the families of his fellow students came to another twenty or thirty taels. Though Dai-ru’s household was poorly off, he was able to see the business through in considerable style.

Now, at the end of that winter, a letter arrived from Lin Ru-hai. He was seriously ill and had written especially to ask for Lin Dai-yu to come home. The news added to Grandmother Jia’s worries and distress, but there was nothing for it: she hurried to prepare Dai-yu for the journey. Bao-yu was deeply unhappy; yet, with the bond between father and daughter to consider, he could hardly try to stop her going. Grandmother Jia insisted that Jia Lian should escort her and bring her back again. There is no need to dwell on the local gifts and travelling expenses: naturally everything was properly provided. A date was chosen without delay, and Jia Lian and Lin Dai-yu took leave of Grandmother Jia and the others. With their servants, they boarded a boat for Yangzhou.
