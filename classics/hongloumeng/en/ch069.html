Chapter 69 Petty stratagems kill with a borrowed sword; Knowing her end is near, Er-jie swallows raw gold and dies

Chapter end

Now Er-jie thanked her again and again for what she had said, and could only follow her. You-shi could hardly stay away: propriety required that she too accompany Xi-feng to make the report.

‘Just leave the talking to me,’ Xi-feng said with a smile.

‘Of course,’ said You-shi. ‘But if anything goes wrong, I shall put the blame on you.’

With that, they all went first to Grandmother Jia’s rooms.

Grandmother Jia was chatting and laughing with the girls from the garden to pass the time. Suddenly she saw Xi-feng come in with a handsome young married woman and screwed up her eyes to look.

‘Whose child is this? What a dear little thing!’

Xi-feng came forward, smiling.

‘Take a good look, Grannie. What do you think of her?’

She quickly drew Er-jie forward.

‘This is your husband’s grandmother. Kowtow to her.’

Er-jie made the full ceremonial obeisance, bowing low before rising. Xi-feng then pointed out the girls by name.

‘Learn who they all are for now. You can exchange greetings properly after you have seen Her Ladyship.’

Er-jie deliberately asked each name over again, then stood to one side with her head bowed. Grandmother Jia looked her up and down and asked with a smile:

‘What is your surname?

‘How old are you, my dear?’

‘Don’t ask her yet, Grannie,’ Xi-feng broke in, still smiling. ‘Just tell me whether she’s prettier than I am.’

Grandmother Jia put on her spectacles and told Faithful and Amber:

‘Bring the child here. Let me see her skin.’

The others pressed their lips together to hide their smiles and had to push Er-jie forward. Grandmother Jia examined her closely, then told Amber:

‘Show me her hands.’

Faithful also lifted Er-jie’s skirt. When Grandmother Jia had finished looking, she took off her spectacles and smiled.

‘A very well-made child, too. I think she’s rather prettier than you.’

At this Xi-feng smiled and promptly knelt down. She repeated in full detail the story concocted over at You-shi’s.

‘We can only beg you to be merciful, Grannie, and let her come into the household now. She can live here for a year before the marriage is consummated.’

‘There’s nothing wrong in that,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘It’s very good of you to be such a virtuous wife. Only they must wait a year before sleeping together.’

Xi-feng kowtowed and rose. She then asked Grandmother Jia to send two women with them to see Their Ladyships, and to have them say that this was Grandmother Jia’s own wish. The old lady agreed and sent two women to take them to Lady Xing and the others. Lady Wang had been deeply troubled by Xi-feng’s unsavoury reputation. How could she fail to be pleased by this action now?

From that day Er-jie could come out into the light, and she moved into the side rooms. Meanwhile Xi-feng sent people secretly to stir up Zhang Hua. He need only demand his promised wife back: she would come with a substantial dowry, and he would also be given money to set up house and live on.

Zhang Hua had originally had neither the courage nor the wish to bring a suit against the Jias. Later he found that Jia Rong had sent a man to answer the charges. This man’s statement was:

‘Zhang Hua had already broken off the engagement. We are all relations. It is true that she was brought into the house to stay, but there was no question of a marriage. Zhang Hua owes us money and would not pay when we asked for it. That is why he has brought these false charges against my master.’

The censor had ties with both the Jia and Wang families and, besides, had accepted bribes. He merely pronounced Zhang Hua a scoundrel trying to extort money because he was poor, refused even to accept his petition, and had him beaten and driven out. Qing-er was making arrangements for him outside, however, and the beating was not severe. He urged Zhang Hua on again.

‘Your family arranged the match in the first place. Demand the girl, and the official is bound to award her to you.’

So Zhang Hua brought another suit. Wang Xin, for his part, sent word privately to the censor, who endorsed the petition:

‘Zhang Hua shall repay in full, within the prescribed time, the money he owes the Jia household. The woman betrothed to him shall remain his to marry when he has the means.’

He also summoned Zhang Hua’s father and confirmed this ruling before him in court.

Qing-er had explained matters to the father too. Glad enough to gain both woman and money, he went to the Jia mansion to collect her. Xi-feng, meanwhile, came to Grandmother Jia in a fright and told her what had happened. It was all Cousin Zhen’s wife’s muddling: the other family had never properly agreed to break off the engagement, they had brought a suit, and this was the official’s ruling.

Grandmother Jia at once sent for You-shi and reproached her for handling things so badly.

‘Your sister was promised to this man before she was even born. You never made a clean end of it, and now you’ve let him bring this foolish suit.’

‘But he even accepted the money,’ You-shi could only say. ‘How can he not have agreed?’

Xi-feng put in:

‘Zhang Hua’s deposition actually says that he never saw any money and that no one came to him. His father says, “The girl’s mother did mention it once, but we never gave our consent. Once she was dead, you took the girl in as a secondary wife.”

‘There’s no one to confront him with, so he can say whatever nonsense he likes. Luckily Master Lian isn’t at home and they haven’t slept together. There’s no harm done there. But she’s come into our house now. How can we send her back? What a loss of face!’

‘If they haven’t slept together, why forcibly keep another man’s wife?’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘That would give us a bad name too. Better send her to him. There are plenty of good girls to be found.’

Hearing this, Er-jie spoke to Grandmother Jia herself.

‘My mother really did give him ten taels on such and such a day, in such and such a month and year, to end the engagement. He agreed. Now poverty has driven him to bring a suit, and he has gone back on his word. My sister did nothing wrong.’

‘It just shows how troublesome these unscrupulous commoners can be,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Well then, Feng, you had better see to it.’

Xi-feng had no way out and could only agree. On returning, she simply sent for Jia Rong. He understood very well what she meant. To let Zhang Hua take Er-jie back would be quite intolerable. He reported to Jia Zhen, and a man was secretly sent to tell Zhang Hua:

‘Now that you have all this money, why must you insist on the same woman?

‘If you go on digging your heels in, aren’t you afraid the masters will lose their temper? They could find some pretext, and you’d die without so much as a place to be buried. You have money now. Go home, and what fine girl couldn’t you find? If you leave, you’ll be given something for the journey as well.’

Zhang Hua thought it over. This was a good idea after all. He settled it with his father, and, having received about a hundred taels of silver altogether, father and son rose at the fifth watch next morning and went back to their native district.

Jia Rong made certain that they had gone, then reported to Grandmother Jia and Xi-feng:

‘Zhang Hua and his father brought false charges and have fled for fear of punishment. The authorities know the facts and won’t pursue the matter. It’s all settled.’

Xi-feng considered this. Had she insisted on Zhang Hua taking Er-jie away, Jia Lian might simply have spent a little more on his return and kept her for himself as before. Zhang Hua would hardly have refused him. Better that Er-jie should stay under her own eye, while she thought what to do next. Only now Zhang Hua had gone, who knew where? Suppose he told someone else about it, or used the same pretext to reopen the case one day? Would she not have brought trouble on herself?

She should never have put the handle of the knife into an outsider’s hands. Bitterly regretting it, she thought of another plan. She secretly ordered Wang-er to send someone after Zhang Hua. They could accuse him of theft and get him sentenced to death, or have him done away with in secret. Zhang Hua must die. Only then would the weed be pulled up by the roots and her good name be safe.

Wang-er took his orders and went out. At home he thought them over. The man had gone: the affair was finished. Why go to such lengths? A human life was a matter for Heaven, no child’s game. He would put her off with a story for now and think what to do later.

Accordingly he kept out of the way for several days, then came back and told Xi-feng that Zhang Hua had been carrying a few taels of silver when he fled. On the third day, in the Jingkou district, robbers had struck him down from behind and killed him during the fifth watch. His father had died of fright at the inn. The bodies had been examined and buried there.

Xi-feng did not believe him.

‘If you’re lying, and I find it out when I send someone else to ask, I’ll knock your teeth out!’

After that, however, she let the matter drop. She and Er-jie lived in the greatest harmony, ten times more affectionate than sisters born.

One day Jia Lian returned with his business completed and went first to the new house. It had been quietly locked up. Only an old man remained to watch it. When Jia Lian asked what had happened, the old man told him the whole story. Jia Lian could only stamp his foot in the stirrup.

He had no choice but to go and see Jia She and Lady Xing and report the completion of his errand. Jia She was delighted. Declaring him a useful fellow, he rewarded him with a hundred taels of silver and also gave him, as a concubine, a seventeen-year-old maid from his own rooms called Autumn.

Jia Lian kowtowed to accept the gift and came away overjoyed. After seeing Grandmother Jia and the rest of the family, he returned to Xi-feng with a somewhat guilty look. But her face was quite unlike what he usually saw. She came out with Er-jie to welcome him, and they exchanged inquiries after one another’s health. When he told her about Autumn, he could not keep a smug, self-satisfied look from his face.

Xi-feng promptly sent two married women in a carriage to fetch the girl. Before one thorn had been taken from her heart, another had been thrust into it from nowhere. She could only swallow her resentment and put on a pleasant face to hide it. She ordered wine for his welcome-home feast and took Autumn to pay her respects to Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the others. Jia Lian privately marvelled at it all.

It was now the twelfth day of the last lunar month. Jia Zhen set out, first paying his respects at the ancestral shrine, then coming over to take leave of Grandmother Jia and the others. The members of the clan accompanied him as far as the Pavilion of Parting Tears before turning back. Jia Lian and Jia Rong alone escorted him for three days and nights before returning.

On the road Jia Zhen had told them to settle down and attend properly to their households. They had answered dutifully and returned the usual ceremonious phrases, which need not trouble us here.

At home, Xi-feng’s outward treatment of Er-jie needs no further telling; in her heart, however, she had other designs. When no one else was present, she would tell her:

‘Sister, there are very ugly stories going round about you. Even Grannie and Their Ladyships have heard them. They say you weren’t chaste when you were a girl at home, and that you had something going on with your brother-in-law. “You’ve picked up a woman nobody else would have. Why don’t you get rid of her and find a better one?” When I heard that, I nearly fell over with rage. I tried to find out who had said it, but I couldn’t discover anyone. How are we to face all these servants, day after day, with their tongues wagging? I’ve landed myself with a fish-head to pick apart.’

She said this a couple of times, then became so angry that she herself fell ill and would take neither food nor tea. Apart from Patience, the maids and married women all made spiteful remarks, pointing at the mulberry tree to curse the locust tree, and mocking Er-jie in covert ways. Autumn regarded herself as Jia She’s gift and would let no one take precedence over her. She thought nothing even of Xi-feng and Patience. How should she tolerate Er-jie?

Every time she opened her mouth it was:

‘A whore who was bedded before she was wedded, whom no man would have! And now she comes here to get the better of me!’

Xi-feng listened with secret delight; Er-jie with secret shame, anger and resentment. Now that Xi-feng was pretending to be ill, she no longer ate with Er-jie. She merely had meals sent to her room each day, and the food and drink were unfit to touch.

Patience could not bear to see it. She spent her own money on dishes for Er-jie, or sometimes said that they were going to amuse themselves in the garden and had soup and other nourishment prepared for her in the garden kitchen. No one dared report this to Xi-feng. But Autumn once came upon them and carried tales to her.

‘It’s Patience who’s ruining your reputation, ma’am. She lets such good food go to waste instead of eating it and goes stealing food in the garden.’

Xi-feng scolded Patience.

‘Other people keep cats to catch mice. Mine only bites the chickens!’

Patience dared say little. From then on she had to keep her distance too, nursing a resentment against Autumn that she could not express.

The girls in the garden, Li Wan, Ying-chun, Xi-chun and the others, all believed that Xi-feng meant well. Bao-yu, Dai-yu and some of the others, however, secretly feared for Er-jie. None could conveniently interfere, but they pitied her, and when she came often to see them, they treated her with compassion.

Whenever they talked where no one else was present, Er-jie would shed tears and wipe her eyes, but dared not complain. Xi-feng, meanwhile, let no sign of ill will show. When Jia Lian came home, he saw what a virtuous wife Xi-feng was and paid no further attention.

Jia She had always kept a great many concubines and maids. Jia Lian had long cast covetous thoughts in their direction but had never dared make a move. Girls like Autumn resented the old master’s age and muddled wits: he wanted more than he could manage, so what was the use of keeping them all there? Apart from a few who had a sense of propriety and shame, some would tease and flirt with the young pages at the inner gate. Some even exchanged looks with Jia Lian and secretly arranged meetings, though fear of Jia She had kept them out of his reach. Autumn was an old acquaintance of his in this way, but they had never once slept together. Now Heaven had obligingly put her in his hands. Dry tinder had met a fierce flame; glue had mingled with lacquer. They were like newlyweds, and for days on end there was no separating them. Jia Lian’s feeling for Er-jie gradually faded. Autumn alone was his life now.

Though Xi-feng hated Autumn, she was glad to have her as a means of disposing of Er-jie first. She herself would stand clear, ‘killing with a borrowed sword’ and ‘watching the tigers fight from the mountainside’. Once Autumn had killed Er-jie, she would kill Autumn in her turn. With her plan settled, she often took Autumn aside and privately cautioned her:

‘You’re young and don’t understand.

‘She’s the secondary wife now, the darling of your master’s heart. Even I give way to her. If you go charging straight at her, aren’t you asking to be killed?’

This only enraged Autumn further. Every day she would let loose a stream of abuse at the top of her voice.

‘You’re too soft, ma’am. I can’t manage that sort of wifely virtue. What’s become of all your old authority? You may be generous enough to put up with anything, but I won’t have grit rubbed into my eyes. Let me have it out with that slut just once, and she’ll learn!’

Xi-feng stayed indoors, pretending not to dare say a word. Er-jie wept in her room in misery, refused her food, yet dared not tell Jia Lian. Next day Grandmother Jia noticed her swollen, red-rimmed eyes and asked about them. She dared not tell her either.

Autumn was now intent on currying favour and showing how charming she could be. She privately told Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the others:

‘She’s determined to make trouble for herself. There’s nothing wrong, but all day long she wails as though someone had died. Behind our backs she’s wishing Mrs Lian and me dead, so she can have the master all to herself.’

‘When a woman’s too pretty and winsome, you may be sure she has a jealous heart,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Feng treats her so kindly, and all she does is vie for favour and make jealous scenes. A thoroughly base creature!’

Gradually, therefore, she ceased to like Er-jie very much.

Seeing that Er-jie was out of favour with Grandmother Jia, the others inevitably began to trample on her still more, until she could neither die nor endure living. Only Patience helped her. Seeing how she suffered, she would often come behind Xi-feng’s back and try to ease her mind.

Er-jie was a creature with a heart of flowers and flesh of snow. How could she endure such torment? A mere month of stifled anger brought on a lingering illness. Her limbs grew too listless to move, she could take no food or tea, and little by little she became yellow and thin.

One night, when she closed her eyes, she saw her younger sister come towards her holding the Mandarin-duck swords.

‘Sister, all your life you’ve been foolishly trusting and soft-hearted. Now you have suffered for it. Don’t believe that jealous woman’s honeyed words. She puts on a show of virtue while hiding malice and deceit within. She has sworn in her anger that she won’t rest until she has killed you. If I were alive, I would never have let you come here. Even if you had come, I would never have allowed her to treat you like this. Yet it is what fate and justice have decreed. In our lives we were wanton and unworthy, entering illicit unions and making others violate the bonds of kinship and abandon decent conduct. This is our retribution. Do as I say: take this sword and kill that jealous woman. Then we can go together before Disenchantment and submit to her judgment. Otherwise you will lose your life for nothing, with no one even to pity you.’

‘Sister,’ Er-jie wept, ‘if my conduct has been at fault all my life, and this is the retribution I ought to suffer, why should I add the wrong of another killing?

‘Let me bear it patiently. If Heaven takes pity on me and lets me recover, won’t that make all well?’

Her younger sister smiled.

‘Sister, you’re a fool to the last. As they have said from of old, “Heaven’s net is vast; wide though its meshes are, nothing slips through.” Heaven always returns what it is given. You may repent and mend your ways, but you have brought fathers, sons and brothers into the incestuous disorder of beasts sharing their mates. How can Heaven allow you to live in peace?’

‘If I cannot live in peace, that too is only just,’ Er-jie said through her tears. ‘I have no complaint.’

Her younger sister gave a long sigh and went away. Er-jie woke with a start. It had been a dream.

When Jia Lian came to see her, there was no one beside them, and she told him, weeping:

‘I shall never recover from this illness. I’ve been here half a year, and I’m with child, though I can’t know yet whether it’s a boy or a girl. If Heaven takes pity and lets the child be born, there may be some hope. Otherwise I shan’t keep my own life, let alone the child’s.’

‘Don’t worry,’ Jia Lian said, weeping too. ‘I’ll get someone who knows his business to treat you.’

He went out and sent for a doctor at once.

But Doctor Wang had managed to obtain a posting with the army, hoping to earn hereditary honours on his return. When the pages went to fetch him, they brought instead an imperial physician surnamed Hu, whose given name was Jun-rong. He came in, felt her pulse and said that her monthly courses were disordered and that she needed powerful restoratives.

‘Her courses haven’t come for three months,’ said Jia Lian, ‘and she keeps retching and bringing up sour fluid. I think she may be pregnant.’

Hu Jun-rong told the old women to ask her to put out her hand again. Er-jie had no choice but to extend it once more from behind the bed curtains. He felt her pulse for a long while.

‘If it were pregnancy, the liver pulse ought to be full and strong. But flourishing Wood generates Fire, and disordered courses can also arise from the Liver-Wood. A physician must be bold. I must ask Her Ladyship to show her face a little, so that I may examine her complexion before venturing to prescribe.’

Jia Lian could do nothing but have the curtains parted a little. Er-jie showed her face. At the sight, Hu Jun-rong’s soul seemed to fly up to the ninth heaven. His whole body went numb, and he knew nothing at all.

Presently the curtains were closed. Jia Lian accompanied him out and asked what he thought.

‘It isn’t pregnancy,’ said Doctor Hu. ‘It’s simply an accumulation of stagnant blood. The urgent thing now is to expel that blood and clear the channels.’

He wrote a prescription, took his leave and went away. Jia Lian had his fee sent after him and ordered the medicines fetched, prepared and given to Er-jie. By midnight she was suffering unremitting pains in her belly. She miscarried a male foetus, already formed. The bleeding would not stop, and she lost consciousness.

When Jia Lian heard, he cursed Hu Jun-rong furiously. He sent for another doctor to treat her and ordered men to bring a charge against Hu Jun-rong. Hu had heard the news, however, and had already packed his bundle and fled.

The physician now attending her said:

‘Her vital energy and blood were weak by nature. Since she conceived, I imagine some anger or distress has become pent up within her. That gentleman rashly used medicines fierce as tigers and wolves. Eight or nine tenths of the mother’s vital strength has now been injured. I cannot promise a quick recovery. She must take both decoctions and pills and hear nothing whatever of idle talk or vexatious affairs. Then there may be hope.’

With that he left.

Distracted, Jia Lian demanded to know who had brought the man called Hu. As soon as he found the culprit, he had him beaten half to death. Xi-feng was ten times more distressed than Jia Lian.

‘We aren’t fated to have a son,’ was all she said. ‘At last, with such difficulty, we had one, and then we met a useless doctor like that!’

She burned incense and worshipped before Heaven and Earth, offering her own prayer aloud:

‘Even if I must be ill myself, I ask only that Sister You be restored to perfect health and conceive again and bear a son. I vow to eat no meat for the rest of my life and recite the Buddha’s name.’

Jia Lian and all the others praised her. While Jia Lian was with Autumn, Xi-feng had soups and broths made and sent to Er-jie. She also scolded Patience for being as ill-favoured by fortune as herself.

‘I’m always ill, but you’re healthy enough, and still you never conceive. Now the second mistress is like this. It must be our lack of good fortune, or some offence we’ve committed, that has brought this baleful influence upon her.’

She sent out to have fortunes told and lots cast. As it happened, the fortune-teller’s answer was:

‘The affliction comes from a woman born in the Year of the Rabbit.’

Everyone reckoned up the birth years. Autumn alone was a Rabbit, and they said she was the cause.

Autumn had lately seen Jia Lian sending for doctors and medicines, beating people and cursing everyone about him, all in his devotion to Er-jie. Her heart was already steeped in a whole vat of vinegar. Now she heard that she was being blamed for a clash of birth signs, and Xi-feng advised her:

‘Why don’t you go somewhere else for a few months to keep out of the way, then come back?’

Autumn flew into a tearful rage.

‘Why listen to those fucking fools and their lying tongues? Her well-water and my river-water don’t mix. How have I brought anything upon her?

‘The precious little darling! Who didn’t she see when she was outside? Yet the moment she comes here, someone’s blighting her! Where did this child suddenly spring from? She’s only using it to hoodwink that cotton-eared master of ours. Even if there was a child, who knows whether its name was Zhang or Wang? You may treasure that mongrel brat, ma’am, but I don’t! Who’s too old, then? Who can’t bear a child? I could have one in a year or half a year, and there’d be no mixing of the blood in mine!’

The others wanted to laugh at her abuse but dared not. Just then Lady Xing came to pay her respects. Autumn tearfully complained to her:

‘The master and mistress want to send me back. I’ve nowhere to live. Your Ladyship must please have mercy on me.’

Lady Xing hurried to give Xi-feng a scolding, then abused Jia Lian as well.

‘You ungrateful wretch! However bad she may be, your father gave her to you. Sending her away for some woman from outside: so you don’t even acknowledge your father now! If you want to turn her out, you’d better give her back to him.’

She left in a huff.

Autumn was more triumphant than ever. She actually went beneath Er-jie’s window and began crying and cursing at the top of her voice. Er-jie could not but grow still more distressed.

That night Jia Lian slept in Autumn’s room. Xi-feng was already in bed when Patience came to see Er-jie and quietly urged her:

‘Take good care of yourself and don’t heed that beast.’

Er-jie caught hold of her and wept.

‘Sister, ever since I came here, it’s you I have to thank for looking after me. Who knows how much spite you’ve had to put up with on my account? If I escape with my life, I shall repay your kindness. Only I’m afraid I shan’t escape. Then I shall have to wait for another life.’

Patience could not keep back her own tears.

‘When I think of it, I’m the one who brought you to this. I was foolishly devoted to her. I’d never hidden anything from her. Once I heard about you living outside, how could I not tell her? Who could have known it would lead to all this?’

‘No, sister, you’re wrong,’ Er-jie said quickly. ‘Even if you hadn’t told her, how could she have failed to find out? You merely spoke first. Besides, I wanted with all my heart to come into the household, so that things would be proper. What has it to do with you?’

The two wept together for a while. Patience added a few more words of counsel, and only then, for it was late, went off to bed.

Left alone, Er-jie thought:

‘The illness has taken hold. Day after day I get nothing to nourish me, only more to hurt me. I’m certain I can never recover. And now that the child is lost, there’s nothing left to hold me here. Why endure all these petty torments? Better to die and be done with them. I’ve often heard people say that raw gold can sink inside you and kill you. Surely that would be cleaner than hanging myself or cutting my throat.’

She struggled up, opened her chest and found a piece of raw gold. She did not know how much it weighed. Weeping, she thrust it desperately into her mouth and, after several fierce efforts with her neck stretched taut, at last swallowed it. Then she hurriedly put on her clothes and ornaments in good order, climbed onto the kang and lay down. No one, living or dead, knew of it.

Next morning, finding that she did not call them, the maids and married women were glad to go about washing and dressing themselves. Xi-feng and Autumn had both gone up. Patience could not bear to see the servants’ behaviour and scolded them:

‘You deserve no better than to be driven with blows and curses by someone without a heart. Can’t you have a little pity for a sick woman?

‘She may be good-tempered, but you ought to show some decency. Don’t go too far. Once a wall starts falling, everyone gives it a push.’

Hearing this, the maids hurriedly pushed open the door and went in. There she lay on the kang, fully and neatly dressed, dead. They took fright at last and began to shout. Patience came in, saw her and burst into tears.

Though everyone had always feared Xi-feng, they remembered that Er-jie had truly been gentle and compassionate towards those below her, far better than Xi-feng. Now she was dead, who could help grieving and shedding tears? They only dared not let Xi-feng see.

Soon the whole household knew. Jia Lian came in, threw his arms round the body and wept without ceasing. Xi-feng too pretended to weep.

‘Cruel sister! How could you leave me? How could you repay my love like this?’

You-shi, Jia Rong and the others came and wept for a while, then persuaded Jia Lian to restrain himself. He asked Lady Wang for the use of Pear-tree Court to keep the body for five days before taking it to the Temple of the Iron Threshold. She agreed. He immediately had the gates of Pear-tree Court opened and the main rooms cleared to receive the dead.

Jia Lian thought it unseemly to take the funeral procession out by the back gate, so he had a large gateway newly opened through the front wall of Pear-tree Court, giving onto the street. Shelters were erected on both sides, and altars set up for Buddhist services. A padded couch was spread with brocade bedding; Er-jie was lifted onto it and covered with a sheet. Eight pages carried her, with several married women about them, along the Neizi Wall towards Pear-tree Court.

A funeral diviner had already been engaged and was waiting there. When the sheet was lifted, Er-jie’s face was as lifelike as ever, more beautiful even than when she was alive. Jia Lian embraced her again and sobbed, crying over and over:

‘My lady, you’ve died an unjust death! I’m the one who brought you to it!’

Jia Rong hurried forward to console him.

‘Uncle, try to ease your grief. My poor aunt had no good fortune of her own.’

As he spoke, he pointed south towards the boundary wall of Prospect Garden. Jia Lian understood. He could only stamp his foot and say under his breath:

‘I was careless. One day I’ll get to the truth and avenge you.’

The funeral diviner reported:

‘The lady died today at the middle of the mao hour. The procession cannot take place on the fifth day. It must be either the third or the seventh. Tomorrow in the yin hour is most auspicious for placing her in the coffin.’

‘The third is quite out of the question,’ said Jia Lian. ‘Make it the seventh. As my uncle and elder cousin are both away, I dare not keep a lesser funeral here too long. Once she is outside, we shall keep her until the fifth seven-day observance1 and hold a great service before entombing the coffin. Next year we’ll take her south for burial.’

The diviner assented, wrote out the death notice and left. Bao-yu had already come and joined in the weeping, and the other members of the clan had arrived too. Jia Lian hurried inside to find Xi-feng and ask for money for the coffin and outer case and the funeral rites.

Once the body had been carried out, Xi-feng pleaded illness.

‘Grannie and Her Ladyship say that as I’m ill I must keep out of the three chambers: bridal, lying-in and mourning. They won’t let me go.’

She therefore did not come out in mourning clothes but went instead into Prospect Garden.

Making her way round the rockeries, she came to the foot of the northern boundary wall and listened to what was happening outside. She could faintly catch a few scraps of talk. On returning, she told Grandmother Jia what she had heard.

‘Don’t listen to his nonsense,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘When a girl dies of consumption, what family doesn’t simply burn her and scatter the ashes? Who seriously sets about a funeral and breaking ground? Since she was his secondary wife, there was a bond of marriage, I suppose. Keep her five or seven days, carry her out, and either burn her or bury her in some common graveyard. That will finish it.’

‘Just what I thought,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘Only I didn’t dare advise him.’

While they were speaking, a maid came to fetch her.

‘The master’s waiting for you to give him some money, ma’am.’

Xi-feng had to go. She asked him:

‘What money? Don’t you know how hard things are at home lately? We can’t make one month’s allowance reach the next. The chickens have eaten next year’s grain. Yesterday I pawned two gold neckrings for three hundred taels, and you’re still dreaming! There are twenty or thirty taels here. Take them if you want them.’

She had Patience fetch the silver and give it to him, then went off again on the pretext that Grandmother Jia had something to say to her.

Jia Lian was too bitter to speak. He could only open Er-jie’s chests and cupboards to fetch his private savings. When he opened them, not a trace of the money remained. There were only some broken hairpins and tattered artificial flowers, and a few half-worn silk clothes, all things Er-jie had used in her daily life. He could not help breaking into tears again. He wrapped them all in a cloth himself and, without calling even a little maid to carry the bundle, took it away in his own hands to burn.

Patience was torn between sorrow and the wish to laugh. She quickly stole out a packet of two hundred taels in broken silver, caught Jia Lian in the side rooms and slipped it to him.

‘Just keep quiet, please. If you want to cry, there’s plenty of room outside. Why come running back here to cry where everyone can see you?’

‘You’re right,’ said Jia Lian.

He took the silver and gave her a skirt.

‘She used to wear this every day. Keep it safely for me, as something to remember her by.’

Patience could only hide it and put it away herself. Jia Lian took the money to the others and ordered them first to buy coffin boards. The good ones were expensive, and he would not have the middling sort. He rode off to look for himself. By evening a fine set of boards was indeed carried in, bought on credit for five hundred taels, and work on the coffin went on through the night. He assigned people to put on mourning and keep watch by the body. That night he did not go back inside but stayed there to keep her company.
