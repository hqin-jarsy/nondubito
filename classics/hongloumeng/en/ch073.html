Chapter 73 A simple-minded maid unwittingly picks up an embroidered erotic scent-bag; A timid young mistress asks no questions about a filigree gold phoenix

Chapter end

Now, while Aunt Zhao was talking to Jia Zheng, a sudden crash came from outside. Neither knew what it was. They hurriedly asked, and learnt that a window frame in the outer room had not been properly fastened: its catch had given way and it had fallen, hanging loose. Aunt Zhao scolded the maids, then went with them to secure it herself. Only then did she come back to attend Jia Zheng as he settled down for the night. Of that we need say no more.

At the House of Green Delights, Bao-yu had only just gone to bed, and the maids were about to disperse for the night, when someone knocked at the courtyard gate. The old woman who opened it found Xiao-que, a maid from Aunt Zhao’s rooms. Asked what she wanted, Xiao-que gave no answer but went straight inside to find Bao-yu. He had just lain down, and Skybright and the others were still sitting beside his bed, laughing and joking with him.

‘What is it? What have you come running here for at this time of night?’ they asked.

Xiao-que smiled at Bao-yu. ‘I’ve come to give you a warning.’ She told him what her mistress had just been saying to his father. ‘Be careful if His Lordship questions you tomorrow.’

With that she turned to go. Aroma called to her to stay for tea, but, afraid the gates would be locked, she went straight away.

To Bao-yu, the news was like the headband-tightening spell to the Great Sage Sun. At once every limb and every organ within him seemed to be in torment. He thought and thought, but could find no way out. He had better get his books by heart again, ready for tomorrow’s examination. If he made no mistakes in reciting, he could parry at least half the attack, whatever else there might be against him. He hastily threw on his clothes and got up to study. Already he was reproaching himself. All these days he had supposed the subject would never come up again, and had let his learning grow rusty. If only he had known, he could have revised a little every day, however little it was.

Taking stock now, he found that the only books he could recite from memory, commentaries and all, were the Great Learning, the Doctrine of the Mean and both parts of the Analects. He was still unsure of half the first part of Mencius. If a sentence were quoted to him out of the blue, he certainly could not carry on from it. As for the second part, he had forgotten most of that. Of the Five Classics, the Book of Poetry had been fairly often in his hands lately because he was writing poems himself. His understanding might not be very profound, but he could give a tolerable account of it.

He could not remember the others, but fortunately Jia Zheng had never told him to read them, so ignorance there need not matter. Classical prose was another question. What he had read some years before amounted to only a few dozen pieces altogether, from the Zuo Commentary, the Intrigues of the Warring States, the Gongyang and Guliang Commentaries, and the writings of the Han and Tang. In the years since, he had not properly revised so much as half a piece or a fragment. He had indeed looked through them all in idle moments, but only on a passing impulse, forgetting as fast as he read. How could he remember what he had never taken pains to learn? There he could certainly give no satisfactory account of himself.

Then there were the eight-legged examination essays. He had always detested these. They were not the work of the sages: how could they unfold the subtle depths of the sages’ thought? They were merely a ladder for later generations to climb, baiting their hooks for fame and official salaries. Before going away, Jia Zheng had selected a hundred or so and told him to read them. Occasionally he had happened upon one or two sections, or a passage in the development of the opening, that was finely wrought, free-flowing, playful or sorrowful, and sufficiently moving to catch his attention. He would read it for the pleasure of the moment. But when had he ever devoted himself to studying a whole essay through?

If he revised this, he feared that tomorrow he would be questioned on that; if he revised that, he feared he would be challenged on this. Nor could a single night’s work possibly cover everything. His agitation only grew. His own studying would not have mattered, but it kept the whole roomful of maids from their sleep. Aroma, Musk and Skybright, the older ones, were of course at his side, trimming the candles and pouring tea. The little ones, their eyes misted with sleep, were nodding backwards and forwards.

‘You little hussies!’ Skybright scolded. ‘You lie there like corpses all day and all night, and still you haven’t had enough? The one time you have to stay up a little later, you put on this performance. Carry on like that and I’ll give you a prick or two with a needle!’

Before she had finished, there was a thump in the outer room. They hurried to look. A little maid had fallen asleep sitting up and knocked her head against the wall. Startled out of her dream at the very moment of Skybright’s threat, she stared blankly, convinced that Skybright had struck her.

‘Please, sister!’ she begged, beginning to cry. ‘I won’t do it again!’

Everyone burst out laughing.

‘Let her off,’ Bao-yu urged. ‘They ought all to have been sent to bed. You should take turns to sleep too.’

‘My little ancestor, do attend to your own business!’ Aroma said at once. ‘You have only this one night. Keep your mind on these few books for now. Once you’re safely through, you can look after everything else as much as you like. Nothing will be lost by it.’

Her earnestness obliged him to go back to his reading. He had read only a few sentences when Musk poured him another cup of tea to moisten his tongue. He took it and drank. Seeing that she had taken off her skirt and was wearing only a short jacket, he said, ‘It’s late, and cold. You really ought to put on something longer.’

Musk smiled and pointed to the book. ‘Forget about us for a little while. Give that some of your attention instead.’

She had hardly finished when Gold-flecked Glass1 came running in through the back door.

‘Something’s wrong! A man has jumped down from the wall!’

They hurriedly asked where, called up the servants and began searching everywhere. Skybright had seen how miserable Bao-yu was over his books. Even if he wore his wits out all night, he might still be in trouble tomorrow. She was already trying to think of some way to get him out of his predicament when this sudden alarm provided just the opportunity. An idea came to her at once.

‘Quick, take this chance to pretend you’re ill,’ she told him. ‘Say you’ve had a fright.’

It was exactly what Bao-yu wanted. The night-watch servants were summoned, and lanterns carried about in a search of every corner. Not a trace was found.

‘The young girls must have gone out half asleep, with their eyes playing tricks on them,’ the servants said. ‘They took a branch waving in the wind for a man.’

‘Don’t talk such bloody rubbish!’ said Skybright. ‘You haven’t searched properly, and now you’re afraid of being blamed and trying to talk your way out of it. It wasn’t just one person who saw him. Bao-yu and the rest of us had gone out for something, and we all saw him with our own eyes.

‘Bao-yu’s gone quite pale with fright and is burning all over. I’m going to the main rooms now for Spirit-calming Pills. When Her Ladyship asks, she’ll have to be told exactly what happened. Do you suppose we can leave it at what you say?’

Frightened into silence, they could only go and search again. Skybright and Glass did indeed go out for medicine, deliberately making such a commotion that everyone knew Bao-yu had had a fright. On hearing of it, Lady Wang at once sent someone to see him and give him medicine. She ordered all the night-watch servants to search thoroughly, and also had the pages questioned who kept watch outside the inner gate, beside the garden wall. The garden was full of lanterns, torches and uproar all night. By the fifth watch, the male and female stewards had been summoned and ordered to investigate carefully and interrogate under punishment the men and women who had been on night duty, inside and out.

Grandmother Jia learnt that Bao-yu had been frightened and asked closely into the cause. No one dared conceal it any longer; they had to give her an account.

‘I knew something of this sort would happen,’ she said. ‘The carelessness of the night watches is a small matter. For all we know, they may be the thieves themselves.’

Lady Xing, You-shi and the others had come to pay their respects. Xi-feng, Li Wan and the girls were in attendance. Hearing this, they all remained silent. Tan-chun alone rose from her place and said with a smile, ‘Since Cousin Feng has been unwell, the garden servants have become much more unruly these last few days. Before, they would only snatch a little time in secret, or three or four would get together on night watch for a game of dice or cards. It was just a small amusement to keep themselves awake.

‘Lately they’ve grown bolder and bolder. They actually run gambling parties now, with bankers and people organising the games, and winnings and losses of thirty, fifty, even three hundred strings of cash. A fortnight ago there was even a quarrel that came to blows.’

‘If you knew, why didn’t you tell us sooner?’ Grandmother Jia asked at once.

‘Mother has so much to see to, and hasn’t been feeling well these last few days, so I didn’t tell her. I only told Sister-in-law Li and the people in charge. They’ve been warned several times, and things have been better lately.’

‘You’re a young girl,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘What can you know of the dangers? You think gambling is an ordinary thing and the only risk is a quarrel. You don’t understand. If they gamble at night, you can’t be sure they won’t drink, and once they drink, they’re bound to unlock the gates whenever they please: buying things, fetching this person or that. In the dead of night, with hardly anyone about, they can take the chance to hide thieves, admit lovers, let in robbers. What might they not do? Besides, the girls in the garden have only maids and married servants about them, good and bad all mixed together. Theft is a small matter. If something else happened, the slightest involvement could have very serious consequences. This is not something we can let off lightly.’

Tan-chun returned silently to her seat. Xi-feng was not yet fully recovered and had less energy than usual, but hearing Grandmother speak in this way, she hastened to say, ‘And this had to be the very time I fell ill!’

She turned and ordered Mrs Lin and the other three women responsible for the household to be summoned at once. In Grandmother’s presence she gave them a thorough reprimand. Grandmother ordered them to find the organisers and gamblers immediately. Anyone who came forward with information was to be rewarded; anyone who concealed what she knew was to be punished.

With Grandmother Jia so angry, neither Mrs Lin nor the others dared show favour. They hurried to the garden, assembled everyone and questioned them one by one. There was the inevitable round of denials, but in the end the truth came out. They discovered three principal organisers and eight lesser ones among more than twenty gamblers in all. These were brought before Grandmother and knelt in the courtyard, knocking their heads audibly on the ground as they begged for mercy.

Grandmother first asked the names of the principal organisers and the amounts of money involved. One of the three was a relation by marriage on Mrs Lin’s maternal side; another was the sister of Mrs Liu, who worked in the garden kitchen; the third was Ying-chun’s wet-nurse. These were the three ringleaders. We cannot record all the rest.

Grandmother ordered the dice and cards to be burnt together, and all the money confiscated for distribution among the servants. Each ringleader was to receive forty strokes of the heavy board and be expelled, never to be admitted again. Each of the followers was to receive twenty strokes, lose three months’ wages and be transferred to work in the privies. Mrs Lin received another reprimand as well.

Her own relation had made a fool of her, and Mrs Lin felt thoroughly ashamed. Ying-chun, sitting among the others, was embarrassed too. Dai-yu, Bao-chai and Tan-chun felt something of the hurt one feels for one’s own kind at seeing Ying-chun’s nurse in such disgrace. They all rose and smilingly pleaded with Grandmother for her.

‘This mamma doesn’t usually gamble. We don’t know what made her take it into her head this once. Do let her off for Second Sister’s sake.’

‘You don’t understand,’ said Grandmother. ‘These wet-nurses generally trade on having nursed a young master or mistress. They have a little more standing than the other servants, and that is what makes them troublesome. They’re worse than the rest, forever coaxing their masters into shielding their faults and taking their side. I’ve seen it all before. Besides, I mean to make an example of someone, and here is one of them ready to hand. Leave it alone. I have my reasons.’

Bao-chai and the others had to give up.

Presently Grandmother Jia lay down for her midday rest, and they all came out. Knowing how angry she was that day, none dared go off home; they stayed nearby to wait. You-shi went to talk to Xi-feng for a while, but as Xi-feng was unwell too, she could only go to the garden to find the other women and girls for a chat. Lady Xing sat with Lady Wang for a time, then also went into the garden to take her mind off things.

She had just reached the garden gate when she saw a little maid from Grandmother Jia’s rooms, known as Simpleton, coming towards her with a broad grin. The girl held something brightly coloured in her hand and was gazing down at it as she walked, so that she almost ran straight into Lady Xing. Only when she looked up and saw her did she stop.

‘You silly girl,’ said Lady Xing, ‘what odd thing have you found now to make you so pleased? Give it here and let me see.’

Simpleton was fourteen or fifteen, newly chosen to carry buckets of water, sweep the courtyard and do the rough work in Grandmother’s rooms. She was stout and broad-faced, and her two large feet took her briskly and efficiently about her work. But she was dull-witted and ignorant, and what she said and did was often quite outside the rules. Grandmother liked her speed and readiness, and liked the things she said because they made her laugh. She had named her ‘Simpleton’, and when she was bored would often draw her out for amusement, with no restraint at all. So the girl was also called ‘the silly maid’.

Even when she behaved improperly, the others refrained from finding fault, seeing how fond Grandmother was of her. The girl made the most of this indulgence. Whenever Grandmother did not want her, she went into the garden to play. That day, while hunting crickets, she had found a scent-bag embroidered in many colours behind some rocks. Its rich, delicate workmanship was certainly attractive, but the embroidery showed neither flowers nor birds. On one side were two naked people, entwined in an embrace; on the other were a few words. The silly maid did not recognise an erotic picture.

‘Could it be two demons fighting?’ she wondered. ‘If not, it must be a husband and wife having a fight.’

Unable to make it out however she looked at it, she was taking it to show Grandmother.

That was why she had been walking along with a grin, studying it as she went. At Lady Xing’s words she smiled again.

‘How clever of you, ma’am! That’s just what it is: an odd thing. Please have a look.’

She handed it over. Lady Xing took one look and, horrified, clenched it tightly in her fist.

‘Where did you get this?’

‘I was hunting crickets and picked it up on the rocks.’

‘Don’t you tell a soul. This is a wicked thing. They could beat even you to death for it. You’re only excused because you’ve always been a fool. Never mention it again.’

Simpleton turned yellow with fright.

‘I won’t, I won’t!’

She kowtowed and went away in a daze. Lady Xing looked round, but all her attendants were young girls, and she could not hand the thing to any of them. She thrust it into her own sleeve. Greatly puzzled, she wondered how it had come there, but betrayed nothing in her voice or face and continued to Ying-chun’s rooms.

Ying-chun was still embarrassed and unhappy about her nurse’s disgrace when her mother was announced. She went to receive her and brought her into the inner room. Once tea had been served, Lady Xing began.

‘You’re old enough now. When that nurse of yours does such things, why don’t you speak to her? Everyone else’s people behave themselves, but ours have to do something like this. What is the meaning of it?’

Ying-chun lowered her head and played with her sash. After a long silence she answered, ‘I did speak to her twice. She wouldn’t listen, and there was nothing I could do. Besides, she’s my mamma. She’s the one who tells me what to do, not I her.’

‘Nonsense! If you misbehave, of course she ought to tell you. But when she breaks the rules, you must assert yourself as her mistress. If she dares disobey, you should report it to me. What do you mean by waiting until outsiders all know about it? And if she’s been running a gambling bank, she may well have wheedled you into lending her hairpins and bracelets, clothes and shoes to raise her stake. You’re so easily persuaded, and so afraid of saying no, that you’ve very likely helped her out. If she’s swindled you of your things, I haven’t a single cash for you. We shall see how you manage when the festival comes.’

Ying-chun said nothing, but went on looking down and playing with her sash. Lady Xing gave a cold laugh.

‘There’s always that fine brother of yours and his fine wife. Such magnificent people, Master Lian and Mistress Feng! Between them they fill the sky and shut out the sun. They see to everything, but when it comes to his one sister, they pay no attention at all. If you had come from my own body, I might have something to say. As it is, I can only leave them to it. You’re not mine, after all. But though you and he were born of different mothers, you have the same father. You ought to look after one another a little, if only to keep other people from laughing.

‘The world’s a strange place, I must say. Your mother was one of the Elder Master’s women, and Tan-chun’s mother is one of the Second Master’s. You come from the same sort of background. Your mother is dead now, but when I think of the two of them in those days, yours was ten times the woman Aunt Zhao is. You ought to be better than Tan-chun. How is it you’re not half as good? Who would have thought it would turn out like this? Isn’t it extraordinary?

‘I’m better off having had neither son nor daughter all my life. At least I’ve kept clear of it all, and given people nothing to laugh or talk about.’

The married servants standing by seized their chance.

‘Our young lady is honest and kind. She’s not like their Third Miss, with that clever tongue of hers, always having to get the better of her sisters. She knows perfectly well what her elder sister is like, yet she hasn’t the least consideration for her.’

‘When her own brother and sister-in-law behave like that,’ said Lady Xing, ‘what can one expect of anyone else?’

She had scarcely finished when someone announced Mrs Lian. Lady Xing laughed coldly twice and told a servant to go out with her answer.

‘Ask her to go and look after her health. I don’t need her attendance here.’

A little maid of Tan-chun’s then came to say that Grandmother was awake. Lady Xing rose and went to the front of the house. Ying-chun saw her beyond the courtyard before coming back.

‘Well?’ said Xiu-ju. ‘The other day I told you that pearl-set filigree gold phoenix had vanished, and you never asked a single question. I said the old nurse must have pawned it to get money for her gambling bank. You wouldn’t believe me. You said Chess had put it away. Chess may be ill, but she still knows what she’s doing. I asked her, and she said she hadn’t put it away: it had been left for the time being in its box on the bookshelf, ready in case you wanted to wear it on the fifteenth of the eighth month. You ought to have asked the old nurse. But you’re too soft-hearted, too afraid of upsetting anyone. Now I’m afraid we shan’t get it back. When everyone else wears theirs tomorrow and we have nothing, what will that look like?’

‘Why ask?’ Ying-chun said. ‘Of course she took it to tide herself over. I thought she’d taken it away quietly and would quietly put it back in a little while, and that would be the end of it. Who could have known she would forget? And now, with all this coming out today, I doubt asking her would do any good.’

‘Forgotten? She hasn’t forgotten at all!

‘She’s taken the measure of your temper, and that’s why she does it. I have an idea. I’ll go straight to Mrs Lian’s rooms and tell her. She can send someone to get it back, or, if she wants to save trouble, give a few strings of cash to make good the loss for her.

‘What do you say?’

‘No, no, no,’ Ying-chun said hastily. ‘Don’t make more trouble. I’d rather do without it. Why stir things up?’

‘Why must you be so weak, miss? If you’re always trying to save trouble, they’ll swindle you out of your very self one day. I’m going, whatever you say.’

She set off. Ying-chun said nothing. There was nothing for it but to let her go.

Now the nurse’s daughter-in-law, Wang Zhu-er’s wife, had come to ask Ying-chun to intercede for her mother-in-law. Hearing them discussing the gold phoenix, she had stayed outside. They had never thought much of Ying-chun, knowing her weakness. But now that Xiu-ju was determined to tell Xi-feng, she reckoned there was no getting out of it. Besides, she needed Ying-chun’s help. She had to come in.

She first addressed Xiu-ju with a conciliatory smile. ‘Don’t go making trouble, sister. My mother-in-law must have been losing her wits. She lost a little at play and had nothing to help her win it back, so she borrowed the young lady’s gold-wire phoenix for the time being. She meant to redeem it in a day or even half a day, but she never did recover her stake, and that held things up. Then today, who knows who let the secret out, and brought all this upon us. Still, it belongs to our mistress. We wouldn’t dare keep her waiting for it. It will be redeemed in the end. But now we must ask the young lady to remember who nursed her as a baby, and go and plead with Her Old Ladyship to save the old woman.’

‘My good sister,’ Ying-chun answered at once, ‘you’d better give up that hope now. If you’re waiting for me to plead for her, you can wait till next year and it will do you no good.

‘Cousin Bao-chai and Cousin Lin and all the others pleaded just now, and Grandmother wouldn’t listen. What could I do on my own? I’m ashamed enough already, without going to ask for another humiliation.’

‘Redeeming the phoenix is one thing,’ Xiu-ju said, ‘and pleading for her is another. Don’t mix them up. Do you mean that if our mistress won’t plead for her, you won’t redeem it? Fetch the phoenix back first, sister, and then we can talk.’

Ying-chun’s refusal, followed by Xiu-ju’s unanswerably sharp words, left Wang Zhu-er’s wife unable to save face. Taking open advantage of Ying-chun’s habitual good nature, she turned on Xiu-ju.

‘Don’t lean too hard on your position, sister. Look through the whole household. Whose mamma or wet-nurse doesn’t get something extra out of her young master or mistress? Why must every last detail be reckoned so exactly with us, while you’re free to wheedle things away on the sly? Ever since Miss Xing came, Her Ladyship has ordered a tael to be saved each month for Miss Xing’s mother. We have Miss Xing’s expenses on top of everything else, and a tael less to meet them with.

‘There’s always something short, something missing. Who supplies it but us? Who goes asking to have it back? We all make do as best we can. By now it comes to thirty taels at the very least. Hasn’t all that money of ours simply gone to fill a hole?’

Xiu-ju spat before she could finish.

‘Thirty taels gone for nothing? What for? Let’s go through the account, then. What has our mistress asked for?’

Hearing the woman expose Lady Xing’s private arrangements, Ying-chun hurried to stop her.

‘Enough, enough. If you can’t bring the phoenix back, there’s no need to drag in a lot of other things and shout about them. I don’t want it any more. If Their Ladyships ask, I shall say I’ve lost it. That won’t hurt you in any way. You’d do better to go out and have a rest.’

She told Xiu-ju to pour tea.

Xiu-ju was angry and distraught. ‘You may not be afraid, miss, but what are we here for, if we let your things be lost?

‘Now she’s accusing you of spending their money, and trying to offset it against the phoenix. Suppose Her Ladyship asks why you’ve spent so much. Will she think we’ve been taking advantage of you? That would never do!’

As she spoke she began to cry. Chess could bear to listen no longer. With an effort she came over to help Xiu-ju question the woman. Unable to stop them, Ying-chun took up the Treatise on Response and Retribution and began to read.

The three were still at it when Bao-chai, Dai-yu, Bao-qin and Tan-chun arrived. Afraid that Ying-chun would be unhappy about the day’s events, they had agreed to come and comfort her. From the courtyard they could hear the argument. Tan-chun looked through the gauze window and saw Ying-chun leaning back on her bed with a book, as though she heard nothing at all. She smiled. The little maids hurried to raise the curtain and announce the visitors. Ying-chun put down her book and rose. Seeing that company had arrived, and Tan-chun among them, the woman stopped without being told and tried to slip away.

Tan-chun sat down. ‘Who was talking here just now? It sounded rather like an argument.’

‘Nothing in particular,’ Ying-chun said with a smile. ‘They’re just making a great deal out of very little. Why ask about it?’

‘I heard something about a “gold phoenix”,’ said Tan-chun, smiling too, ‘and something about “no money, so they come asking us servants for it”. Who has been asking servants for money? Surely you haven’t, sister? Surely you have the same monthly allowance and the same provision for your needs as the rest of us?’

‘Exactly, miss,’ said Chess and Xiu-ju. ‘All the young ladies are alike. Their money is left in the hands of their nurses and mammas. Even we don’t know how the accounts are kept. When something is wanted, we only have to say so. Now she insists that our mistress has spent more than her allowance, and they’ve had to make up a great deal out of their own pockets. But what has our mistress ever asked her for?’

‘If my sister hasn’t been asking them for money,’ Tan-chun said with a smile, ‘perhaps it was one of us!

‘Call her back in. I should like to ask her.’

‘What a funny thing to say,’ Ying-chun said, smiling. ‘You have nothing to do with it. How could you have cost her anything?’

‘That isn’t how I see it,’ said Tan-chun. ‘You and I are alike. Your affairs are the same as mine. If she says such things about you, she says them about me. If someone in my rooms complained about me and you heard it, you would be included in the complaint in just the same way.

‘We are their mistresses. Naturally we don’t trouble ourselves over these small money matters. If something occurs to us, we ask for it. That does happen. But I don’t see how a filigree gold phoenix comes into it.’

Terrified that Xiu-ju and the others would expose her, Wang Zhu-er’s wife hurried back in and tried to cover up the matter with talk. Tan-chun understood exactly what she was doing.

‘You really are foolish,’ she said with a smile.

‘Your mother-in-law has already been found out. Why not take the opportunity to ask Mrs Lian for some of the money that hasn’t yet been distributed, and redeem it with that? Then it would all be settled. It isn’t as though the affair were still secret, with everyone keeping quiet to save face. Now that all face is lost, even if there were ten offences, there is still only one person to punish. You can’t cut off two heads from one body. If you take my advice, you’ll go and speak to Mrs Lian. What good can it do, making such a fuss in here?’

Tan-chun had put her finger on the trouble, and the woman could deny it no longer. But she still dared not go and confess to Xi-feng.

‘If I hadn’t heard, I could have left it alone,’ Tan-chun said with a smile. ‘Since I have, I must help you settle it.’

Unknown to the others, she had already given Scribe a look and sent her out.

They were still talking when Patience came in.

Bao-qin clapped her hands and laughed. ‘Has Third Sister got a magic charm for summoning spirits and their generals?’

‘No Taoist mysteries here,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘This is the very finest military art: “Guard yourself like a maiden; break away like a cunning hare.” A splendid plan for taking the enemy unawares.’

While the two were joking, Bao-chai signalled to them to stop and changed the subject.

As soon as she saw Patience, Tan-chun asked, ‘Is your mistress any better? She must be quite muddled by her illness. Nothing concerns her any more, and we’re left to put up with treatment like this.’

‘What treatment, miss?’ Patience asked at once. ‘Who dares upset you? Please tell me what you want me to do.’

Now Wang Zhu-er’s wife began to panic. She hurried up to Patience.

‘Sit down, sister. Let me explain how it happened. Please listen.’

Patience’s face grew stern. ‘When the young lady is speaking, what right have you or I to interrupt? If you knew anything of manners, you’d be waiting outside. You have no business coming in here without being called. Since when have married servants from outside been allowed to walk into the young ladies’ rooms for no reason?’

‘You don’t understand,’ said Xiu-ju. ‘We have no rules in these rooms. Anyone who pleases can come in.’

‘Then it’s your fault,’ Patience said. ‘If your mistress is good-natured, you ought to drive them out yourselves, and report it to Her Ladyship afterwards.’

At Patience’s rebuke, Wang Zhu-er’s wife flushed and at last withdrew.

‘Let me tell you,’ Tan-chun went on. ‘If it were someone else who had offended me, I could let it pass.

‘But this woman and her mother-in-law trade on the old woman’s being a mamma, and take advantage of Second Sister’s good nature. They have taken her jewellery without asking and used it for gambling. Then they produce false accounts and clever reckonings, try to force her to plead for them, and shout and scream at these two maids in her bedroom. Second Sister can do nothing to control them. I couldn’t stand by and watch, so I sent for you to ask: are they creatures from another world who have never heard of proper behaviour? Or is someone putting them up to it, so that once they’ve subdued Second Sister, they can come for me and Fourth Sister next?’

‘Miss, how can you say such a thing?’ Patience protested with an anxious smile. ‘How could my mistress bear that charge?’

Tan-chun gave a cold laugh. ‘As the sayings go, “A creature grieves for its own kind”, and “When the teeth are gone, the lips perish.” Naturally I feel a little alarmed.’

‘This affair itself is no great matter,’ Patience said. ‘It can easily be dealt with. But as she is your milk-brother’s wife, Second Miss, what would you like done?’

All this time Ying-chun had been reading the stories in the Treatise with Bao-chai. She had not even heard what Tan-chun said. Now, at Patience’s question, she smiled.

‘If you ask me, I don’t know what to do. They’ve done wrong, and they must suffer for it themselves. I can’t plead for them, but I shan’t scold them either. As for what they took without asking, if they bring it back I’ll accept it. If not, I don’t want it. Should Their Ladyships ask, if I can conceal it or smooth it over, that will be their good fortune. If I can’t, there’s nothing I can do. I can’t deceive Their Ladyships on their account. I shall have to tell the truth. You may say I’m too good-natured and can’t make up my mind. If you have a good plan that will please everyone and keep Their Ladyships from being angry, do whatever you think best. I know nothing about it.’

Everyone found this amusing.

‘Truly, “With tigers and wolves massed at the steps, she still discourses on cause and retribution”,’ said Dai-yu, laughing. ‘If you were a man, Second Sister, how would you ever manage all the people in this household?’

‘Exactly,’ Ying-chun said with a smile.

‘So many men are like that. What can you expect of me?’

Before she had finished, someone else came in. Who it was we do not yet know. To find out, listen to the next chapter.
