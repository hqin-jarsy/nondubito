Chapter 75 At a night feast a strange omen sounds a note of sorrow; At Mid-Autumn new verses earn an auspicious prediction

Chapter end

You-shi left Xi-chun in a temper and was about to go to Lady Wang when the old nurses accompanying her quietly intervened.

‘Better not go to the main apartments just now, ma’am. Some people from the Zhen family have arrived with some things. We don’t know what private business they’re about. It might be awkward if you went in.’

‘Yesterday your master told me he’d seen in the Court Gazette that the Zhen family had committed an offence,’ said You-shi. ‘Their property is being confiscated and they’re being brought to the capital for trial. How can they be sending people here?’

‘Exactly, ma’am. A few women came just now, looking quite unlike themselves, all flustered and frightened. I dare say there’s something they don’t want people to know.’

You-shi went no further, but turned back to Li Wan’s. The Imperial physician had just left after taking her pulse. Li Wan had been feeling a little brighter lately and was sitting up in bed, wrapped in her quilt and propped against pillows, wishing for a visitor or two to gossip with. You-shi, however, showed none of her usual pleasant friendliness. She merely sat there staring.

‘You’ve been over here quite a while,’ said Li Wan. ‘Have you had anything to eat in the other rooms? You must be hungry.’ She told Su-yun to see what fresh cakes there were and bring a selection.

‘No, no,’ You-shi said at once. ‘You’ve been ill all this time. What fresh things could you have? Besides, I’m not hungry.’

‘Lan’s aunt sent some very good cereal-tea flour yesterday. Let them mix you a bowl of that.’ She gave the order, but You-shi remained silent, lost in thought.

‘You haven’t had your midday wash, ma’am,’ the maids and married servants with her said. ‘Would you like to do it now, while you have the chance?’

You-shi nodded.

Li Wan promptly told Su-yun to fetch her toilet case. Su-yun brought it, together with her own rouge and powder.

‘This is the one thing our mistress hasn’t got,’ she said with a smile. ‘These are mine, ma’am. If you don’t think them too dirty, perhaps you could make do with a little.’

‘I may not have any,’ said Li Wan, ‘but you ought to have gone to the young ladies for some. Fancy bringing out your own so openly! Luckily it’s only her. Anyone else might have taken offence.’

‘What does it matter?’ said You-shi, smiling. ‘I’ve used everyone’s things when I’ve come over before. Am I suddenly to find them dirty today?’

As she spoke, she sat cross-legged on the edge of the kang.

Silver Butterfly hurried forward to remove You-shi’s bracelets and rings, then spread a large towel over her lap, carefully covering her clothes. A little maid, Chao-dou-er, brought a large basin of warm water and stood before her, bending at the waist to hold it out.

‘Where are your manners?’ said Li Wan.

‘Not one of them has a grain of sense,’ Silver Butterfly said with a laugh. ‘Say “gourd” and they can think of nothing but a ladle. Our mistress is easy with us, and at home she lets things pass. So you take advantage of it and do as you please, at home or out, even in front of her relations.’

‘Let her be,’ said You-shi. ‘Once I’ve washed, it’s over and done with.’

Chao-dou-er hastily dropped to her knees.

‘High and low, our people know all about sham manners and keeping up appearances,’ You-shi said with a smile. ‘It’s what they actually do that takes some beating.’

From this Li Wan knew that You-shi had heard about the night before.

‘There’s something behind that,’ she said, smiling. ‘Whose doings take some beating?’

‘You ask me! Have you been so ill you died and missed it all?’

Before she had finished, someone announced Miss Bao-chai. They were calling for her to be shown in when she entered. You-shi hurriedly dried her face, rose and offered her a seat.

‘What brings you here all by yourself? Where are the other girls?’

‘I haven’t seen them either,’ said Bao-chai. ‘Mother isn’t well today, and both the women at home are still in bed with the illness that’s going round. The others can’t be relied on, so I’m going out today to keep her company, and stay with her at night. I meant to tell Grandmother and Aunt Wang, but then I thought it wasn’t anything very serious and there was no need to mention it. I shall be back when she’s better, after all. So I came to let you know, Cousin.’

Li Wan merely looked at You-shi and smiled. You-shi looked back at her with a smile of her own. When You-shi had finished washing, they all had cereal tea.

‘In that case,’ Li Wan said with a smile, ‘I must send someone to ask after Aunt and find out what’s wrong. Being ill myself, I can’t go in person. Of course you must go, my dear. I’ll send someone over to look after your rooms.

‘Only do come back after a day or two. Don’t leave me to be blamed.’

‘Blamed for what?’ Bao-chai asked, smiling. ‘It’s an ordinary enough thing to do. You haven’t taken a bribe to let a thief escape. I don’t think you need send anyone else over. Why not ask Xiang-yun here? You could have her with you for a day or two, and save the trouble.’

‘Where has Cousin Shi got to, by the way?’ asked You-shi.

‘I sent her to find Tan-chun just now and bring her here,’ said Bao-chai. ‘Then I can explain it to her too.’

Sure enough, while they were speaking, Miss Shi and the Third Miss were announced. Once everyone was seated, Bao-chai told them she was going out.

‘Excellent,’ said Tan-chun. ‘You can come back when Aunt is better, of course. But even when she’s better, you needn’t come back at all.’

‘What an odd thing to say!’ You-shi said with a laugh. ‘Are you driving our relations away now?’

‘Exactly.’ Tan-chun gave a cold laugh. ‘Better I should send them off first than have someone else turn them out. Relations can be fond of each other without clinging on here till they die. Look at us: we’re all one family, flesh and blood, and every one of us is like a fighting cock, longing to gobble the others up!’

‘What ill luck brought me here today?’ You-shi said hastily, trying to laugh it off. ‘Whichever sister I meet, I catch her in a temper.’

‘Who told you to come to a hot stove?’ said Tan-chun. ‘Who else has offended you?’ She thought for a moment. ‘Surely Fourth Sister wouldn’t make a fuss with you. Who can it have been?’

You-shi gave a vague answer.

Tan-chun knew she was afraid of trouble and would not say much.

‘Don’t pretend to be so meek,’ she said with a laugh. ‘Unless the court sentences you, no one’s going to cut your head off. There’s no need to be frightened of everything. I’ll tell you plainly: I slapped that old woman Wang Shan-bao’s wife yesterday, and I’ve still got that crime on my head. What can they do but talk behind my back? They can hardly give me a beating!’

Bao-chai quickly asked why she had slapped her. Tan-chun gave a full account of the search the night before and of the slap. Now that Tan-chun had spoken, You-shi told them what had just happened with Xi-chun.

‘That’s her queer temper,’ said Tan-chun. ‘She carries her aloofness too far. None of us could be as proud as she is.’

Then she went on, ‘Nothing happened first thing this morning. I asked and heard that Peppercorn Feng was ill again. So I sent my nurse out to find out how Wang Shan-bao’s wife was getting on. She came back and told me the woman had had a beating. Aunt Xing blamed her for meddling.’

‘That’s only right,’ said You-shi and Li Wan.

Tan-chun laughed coldly. ‘Anyone can put on a show like that. We shall see.’

Neither You-shi nor Li Wan answered.

Presently, judging that dinner would soon be served in the main apartments, Xiang-yun and Bao-chai went back to sort out their clothes. We need say no more of them for the moment. You-shi and the others took leave of Li Wan and went to Grandmother Jia’s. Grandmother was reclining on her couch while Lady Wang told her how the Zhen family had incurred punishment, had their property confiscated and been summoned to the capital for trial. Grandmother was feeling distressed by it when the younger women came in.

‘Where have you been?’ she asked. ‘Do you know how Xi-feng and Li Wan are today?’

‘They’re both a little better today,’ You-shi and the others promptly replied.

Grandmother nodded and sighed.

‘Let’s leave other people’s affairs alone. What we ought to be discussing is our moon-viewing on the fifteenth of the eighth month.’

‘Everything’s ready,’ Lady Wang said with a smile. ‘We only need to know where you’d like it. The garden is rather open, and the wind will be cold at night.’

‘What harm is there in putting on another couple of layers?’ Grandmother said, smiling. ‘It’s just the place for looking at the moon. We can hardly stay away.’

Meanwhile the married servants and maids had brought in the dining table. Lady Wang, You-shi and the others hurried forward to lay chopsticks and serve rice. When Grandmother saw that her own dishes were all in place and more were being brought from two large food boxes, she knew that the separate branches of the family were following their old custom of sending dishes for her.

‘What have they all sent?’ she asked. ‘I’ve told you several times that this can be stopped now, but you won’t listen. We’re not as well off as we used to be.’

‘I’ve told them several times too,’ Faithful said at once. ‘They won’t listen. There’s nothing to be done.’

‘It’s only ordinary things from our own table,’ said Lady Wang, smiling.

‘I’m keeping a vegetarian fast today, so there’s nothing much. You’re not very fond of wheat gluten or bean curd, Mother, so I only chose some water-shield relish with pepper oil.’

‘Just the thing,’ said Grandmother with a smile. ‘That’s exactly what I feel like eating.’

Faithful moved the dish close to her. Bao-qin courteously invited each of the others to eat before taking her seat. Grandmother called Tan-chun to join them. She too invited everyone else first, then sat down opposite Bao-qin. Scribe hurried off for a bowl.

Pointing to the remaining dishes, Faithful said, ‘These two are from the Elder Master. I can’t make out what they are. This bowl is bamboo shoots with chicken marrow, from Master Zheng.’

She placed only the bamboo shoots on the table. Grandmother tasted a little, then gave instructions.

‘Have those other two sent back. Say I’ve eaten some. There’s no need to send things every day. If I want anything, I shall ask.’

The women agreed and took the dishes back. We need not follow them.

‘Is there any gruel?’ Grandmother asked. ‘A little of that will do.’

You-shi was already holding out a bowl, telling her it was red-rice gruel. Grandmother took it and ate half a bowl.

‘Send this gruel to Xi-feng,’ she said. Then she pointed. ‘This bowl of bamboo shoots and this plate of air-cured civet are for Pin-pin and Bao-yu. Give that bowl of meat to young Lan.’

She turned to You-shi. ‘I’ve finished. You can eat now.’

You-shi assented and waited while Grandmother rinsed her mouth and washed her hands. Grandmother got down and walked about, talking to Lady Wang as she digested her meal. You-shi asked leave to sit. Tan-chun and Bao-qin rose.

‘Forgive us for leaving you,’ they said, smiling.

‘All on my own!’ said You-shi. ‘I’m not used to sitting at a great table by myself.’

‘Faithful, Amber,’ Grandmother said with a smile, ‘you may as well have something too, and keep her company.’

‘Good, good, good!’ said You-shi. ‘I was just going to ask.’

‘I love watching a crowd of people eat,’ Grandmother said. She pointed to Silver Butterfly. ‘This is a good girl too. Come and eat with your mistress. You can go back to your rules when you’re away from me.’

‘Come along,’ You-shi said. ‘No need to make a pretence of it.’

Grandmother stood watching them with her hands behind her back, enjoying herself.

Seeing that the woman waiting to replenish their bowls was holding a bowl of the rice served to the servants, while You-shi was still eating ordinary white short-grain rice, Grandmother asked, ‘Have you lost your wits? Why bring that rice for your mistress?’

‘Your rice has all been eaten, ma’am. There was an extra young lady today, so it ran a little short.’

‘These days every cap is made to fit its head exactly,’ said Faithful. ‘You can’t have the least bit over.’

Lady Wang hastened to explain. ‘With the droughts and floods this last year or two, the estates haven’t been able to deliver their full quota of rice. These fine varieties are harder still to get. We issue just as much as will be eaten, for fear of running short all at once and having to buy rice that isn’t to your taste.’

‘So this really is a case of “Even a clever daughter-in-law can’t make rice gruel without rice,”’ Grandmother said with a smile.

Everyone laughed.

‘Well then, fetch the Third Miss’s rice to make it up,’ said Faithful. ‘That will do just as well. How can you be so stupid?’

‘There’s enough here for me,’ You-shi said, laughing. ‘No need to fetch it.’

‘Enough for you, perhaps. Am I not going to eat?’ said Faithful.

Only then did the women standing by hurry off to get it.

Presently Lady Wang went to her own dinner. You-shi stayed talking and joking with Grandmother until the first watch began.

‘It’s dark,’ Grandmother said. ‘You had better go home.’

You-shi took her leave and went to the main gate, where she got into her carriage. Silver Butterfly sat on its edge. The women lowered the curtains and walked ahead with the little maids to wait at the gate of the other house. The gates of the two mansions were less than an arrow’s flight apart. There was no need for a full escort on these everyday visits, especially since she so often returned after dark. The old nurses and little maids were across in a few steps. The gatekeepers from both houses had already gone to the east and west ends of the street to stop passers-by. No animal was harnessed to You-shi’s large carriage. Seven or eight pages took hold of its rings and wheels and gently pushed and pulled it over to the entrance steps. They then withdrew beyond the stone lions. The old nurses raised the curtains, Silver Butterfly got down first, and helped You-shi out.

Seven or eight lanterns, large and small, lit everything clearly. You-shi saw four or five large carriages standing by the lions on either side and knew they belonged to the gambling guests.

‘Look,’ she said to Silver Butterfly and the others. ‘That’s just the ones who came in carriages. Who knows how many came on horseback? Their horses will be tied up in the stables, where we can’t see them. I wonder how much their fathers and mothers put by for them, to let them enjoy themselves like this.’

As she spoke, she reached the hall. Jia Rong’s wife came out to meet her with the married servants and maids, all carrying candles.

‘Every day I mean to steal a look at them, and I never get the chance,’ You-shi said with a smile. ‘Tonight is just right. I’ll go past their window on my way in.’

The women agreed and led the way with lanterns. One went ahead to warn the pages in attendance, quietly, not to make a fuss or show surprise. You-shi and her party stole up beneath the window. Inside, amid the merry calls of praise for threes and fours, there was no lack of angry curses for fives and sixes.

Jia Zhen, being in mourning, had lately been unable to roam about in search of pleasure, or amuse himself with actors and music. Bored beyond endurance, he had devised a way to pass the time. Under the pretext of archery practice, he invited the young men of other great families, along with his rich and noble friends and relations, to come and compete during the day.

‘Shooting away at random like this does no good,’ he said. ‘We shan’t improve. We’ll only spoil our form. We must agree on penalties and put up something to play for, to make everyone try.’

A target was therefore erected in the archery ground below the Tower of Heavenly Fragrance, and they agreed to meet there every day after breakfast. Jia Zhen did not wish to appear as the organiser, so Jia Rong was made host. The visitors were all sons of hereditary houses, every one of them rich, young and idle, a set of fine-clothed rakes whose pursuits were cockfighting, coursing and the appraisal of women in the pleasure quarters. They agreed to provide supper in turn: coming every day to shoot, they could hardly expect Jia Rong to entertain them alone. Pigs and sheep, geese and ducks were slaughtered day after day. It was like the contest of treasures at Lintong, each man showing off the excellence of his cooks and their cooking.

In less than half a month Jia She and Jia Zheng heard of the gatherings. Knowing nothing of what lay behind them, they thoroughly approved. If the boys had fallen short in letters, they ought at least to practise arms, particularly since the hereditary privileges of their house derived from military service. Both branches accordingly ordered the four boys, Jia Huan, Jia Cong, Bao-yu and Jia Lan, to go over after their meal and practise archery with Jia Zhen before they were allowed home.

But Jia Zhen’s purpose lay elsewhere. After another day or two, the need to rest their arms and recover their strength became an excuse for an evening game of dominoes, with no more at stake than who should pay for the wine. Gradually they began to play for money. Three or four months had now gone by, and with each passing day gambling had gained on archery. They openly played cards and threw dice, taking a cut for the house and running games far into the night. The servants all made something out of it and could wish for nothing better, so the practice was firmly established. Outsiders knew not a word.

Lady Xing’s younger brother Xing De-quan, who had lately developed a passion for this sort of thing, was one of the company. So was Xue Pan, the foremost of those who habitually delighted in giving their money away. How could such a gathering fail to please him? Xing De-quan, though Lady Xing’s own brother, differed greatly from her in both his disposition and his conduct.

His only pleasures were drinking, gambling and visiting the pleasure quarters. He spent freely and had no double dealings with anyone. He liked people who enjoyed a drink and kept his distance from those who did not. He treated high and low, masters and servants, all alike, making no distinction of rank. Everyone therefore called him Foolish Uncle. Xue Pan was already celebrated as the Foolish Master. Brought together today, the pair found that they both liked the brisk pace of qiang xinkuai, and recruited two more players for a game on the kang in the outer room.

Another group was playing gongfan at the large table on the floor. In the inner room a rather more decorous party was playing tien gow with dominoes. The pages serving here were all boys under fifteen; grown men were not admitted to serve here. This was why You-shi had ventured to steal a look through the window. There were also two boy favourites, sixteen or seventeen years old, to serve wine, both dressed and made up to look as fine as painted jade.

Xue Pan had just lost a round and was in a bad temper. Fortunately, when the second round ended and the reckoning was made, his winnings more than covered his loss. His spirits rose at once.

‘Let’s stop and have something to eat before we go on,’ said Jia Zhen. He asked how the other two parties were getting on. The tien gow players inside settled their accounts and waited for the meal. The gongfan game was not finished, and its players would not stop to eat. Since they could not all be hurried along together, a large table was laid for the first party, with Jia Zhen to keep them company. He told Jia Rong to wait and entertain the others. Xue Pan, now in high spirits, put an arm round one of the boy favourites as he drank, and told them to take wine to Foolish Uncle Xing as well.

Foolish Uncle had lost and was out of sorts. Two bowls of wine made him a little drunk. He was angry with the two boys for attending only to the winners and neglecting those who had lost.

‘You little catamites are all alike,’ he cursed. ‘Always swimming towards the money.

‘We’re together every day. Which of us haven’t you had favours from? I lose a few taels for once, and suddenly you sort us into grades! Do you think you’ll never want anything from us again?’

Seeing that he was drunk, the others hastened to agree.

‘Quite right, quite right. Their ways are disgraceful.’

They called to the boys, ‘Quickly, offer him wine and apologise.’

The two boys had been well rehearsed in this performance. They promptly knelt to present the wine.

‘In our line, sir, our master teaches us to make no distinction between old friends and new, close friends and distant ones. We must only honour whoever has money and power at the moment. Even if he were a living Buddha or an immortal, once he lost his money and power we wouldn’t be allowed to notice him. We’re young, sir, and this is the trade we’re in. Please make allowances and let us off this once.’

They remained on their knees with the wine held up. Uncle Xing’s heart had softened, but he still pretended to be angry and ignored them.

‘The boys are telling the truth,’ the others urged. ‘You’ve always been tender towards the pretty ones, Uncle. Why are you so different today? How can they get up if you won’t drink?’

Uncle Xing could hold out no longer.

‘If you gentlemen hadn’t spoken, I wouldn’t have anything more to do with them.’

He took the bowl and drained it at a draught. Another was poured for him. The wine now stirred old memories and loosened his tongue. He struck the table and sighed to Jia Zhen.

‘No wonder they think money is life itself.

‘How many people born into great old official families forget their own flesh and blood the moment money and power come into it! My dear nephew, did you hear about my quarrel with your aunt over there yesterday?’

‘I hadn’t heard,’ said Jia Zhen.

‘All over that damned stuff, money,’ Uncle Xing sighed. ‘Terrible, terrible!’

Jia Zhen knew very well that he and Lady Xing did not get on, and that her repeated rebuffs gave him grievances to air. He tried to reason with him.

‘You’re a little too free with your spending, Uncle. If you keep on throwing money away, how much could anyone give you that would be enough?’

‘My dear nephew, you don’t know the inside of our family affairs. I was only a child when my mother died. I knew nothing of the world. There were three sisters. Your aunt alone was grown up and married, and she took charge of every bit of the property and brought it here. My second sister is married now too, but her family is very hard up. The third is still at home. Everything they need is controlled by Wang Shan-bao’s wife, the dowry servant here.

‘When I come asking for money, it isn’t Jia family money I want. The Xing family property would be quite enough for me. But I can’t get my hands on it, and there’s no one I can take my grievance to.’

As he rambled on in his drunkenness, Jia Zhen feared that others might hear this unseemly talk and hurriedly tried to soothe him.

Outside, You-shi heard every word distinctly.

‘Did you hear that?’ she whispered to Silver Butterfly, smiling. ‘That’s the brother of Her Ladyship in the north courtyard, complaining about her. If even her own brother talks like that, you can hardly blame the others.’

She was about to listen further when the gongfan players stopped and called for wine. One of them asked, ‘Who was it that offended Uncle just now? We didn’t quite catch it. Tell us and we’ll judge between you.’

Xing De-quan explained how the two boy favourites had neglected the loser and run after the winner. The young rake said, ‘Well, that is annoying. No wonder you’re angry, Uncle. Let me ask you two: Uncle may have lost, but it was only money he lost. He hasn’t lost his cock. Why are you ignoring him?’

Everyone burst out laughing. Even Xing De-quan sprayed the floor with a mouthful of food. Outside, You-shi spat softly in disgust.

‘Listen to them! Shameless little wretches, fit for the knife. The soft spots on their skulls have barely closed, and they’re already spouting filth. Let them stuff down some more of that damned yellow swill and who knows what’ll come out next.’

She went in to take off her ornaments and go to bed. Not until the fourth watch did Jia Zhen dismiss the party and go to Pei-feng’s room.

When he got up the next day, he was told that the watermelons and mooncakes were all ready, waiting to be allotted and sent out.

‘Ask your mistress to see to sending them,’ he told Pei-feng. ‘I have other things to do.’

Pei-feng went and told You-shi, who had to arrange the portions and send people out with them herself. Presently Pei-feng returned.

‘The master wants to know whether you’re going out today, ma’am. He says we’re a house in mourning and can’t celebrate the festival tomorrow, on the fifteenth. But this evening would do. We could all mark the occasion with some melon, mooncakes and wine.’

‘It’s not that I want to go out,’ said You-shi. ‘Mrs Zhu is ill over there, and now Xi-feng’s laid up too. If I don’t go, there’ll be nobody left. Besides, I haven’t a moment to spare. What occasion am I to mark?’

‘The master says he’s told everyone not to come today. They’re not coming again till the sixteenth. Whatever happens, he insists on inviting you to drink with him.’

‘Inviting me?’ You-shi laughed. ‘I haven’t the means to invite him back.’

Pei-feng went off laughing, and soon returned with another smile.

‘The master says the invitation includes supper. You must come back early, ma’am. He’s told me to go with you.’

‘In that case, what are we having for breakfast?’ asked You-shi. ‘Let’s eat quickly, so I can be off.’

‘The master says he’s eating in the outer rooms. You’re to eat by yourself.’

‘Who’s out there today?’

‘I heard there were two people newly arrived from Nanjing. I don’t know who.’

While they were talking, Jia Rong’s wife, dressed and ready, came to pay her respects. Breakfast was soon served. You-shi took the place of honour, with her daughter-in-law below her to keep her company. When they had finished, You-shi changed her clothes and went over to the Rong mansion again. She did not return till evening.

Jia Zhen had indeed had a whole pig boiled and a whole sheep roasted, besides more dishes and fruit than could be listed. In the Hall of Clustered Green, in the Garden of Gathered Fragrance, peacock screens were unfolded and lotus-patterned seat cushions laid out. He gathered his wife and concubines there, first for supper, then for wine, to enjoy the moon and make merry to their hearts’ content.

As the first watch approached, the breeze was fresh, the moon radiant, and all above and below shone silver. Jia Zhen wanted a drinking game, so You-shi called Pei-feng and the other three women to join them, seated in a row at the lower end. They played guessing games and finger games, drinking for a while. The wine made Jia Zhen merrier still. He had a purple-bamboo vertical flute brought in and told Pei-feng to play while Wen-hua sang. The clear, tender voice was enough to ravish the soul. When the singing ended they resumed their games.

It was nearing the third watch, and Jia Zhen was thoroughly drunk. They were putting on extra clothes, drinking tea and changing cups before another round of wine when a long sigh sounded from beside the wall. Everyone heard it plainly. They started in fear and bewilderment.

‘Who’s there?’ Jia Zhen shouted fiercely.

He called several times. No one answered.

‘Perhaps it’s one of our people outside the wall,’ said You-shi.

‘Nonsense,’ said Jia Zhen. ‘There are no servants’ rooms anywhere round these walls. And that side is right beside the ancestral shrine. Who could be there?’

Before he had finished, they heard a gust of wind sweep over the wall. From inside the shrine came what seemed to be the sound of its panelled doors opening and shutting. The air felt eerily chill, colder than before. The moonlight had grown wan; it no longer shone with its earlier brightness. Everyone’s hair stood on end.

Jia Zhen was half sobered. He kept his composure rather better than the rest, but inwardly he too was frightened and perplexed, and all his pleasure was gone. He forced himself to sit a little longer, then went back to his rooms to bed.

Early next morning, the fifteenth, he led the sons and nephews to open the ancestral shrine for the new- and full-moon rites. He examined the place carefully. Everything was as sound and orderly as ever, with no sign of anything strange. He put it down to his own drunken fancies and said nothing. When the rites were over, he had the doors shut again and watched them being locked.

Jia Zhen and You-shi did not go to the Rong mansion till after supper. Jia She and Jia Zheng were sitting in Grandmother’s room, chatting and making her laugh. Jia Lian, Bao-yu, Jia Huan and Jia Lan stood below them in attendance. On Jia Zhen’s arrival, the proper greetings were exchanged. After a few words Grandmother told him to sit. He asked leave and perched sideways on a little stool near the door, holding himself respectfully alert.

‘How has your cousin Bao-yu been getting on with his archery these last two days?’ Grandmother asked with a smile.

Jia Zhen stood up at once. ‘He’s improved a great deal. Not only is his form good, he can draw a bow one degree stronger now.’

‘That will do,’ said Grandmother. ‘Don’t let him try for too much strength. He must be careful not to strain himself.’

Jia Zhen hastened to assure her that he would.

‘The mooncakes you sent yesterday were good,’ Grandmother went on. ‘The watermelons looked good, but when we cut them open they were nothing much.’

‘We have a new cook who specialises in pastries,’ Jia Zhen replied with a smile. ‘I tried his mooncakes and found them really good. Only then did I venture to have some made for you.

‘The watermelons have always been quite good in other years. I don’t know why they’re so poor this year.’

‘Too much rain, probably,’ said Jia Zheng.

‘The moon is up now,’ Grandmother said with a smile. ‘Let’s go and offer incense.’

She rose, rested a hand on Bao-yu’s shoulder and led them all into the garden.

The main garden gates stood wide open, with large horn lanterns hanging from them. On the terrace before the Hall of Auspicious Shade, a bushel-shaped mass of incense burned beside wind-resistant candles, and melons, mooncakes and fruit of every kind were set out as offerings. Lady Xing and the other women had been waiting inside for some time. Moonlight and coloured lanterns, the gathered company and drifting incense made a scene of glowing, misty brilliance beyond description. Prayer rugs and brocade cushions were spread on the ground. Grandmother washed her hands, offered incense and made her obeisance. Everyone else followed.

‘The hill is the best place to look at the moon,’ she said, and ordered them to go to the great hall on its ridge. People hurried off to make the arrangements.

Grandmother remained for a little while in the Hall of Auspicious Shade, resting, drinking tea and chatting. When word came that everything was ready, she set off up the hill with support on either side.

‘The moss on the stones may be slippery,’ Lady Wang and the others said. ‘Wouldn’t it be better to go up in the bamboo chair?’

‘They sweep it every day,’ said Grandmother. ‘Besides, it’s a broad, smooth path. Why shouldn’t I stretch my limbs a little?’

Jia She and Jia Zheng led the way. Two old women carried two horn hand-lanterns; Faithful, Amber and You-shi stayed close to support her, with Lady Xing and the others following round her. The path wound upwards for a little over a hundred paces to the open hall on the summit ridge. Its position on this high green crest had given it the name Green Crest Lodge.

Tables and chairs had been set out on the level terrace before the hall, and a large folding screen divided the space in two. All the tables and chairs were round, chosen expressly to suggest the completeness of a family reunion. Grandmother took the central place at the head. Jia She, Jia Zhen, Jia Lian and Jia Rong sat down the left side; Jia Zheng, Bao-yu, Jia Huan and Jia Lan down the right, forming a circle. They occupied only half the space; the lower half remained empty.

‘We don’t usually seem so few,’ Grandmother said with a smile. ‘But looking at us today, there really aren’t many of us. We hardly amount to anything. In the old days, on a night like this there would have been thirty or forty, men and women together. How lively it was! Today there are only these few. Too few. I’d ask some more people, but they all have parents of their own and must keep the festival at home. We can’t expect them to come.

‘Let’s have the girls over to sit on that side.’

Someone was sent behind the screen to Lady Xing’s table to invite Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun out. Jia Lian, Bao-yu and the others all rose from their seats, letting the girls sit first, then taking their own places below them in order. Grandmother had a sprig of osmanthus cut and told one of the married women to beat a drum behind the screen while they passed the flower.

Whoever held it when the drum stopped had to drink a cup of wine and tell a joke. Grandmother began, passing it to Jia She, and so on round the company. After two rounds of drumming it stopped in Jia Zheng’s hand, and he had to drink. The young people slyly tugged and pinched one another, all smiles, eager to hear what sort of joke he would tell. Seeing his mother so pleased, Jia Zheng could only do his best to amuse her. He was about to begin when she added, laughing, ‘If it doesn’t make us laugh, there’s another forfeit.’

‘I only know one,’ he said with a smile. ‘If it doesn’t make you laugh, I shall just have to pay.’ He began, ‘There was once a man who was terribly afraid of his wife.’

At this first sentence everyone laughed. They had never heard Jia Zheng tell a joke before.

‘This is bound to be a good one,’ said Grandmother.

‘If it is, Mother, you must drink an extra cup,’ Jia Zheng said, smiling.

‘Of course,’ said Grandmother with a laugh.

‘This man was so afraid of his wife that he never dared take a step beyond what he was told,’ Jia Zheng went on. ‘But one year, on the fifteenth of the eighth month, he went out to buy some things and met a few friends in the street. Nothing would do but he must go home with them for a drink. Before he knew it he was drunk, and fell asleep at his friend’s house. He didn’t wake till the next day. It was too late for regrets. He had to go home and beg forgiveness. His wife was washing her feet. “Very well,” she said. “Lick my feet clean and I’ll forgive you.”

‘He had no choice but to lick them, but it made him feel sick and he began to retch. His wife was furious and made to beat him. “How dare you!” she said. He was so frightened he dropped to his knees and begged, “It’s not that your feet are dirty, ma’am. It’s just that I had too much yellow wine last night, and several pieces of mooncake filling. That’s what’s turned a bit sour today.”’

Grandmother and everyone else laughed. Jia Zheng quickly poured a cup of wine and presented it to her.

‘In that case,’ she said, laughing, ‘have them bring spirits at once. I don’t want to put you through all that trouble.’

Everyone laughed again. The drum started, and the flower went on from Jia Zheng. This time it stopped with Bao-yu. His father’s presence had already made him uneasy; now he had to be caught with the flower as well.

‘If I tell a joke and they don’t laugh,’ he thought, ‘he’ll say I can’t even tell a joke, let alone do anything else. That’ll be wrong. If I tell it well, he’ll say I’m no good at proper work and only know how to wag my tongue. That’ll be worse. Better not to tell one.’

He stood up and begged to be excused.

‘I can’t tell jokes. Please give me something else to do.’

‘Very well,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘Take “autumn” as your rhyme and make a poem on the scene before you. If it’s good, I’ll reward you. If not, look out tomorrow.’

‘We were enjoying our drinking game,’ Grandmother said at once. ‘Why must we start making poems?’

‘He can do it,’ said Jia Zheng.

‘Well then, let him.’ Grandmother had paper and brush brought.

‘But no piling up the usual words,’ Jia Zheng said. ‘Ice, jade, crystal, silver, colour, light, brightness, whiteness: you may use none of them. Find a thought of your own. Let us see what these years have done for your poetic imagination.’

This was just what Bao-yu wanted. He thought of four lines on the spot, wrote them down and presented them to his father. They ran…1

Jia Zheng read them and nodded without speaking. Seeing this, Grandmother knew there could be nothing very wrong.

‘Well?’ she asked.

Wishing to please her, Jia Zheng said, ‘A creditable effort. Only he won’t study, so his language still lacks refinement.’

‘That will do well enough,’ said Grandmother.

‘How old is he, after all? Must you insist on making him a great poet? You ought to encourage him for this. Then he’ll try all the harder.’

‘Yes, Mother,’ said Jia Zheng. He turned and told an old nurse to go out and tell the pages in the study to give Bao-yu two of the fans he had brought from Hainan.

Bao-yu hastily bowed his thanks and sat down again to resume the game. Jia Lan, seeing him rewarded, left his seat and composed a poem of his own, which he handed to Jia Zheng. It read…

Jia Zheng was delighted. He explained it to Grandmother, who was delighted too and at once told him to reward the boy. Everyone then sat down again and the game resumed.

This time the flower stopped with Jia She. He had to drink and tell a joke.

‘There was once a very dutiful son. His mother fell ill, and he searched everywhere for a doctor, without success. At last he brought in an old woman who practised acupuncture and moxibustion. She knew nothing about the pulse, but declared it was heat in the heart: a little needling and moxa would soon put that right. The son was alarmed. “If iron touches the heart, you die,” he said. “How can you put a needle into it?” “There’s no need to needle the heart,” she told him. “We’ll needle the ribs.” “But the ribs are a long way from the heart,” he said. “How will that cure her?” “It doesn’t matter,” the old woman said. “Don’t you know how many parents have hearts that lean to one side?”’

Everyone laughed. Grandmother too had to drink, though she took only half a cup. After a while she said with a smile, ‘Perhaps I should have that old woman put a needle in me too.’

Jia She realised that he had spoken rashly and his mother suspected a meaning behind it. He hurriedly rose to offer her wine, laughing and trying to explain it away with other talk. Grandmother could hardly pursue the subject, and they went on with the game.

Unexpectedly, this time the flower stopped in Jia Huan’s hand. He had lately made some progress with his books, but shared Bao-yu’s distaste for serious application. Like Bao-yu, he often enjoyed reading verse, with a particular liking for the strange and fantastic, for poems of spirits and immortals. Seeing Bao-yu rewarded for his poem, he had been itching to show what he could do, but had not dared take the liberty in front of his father. Now that the flower had come to him, he asked for paper and brush and dashed off a quatrain, which he handed to Jia Zheng.

Jia Zheng found it unusual, but its language still betrayed an unwillingness to study. He was displeased.

‘Brothers indeed! Every word you utter belongs to some perverse school. Neither of you will submit to rule or measure when you grow up. A pair of good-for-nothings! The ancients had their “two remarkable brothers”, so equally gifted that it was difficult to choose between them. You two may claim the name as well.

‘Only in your case the difficulty is in teaching you. The elder openly sets himself up as Wen Fei-qing, and now the younger thinks himself Cao Tang reborn.’

Jia She and the others laughed.

Jia She asked for the poem, read it through and praised it repeatedly.

‘To my mind this poem shows real spirit. Families like ours aren’t like those poor, pinched scholars, who must study by snow-lit windows and the glimmer of fireflies, then pluck the cassia in the Moon Palace before they can hold their heads up. Our boys ought to read a few books, of course, but only to understand a little more than other people. Once they’re fit for office, an office won’t escape them. Why waste so much effort and turn them into bookworms? That’s why I like this poem. It has the spirit of our noble house.’

He turned and sent for a number of his own curios to reward Jia Huan. Patting the boy on the head, he smiled.

‘Keep writing like that. That’s the way our family ought to speak. When the time comes, you’re sure to succeed to the hereditary post.’

‘It’s only a few lines he’s scribbled,’ Jia Zheng hurriedly protested. ‘There’s no occasion to talk of the future.’

He poured more wine, and they played another round.

‘You had better go now,’ said Grandmother. ‘Your literary gentlemen will be waiting outside, and you mustn’t neglect them. Besides, it’s well into the second watch. Once you’ve gone, I can enjoy myself a little longer with the girls, and then go to bed.’

Jia She and the others stopped the game. Together they offered her one more cup of wine, then went out with the sons and nephews.
