Chapter 24 The Drunken Vajra esteems generosity and honour above wealth; A lovestruck girl loses a handkerchief and awakens longing

Chapter end

Now, as Lin Dai-yu’s thoughts of love grew ever more entangled and held her fast, someone suddenly clapped her on the back.

‘What are you doing here all by yourself?’

Dai-yu gave quite a start. She turned to find that it was Caltrop.

‘You silly girl! What a fright you gave me. Where have you just come from?’

Caltrop giggled. ‘I came to look for our young lady, but I can’t find her anywhere. Your Nightingale is looking for you too. She says Mrs Lian has sent you some sort of tea. Come along. Let’s go back to your rooms and sit down.’

As she spoke, she took Dai-yu by the hand and led her back to the Naiad’s House. Sure enough, Xi-feng had sent two little jars of new tea of the kind reserved for imperial use. Dai-yu and Caltrop sat down.

What serious business could they have had to discuss? They merely remarked on how well this piece was embroidered and how fine the stitches were in that, played a game of chess and read a few lines of a book. Then Caltrop left, and we need say no more of them.

Let us return to Bao-yu. Aroma had found him and brought him back to his room, where Faithful was indeed reclining on the bed, examining Aroma’s needlework. When she saw him she said, ‘Where have you been? Her Old Ladyship is waiting for you. She wants you to go over and ask after the Elder Master. Hurry up and change your clothes so you can go.’

Aroma went into the inner room to fetch his clothes.

Bao-yu sat on the edge of the bed and slipped off his shoes. While he was waiting for his boots, he looked round at Faithful. She wore a pale-pink silk-damask jacket, a dark-blue satin waistcoat and a white silk-crêpe sash. With her face turned away, she had bent her head over the needlework; round her neck she wore a flowered collar. Bao-yu brought his face close to her neck to smell the scented oil, stroking her all the while. Her skin was as white and smooth as Aroma’s. He clambered on to her like a monkey and said with an importunate grin, ‘Dear sister, spare me a taste of the rouge on your lips.’ As he spoke, he clung to her like a sticky twist of sugar.

‘Aroma, come out and look at him!’ Faithful cried. ‘You’ve been with him all your life, and you never tell him off. He’s still just the same.’

Aroma came out with his clothes in her arms.

‘Whatever I say, you won’t change,’ she told Bao-yu. ‘What am I to do with you? If you go on like this, it will be impossible to stay here.’

As she spoke, she urged him into his clothes. He went with Faithful to the front to see Grandmother Jia. Having paid his respects, he came outside, where his attendants and horses were ready. He was just about to mount when Jia Lian came back from his own visit to ask after his father and was dismounting. Meeting face to face, the two exchanged a few words. Then someone stepped forward from one side.

‘My respects to you, Uncle Bao.’

Bao-yu looked at him. He was a tall, slender young man with a long face, no more than eighteen or nineteen, and decidedly gentle and handsome in appearance. He looked very familiar, yet Bao-yu could not remember which branch of the family he belonged to or what his name was.

‘Why are you staring like that?’ Jia Lian asked with a laugh. ‘Don’t you even recognise him?

‘He’s Yun, the son of our Fifth Sister-in-law who lives in the rear lane.’

‘Of course, of course!’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘How could I have forgotten?’

He asked after the young man’s mother and what business had brought him there.

‘I came to have a word with Second Uncle,’ Jia Yun replied, pointing to Jia Lian.

‘You’ve grown even better-looking than before,’ said Bao-yu with a smile. ‘You look quite like a son of mine.’

‘Have you no shame?’ Jia Lian laughed. ‘He’s four or five years older than you, and you’re making him your son!’

‘How old are you now?’ Bao-yu asked, smiling.

‘Eighteen,’ Jia Yun replied.

Jia Yun was a particularly quick-witted, perceptive young fellow. Hearing Bao-yu speak in this way, he smiled.

‘As the saying goes, “A grandfather in a cradle, a grandson leaning on a stick.” I may be older, but however high the mountain, it cannot rise above the sun. These past few years, since my father died, I’ve had no one to look after me or guide me. If you don’t think your nephew too stupid, Uncle Bao, it would be my good fortune to have you take me for your son.’

‘Do you hear that?’ Jia Lian laughed. ‘Taking a son isn’t something you can easily get out of!’ With that he went inside.

‘Come and see me tomorrow, if you have time,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘Don’t go skulking about with those others. I haven’t a moment just now. Come to the study tomorrow and we’ll have a chat. I’ll take you into the garden to amuse ourselves.’

He took hold of the saddle and mounted, and rode over to Jia She’s quarters surrounded by his pages.

On seeing Jia She, he found that his uncle had merely caught a slight chill. First he delivered Grandmother Jia’s enquiries, then paid his own respects. Jia She rose to give his reply to Grandmother Jia before calling someone in.

‘Take the young master inside to sit with Her Ladyship.’

Bao-yu withdrew and went round to the back, into the principal room.

When Lady Xing saw him, she first rose to ask after Grandmother Jia; only then did Bao-yu pay his own respects. She drew him up on to the kang to sit beside her, asked after the others and ordered tea. Before he had finished a cup, Jia Cong came in to greet him.

‘Where did this monkey spring from?’ Lady Xing said. ‘Has that nurse of yours dropped dead? Can’t she tidy you up? Look at your eyebrows and mouth, all black with dirt! Is that how a schoolboy from a respectable family ought to look?’

While she was speaking, the young uncle and nephew, Jia Huan and Jia Lan, arrived together. After they had paid their respects, Lady Xing told them to sit on chairs. Jia Huan saw Bao-yu sharing Lady Xing’s cushion on the kang while she fondled and caressed him in every possible way, and already felt thoroughly out of sorts. Before long he gave Jia Lan a look to say he wanted to leave. Jia Lan had to comply, and they rose together to take their leave. Seeing that they were going, Bao-yu stood up too, intending to go back with them.

‘Stay where you are,’ Lady Xing said with a smile. ‘I still have something to say to you.’

Bao-yu could only sit down again. Lady Xing turned to the other two.

‘When you get back, each of you give your mother my regards. All those aunts and sisters of yours are here, making my head spin, so I shan’t keep you to dinner today.’

Jia Huan and Jia Lan assented and went home.

‘Are all the girls over here, then?’ Bao-yu asked with a smile. ‘How is it I haven’t seen them?’

‘They sat here for a while, then went to one of the rooms at the back. I don’t know which.’

‘You said you had something to tell me, Aunt. What was it?’

‘Nothing at all,’ Lady Xing said, laughing. ‘I only wanted you to wait and have dinner with the girls before you went. And I have something amusing for you to take back and play with.’

As aunt and nephew talked, dinnertime came round before they knew it. Tables and chairs were set out, cups and dishes arranged, and Lady Xing and the young people ate. Afterwards Bao-yu went to take his leave of Jia She and returned home with the girls. They paid their respects to Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the others, then went to their own rooms to rest. Of this we need say no more.

Meanwhile Jia Yun had gone in to see Jia Lian and ask whether there was any work available.

‘Something did come up the other day,’ Jia Lian told him, ‘but your aunt begged me so persistently that I gave it to Jia Qin. She has promised me that, as there are still several places in the garden where flowers and trees have to be planted, you shall certainly have the job when that work comes up.’

Jia Yun listened, then after a pause said, ‘In that case, I’ll wait. You needn’t mention to Aunt that I came to ask today, Uncle. There will be time enough when the job comes up.’

‘Why should I mention it? As though I had time for such idle talk! I have to go to Xingyi at the fifth watch tomorrow, and must manage to get back the same day. Go and wait for now. Come for news after the first watch has begun the day after tomorrow. If you come earlier I shan’t have time to see you.’

With that he went back to change his clothes. Jia Yun left the Rong mansion to go home, thinking as he walked. Having hit upon a plan, he went straight to the house of his mother’s brother, Bu Shi-ren.

Bu Shi-ren kept a shop selling aromatics and had only just returned from it when Jia Yun unexpectedly came in. They exchanged greetings, and he asked what had brought his nephew there at that hour.

‘There’s something I need your help with, Uncle. I have a piece of business that calls for some bingpian and musk. Do be a good uncle and let me have four taels’ weight of each on credit. I’ll bring you the full payment in the eighth month.’

Bu Shi-ren gave a cold laugh.

‘Don’t speak to me of credit again. Only the other day, one of the men at our shop let a relation have several taels’ worth of goods on credit, and the money still hasn’t been paid. We all had to make up the loss. So we drew up an agreement: no more credit for friends or relations. Anyone who breaks it must pay for a feast costing twenty taels of silver. Besides, these goods are in short supply now. Even if you brought cash to buy them at our little second-rate shop, we wouldn’t have that much. We’d have to buy it in from elsewhere.1

‘That’s the first thing. The second is, what proper business can you possibly have? You’d only take the goods on credit and make more mischief. You’ll say your uncle finds fault with you every time he sees you. But you’re a young fellow with no idea what’s good for you. Set your mind to something, earn a little money and see that you have proper food and clothes. I should be pleased to see it.’

‘You make it sound very simple, Uncle,’ Jia Yun said with a smile. ‘When my father died I was too young to understand anything. Afterwards my mother told me how much we owed to you and my other uncles for coming to advise us and arrange the funeral.

‘Don’t you know, Uncle? Did we have a mu of land or a couple of rooms that I’ve since squandered? The cleverest housewife cannot make rice gruel without rice. What am I to do? You’re fortunate it’s me. Someone else might have the cheek to pester you every few days for three sheng of rice or two sheng of beans, and there would be nothing you could do about it.’

‘My boy, if your uncle had it, of course he ought to give it to you. I tell your aunt every day that what worries me is your lack of sense. If you had any spirit, you’d go to the senior branch of your family. Even if you couldn’t see the masters themselves, you could swallow your pride, make yourself agreeable to their steward or one of the managers, and get something to take charge of. The other day, when I was going out of the city, I met the fourth young man of your third branch riding a great jackass, with five carts and forty or fifty monks and Taoists on their way to the family temple. There’s a capable fellow for you! He got the job, didn’t he?’

Unable to bear any more of this harangue, Jia Yun stood up to leave.

‘Why such a hurry?’ Bu Shi-ren said. ‘Stay and eat before you go.’

He had scarcely finished when his wife said, ‘You’ve lost your senses again. Haven’t I just told you there’s no rice? I bought half a catty of flour to make you some noodles, and here you are putting on airs of plenty! Are you keeping your nephew here to go hungry?’

‘Buy another half-catty to add to it, then,’ said Bu Shi-ren.

His wife called to their daughter. ‘Yin-jie, go across the way and ask Mrs Wang whether she can lend us twenty or thirty cash. We’ll send it back tomorrow.’

While the couple were talking, Jia Yun had already said several times, ‘Please don’t trouble yourselves,’ and vanished.

We shall leave the Bu couple and follow Jia Yun, who, stung by his uncle’s treatment, went straight back the way he had come. Troubled at heart, he walked on with his head down, thinking, and unexpectedly ran headlong into a drunken man. He had quite a fright.

‘Damn your mother!’ the drunk swore. ‘Are you blind, barging into me?’

Jia Yun tried to dodge away, but the man had already caught hold of him. Looking into his face, he recognised his next-door neighbour, Ni Er. This Ni Er was a ruffian who lent money at exorbitant interest, took a cut of the gambling dens’ takings and made a business of brawling, bullying and drinking. He was now on his way back, drunk, from collecting interest at a debtor’s house. Jia Yun’s collision had put him in a vile temper, and he raised his fist to strike.

‘Old Er, don’t hit me!’ the other cried. ‘It was my fault for bumping into you.’

Recognising a familiar voice, Ni Er opened his drunken eyes and saw Jia Yun. He hastily let go and, swaying on his feet, said with a laugh, ‘Why, it’s Second Master Jia! I deserve to die! I deserve to die! Where are you off to now?’

‘Don’t ask. I’ve just had another snub, for no reason at all.’

‘Never mind, never mind. If someone’s treated you badly, tell me and I’ll settle it for you. In all these streets and alleys, whoever he may be, if he offends a neighbour of mine, of Drunken Vajra Ni Er, I’ll see his household scattered to the winds!’

‘Don’t get angry yet, Old Er. Let me tell you what happened.’

He told him the whole business with Bu Shi-ren. Ni Er was furious.

‘If he weren’t your uncle, I’d have some names to call him! It makes my blood boil. Well, don’t you worry. I have a few taels on me. Whatever you need, take them and buy it.

‘There’s just one thing. We’ve been neighbours all these years, and everyone knows I lend money, but you’ve never once asked me for any. Is it that you despise me as a ruffian and think it beneath your dignity? Or are you afraid I’d be hard to deal with and charge too much interest? If it’s the interest you’re afraid of, I won’t charge any on this, and there’s no need for a written agreement. But if you think it beneath you, I wouldn’t presume to lend it. We can each go our own way.’

As he spoke, he actually drew a roll of silver out of his shoulder-bag.

Jia Yun thought to himself, ‘Ruffian and scoundrel though Ni Er is, he treats different people differently, and has quite a name for generosity and honour. If I refuse his kindness today, he may feel slighted and make trouble. I’d better borrow it and pay him back double another day.’

Having made up his mind, he smiled.

‘Old Er, you really are a fine fellow. Don’t suppose I’ve never thought of asking you. But all the people you mix with are bold men who can do things. You wouldn’t take any notice of someone as helpless and useless as me. If I’d asked, would you have lent me anything? Now you’ve been so generous, how could I refuse? I’ll go home and draw up the usual agreement and bring it over.’

Ni Er burst out laughing.

‘You’ve a clever tongue, but I won’t listen to that. If they’re people I call my friends, how could I lend them money and take interest from them? If I lend to someone for the sake of the interest, that isn’t friendship.

‘Enough of this talk. Since you’re willing to take notice of me, here’s a little over fifteen taels and three qian of silver. Take it and buy what you need. If you’re going to write out some bond, give me my silver back now, and let me lend it to people I can expect a return from.’

Taking the silver, Jia Yun smiled. ‘All right, I won’t write one. Why get so worked up?’

‘That’s more like it,’ Ni Er said with a laugh. ‘It’s getting dark. I won’t keep you for tea or wine; I have something to see to over there. You go on home. And do me a favour: take a message to my household. Tell them to shut the doors early and go to bed. I shan’t be coming home. If there’s anything urgent, have my daughter come and find me first thing tomorrow at the horse-dealer Wang Shortlegs’.’

With that he staggered away, and we shall leave him there.

Jia Yun found this chance encounter very remarkable. He thought Ni Er was a decent sort after all. Yet he was still afraid it might be drunken generosity: what would he do if the man demanded twice as much tomorrow? He could not make up his mind. Then another thought came to him. ‘Never mind. Once that business comes off, I can pay him back double.’

He went straight to a money shop and had the silver weighed: fifteen taels, three qian, four fen and two li. Finding that Ni Er had told the truth, Jia Yun was more pleased than ever. He put the silver away and went home, first stopping next door to give Ni Er’s wife his message.

His mother was sitting on the kang twisting thread. When she saw him, she asked where he had been all day. Afraid of upsetting her, Jia Yun said nothing about Bu Shi-ren, but told her only that he had been waiting at the Rong mansion for Uncle Lian. Then he asked whether she had eaten.

She had, his mother said, and told him where she had left his food. The little maid brought it for him. It was already time to light the lamps. Jia Yun ate, made ready for bed and went to sleep. The night passed without incident.

Early next morning he got up, washed his face and went out through the south gate to buy bingpian and musk at a large aromatics shop. Then he made for the Rong mansion. On learning that Jia Lian had gone out, he went round to the back.

At the entrance to Jia Lian’s courtyard he saw several pages sweeping with great long brooms. Just then Zhou Rui’s wife came out through the gate and called to them, ‘Stop sweeping for a moment. Your mistress is coming out.’

Jia Yun hurried forward with a smile. ‘Where is Second Aunt going?’

‘Her Old Ladyship has sent for her,’ Zhou Rui’s wife replied. ‘I suppose it’s to cut out some lengths of cloth.’

As they spoke, Xi-feng appeared with a crowd of attendants about her. Jia Yun knew very well that she liked flattery and display. He hastily held his hands close to his sides and stepped smartly forward to pay his respects with the greatest deference. Xi-feng did not even give him a proper look, but walked on, merely asking after his mother.

‘Why doesn’t she come to see us?’

‘She hasn’t been very well. She’s always thinking of you, Aunt, and wanting to come and see you, but she hasn’t been able to.’

‘What a liar you are!’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘If I hadn’t mentioned her, you wouldn’t have told me she was thinking of me.’

‘Would I dare lie to an elder unless I wanted to be struck by lightning?’ Jia Yun asked with a smile. ‘Only last night we were talking about you. Mother was saying how delicately built you are, with so much to do, and how extraordinary your energy must be, to manage everything so thoroughly. Anyone with a little less would have worn herself to a shadow long ago.’

Xi-feng’s face was all smiles. In spite of herself, she stopped walking.

‘And what set the pair of you talking about me behind my back?’

‘There was a reason. I have a friend whose family has a little money and who keeps an aromatics shop.

‘He had bought the rank of assistant prefect, and the other day he was appointed to some place in Yunnan, I don’t know where. He’s taking his whole household with him and closing the shop here. So he went through his accounts and stock, paid what he owed, sold off what he could cheaply, and shared out the fine, costly goods among his friends and relations. What he gave me was some bingpian and musk.

‘I talked it over with my mother. If I tried to sell it on, I couldn’t get the original price. Besides, who would pay all that silver for it, and what would they want it for? Even a very wealthy household would be doing well to use a few fen or qian. As for giving it away, I don’t know anyone whose station calls for such things. They’d only sell it for half a coin when it was worth a whole one. That was what made me think of you, Aunt. In past years I’ve seen you spend great packets of silver on these things. Even leaving aside what Her Imperial Highness will need in the palace this year, you’re bound to use ten times the usual amount of aromatics for the Dragon Boat Festival alone.

‘So the more I thought about it, the more I felt that giving it to you was the only fitting way to show my respect and not let it go to waste.’

As he spoke, he held up a brocade-covered box. Xi-feng was just at the point of preparing gifts for the Dragon Boat Festival and buying aromatics and medicinal ingredients. Jia Yun’s unexpected offering, together with his long speech, left her both flattered and delighted.

‘Take it from Master Yun,’ she told Felicity, ‘and carry it home to Patience.’

Then she added, ‘Now I see how well you understand things, it’s no wonder your uncle keeps mentioning you. He says you speak sensibly and have a good head on your shoulders.’

Hearing the talk come round to the point he wanted, Jia Yun ventured a little further and deliberately asked, ‘So Uncle has mentioned me?’

Xi-feng was just about to tell him about the work he was to be given when she checked herself. ‘If I tell him now,’ she thought, ‘he’ll think I can’t see a present without snatching at it, promising him a job merely for this little bit of perfume. I won’t mention it today.’

She therefore kept back every word about putting him in charge of the flower- and tree-planting, made a couple of indifferent remarks and went on to Grandmother Jia’s. Jia Yun could hardly raise the matter himself and had to withdraw.

When he had seen Bao-yu the day before, he had been told to wait for him in the outer study. So, after his meal, Jia Yun came back to Patterned Snow Studio, the study outside the ceremonial gate of Grandmother Jia’s quarters. He found the two pages Tealeaf and Chu-yao playing Chinese chess and quarrelling over the capture of a chariot. Four or five others, Yin-quan, Sao-hua, Tiao-yun and Ban-he among them, were up at the eaves, taking little sparrows from their nests for amusement.

Jia Yun entered the courtyard and stamped his foot.

‘You mischievous monkeys! Here I am.’

Only when the pages saw him coming did they scatter. Jia Yun went into the room, sat down on a chair and asked, ‘Hasn’t Master Bao come out yet?’

‘He hasn’t been out at all today,’ said Tealeaf. ‘What is it you want, sir? I’ll go and find out for you.’

With that he went off.

Jia Yun looked over the calligraphy, paintings and curios. Enough time passed to eat a meal, but still Tealeaf did not return. When he looked for the other pages, they had all gone off to play. He was growing thoroughly bored when a soft young voice called ‘Brother!’ from the doorway.

Jia Yun looked out and saw a maid of sixteen or seventeen, delicately made and neat in appearance. At the sight of him she drew back and slipped out of view. Just then Tealeaf came along. Seeing the girl at the door, he said, ‘Good, good! I couldn’t find anyone to take a message.’

Jia Yun too had seen Tealeaf and hurried out to ask what had happened.

‘I’ve been waiting all day and no one’s come by,’ Tealeaf said. ‘This girl belongs to Master Bao’s rooms. Be a good girl and take a message in: say that the Second Master from the rear lane has come.’

Only then did the girl realise that this was a young gentleman of the family. No longer avoiding him as she had at first, she fixed Jia Yun with a couple of very intent looks. She heard him say, ‘Never mind lanes, rear or front. Just say it’s Yun.’

After a pause the girl gave a cool little laugh.

‘If you ask me, sir, you’d better go home. Whatever you have to say can wait until tomorrow. If I have a chance this evening, I’ll tell him.’

‘What do you mean by that?’ Tealeaf asked.

‘He didn’t have his midday sleep today, so naturally he’ll have his evening meal early.

‘And he won’t be coming out here tonight. Are we to keep the gentleman waiting hungry just for our amusement? He’d do better to go home and come back tomorrow. Even if someone comes along later and offers to take a message, it’s no use. They’ll say yes readily enough, but do you suppose they’ll take it?’

Jia Yun found the girl’s speech direct and charming. He wanted to ask her name, but since she belonged to Bao-yu’s rooms, he felt it would not be proper. He could only say, ‘You’re quite right. I’ll come again tomorrow.’

He started out.

‘I’ll make some tea,’ said Tealeaf. ‘Have a cup before you go, sir.’

‘No tea, thank you. I still have something to do,’ Jia Yun said, looking back as he walked. While he spoke, his eyes sought the girl, who was still standing there.

Jia Yun went straight home. When he reached the main gate the next day, he happened to meet Xi-feng setting out to pay her respects in the other quarters. She had just got into her carriage. Seeing him, she had someone call him over and said with a smile through the window, ‘Yun, you’ve a nerve, trying your tricks on me! I thought you were giving me a present, but it turns out you want something. Your uncle told me yesterday that you’d been asking him for a job.’

‘Please don’t mention my asking Uncle, Aunt,’ Jia Yun said with a smile. ‘I was regretting it only yesterday. If I’d known how it would turn out, I’d have come to you from the start and it would all have been settled by now. Who could have thought Uncle wouldn’t be able to manage it?’

‘No wonder you came looking for me yesterday, then, when you’d got nowhere with him,’ Xi-feng said, laughing.

‘You misjudge my wish to honour you, Aunt. I never meant anything of the kind. If I had, wouldn’t I have asked you yesterday? But now that you know about it, I shall have to give up on Uncle and beg you to show me a little kindness.’

Xi-feng gave a cold laugh.

‘What can I say if you insist on going the long way round? If you’d told me at the start, what couldn’t have been done? Such a little matter, and it’s been held up all this time. There are still flowers to be planted in the garden, and I couldn’t think of anyone for it. If you’d come earlier, wouldn’t it all have been settled?’

‘In that case, Aunt, give me the job tomorrow,’ Jia Yun said, smiling.

After a pause Xi-feng said, ‘On second thoughts, I don’t think this one would do. Wait until that big order for fireworks, lanterns and candles comes up in the first month of next year. I’ll give you that instead.’

‘Dear Aunt, let me have this one first,’ Jia Yun pleaded. ‘If I really do it well, you can give me the other too.’

‘You know how to cast a long line!’ Xi-feng laughed. ‘Very well. If your uncle hadn’t spoken for you, I wouldn’t bother with your affairs.

‘I shall be back as soon as I’ve eaten. Come for the money a little after noon, and go in to plant the trees the day after tomorrow.’

She ordered the driver to start, and her scented carriage went straight on its way. Beside himself with delight, Jia Yun went to Patterned Snow Studio to ask for Bao-yu, only to learn that he had gone to the Prince of Beijing’s mansion early that morning. Jia Yun sat idly until midday. On hearing that Xi-feng had returned, he wrote out a requisition and went to collect an authorisation tally.

Outside the courtyard he sent in his name. Sunshine came out, took only the requisition and carried it in. The amount of silver and the month and year were endorsed on it, and it was handed to Jia Yun together with the tally. He took them and saw that the approved amount was two hundred taels. Unable to contain his delight, he turned and went straight to the silver treasury, handed them to the man who received the tallies and requisitions, and collected the money.

When he went home and told his mother, both were naturally delighted. At the fifth watch next morning, Jia Yun first sought out Ni Er and repaid the exact sum he had borrowed.

Seeing that Jia Yun now had money, Ni Er accepted the repayment in full, and we need say no more of him. Jia Yun then took another fifty taels and went out through the west gate to the gardener Fang Chun’s house to buy trees. We shall leave him there.

Now let us return to Bao-yu. On the day he met Jia Yun, he had told him to come and talk the following day. But it had been merely the talk of a young gentleman accustomed to wealth and privilege. Why would he keep it in mind? He had promptly forgotten it. This evening, on returning from the Prince of Beijing’s mansion, he paid his respects to Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the others, then went back into the garden, changed his clothes and prepared to take a bath.

Aroma had been asked over by Xue Bao-chai to make ornamental knots. Ripple and Emerald had gone to hurry along the hot water. Sandal had been fetched home for her mother’s birthday, and Musk was at home recovering from illness. There were still a few maids who did the rough work and answered calls, but, thinking they were unlikely to be wanted, they had all gone out to find companions to play with. So it happened that for this brief interval Bao-yu was alone in the room. As luck would have it, he wanted tea. Only after he had called two or three times did two or three old women come in. At the sight of them, he hurriedly waved them away.

‘Never mind, never mind. I don’t need you.’

The old women had to withdraw. With no maids about, Bao-yu could only get up himself, take a bowl and go to the teapot to pour some tea.

‘Careful you don’t scald your hand, sir. Let me pour it,’ a voice behind him said.

As she spoke, she came forward and took the bowl from him. Bao-yu gave a start.

‘Where were you? You appeared so suddenly you gave me a fright.’

Handing him the tea, the maid replied, ‘I was in the back courtyard. I’ve just come through the rear door of the inner room. Didn’t you hear my footsteps?’

As he drank, Bao-yu looked the girl over carefully. Her clothes were neither new nor particularly old, but she had a head of thick black hair coiled into a bun, a long face and a slender, delicate figure. She was decidedly pretty and neat. Having looked at her, Bao-yu smiled and asked, ‘Do you belong to my rooms too?’

‘Yes,’ the maid replied.

‘If you belong here, how is it I don’t know you?’

The girl gave a cool laugh.

‘There are plenty you don’t know, not just me. I never serve tea or water, or bring this and carry that. I don’t do any of the work you can see, so how would you know me?’

‘Why don’t you do the work I can see?’

‘That’s not easy for me to say. But there is one thing I ought to tell you, sir. Someone called Yun came to see you yesterday. I thought you wouldn’t have time, so I told Tealeaf to tell him to come early today. I didn’t expect you to go off to the Prince of Beijing’s instead.’

She had just reached this point when Ripple and Emerald came in laughing and talking. Between them they carried a bucket of water, each holding up her clothes with her free hand, stumbling along and splashing water everywhere. The girl hurried forward to take it from them.

Ripple and Emerald were complaining to one another.

‘You’ve wet my skirt!’

‘And you’ve trodden on my shoe!’

Suddenly someone came out to take the water. They looked and saw that it was none other than Crimson. Both were astonished. Setting down the water, they hurried into the room and looked all about, but there was nobody there except Bao-yu. This made them thoroughly uneasy. They could only prepare the things for his bath. When he had taken off his clothes, they came out, pulling the door shut behind them, and went to the room on the other side to find Crimson and ask what she had been saying in his room just now.

‘When have I been in his room?’ Crimson said. ‘My handkerchief had gone missing, and I went round to the back to look for it. Then the young master wanted tea, and when he called, not one of you sisters was there. So I went in. I’d only just poured the tea when you came.’

At this Ripple spat straight in her face.

‘Shameless little slut! We told you to go and hurry the water along, and you said you had something to do. You made us go instead, while you waited to seize this fine opportunity. Edging your way in, bit by bit, and here you are! Do you think we can’t keep up with you? Take a look in the mirror and see whether you’re fit to serve tea and water!’

‘Tomorrow I’ll tell the others,’ Emerald said. ‘Whenever there’s tea or water wanted, or anything to be carried in or out, none of us need stir. Let her do it all.’

‘In that case,’ said Ripple, ‘we might as well all go away and leave her alone in his rooms.’

They were still keeping up this barrage, one after the other, when an old woman came in with a message from Xi-feng.

‘Someone will be bringing gardeners in to plant trees tomorrow. You’re to be more careful. Don’t leave your jackets and skirts hanging out to dry all over the place. That whole stretch along the artificial hill is being screened off with hangings. Don’t go running about where you please.’

‘Do you know who’s bringing the workmen in to supervise them?’ Ripple asked.

‘Someone called Master Yun, from the rear lane.’

Neither Ripple nor Emerald knew him, and they went on asking all sorts of other questions.

Crimson, however, understood at once: it was the young man she had seen in the outer study yesterday.

Her surname was Lin and her childhood name Hong-yu, ‘red jade’. But because the ‘yu’, or ‘jade’, encroached on the names of Lin Dai-yu and Bao-yu, everyone had dropped that part and called her simply Crimson. Her family had served the Rong mansion for generations, and her parents now managed the houses and land in various places. She was only sixteen. When servants were being assigned to Prospect Garden, she had been sent to the House of Green Delights, where life was quiet and secluded enough. But then came the order for people to move into the garden, and Bao-yu happened to be given that very house.

Inexperienced girl though she was, Crimson had a certain prettiness and an earnest, foolish longing to rise in the world. Time and again she wanted to show what she could do in front of Bao-yu. But all the people about him had sharp tongues and ready claws. Where could she find a place among them? Today, unexpectedly, she had at last had a little opening, only to meet this spiteful attack from Ripple and the others. Already half her hopes had turned to ashes.

She was brooding over it when the old woman’s mention of Jia Yun stirred something in her heart. Still troubled, she returned to her room and lay on the bed thinking it over in secret, turning this way and that without knowing what to do.

Suddenly a low voice called outside the window, ‘Crimson, I’ve found your handkerchief. I have it here.’

She hurried out to look. It was none other than Jia Yun. Her fair face coloured with bashfulness.

‘Where did you find it, sir?’

‘Come here and I’ll tell you,’ Jia Yun said with a smile, reaching out to take hold of her as he spoke.

Crimson turned quickly to run, but caught her foot on the threshold and fell.
