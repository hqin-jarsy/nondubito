Chapter 56 Astute Tan-chun seeks new gains and sweeps away old abuses; Discerning Bao-chai secures the general good with small kindnesses

Chapter end

Now Patience had eaten with Xi-feng and helped her wash and rinse her mouth before going to Tan-chun. The courtyard was quiet. Only the maids and older women who attended them closely in the inner quarters were waiting outside the windows. She went into the hall, where the three ladies were discussing household affairs. Their talk concerned the garden at Lai Da’s house, where they had been invited to a feast the previous year. Seeing her, Tan-chun told her to sit on a footstool.

‘What I have in mind is simply this,’ she began. ‘Each of us has two taels a month, quite apart from the maids’ monthly allowances.

‘Then someone came the other day to report that our hair oil, rouge and powder cost another two taels a month for each of us. It’s just like those eight taels for the school we were discussing: one allowance on top of another. The matter is small and the money isn’t much, but it still doesn’t seem right. How is it that your mistress never thought of this?’

‘There’s a reason for that, miss,’ Patience answered with a smile. ‘Naturally, the young ladies are entitled to a regular supply of these things. The purchasing steward buys them each month and sends women round to the different apartments to hand them over to us for safekeeping, ready for the young ladies to use. We can hardly each be going about every day with money in our hands, looking for someone to buy hair oil one moment and rouge and powder the next. So the purchasing steward outside draws the whole sum and has the things delivered to us in our different rooms every month.

‘The young ladies’ own two taels a month were never meant to buy these things. They were provided in case the mistress in charge happened to be out or too busy just when one of the young ladies wanted a little money for something. It saves her having to send about looking for someone.

‘The whole idea was that the young ladies shouldn’t be put to any inconvenience. You see, that money wasn’t provided to buy these things at all. But I’ve been quietly watching, and a good half of the girls in the different apartments do pay cash for them. I began to wonder whether the purchasing steward was failing to supply them, or supplying them late, or buying rubbish and fobbing us off with things we couldn’t use.’

‘So you’ve noticed it too!’ Tan-chun and Li Wan said, laughing. ‘They don’t fail to supply them. They wouldn’t dare. But they do come late, and when we press them, they find something somewhere and send it along. It’s the right thing in name only, quite unfit to use, and we still have to buy our own. We use those two taels and have someone’s nurse’s son, or a brother’s son, buy us something fit to use. If we send the household staff, we only get the same thing again.

‘What can they be doing? Do they collect everything the shops have spoiled and thrown out, just to keep it for us?’

‘The purchasing steward buys that sort of thing,’ said Patience, smiling. ‘If someone else brought good things, would he let the man off? He’d say he was deliberately making trouble to get the purchasing job for himself. So they have to do the same. They’d rather offend us inside than the men who manage affairs outside. The young ladies can only send their nurses. They daren’t say anything about them.’

‘That’s what troubles me,’ said Tan-chun. ‘The money is spent twice, and half the things are thrown away besides. Take it all together, and we lose twice over. We might as well cancel the purchasing steward’s monthly allowance for them altogether.

‘That’s one thing. Then there’s another. You went with us to Lai Da’s before the New Year. How did their little garden compare with ours?’

‘It wasn’t half the size,’ said Patience, smiling, ‘and there were far fewer trees and flowers.’

‘I was talking to their daughter. Would you believe that a garden like that, after supplying them with flowers to wear and bamboo shoots, vegetables, fish and shrimps to eat, is put in someone’s charge for the year and still brings them a clear two hundred taels at the end of it? Only then did I realise that even a torn lotus leaf or a withered grass root was worth money.’

‘Spoken like a true child of luxury!’ Bao-chai said with a laugh. ‘Of course a pampered young lady wouldn’t know about such things. But you’ve all learnt to read. Surely you’ve seen Master Zhu’s essay, “Do Not Abandon Yourself”?’

‘I’ve read it,’ Tan-chun replied, smiling. ‘But it’s only meant to encourage people to make an effort. All those fanciful comparisons and fine phrases! They can’t all be true.’

‘Master Zhu, all fanciful comparisons and fine phrases? Every word of it is true. You’ve had two days’ experience of practical affairs, and already the fumes of profit have gone to your head. Even Master Zhu seems empty and fanciful to you! Wait till you go out and see what there is to be gained and lost in great affairs. You’ll think Confucius emptier still!’

‘Someone as learned as you,’ Tan-chun said, laughing, ‘and you’ve never read the philosophers? Doesn’t the Jizi say, “Those who enter the arena of profit and advancement and move in the sphere of calculation steal the words of Yao and Shun, yet turn their backs on the way of Confucius and Mencius”?’

‘And the next sentence?’ Bao-chai asked with a smile.

‘I’m only quoting the part that suits me. If I recited the next sentence, wouldn’t I be abusing myself?’

‘Nothing in the world is without a use,’ said Bao-chai. ‘And if it has a use, it is worth money. To think that someone as clever as you has never had any experience of these important practical matters! It’s a pity you’ve come to them so late.’

‘You sent for her to discuss business,’ Li Wan said with a laugh, ‘and now you’ve left the business alone and started exchanging learned arguments.’

‘But there’s business in learning,’ said Bao-chai.

‘Apply a little learning to a small affair and you raise it to a higher level. Without learning to lift it, it all sinks into vulgar money-grubbing.’

The three of them were only teasing. After talking and laughing for a while, they returned to their business.

Tan-chun went on:

‘Suppose our garden is only half as large again as theirs. Reckon twice the return, and that gives four hundred taels a year. Of course, to turn it over to someone simply to make money would be petty. That isn’t what a family like ours does. But we could appoint a couple of people to take definite charge of it. With so much of value there, to let everyone go on spoiling it seems a waste of Heaven’s gifts. Why not choose a few of the older women already in the Garden, honest, steady women who know something about gardening, and give them definite charge of its upkeep? We needn’t ask them for rent or taxes. We’d only ask what they could offer us each year.

‘First, there would be people expressly responsible for the Garden. The trees and flowers would improve from year to year, and there would be no need to rush about whenever something had to be done. Second, things wouldn’t be spoilt and their usefulness wasted. Third, the old women could make a little extra for themselves, so that all their year’s hard work here wouldn’t be for nothing. Fourth, we could save the wages of the gardeners, rockery men, sweepers and the rest.

‘What we have to spare in one place would make up for what we lack in another. Surely there’s no harm in that.’

Bao-chai was standing looking at the calligraphy and paintings on the wall. At each point in the list she had nodded. When Tan-chun finished, she said with a smile:

‘Excellent! Within three years there shall be no famine.’

‘A good idea,’ Li Wan said, laughing.

‘Her Ladyship will certainly be pleased if we carry it out. Saving money is the least of it. The chief thing is to have people to keep the place in order, with definite duties, and permission to sell what it produces. Give them authority, make it worth their while, and they’re bound to do their work.’

‘It needed you to propose this, miss,’ said Patience. ‘Even if my mistress had it in mind, she could hardly have brought herself to say it. The young ladies are living in the Garden. Instead of finding more pretty things for them to enjoy, she would be putting people in to supervise it and keep it up, simply to save money. She could never have said that.’

Bao-chai came quickly over, stroked her cheek and laughed.

‘Open your mouth. Let me see what those teeth and that tongue are made of! Ever since this morning, you’ve had a fresh answer for everything. You haven’t flattered the Third Miss, but you haven’t suggested that your mistress lacked the wit to think of any of this, either. Nor have you simply said yes to everything the Third Miss said. Whatever she brings forward, you have an answer ready. Anything she has thought of, your mistress has thought of too. Only there’s always some reason why it couldn’t be done.

‘Now it’s that the young ladies live in the Garden, so it wouldn’t do to put people in charge just to save money. Think what that means. If the Garden really were handed over to someone to make money from, naturally they wouldn’t let anyone pick a single flower or touch a single fruit. They wouldn’t dare interfere with the young ladies’ share, of course, but there would be endless quarrels with the little maids. She thinks of troubles near and far, without either presumption or servility. Even if her mistress weren’t on good terms with us, hearing all that would shame her into being kinder. It would turn ill feeling into friendship.’

‘I was furious this morning,’ Tan-chun said with a smile. ‘When I heard she’d come, I suddenly thought of her mistress and the people she usually sends to deal with things, so ready to throw their weight about. The sight of Patience was enough to make me angry. But there she stood for ages, like a mouse hiding from a cat. I felt sorry for her. Then came all those things she said. Not how well her mistress had treated me, but “It will be a fitting expression of the kindness you’ve always shown my mistress.” That one sentence took all my anger away. I felt ashamed instead, and then I felt hurt.

‘When I thought about it, I’m only a girl who’s managed to leave herself with no one to love or look after her. What kindness have I to offer anyone else?’

As she said it, tears began to fall again. Li Wan and the others were moved by her earnestness. They thought, too, of the slights Aunt Zhao was always casting on her and of how Aunt Zhao had damaged her standing with Lady Wang. They could not help shedding tears themselves, and hurried to comfort her.

‘We’ve a quiet day today. Let’s settle a couple of things that will bring some benefit and put an end to abuses. Then Her Ladyship won’t have trusted us for nothing. Why bring up these unhappy matters again?’

‘I understand it all now,’ Patience said quickly. ‘You need only say who you think suitable, miss. Appoint them, and it’s done.’

‘Even so, we must tell your mistress first,’ said Tan-chun. ‘It’s hardly proper for us to be picking over the little things she’s overlooked. I only do it because she’s a sensible person. If she were muddle-headed, suspicious and jealous, I wouldn’t. It would look as though I were trying to catch her out. How could we go ahead without consulting her?’

‘In that case, I’ll go and tell her,’ Patience said with a smile.

She went away and was some time returning.

‘I said it would be a wasted journey,’ she told them, laughing. ‘How could my mistress possibly object to anything so good?’

At this, Tan-chun and Li Wan sent for a list of all the older women in the Garden. They considered it together and provisionally settled on several names, then summoned all the women. Li Wan outlined the plan to them. They were willing to a woman.

‘Give me sole charge of that patch of bamboo,’ said one, ‘and after a year’s work there’ll be another whole patch next year.

‘Besides all the shoots the household can eat, I can still make a payment every year.’

‘Give me that rice plot,’ said another. ‘All the birds you keep for pleasure, big and small, can have their grain for the year without drawing on household funds. And I can make a payment as well.’

Tan-chun was about to speak when someone came to report:

‘The doctor has arrived. He’s coming into the Garden to see the young ladies.’

The older women had to go and receive him.

‘A hundred of you on your own wouldn’t make a proper escort,’ Patience said quickly. ‘Aren’t there a couple of senior housekeepers to bring the doctor in?’

‘Yes,’ the messenger replied. ‘Mrs Wu and Mrs Shan are waiting at the Gate of Gathered Splendour, in the south-west corner.’

Only then was Patience satisfied.

When the women had gone, Tan-chun asked Bao-chai what she thought.

Bao-chai smiled.

‘Those eager at the start grow slack at the end; those who polish their words have an appetite for profit.’

Tan-chun nodded appreciatively and pointed out several names on the list for the other three to see. Patience hurried to fetch brush and inkstone. The three said:

‘Old Mamma Zhu is reliable. Besides, her husband and son have tended the bamboo, one generation after another. We might as well give her charge of all of it now.

‘And old Mamma Tian was a farmer. The vegetables, rice and barnyard millet round Sweet-rice Village may only be there for amusement, with no need for serious, large-scale cultivation, but she ought to be the one to look after them. A little extra tending at the proper seasons would surely improve them.’

‘What a pity,’ Tan-chun said with a smile, ‘that Allspice Court and the House of Green Delights, two such large places, have nothing that brings in any money.’

‘Allspice Court is worth more than any of them,’ Li Wan said at once, laughing. ‘What are all the fragrant plants and herbs sold in the incense shops, the great markets and the temple fairs, if not these very things? They would bring in more than anything else. And never mind the other things at Green Delights. Just think how many roses it produces over the spring and summer! Then there are the climbing roses, monthly-flowering roses, baoxiang flowers and honeysuckle along the fence. Even those ordinary little flowers, dried and sold to tea shops or apothecaries, would be worth something.’

‘I see,’ said Tan-chun with a smile. ‘But we haven’t anyone who knows how to handle fragrant plants.’

‘The mother of Oriole, Miss Bao’s maid, knows all about them,’ Patience put in quickly, smiling. ‘Last time she picked some and dried them, then wove them into little flower-baskets and gourds for me to play with. Have you forgotten, miss?’

‘I’ve only just praised you,’ Bao-chai said, laughing, ‘and now you start playing tricks on me.’

The other three were astonished and asked what she meant.

‘It’s quite out of the question. You have so many capable people here, all sitting about with nothing to do. If I brought in someone of my own now, they’d think very little of me too. But I’ve thought of someone for you. There’s old Mamma Ye at the House of Green Delights, Tealeaf’s mother. She’s an honest old woman, and a great friend of Oriole’s mother. Why not give the work to her? If there’s anything she doesn’t know, she’ll go and ask Oriole’s mother without a word from us.

‘Even if Mamma Ye did none of it herself and left the whole thing to her friend, that would be a private arrangement between them. Whatever anyone might say, they couldn’t blame us. You’d have acted with perfect fairness, and the work would be in very good hands.’

‘Exactly,’ Li Wan and Patience agreed.

‘Even so,’ Tan-chun said with a smile, ‘I’m afraid the prospect of profit might make them forget their friendship.’

‘There’s no danger of that,’ Patience answered, smiling. ‘Only the other day Oriole took Mamma Ye as her godmother and gave a feast for her. The two families couldn’t be closer.’

Tan-chun was satisfied. Together they chose a few more women, all people whom the four of them had quietly observed and thought well of, and circled their names with the brush.

Presently the women came back to report that the doctor had gone, and handed up the prescription. The three ladies looked it over. While they sent someone out to fetch the medicine and appointed people to oversee its preparation and administration, Tan-chun and Li Wan explained the assignments to the women: each would have charge of a specified place. After supplying the household’s regular requirements for each of the four seasons, they could gather what remained and make what they could from it. The accounts would be settled at the end of the year.

‘Something else has just occurred to me,’ Tan-chun said, smiling. ‘If you settle the accounts and hand in the money at the end of the year, naturally it will go to the accounts office. That puts another set of masters over you. You’re in their hands again, with another skin to be stripped off you. By starting this arrangement and appointing you ourselves, we’ve already gone over their heads. They’re angry, even if they can’t say so. What will they do when you come to settle up at the end of the year, if not make you suffer for it? Besides, whatever they manage during the year, for every full share the master has, they take half a share. That’s an old custom of this household, known to everyone. Whatever they take secretly is over and above it.

‘This Garden arrangement is my own new scheme. Let’s keep it out of their hands altogether. You’d better settle your yearly accounts directly with us inside.’

‘I don’t think they need settle accounts here either,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘One brings more, another less, and it only makes more trouble. Why not let whoever takes a share of the work take responsibility for a particular expense? They’re only things the people in the Garden use. I’ve worked it out for you. There are very few kinds: hair oil, rouge and powder, incense and paper. There is a fixed allowance of these things for each young lady and her maids. Then there are the brooms, dustpans and dusters for the different places, and grain for the birds, big and small, the deer and the rabbits. That’s all. Let the women undertake to supply them, without drawing money from the accounts office. Just work out how much you would save.’

‘Small as these things are,’ Patience said with a smile, ‘over a whole year they’d save four hundred taels.’

‘There you are again,’ Bao-chai replied, smiling. ‘Four hundred in a year, eight hundred in two. You could look about for a few rooms to let out, or add a few mu of poor land.

‘There would still be something left over, but after working hard for a whole year, they ought to have something to spare to help their own families. Increasing income and cutting expenses may be the guiding principle, but we mustn’t be too miserly. Even if you saved another two or three hundred taels, it wouldn’t do if you lost the dignity proper to the household. This way, the accounts office outside would have four or five hundred taels less to pay out each year, without feeling that any severe economies were being made. The women inside would get a little extra too. These old women who have no other means of earning would be better off, the Garden’s trees and flowers would flourish more each year, and you would have the things you need. That should preserve the dignity of the whole arrangement. If saving money were all you wanted, you could scrape together a few cash almost anywhere. Take every last bit of profit into the household funds, though, and there would be complaints inside and out. What would become of the dignity of a family like yours then?

‘Now there are several dozen older women in this Garden. If you gave the work to only some, the rest would certainly complain of unfairness. As I was saying, merely supplying those few things would still leave the women who have the work rather well off.

‘So, besides supplying them, let each woman contribute a certain number of strings of cash every year, whether she has a surplus or not. Pool it all and distribute it among the other older women in the Garden. They may not tend the plants, but they’re here on duty day and night all the same. They shut the gates and doors, rise early and go to bed late. In rain and snow, when the young ladies go out, they carry the chairs, pole the boats and pull the ice-sledges. Every kind of heavy work falls to them. They labour here from one year’s end to the next. If the Garden is bringing in money, they deserve a share.

‘And there’s one very small point I may as well say outright. If you think only of making yourselves comfortable and give them nothing, they may not dare complain openly, but they won’t be content. All they need do is pick a few extra fruits or flowers for themselves on the pretence of doing it for the household. You’ll have a grievance and nowhere to take it. But if they share in the profits, they’ll look after things for you whenever you can’t.’

The women were delighted with this plan. It freed them from the accounts office’s control and spared them having to settle accounts with Xi-feng. All they had to do was contribute a certain number of extra strings of cash each year.

‘We’re willing,’ they all said. ‘It’s better than going outside to be squeezed and bullied, and still having to pay.’

Those who had not been given any ground to manage were pleased too, now that they would receive money at the year’s end without having done anything for it.

‘They’ll work hard to keep everything in order,’ they said. ‘It’s only right they should have something over to help them out.

‘How can we “sit still and pocket three stakes”?’

‘Don’t refuse it, mammas,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘You’re perfectly entitled to it. All you need do is take a little more trouble, day and night, instead of shirking and letting people drink and gamble.

‘Otherwise I’ve no business interfering. You all heard my aunt ask me, several times over, to keep an eye on things. Mrs Zhu is busy now, she said, and the other young ladies are still too young. If I refused, I’d only be making my aunt worry. Your mistress has so much illness to contend with, and all the household affairs to attend to as well. I’m an idle person. I ought to help even if we were only neighbours, let alone when my own aunt has asked me. I must put the lesser considerations aside for the greater ones. I can’t afford to worry about people resenting me. Suppose I were to think only of those little personal scruples and win a reputation for being agreeable, and then drunkenness and gambling led to trouble. How could I face my aunt? It would be too late for you to be sorry then. Even the respect you’ve earned with your years would be gone.

‘All these young ladies, and this great Garden, are left in your care because you come of families that have served here for three or four generations and are thought the most reliable in observing our rules. You ought to work together and preserve some proper order. Instead you let others drink and gamble as they please. If my aunt hears of it and gives you a scolding, that’s one thing. But suppose those managing housekeepers hear of it. They needn’t even report to my aunt. They can give you a dressing-down themselves. At your age, to be scolded by women younger than you! They may be housekeepers and entitled to direct you, but wouldn’t it be better to keep your own dignity? Then how could they find a chance to humiliate you?

‘That’s why I’ve thought of this way for you to make something extra: so that you’ll all work together to keep this Garden in the most careful order. When those who hold authority see how strictly and attentively it is managed, without their having to worry about it, surely they’ll feel respect for you. Then my efforts to increase your earnings won’t have been wasted. If we can take the power out of their hands and bring profit to yours, surely we can also govern without meddling and take some of their cares away.

‘Go and think carefully about what I’ve said.’

The servants broke into a clamour of agreement.

‘You’re quite right, miss! From now on, miss, ma’am, you needn’t worry. You’ve shown us so much kindness and consideration that Heaven and Earth wouldn’t forgive us if we failed to appreciate it.’

They were still speaking when Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife came in.

‘The ladies of the Zhen household in Jiangnan arrived in the capital yesterday,’ she reported. ‘They’ve gone to the palace today to pay their respects. Meanwhile they’ve sent people ahead with presents and enquiries after your health.’

She handed up the list of gifts. Tan-chun took it and read:

‘For imperial use, figured satin and satin with python designs, twelve bolts; for imperial use, satin in assorted colours, twelve bolts; for imperial use, gauze in various colours, twelve bolts; for imperial use, palace silk, twelve bolts; for official use, satin, gauze, silk and damask in various colours, twenty-four bolts.’

Li Wan read it too.

‘Reward the bearers with the first-class gratuity packets,’ she said, and sent word to Grandmother Jia.

Grandmother Jia summoned Li Wan, Tan-chun, Bao-chai and the others to her rooms, where they inspected the gifts. Li Wan received them, but told the women in charge of the inner storerooms:

‘Wait till Her Ladyship has come back and seen them before you put them away.’

‘The Zhens aren’t like other families,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘Give the men first-class gratuity packets. I expect they’ll be sending women over to enquire after us at any moment. Have some dress lengths ready.’

Before she had finished, someone announced that four women from the Zhen household had come to pay their respects. Grandmother Jia at once had them shown in.

All four were over forty. Their clothes and ornaments differed little from those of their mistresses. When they had paid their respects and made their enquiries, Grandmother Jia ordered four footstools to be brought. The women thanked her for the seats, but waited until Bao-chai and the others were seated before sitting down themselves.

‘When did you reach the capital?’ Grandmother Jia asked.

They hurried to their feet to answer.

‘Yesterday, ma’am. Our mistress has taken the young lady to the palace today to pay her respects. That’s why she sent us to enquire after Your Old Ladyship and the young ladies.’

‘It’s years since you came to the capital,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘I hadn’t expected to see you this year.’

‘Indeed, ma’am,’ they answered, smiling too. ‘This year we were commanded to come by His Majesty.’

‘Has the whole family come?’

‘No, ma’am. Our old lady, the young master, two of the young ladies and the other married ladies have all stayed behind. Only our mistress and the Third Miss have come.’

‘Is she promised to anyone?’

‘Not yet, ma’am.’

‘Both the families your eldest and second young ladies married into are very friendly with ours,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile.

‘Yes, ma’am. Every year, when they write home, they tell us how much they owe to your care.’

‘Care! Our families have been friends for generations, and we’re old relations too. It’s only what’s right. Your second young lady is particularly good. She has no airs about her, and that’s why we see so much of one another.’

‘Your Old Ladyship is too modest,’ the women replied, smiling.

‘And your young master, does he live with your old lady too?’

‘Yes, ma’am. He lives with her too.’

‘How old is he? Has he begun school?’

‘Thirteen this year,’ they said, smiling.

‘He’s so good-looking that our old lady dotes on him. He’s been terribly mischievous ever since he was little and is always running away from school. The master and mistress cannot be very strict with him.’

‘Why, he might be ours!’ Grandmother Jia said with a laugh. ‘What is his name?’

‘Our old lady prized him like a treasure, and he was so fair that she called him Bao-yu.’

‘Bao-yu too, of all things!’ Grandmother Jia said, turning to Li Wan and the others.

Li Wan half rose at once and answered with a smile:

‘There have always been plenty of people with the same name, whether they lived at the same time or generations apart.’

The four women smiled too.

‘After that pet name was chosen, everyone in our household, high and low, had a feeling that someone among our relations or friends had a child with the same name. But it’s ten years or more since we came to the capital, and we couldn’t quite remember.’

‘You honour him,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘It’s my grandson. Someone come here.’

The women and maids answered and stepped forward.

‘Go to the Garden and fetch our Bao-yu. Let these four housekeepers see him and tell us how he compares with theirs.’

The women hurried off and soon came back surrounding Bao-yu. At the sight of him, the four visitors rose at once, laughing.

‘You’ve given us quite a fright! If we hadn’t come into this house, but had met you somewhere else, we’d have thought our Bao-yu had followed us to the capital.’

As they spoke they all came forward to take his hands, asking him question after question.

Bao-yu hastily greeted them in turn, smiling.

‘How does he compare with yours in looks?’ Grandmother Jia asked.

‘From what the four mammas have just said,’ Li Wan and the others answered with a laugh, ‘they must look alike.’

‘Could there really be such a coincidence?’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘Children in great households are all brought up delicately. Unless there’s some blemish on their faces, or they’re very dark and ugly, they all look much alike in being good-looking. There’s nothing very strange in that.’

‘Now we’ve seen him, we can say their looks are the same,’ the women replied, smiling.

‘And from what Your Old Ladyship says, so is their naughtiness. But we think this young gentleman has a better disposition than ours.’

‘How can you tell?’ Grandmother Jia asked at once.

‘From the way he let us take his hands and talk to him just now.

‘Ours does nothing but call us stupid. Take his hands! He won’t even let us so much as touch his things. He has only young girls to wait on him.’

Before they had finished, Li Wan and the others burst out laughing. Grandmother Jia laughed too.

‘If we sent people to see your Bao-yu now, and they took his hands, he’d put up with it for a little while too, however reluctantly. Children in families like yours and ours may have all sorts of perverse, outlandish ways, but in front of outsiders they must show the proper manners. If they didn’t, they certainly wouldn’t be allowed their whims. When their elders spoil them, it’s partly because they’re pleasing to look at, but also because they can observe the forms in company every bit as correctly as grown people. They make everyone fond of them, and so they’re indulged a little in private. If a child had no idea how to behave either at home or abroad, and did nothing to make his elders proud, he might be as handsome as you please: he’d deserve to be beaten to death.’

‘Your Old Ladyship is quite right,’ the four women replied, smiling.

‘Naughty and odd as our Bao-yu is, when he meets guests he can be more punctilious and well-mannered than the grown-ups. Everyone who sees him loves him and wonders why anyone should ever beat him. They have no idea how impossible he is at home. He says things his elders would never dream of saying and insists on doing things they’d never think of doing. The master and mistress are exasperated with him and don’t know what to do. Fits of temper are only natural in a child. Spending money recklessly is only natural in a young gentleman. Being afraid of school is natural in a child too. Those things can all be dealt with. But what can one do about this perverse, outlandish disposition he was born with?’

Before they had finished, someone announced that Lady Wang had returned. She came in and paid her respects to Grandmother Jia.

The four women greeted her and exchanged a few words. Grandmother Jia told her to go and rest. Lady Wang first presented her tea with her own hands, then withdrew. The four visitors took leave of Grandmother Jia and went to Lady Wang’s rooms, where they discussed household matters for a while before she sent them home. We need not go into all that.

Grandmother Jia was so delighted that she told everyone she met about this other Bao-yu, with ways just like their own. The others thought that in so large a world, with so many families of hereditary office-holders, there must be plenty of people with the same name. Grandmothers had always spoilt their grandsons. There was nothing extraordinary in any of it, and they gave it no further thought. Bao-yu alone, being an impractical young simpleton, decided that the four women had merely been saying what would please his grandmother.

Later, when he visited Xiang-yun at Allspice Court, where she lay ill, she said:

‘Now you can make all the trouble you please. Before, it was “One filament makes no thread, one tree makes no forest.” But now you have a partner. When you’ve made enough trouble and been beaten hard enough, you can run away to Nanjing and find the other one.’

‘You believe those lies too?’ Bao-yu said. ‘As though there just happened to be another Bao-yu!’

‘Why was there a Lin Xiang-ru in the Warring States, then, and a Sima Xiang-ru in the Han dynasty?’

‘Very well,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘But the same looks as well? That’s impossible.’

‘Why did the people of Kuang take Confucius for Yang Hu?’

‘Confucius and Yang Hu looked alike, but they had different names. Lin and Sima had the same name, but they didn’t look alike. Must this boy and I have both?’

Xiang-yun could find no answer, and laughed.

‘You only know how to twist things. I shan’t argue with you.

‘Whether there is such a boy or not is no concern of mine.’

She lay down, leaving Bao-yu uncertain again. Say there couldn’t be such a boy, and yet there seemed to be. Say there must be, and yet he had never seen him. Troubled, he went back to his rooms and lay on the couch, turning it over silently in his mind. Before he knew it he had drifted off to sleep, and found himself in a garden.

‘Can there be another garden besides our Prospect Garden?’ he wondered.

While he was puzzling over this, a group of girls came towards him. They were all maids.

‘And can there be girls like these besides Faithful, Aroma and Patience?’ he wondered again.

‘What’s Bao-yu doing here?’ he heard them say with a laugh.

Thinking they meant him, he hurried up with a smile.

‘I happened to stray this way. I don’t know which of our family friends this garden belongs to. Dear sisters, do show me round.’

‘Why, he isn’t our Bao-yu!’ the maids said, laughing.

‘He’s quite nice-looking, though, and he’s got a ready little tongue.’

Bao-yu quickly asked:

‘Sisters, is there another Bao-yu here too?’

‘When we say “Bao-yu”,’ the maids retorted, ‘it’s on the old lady’s and Her Ladyship’s orders, to bring him long life and keep misfortune away. He likes to hear us call him that. What stinking little servant-boy are you, from goodness knows where, to go calling him by his name? Mind that filthy hide of yours! We’ll beat it to a pulp.’

‘Come on,’ another maid said, laughing. ‘If Bao-yu sees us, he’ll say we’ve made ourselves stink by talking to this smelly little boy.’

They went straight off.

‘No one has ever treated me so cruelly,’ Bao-yu thought in bewilderment. ‘Why should they be worse than anyone? Can there really be another person like me?’

Thinking this, he wandered on and soon found himself in a courtyard.

‘Another courtyard like this, besides the House of Green Delights!’

Up the steps he went and into the room. Someone was lying on a couch. Beyond it, several girls were doing needlework, while others laughed and played.

The boy on the couch gave a sigh.

‘Bao-yu,’ one of the maids asked with a smile, ‘if you aren’t asleep, what are you sighing for? I suppose it’s your cousin’s illness, and you’re fretting and grieving over nothing again.’

Bao-yu was startled to hear it.

The boy on the couch said:

‘I heard Grandmother say there was another Bao-yu in the capital, Chang’an, with a disposition just like mine. I couldn’t believe it. Just now I had a dream. I went into a garden in the capital and met some girls, but they all called me a stinking little servant-boy and wouldn’t have anything to do with me. At last I found his room, but he was asleep. Only his bodily shell was there. Where his true self had gone, I couldn’t tell.’

‘I came here looking for Bao-yu,’ Bao-yu said at once. ‘So you’re Bao-yu?’

The boy on the couch scrambled down and caught hold of him.

‘So you’re Bao-yu! This isn’t a dream, then.’

‘How could it be a dream?’ said Bao-yu. ‘It’s real, quite real.’

Before he had finished, someone came in to say:

‘The master wants Bao-yu.’

Both boys took fright. One Bao-yu hurried away, and the other called after him:

‘Bao-yu, come back! Come back!’

Aroma, beside him, heard him calling his own name in his sleep and hurried to shake him awake.

‘Where’s Bao-yu?’ she asked with a smile.

He was awake now, but still dazed. He pointed towards the door.

‘He’s just gone out.’

‘You’re still muddled by your dream,’ Aroma said, laughing. ‘Rub your eyes and look properly. That’s your own reflection in the mirror.’

Bao-yu looked in front of him. There, facing him, was the large fitted mirror, reflecting his image. He laughed too. Someone had already brought a rinsing bowl and strong tea, and he rinsed his mouth.

‘No wonder the old lady is always telling us not to have too many mirrors in children’s rooms,’ said Musk. ‘A child’s soul isn’t fully formed. Too much looking in mirrors frightens them in their sleep and gives them wild dreams. And now we’ve put a bed right by that great mirror. It’s all right when we lower the mirror cover. But before long it’ll be hot, and he may doze off at any time. Who’ll remember to let it down? Just now we forgot. Of course he lay there playing with his reflection first, and when his eyes closed, it turned into a topsy-turvy dream. How else could he see himself and call his own name?

‘We really ought to move the bed further in tomorrow.’

Before she had finished, someone arrived from Lady Wang to summon Bao-yu. What she wished to say to him was not yet known—
