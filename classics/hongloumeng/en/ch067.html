Chapter 67 At the sight of gifts from home, Dai-yu longs for her native place; On hearing a secret, Xi-feng questions a household page

Chapter end

After You San-jie had taken her own life, Old Mrs You, Er-jie, Jia Zhen and Jia Lian were all overcome with grief, as need hardly be said. They hastily had her laid in a coffin, taken outside the city and buried. Liu Xiang-lian, seeing San-jie dead, had been lost in passionate grief and longing, until a few cold words from the Taoist broke through the barrier of his delusion. He cut off his hair, renounced the world and went lightly away with the mad Taoist. No one knew where they went. For the present we shall leave them there.

When Aunt Xue learnt that Xiang-lian's marriage to You San-jie had been settled, she was delighted. Full of happy anticipation, she was planning to buy him a house, furnish it and choose an auspicious day for the wedding, in repayment for his saving her son's life. Then some of the household pages were heard shouting, ‘San-jie's killed herself!’ The little maids overheard them and told Aunt Xue. Not knowing the reason, she sighed deeply. While she was still wondering about it, Bao-chai came over from the garden.

‘My dear,’ Aunt Xue said, ‘have you heard? Your cousin Zhen's wife has a younger sister, the third Miss You. Wasn't she promised to your brother's sworn brother, Liu Xiang-lian? For some reason she's cut her throat, and now no one knows where Liu Xiang-lian has gone either. What an extraordinary business. Who could ever have expected it?’

Bao-chai did not take the news to heart.

‘There's a good saying,’ she replied. ‘“No one can foretell the wind and clouds in heaven, or the good and ill that a day may bring to men.” It must have been appointed for them in a former life.

‘The other day, Mother, you were talking of making arrangements for him because he'd saved my brother. Now one is dead and the other gone, I think we can only let it be. There's no need for you to grieve over them. But my brother has been home from Jiangnan for ten or twenty days, and I should think the goods he brought back to sell must all have been sent out by now. The men who went with him worked so hard. They've been back for several months. You ought to speak to my brother about inviting them to a meal and thanking them properly. We mustn't let them think we have no manners.’

Mother and daughter were still talking when Xue Pan came in from outside, the traces of tears still in his eyes. The moment he entered, he clapped his hands and said to his mother:

‘Have you heard about Brother Liu and You San-jie, Mother?’

‘I've only just heard. Your sister and I were talking about the whole affair.’

‘Did you hear that Liu Xiang-lian has gone off with a Taoist and renounced the world?’

‘That's stranger still,’ said Aunt Xue. ‘How could a clever young man like Mr Liu lose his head all of a sudden and go off with a Taoist? You were such good friends, and he's here all alone, with neither parents nor brothers. You ought to look for him wherever you can.

‘How far can he go with that Taoist? They can't be anywhere but in one of the temples or monasteries hereabouts.’

‘Of course I looked,’ said Xue Pan. ‘The moment I heard, I took the boys out and searched everywhere. Not a shadow of him. I asked people too, and they all said they hadn't seen him.’

‘Well, if you've searched and can't find him, you've done your duty as a friend,’ Aunt Xue said. ‘Who knows whether leaving the world may not turn out for his good? But you ought to see to the business now, and start getting things ready for your own wedding as well. We haven't many people in our family. As the saying goes, “The slow bird must be the first to fly.” That way we shan't be forgetting this and that at the last moment, with things missing and everyone laughing at us. Your sister was just saying, too, that you've been home more than half a month, so the goods must all be sent out by now. You ought to give the men who went with you a dinner to thank them for their trouble.

‘They travelled two or three thousand li with you and worked hard for four or five months. And think of all the anxiety and burdens they bore for you on the road.’

‘Quite right, Mother,’ said Xue Pan. ‘My sister's thought of everything. I had the same thing in mind, only these last few days I've had so much trouble sending goods here and there that my head's been ready to burst. Then I've been running about over Brother Liu for days, and nothing came of that either. A lot of fuss for nothing, while I neglected the real business. Why don't I settle on tomorrow or the day after and send out invitations?’

‘Do as you think best,’ said Aunt Xue.

She had scarcely finished when a page came in to report:

‘Mr Zhang, the head manager, has sent two trunks of things. He says these were the master's private purchases and aren't entered in the trading accounts. He meant to send them before, but they were buried under the chests of goods and he couldn't get at them. The last goods went out yesterday, so he was only able to send them today.’

As he spoke, two more pages carried in two large palm-fibre-covered trunks, protected by boards.

‘Oh dear!’ cried Xue Pan at the sight. ‘How could I have grown such a fool? Things I bought specially for you and my sister, Mother, and I forgot to have them brought home. The men had to send them after me!’

‘You can say that!’ Bao-chai replied. ‘Bought specially for us, and left there for ten or twenty days. If you hadn't bought them specially, I suppose we'd have waited till the end of the year.

‘You really are far too careless about everything.’

‘I suppose they frightened my soul out of me on the road, and it hasn't found its way back yet,’ said Xue Pan with a laugh.

They all laughed for a while. Then he told a little maid:

‘Go and tell the boys we've received the things. They can go back.’

‘What have you got in there, to be tied and bound up like this?’ Aunt Xue and Bao-chai asked.

Xue Pan called in two pages to untie the ropes, take off the boards and unlock the trunks. The first proved to contain silk, satin, damask, brocade, foreign goods and other things for household use.

‘That one's for my sister,’ said Xue Pan, smiling, and went to open it himself.

Mother and daughter looked inside. There were brushes, ink, paper and inkstones, letter paper in all colours, scented sachets and beads, fans and fan pendants, face powder and rouge. From Tiger Hill there were walking figures, drinking-game toys, little mercury-filled tumbling boys, sand lamps and clay figures arranged in scene after scene from plays, in boxes covered with green gauze. There was also a little clay likeness of Xue Pan himself, made on Tiger Hill, which resembled him to perfection.

Bao-chai paid little attention to the other things. She picked up the likeness of Xue Pan, studied it closely, then looked at her brother, and could not help laughing. She told Oriole to take several old women and carry the things, trunk and all, into the garden. After a little more conversation with her mother and brother, she went back there herself. Aunt Xue meanwhile took out the contents of her trunk, arranged them carefully in separate portions, and sent Tong-xi with gifts for Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the others. Of that we need say no more.

Back in her own room, Bao-chai looked through the little articles one by one. Having put aside what she wanted for herself, she made up suitable parcels: brushes, ink, paper and inkstones for some; scented sachets, fans and scented pendants for others; rouge, powder and hair oil for others again; and for some, toys alone. Dai-yu's was the only portion she made different from the rest, and she doubled it besides. When everything was ready, she sent Oriole, accompanied by an old woman, to deliver the parcels to the various apartments.

The girls all received their gifts, rewarded the messengers and said they would thank Bao-chai when they saw her. Only Dai-yu, at the sight of things from her own native place, felt her grief stirred afresh. Both her parents were dead, she thought, she had no brothers and was living with relations. Who was there to bring her such gifts from home? At the thought, sorrow overcame her again.

Nightingale knew very well what was in her mind, but did not dare say it outright. Standing beside her, she tried to comfort her.

‘You're so often ill, miss, and taking medicine morning and night. These last two days you've looked a little better than before. You've picked up a little spirit, but you're still far from well. The things Miss Bao sent today show how much she always thinks of you. You ought to be pleased when you see them. Why should they make you sad instead? Anyone would think Miss Bao had only sent them to distress you. If she heard of it, she'd feel awkward herself. Besides, the old lady and everyone here have tried every possible way of finding good doctors to examine you and prescribe for you, all to make you well.

‘Now you're a little better, and you begin crying again. Aren't you harming your own health, and giving the old lady more to worry about when she sees you? It's all this worrying that's damaged your blood and vital energy and made you ill in the first place. Your health is too precious for you to treat it lightly.’

While Nightingale was reasoning with her, a little maid was heard in the courtyard.

‘Master Bao is here!’

‘Ask him in,’ Nightingale said at once.

Bao-yu entered, and Dai-yu invited him to sit down. Seeing the traces of tears all over her face, he asked:

‘Who's been upsetting you again, coz?’

‘Who's upset about anything?’ said Dai-yu, forcing a smile.

Nightingale, standing beside her, pursed her lips towards the table behind the bed. Bao-yu took the hint and looked. Seeing the heap of things there, he knew they had come from Bao-chai.

‘Where did all these come from?’ he said teasingly. ‘Are you going to open a general shop?’

Dai-yu made no answer.

‘And now you're talking about the things too, sir!’ Nightingale said with a smile. ‘Miss Bao sent them over, and the moment Miss Lin saw them she began to cry. I've been trying to comfort her. You've come at just the right moment. Do try for us.’

Bao-yu knew perfectly well what had caused Dai-yu's grief, but dared not touch on it. He could only smile and say:

‘There can only be one reason. Miss Bao hasn't sent her enough, and that's why she's upset. Don't worry, coz. Next year I'll send someone to Jiangnan and have two whole boatloads brought back for you. Then you won't have to weep and wipe your eyes.’

Dai-yu knew he was trying to cheer her up. She could neither rebuff him nor agree with him.

‘However little I may have seen of the world,’ she said, ‘I haven't come to the point of crying because someone hasn't sent me enough presents. I'm not a child of two or three. You make me out far more petty than I am.

‘I have my own reasons. What do you know of them?’

Her tears began to fall again. Bao-yu hurried to the bed and sat down close beside her. Picking up the things one after another, he turned them over and examined them, deliberately asking what this was, what it was called, what that very pretty thing was made of, and what another was meant to be used for. One, he said, would look well set out in front of her; another would make a fine ornament on the long table, like an antique. He kept on with this stream of inconsequential chatter. At last Dai-yu felt she could not let him go on making such efforts for her.

‘You needn't keep trying to distract me,’ she said. ‘Let's go over to Cousin Bao's.’

Nothing could have suited Bao-yu better than to get her out and dispel some of her sadness.

‘We ought to go and thank her for the things she sent us,’ he said.

‘We're all cousins. There's no need for that. But now her brother's back, he must have told her about some of the old sights in the south. I should like to hear. It will be almost like going home once myself.’

Her eyes reddened again as she spoke.

Bao-yu stood waiting for her, and Dai-yu had no choice but to come out with him and go to Bao-chai's.

Now Xue Pan, following his mother's advice, had hurriedly sent out invitations and ordered a dinner. Next day all four of the invited partners arrived. Naturally there was some talk of buying and selling, accounts and the dispatch of goods. Before long dinner was served. Xue Pan invited them to take their places and poured their wine in turn. Aunt Xue sent someone out with her greetings. As they drank and talked, one of the men remarked:

‘There are two good friends missing from this table today.’

The others all asked who he meant.

‘Who else? Master Lian of the Jia family and our host's sworn brother, Master Liu.’

They all remembered them then.

‘Why didn't you invite Master Lian and Master Liu?’ they asked Xue Pan.

Xue Pan frowned and sighed.

‘Master Lian's gone to Ping-an Zhou again. He left a couple of days ago. As for Master Liu, don't speak of him! It's the strangest thing in the world.

‘Master Liu, indeed! He's Reverend Liu somewhere or other now.’

‘What do you mean?’ they all asked in astonishment.

Xue Pan told them the whole story of Xiang-lian. They were more astonished than ever.

‘No wonder! The other day, when we were at the shop, we vaguely heard people making a commotion about a Taoist who'd talked a man into renouncing the world with two or three words and taken him away. Then they said a gust of wind had blown him away. We didn't know who it was. We were busy sending out goods. Where had we the time to ask about it? Even now we hardly knew whether to believe it. Who would have thought it was Master Liu? If we'd known, we ought all to have tried to reason with him. Whatever happened, we wouldn't have let him go.’

‘Perhaps it's something else,’ one of them said.

‘What?’ the others asked.

‘Master Liu's a clever fellow. Perhaps he hasn't really gone off to become a Taoist at all. He knows something of the martial arts, and he's strong too. He may have seen through the Taoist's sorcery and wicked tricks and gone with him on purpose to deal with him out of sight. You never know.’

‘If that were so, it wouldn't be so bad,’ said Xue Pan.

‘Why doesn't someone do something about all these people who fool the world with their wicked nonsense?’

‘But surely you went to look for him when you heard?’ the others said.

‘Inside the city and out, where didn't I look? You may laugh at me, but when I couldn't find him, I cried.’

After that he did nothing but sigh, utterly listless and unlike his usual cheerful self. Seeing his state, the partners could hardly linger. They drank only a few cups of wine, ate their meal and dispersed.

Meanwhile Bao-yu and Dai-yu had arrived at Bao-chai's.

‘Your brother went to all the trouble of bringing those things back,’ Bao-yu said to her. ‘You ought to have kept them for yourself, instead of giving them to us.’

‘They're nothing very fine,’ Bao-chai replied, smiling. ‘Only local things brought from a long way off. They'll do to give us all something new to look at.’

‘When we were little, we never thought anything of such things,’ said Dai-yu. ‘Now they really are novelties.’

‘You see, coz,’ Bao-chai said with a smile, ‘it's just as the saying goes: “Things grow precious far from home.” What are they worth in themselves?’

Hearing words that touched so closely on Dai-yu's recent thoughts, Bao-yu hastily changed the subject.

‘If your brother goes again next year, you must make him bring us plenty more.’

Dai-yu glanced at him.

‘If you want them, say so for yourself. You needn't bring other people into it. Look, cousin, he hasn't come to thank you at all. He's come to order next year's presents already.’

Both Bao-chai and Bao-yu laughed. The three talked for a while longer, until Dai-yu's illness came up. Bao-chai offered her some encouragement.

‘When you aren't feeling well, you really ought to make an effort to come out and walk about,’ she said. ‘It will take your mind off things. It's much better than sitting shut up in your room.

‘Didn't I feel so listless the other day, hot all over and wanting only to lie down? I thought there was sickness in the air and was afraid I might be falling ill, so I found things to keep myself occupied. I've only begun to feel better these last two days.’

‘What you say is perfectly true,’ said Dai-yu. ‘I've been thinking the same myself.’

They sat a little longer before parting. Bao-yu saw Dai-yu back to the gate of Naiad's House, and then each went home.

Now Aunt Zhao was delighted to find that Bao-chai had sent Jia Huan some things.

‘No wonder everyone says what a good girl she is,’ she thought. ‘So considerate and generous. I can see now that it's true. How many things can her brother have brought back? Yet she's sent to every door, missing no one, and never let it show that she's favoured one above another. She's even remembered such unlucky people as us. That Lin girl won't so much as look us in the face, let alone give us anything.’

Turning this over in her mind, she kept picking up the presents, handling them and looking at them from every side. Then it struck her that Bao-chai was a relation of Lady Wang's. Why not take the opportunity to win a little favour there? Bustling officiously, she carried the things to Lady Wang's room and stood beside her with a smile.

‘Miss Bao has just sent these for Huan. How thoughtful of such a young lady! She's a true daughter of a great household, so gracious and generous. How could anyone help admiring her?

‘No wonder the old lady and Your Ladyship are always praising her and making much of her. I didn't like to put the things away on my own authority, so I've brought them for Your Ladyship to see and be pleased with too.’

Lady Wang had already understood her purpose. She found Aunt Zhao's remarks ill-judged, but could not simply ignore her.

‘You can take them and put them away for Huan to play with,’ she said.

Aunt Zhao had come full of enthusiasm, only to be met with a rebuff. She was furious, but dared not show it, and could only withdraw in embarrassment. Back in her own room, she flung the things aside and muttered to herself:

‘What was all that supposed to amount to?’

She sat there nursing her anger for a while.

When Oriole and the old women returned from delivering the gifts, Oriole reported to Bao-chai all the thanks she had received and all the silver and cash they had been given in reward. The old woman then left. Oriole stepped closer to Bao-chai and spoke softly at her side.

‘When I went over to Mrs Lian's just now, she looked furious.

‘After I'd left the things and come out, I quietly asked Crimson. She said Mrs Lian had just come back from the old lady's rooms and wasn't all smiles as she usually is. She'd called Patience in, and the two had been whispering away, but Crimson didn't know what about. From the look of it, something serious had happened. Haven't you heard of anything going on at the old lady's, miss?’

Bao-chai too was puzzled and could think of no reason for Xi-feng's anger.

‘Every household has its own affairs,’ she said. ‘We can't concern ourselves with all of them. Go and pour me some tea.’

Oriole went out to see to the tea, and there we shall leave her.

After seeing Dai-yu home, Bao-yu returned thinking of her loneliness and began to feel sad on her behalf. He wanted to tell Aroma about it, but when he came in, only Musk and Ripple were in the room.

‘Where's your sister Aroma gone?’ he asked.

‘She can't be anywhere but in one of these courtyards,’ said Musk. ‘She's hardly going to get lost. You can't be without her for a moment before you start looking.’

‘I'm not afraid of losing her,’ said Bao-yu, smiling. ‘I was just over at Miss Lin's, and she was upset again. When I asked, it turned out to be because of the things Cousin Bao had sent her. She saw they were from her native place, and the sight naturally made her sad. I wanted to tell Aroma, so she could go and cheer her up when she had a moment.’

As he was speaking, Skybright came in.

‘So you're back. Who do you want comforted now?’

Bao-yu told her what he had just said.

‘Aroma only went out a little while ago,’ Skybright replied. ‘I heard her say she was going to Mrs Lian's.

‘She may very well go on to Miss Lin's too.’

Bao-yu said no more. Ripple poured him some tea. He rinsed his mouth with a sip and handed the cup to a little maid. Feeling thoroughly out of sorts, he lay down on the bed just as he was.

Now after Bao-yu had gone out, Aroma had sat at her needlework for a while. Then it occurred to her that Xi-feng was unwell and she had not been over to see her for several days. She had heard, too, that Jia Lian was away. It would be a good opportunity for a quiet chat.

‘Stay here and mind the room,’ she told Skybright. ‘Don't all go out and leave Bao-yu with no one to turn to when he comes back.’

‘Oh dear!’ said Skybright. ‘You're the only one in the room who ever thinks of him. The rest of us do nothing but sit about and eat for nothing.’

Aroma smiled and went away without answering.

She had just reached the Drenched Blossoms Bridge. It was the turn of summer into autumn, and in the pool fresh lotus mingled with fading growth, its red and green falling in loose disarray. As she went along the bank, Aroma lingered to look. Suddenly she raised her head and saw someone beneath the grape arbour, flicking at something with a duster. Coming closer, she found it was Old Mamma Zhu. The old woman came forward to greet her, all smiles.

‘How have you found time for a stroll today, miss?’

‘Yes, it's a rare chance. I'm going to call on Mrs Lian. What are you doing here?’

‘Driving off the honeybees. There was so little rain in the dog-days this year, the fruit trees are all full of insects. They've eaten the fruit till it's covered in scars, and a good deal has fallen off. You wouldn't know, miss, but these wasps are the worst of all. They only need to bite into two or three grapes in a bunch. The juice drips onto the sound ones, and the whole bunch begins to rot.

‘Look, miss. In the little while we've been talking and I haven't been shooing them away, all those have settled again.’

‘You couldn't drive them all off if you never stopped,’ Aroma said. ‘Why don't you tell the buyer to have plenty of little open-weave cloth bags made? Put one over each bunch. They'll let the air through and keep the fruit from being spoiled.’

‘That's a good idea, miss,’ said the old woman, smiling. ‘I've only taken this over this year. How should I know such a clever trick?’ She smiled again. ‘Though some of the fruit's been spoiled, it tastes good this year. If you don't believe me, I'll pick one for you to try.’

Aroma grew serious.

‘You mustn't do that. Apart from their not being ripe enough to eat, even if they were ripe, the first fruit hasn't been offered to our elders yet. How could we eat it first? You've served in this household for years. Surely you know that rule.’

‘Quite right, miss,’ Mamma Zhu said hastily, still smiling. ‘I saw how much you liked them, and that was why I ventured to offer. But I'd forgotten the proper order of things. I am an old fool.’

‘There's no great harm done,’ said Aroma.

‘Only you elderly mammas mustn't be the first to set such an example.’

She went straight out through the garden gate and on to Xi-feng's. As soon as she entered the courtyard, she heard Xi-feng saying:

‘Heaven knows my conscience is clear! I wear myself out in this house, and they treat me more and more like a thief.’

Aroma knew at once that something had happened. She could neither turn back nor comfortably go in. Making her footsteps a little heavier, she called through the window:

‘Is Sister Patience at home?’

Patience answered quickly and came out to meet her.

‘Is Mrs Lian at home too?’ Aroma asked. ‘Is she feeling quite well again?’

As she spoke she came in. Xi-feng had made a show of lying back on the bed, but on seeing Aroma she too smiled and rose.

‘I'm better, thank you for thinking of me. Why haven't you come to sit with us these last few days?’

‘Since you've been ill, ma'am, I ought to have come every day to ask after you. Only I was afraid you might not be feeling well enough and would want to rest quietly. Our coming might make too much noise and tire you.’

‘Tire me? Nothing of the kind,’ said Xi-feng, smiling.

‘But however many people there are in Cousin Bao's rooms, you're the one he depends on to look after him. You really can't be spared. Patience often tells me that you're thinking of me even when you're not here, and asking how I am. That's all the kindness I could wish for.’

As she spoke, she told Patience to move a stool beside the bed and invited Aroma to sit down. Felicity brought in the tea. Aroma half rose in acknowledgement.

‘Do sit down, sister,’ she said.

They chatted for a while. Then a little maid was heard whispering to Patience in the outer room:

‘Wang-er is here. He's waiting at the second gate.’

Patience's reply was equally quiet.

‘I know. Tell him to go away for now and come back later. He isn't to stand at the door.’

Knowing they had business to attend to, Aroma stayed for only a few more words before rising to go.

‘Come and sit with me when you have a moment,’ said Xi-feng. ‘A little talk cheers me up.’

‘See your sister out,’ she told Patience.

Patience assented and went with her. Two or three little maids stood waiting there in a silent, motionless row.

Aroma did not know what it was all about and went on her way. Having seen her out, Patience came back to report:

‘Wang-er was here just now. Since Aroma was with you, I told him to wait outside for a little. Would you like him called in at once, ma'am, or should he wait?’

‘Call him,’ said Xi-feng.

Patience promptly sent a little maid to bring him in. Xi-feng turned to Patience again.

‘How exactly did you hear about it?’

‘It was what that little maid said earlier.

‘She said she was just inside the second gate and heard two pages outside saying, “This new Second Mistress is even prettier than our old Second Mistress, and she's good-tempered too.” Then Wang-er, or someone else, shouted at them: “What's all this about new mistresses and old mistresses? Hold your tongues at once. If they hear of it inside, they'll have your tongues cut out.”’

While Patience was speaking, a little maid came in.

‘Wang-er is waiting outside, ma'am.’

Xi-feng gave a cold laugh.

‘Bring him in.’

The little maid went out.

‘The mistress is calling you.’

Wang-er answered promptly and entered. After paying his respects, he stood at the outer doorway with his hands at his sides.

‘Come here. I want to ask you something,’ said Xi-feng.

Only then did he move to stand beside the door of the inner room.

‘Your master has taken up with a woman outside. Do you know about it or not?’

Wang-er made another obeisance.

‘I spend every day at the second gate waiting for orders, ma'am. How could I know about the master's affairs outside?’

Xi-feng laughed coldly.

‘Of course you don't know.

‘If you did know, why would you stop other people talking?’

Wang-er realised from her words that the earlier conversation had already leaked out. Knowing he could not conceal it, he knelt again to answer.

‘I truly don't know, ma'am. Joker and Xi-er were talking nonsense there just now, and I shouted at them, that's all. I don't know the ins and outs of it, and I daren't give you a false account. Please ask Joker, ma'am. He's the one who usually goes out with the master.’

Xi-feng spat with all her force.

‘You pack of conscienceless, worthless little bastards! You're all on the same vine, and you think I don't know it. Go and fetch that bastard Joker, and don't you leave either. When I've got the truth out of him, I'll question you again. Good, good, very good! Fine people I've trained up to serve me!’

Wang-er could only repeat ‘Yes, ma'am’ several times, knock his head on the ground and scramble up to fetch Joker.

Joker was in the accounts office amusing himself with the other pages. He started with fright when told that the mistress had sent for him, though he never guessed that this was the business which had come to light. He hurried after Wang-er. Wang-er went in first.

‘Joker is here, ma'am.’

‘Call him!’ Xi-feng snapped.

The sound of her voice robbed Joker of his wits. He could only screw up his courage and enter.

‘A fine lad you are!’ Xi-feng began the moment she saw him.

‘A fine piece of work you and your master have done! Now tell me the truth.’

At these words, and at the sight of Xi-feng's face and the maids standing on either side, Joker went weak with fright. He dropped to his knees and began knocking his head on the ground.

‘As far as the business itself goes,’ said Xi-feng, ‘I've heard that you weren't responsible. But you didn't come and tell me at once, and that was your fault. If you tell the truth now, I'll still let you off. Give me one false word, and you'd better feel how many heads you've got on those shoulders.’

Trembling all over, Joker knocked his head towards her.

‘What business do you mean, ma'am? What have I done wrong with the master?’

At this, all Xi-feng's pent-up fury burst out.

‘Slap his face!’ she shouted.

Wang-er stepped forward and was about to strike when Xi-feng rounded on him.

‘You stupid bastard! He's to slap himself. Who asked you to do it? There'll be time enough for you to slap your own face presently.’

Joker did indeed begin striking his own cheeks, right and left, and had given himself a dozen or more slaps before Xi-feng shouted ‘Stop!’

‘This business of your master marrying some new mistress or old mistress outside. I suppose you know nothing about that?’

Now that the matter had been named, Joker was more frightened than ever. He snatched off his cap and banged his head on the brick floor with resounding thuds.

‘Only spare my life, ma'am! I won't dare tell you a single lie.’

‘Speak, then!’

Joker straightened himself stiffly on his knees.

‘I didn't know anything about it at first, ma'am. It was that day when the old master's funeral procession had left the Ning mansion, and Yu Lu had gone to Master Zhen at the temple to collect some silver. Master Lian went to the Ning mansion with Master Rong. On the way, the two began talking about Mrs Zhen's two younger sisters.

‘The master was praising her, and Master Rong coaxed him along, saying he'd arrange for Second Aunt to marry him.’

At this Xi-feng spat fiercely.

‘Pah! Shameless bastard! What sort of aunt is she to you?’

Joker hastily knocked his head on the ground again.

‘I deserve to die, ma'am!’

He glanced up at her, not daring another word.

‘Finished, have you?’ Xi-feng said. ‘Why don't you go on?’

‘If you'll forgive me, ma'am, I'll dare to tell you.’

‘Your mother's farts!’ Xi-feng spat. ‘What's all this about forgiving you? Go on and tell me properly. There's plenty more.’

‘The master was delighted at the suggestion,’ Joker continued. ‘I don't know how they managed to make it a reality after that.’

Xi-feng gave a faint, cold smile.

‘Naturally. How could you know?

‘I dare say you're sick of all the things you do know. Very well, go on.’

‘After that, Master Rong found a house for the master.’

‘Where is the house now?’ Xi-feng asked at once.

‘Just behind our mansion.’

‘Oh.’ Xi-feng turned to Patience. ‘We must all be dead. Listen to that!’

Patience dared not make a sound.

‘Master Zhen's people gave the Zhang family some silver,’ Joker continued. ‘I don't know how much. Then the Zhangs stopped troubling about it.’

‘How have we got mixed up with some Zhang family or Li family now?’ Xi-feng asked.

‘You see, ma'am, this Second Mistress...’

Joker stopped short and slapped his own face. The sight provoked even Xi-feng into an exasperated laugh. The maids on either side pressed their lips together to hide their smiles. Joker considered his words.

‘Mrs Zhen's younger sister...’

‘Well?’ Xi-feng prompted.

‘Go on!’

‘Mrs Zhen's younger sister had been promised to someone since she was little. His family name was Zhang, Zhang Hua or something. He's so poor now he's almost begging. Master Zhen promised him some silver, and he withdrew from the engagement.’

Xi-feng nodded and turned to the maids.

‘Did you all hear that? Little bastard! He said at first he didn't know.’

‘Then the master had the house papered and brought her there as his bride,’ Joker went on.

‘Where did he bring her from?’

‘She was carried over from her mother's house.’

‘Very good. Was there no one to accompany the bride?’

‘Only Master Rong and a few maids and old women. No one else.’

‘Didn't your mistress from the other house come?’

‘She brought some things and came to see them two days later.’

Xi-feng gave a little smile and turned to Patience.

‘No wonder he couldn't stop praising her those two days.’

She turned back to Joker.

‘Who waits on them? You, of course.’

Joker kept knocking his head on the ground without speaking.

‘All those days when he said he had business at the other mansion, I suppose this was the business?’

‘Sometimes he did go on business, ma'am, and sometimes he went to the new house.’

‘Who lives with her?’

‘Her mother and her younger sister.

‘Her sister cut her own throat yesterday.’

‘What was that for?’

Joker told her the story of Liu Xiang-lian.

‘That man has had a lucky escape,’ said Xi-feng. ‘He's been spared becoming a notorious cuckold.’

Then she asked:

‘Is there anything else?’

‘I don't know of anything else, ma'am. Every word I've just told you is true. If you find a single lie, you can have me beaten to death, and I shan't complain.’

Xi-feng sat with her head lowered for a while, then pointed at him again.

‘You little monkey's brat, you ought to be beaten to death. What was there to hide from me? You thought that if you kept me in the dark, you'd win favour with that fool of a master, and your new mistress would be fond of you. If I hadn't seen that you were frightened enough just now not to dare lie, I'd have had your legs smashed. Get out!’

Joker knocked his head once more, scrambled to his feet and backed to the outer doorway, not daring to leave outright.

‘Come here. I haven't finished.’

Joker hurried back and stood with his hands at his sides, listening respectfully.

‘What's your hurry?’ Xi-feng asked. ‘What reward is your new mistress waiting to give you?’

Joker dared not even raise his head.

‘From today you're not to go over there. When I call, you come. Just try being a moment late! Go.’

Joker hastily repeated ‘Yes, ma'am’ several times and withdrew through the door. Xi-feng called again:

‘Joker!’

He promptly answered and came back.

‘Now you'll run off and tell your master, won't you?’

‘I wouldn't dare, ma'am.’

‘Let one word of this pass your lips outside, and look out for your skin!’

Only after another hasty assent did Joker get away. Xi-feng called again:

‘Where's Wang-er?’

Wang-er answered at once and came forward. Xi-feng fixed her eyes on him and stared, for as long as it would have taken to say two or three sentences, before she spoke.

‘Good Wang-er. Very good. Go. If anyone outside says one word, I'll hold you answerable for it all.’

Wang-er assented and went out too.

Xi-feng called for tea. The little maids took the hint and all withdrew. Only then did she speak to Patience.

‘You heard it all? This is fine, isn't it?’

Patience dared not answer and could only smile in response. The more Xi-feng thought, the angrier she grew. She lay back on her pillow, staring into space. Suddenly her brows drew together: a plan had come to her.

‘Patience, come here.’

Patience promptly answered and came to her.

‘I've thought what we ought to do about this,’ said Xi-feng. ‘We needn't wait for your master to come back to discuss it.’
