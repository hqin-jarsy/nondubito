Chapter 6 Jia Bao-yu first tastes the love of cloud and rain; Grannie Liu makes her first visit to the Rong mansion

Chapter end

Now Qin-shi, hearing Bao-yu call her childhood name in his dream, was naturally puzzled, but could hardly question him closely. Bao-yu himself was dazed, as though something had slipped away from him. The others hurried to bring him some longan soup. After sipping two mouthfuls, he got up to straighten his clothes. As Aroma reached to tie his trouser-sash, her hand happened to brush his thigh and felt a patch of cold dampness. Startled, she snatched it away and asked what had happened. Bao-yu flushed scarlet and gave her hand a squeeze.

Aroma was an intelligent girl, two years older than Bao-yu, and had lately begun to understand something of these matters. Seeing his condition, she guessed half the truth. She too blushed crimson with embarrassment and dared ask no more.

She finished putting his clothes in order, and they went to Grandmother Jia’s. After a hasty supper he came back to his own rooms. When the wet nurses and other maids were out of the way, Aroma hastily fetched him a fresh undergarment and helped him change.

‘Dear sister,’ Bao-yu pleaded, blushing, ‘whatever you do, don’t tell anyone.’

Blushing herself, Aroma asked with a smile, ‘What was it you dreamed about?

‘Where did all that dirty stuff come from?’

‘It’s a long story,’ Bao-yu said.

He told Aroma his dream in detail. When he came to the love of cloud and rain that Disenchantment had taught him, she hid her face and bent double, laughing with embarrassment. Bao-yu had always been fond of Aroma’s gentle, winning ways and her pretty looks, and now pressed her to join him in what Disenchantment had taught. Aroma had long known that Grandmother Jia had given her to Bao-yu, and felt that even now this would not be a breach of propriety. So they secretly tried it together; fortunately no one came upon them. From then on Bao-yu treated Aroma differently from all the others, and she served him with still greater devotion. For the moment there is nothing more to tell of them.

Taking the Rong household as a whole, there were not so very many people: three or four hundred, from highest to lowest. Nor were there so very many affairs: ten or twenty a day. Yet they were as tangled as a skein of hemp, with no end to catch hold of and draw into order.

Just as we were wondering which affair and which person would make the best beginning, there happened to come, from a thousand li away, a tiny household, insignificant as a mustard seed or a bean. Through a slight connection with the Rong family, members of it were on their way to the mansion that very day. This little household will therefore give us a thread to follow.

What was their surname? Who were they, and what connection did they have with the Rong mansion? If you gentlemen find such things petty and vulgar, put this book down at once and seek a better one to refresh your eyes. But if you think they may pass an idle hour, let this blockhead tell you all about them.

The little household just mentioned belonged to the Wang family, natives of the district. An ancestor had held a very minor post in the capital and had known Lady Wang’s father, Xi-feng’s grandfather. Coveting the influence and advantages of that family, he had claimed kinship through their shared surname and acknowledged himself a nephew. At the time, only Lady Wang and her eldest brother, Xi-feng’s father, had been living in the capital, so they alone knew of this branch of nominal relations; the others knew nothing of it. That ancestor was now dead. His only son, Wang Cheng, had seen the family fortunes decline and moved out of the city to their old village. Wang Cheng too had recently died of illness, leaving only his son, known by the childhood name Gou-er. Gou-er in turn had a son, called Ban-er, and his lawful wife, née Liu, had also borne him a daughter, Qing-er. This family of four still lived by farming. As Gou-er did other work during the day to earn a living, and his wife was busy drawing water, grinding grain and doing the housework, no one was left to look after the two siblings, Qing-er and Ban-er. Gou-er therefore brought his mother-in-law, Grannie Liu, to live with them. She was an old woman who had been widowed for many years, with no children at her side, subsisting on two mu of poor land.

Naturally she was glad to have her son-in-law take her in and support her, and she devoted herself to helping him and her daughter get by. It was the end of autumn, the beginning of winter. The weather was turning cold, and nothing had yet been got ready for the season. Gou-er could not help worrying. After a few cups of wine drunk to drown his troubles, he began finding things to rage about at home. His wife dared not answer back. Grannie Liu could bear it no longer.

‘Son-in-law,’ she said, ‘don’t be cross with me for speaking out. We country people live honestly, don’t we? We eat as much rice as our bowl will hold. When you were little, you had your old father to thank for easy eating and drinking, and now you can’t keep yourself in hand. When you have money, you think of today and never tomorrow; when it’s gone, you fly into a temper. Is that how a grown man behaves? We may live outside the city, but we’re still at the Son of Heaven’s feet. There’s money everywhere in Chang’an, only nobody knows how to pick it up. Jumping up and down at home won’t do any good.’

‘All you can do is talk nonsense from the kang!’ Gou-er burst out. ‘Do you want me to rob people or steal?’

‘Who’s telling you to steal?

‘We ought at least to put our heads together and think of something. You don’t suppose the money will come running to our house by itself?’

Gou-er gave a cold laugh. ‘If there were a way, would I have waited till now? I’ve no relations collecting taxes, no friends in office, no friends in office. What can I possibly think of? Even if I had any, I doubt they’d bother with us!’

‘That’s not necessarily so,’ said Grannie Liu. ‘We make the plans; Heaven decides whether they work. Once we’ve done our part, who knows? With the Bodhisattva’s blessing, a chance may come. In fact, I’ve thought of one for you. Your family used to claim kinship with the Wangs of Jinling. Twenty years ago they treated you well enough. It’s only because you’ve been so stiff-necked, refusing to go near them, that you’ve drifted apart. Your wife and I went there once, back then. The second daughter was a lively, open-handed girl, knew how to treat people and never put on airs. She’s now the wife of the Second Master Jia at the Rong mansion. I hear that as she’s grown older she’s become still kinder to the poor and the old. She loves feeding Buddhist monks, honouring Taoist priests, giving out rice and money. The Wang family may have gone off to a frontier appointment on promotion, but I dare say that married daughter would still remember us. Why don’t you pay her a visit? She may remember old times and do something for us. If she takes it into her head to be kind, a single hair plucked from her body would be thicker than our waists.’

His wife put in, ‘What you say is true, Mother, but how could people like you and me show our faces at her door?

‘To begin with, the gatekeepers might not even take in a message. We’d only go there to be snubbed and make fools of ourselves.’

Gou-er, however, cared more for advancement and gain than anything else, and the suggestion had begun to stir him. Hearing his wife’s objection, he smiled.

‘Since you say so, Grannie, and you’ve met the lady once before, why don’t you go yourself tomorrow and see which way the wind blows?’

‘Good heavens!’ said Grannie Liu. ‘There’s a saying: “A great lord’s gate is as deep as the sea.” What sort of creature am I? The people there don’t know me. I’d be going for nothing.’

‘Never mind,’ Gou-er said, smiling. ‘I’ll tell you how to manage it. Take your grandson Ban-er, and ask first for Zhou Rui, the servant she brought with her from her own family when she married. If you see him, you may get somewhere. Zhou Rui and my father once had a piece of business together. We’re on very good terms.’

‘I know him too,’ said Grannie Liu. ‘But it’s been so long since we saw anything of him. Who knows how he is now?

‘Well, there’s no help for it. You’re a man, and with that face of yours you plainly can’t go. My daughter’s a young married woman, and she can hardly go showing herself about. I’d better risk this old face of mine and try. If anything good comes of it, we’ll all benefit. And even if I bring back no money, I’ll have seen something of the world inside a duke’s or a marquis’s gates. My life won’t have been wasted.’

They all laughed at this, and that evening settled what was to be done.

Next day Grannie Liu rose before dawn to wash and comb her hair, and gave Ban-er a few instructions. The child was only five or six and understood nothing of it. Delighted to hear that his grandmother was taking him into town for an outing, he agreed to everything. She led him into the city and found her way to Ning-Rong Street. Before the stone lions at the Rong mansion’s main gate she saw such a crowd of sedan-chairs and horses that she dared go no further. She brushed down her clothes, gave Ban-er a few more instructions, then edged towards a side gate. Several men sat on a long bench, chests thrust out and bellies prominent, gesticulating as they talked of this and that. Grannie Liu had to sidle up to them.

‘Blessings upon you, masters.’

They looked her over for a while, then asked, ‘Where are you from?’

‘I’m looking for Master Zhou, Zhou Rui, Her Ladyship’s family servant,’ she said with an ingratiating smile. ‘Would one of you masters be so kind as to ask him to come out?’

They paid no attention. At length one said, ‘Go and wait over there by the corner of the wall. Someone from his household will be coming out presently.’

An older man among them said, ‘Don’t hold her up. Why make sport of her?’ He turned to Grannie Liu. ‘Master Zhou has gone south. His home’s in the row at the back, and his wife’s in. If you want her, go round this way to the street behind and ask at the back gate.’

Grannie Liu thanked him and led Ban-er round to the back gate. Hawkers had set down their carrying-poles outside, some selling food, others toys. Twenty or thirty children were romping about in a noisy crowd. Grannie Liu caught hold of one.

‘Let me ask you, young fellow: is Mrs Zhou at home?’

‘Which Mrs Zhou?’ the children asked.

‘We’ve three Mrs Zhous here, and two Mistress Zhous besides. Which sort is yours?’

‘Zhou Rui, Her Ladyship’s family servant.’

‘That’s easy. Follow me.’

Skipping and scampering, the child led Grannie Liu through the back gate to a courtyard wall and pointed.

‘That’s where she lives.’

Then he called, ‘Mrs Zhou! An old lady’s come to see you. I’ve brought her here.’

Hearing this, Zhou Rui’s wife hurried out.

‘Who is it?’

Grannie Liu stepped forward. ‘How are you, Sister Zhou?’

Zhou Rui’s wife stared for a while before recognising her, then smiled.

‘Grannie Liu! How are you? Tell me, how many years has it been? I’ve quite forgotten. Come in and sit down.’

‘An important person like you has so much to remember,’ Grannie Liu said, smiling as she went in. ‘How could you remember us?’

Inside, Zhou Rui’s wife told a young maid she employed to pour tea.

‘How you’ve grown!’ she said to Ban-er.

After asking about various things that had happened since they last met, she turned to Grannie Liu.

‘Were you passing today, or have you come specially?’

‘I came specially to see you, Sister, and to pay my respects to Her Ladyship as well. If you could take me to see her, so much the better. If not, I’ll rely on you to pass on my respects.’

Zhou Rui’s wife had already guessed something of her purpose.

Years before, when her husband had been competing to buy a piece of land, Gou-er had given him a great deal of help. Seeing Grannie Liu arrive like this, she could hardly refuse her. Besides, she wanted to display her own standing. At these words she smiled.

‘Don’t worry, Grannie. You’ve come all this way in good faith. Surely we can’t let you go without seeing the living Buddha herself?

‘Strictly speaking, receiving visitors and announcing them is nothing to do with me. Everyone here has a particular job. My husband only looks after the rents in spring and autumn. At other times all he has to do is go out with the young masters. My job is to attend the ladies when they go out. But since you’re a relation of Her Ladyship’s, and you’ve thought enough of me to come to me for help, I’ll make an exception and take in a message for you. There’s just one thing you don’t know, Grannie. This place isn’t what it was five years ago. Her Ladyship hardly attends to business now. Mrs Lian manages the household. Do you know who Mrs Lian is? Her Ladyship’s own niece, the daughter of her eldest brother, the one they used to call Feng.’

‘So it’s her!’ Grannie Liu exclaimed in surprise. ‘Well, no wonder. I said in those days she was a fine girl. Then I’ll get to see her today as well?’

‘Of course,’ said Zhou Rui’s wife. ‘Her Ladyship has so much to trouble her now that she passes on any visitor she can, and Miss Feng sees to them all. Even if you don’t see Her Ladyship today, you must see her, or your journey will have been wasted.’

‘Blessed Buddha! I’m entirely in your hands, Sister.’

‘Don’t say that. As the saying goes, “Make things easy for others and you make them easy for yourself.” It only costs me a word. What harm can it do me?’

She sent the young maid to the front hall to find out quietly whether the meal had been laid in Grandmother Jia’s rooms. The girl went off, and the two women talked of other things.

‘Miss Feng can’t be more than twenty this year,’ said Grannie Liu. ‘To be so capable, and manage a household like this! That’s something you don’t often see.’

‘Oh, Grannie, I couldn’t begin to tell you!’ said Zhou Rui’s wife. ‘Young as she is, she can outdo anyone in the way she handles things. She’s grown into a real beauty, and has ten thousand little ways about her, at the very least. As for arguing, ten clever-tongued men couldn’t get the better of her. You’ll believe me when you see her. There’s only one thing: she’s rather too hard on the servants.’

As they talked, the young maid returned.

‘The meal has been served in Her Old Ladyship’s rooms. Mrs Lian is with Her Ladyship.’

Zhou Rui’s wife got up at once and urged Grannie Liu to hurry.

‘Quick, quick! When she comes back to have her own meal, there’ll be a moment free. Let’s get there first. If we’re late, people will be coming to report on business and it’ll be hard to get a word in. Then she’ll have her nap, and there’ll be no time at all.’

They climbed down from the kang, brushed their clothes, gave Ban-er a few more instructions and followed Zhou Rui’s wife by a winding route towards Jia Lian’s quarters.

At the front hall Zhou Rui’s wife settled Grannie Liu to wait a little, then went past the screen wall and through the courtyard gate herself. Finding that Xi-feng had not yet returned, she first sought out Patience, one of Xi-feng’s trusted senior chamber-maids, who also shared her master’s bed. Zhou Rui’s wife explained who Grannie Liu was and how she came to be there.

‘She’s come all this way today specially to pay her respects. Her Ladyship used to see her often, so we really couldn’t refuse to see her today. That’s why I’ve brought her in. When the mistress comes back, I’ll explain it all. I don’t think she’ll blame me for taking too much on myself.’

Patience made the decision.

‘Bring them in. They can sit here for the time being.’

Only then did Zhou Rui’s wife go out and bring the two of them into the courtyard. They mounted the steps of the main rooms, and a young maid lifted a scarlet felt curtain. As soon as they entered the central room, a wave of fragrance struck Grannie Liu’s face. She could not tell what scent it was and felt as though she were floating among clouds. Everything in the room glittered and shone, dazzling her eyes and making her head swim. All she could do was nod, click her tongue and invoke the Buddha.

They went into the room on the east, where Jia Lian’s little daughter, Da-jie, slept. Patience stood by the edge of the kang, looked Grannie Liu over once or twice, then greeted her and offered her a seat. Seeing Patience dressed from head to foot in fine silks, wearing gold and silver, with a face as lovely as flowers and jade, Grannie Liu took her for Xi-feng. She was just about to address her as ‘Madam’ when she heard Zhou Rui’s wife call her ‘Miss Patience’, and Patience in turn address the other woman as ‘Mrs Zhou’. Only then did she realise that this was merely a maid of some standing. Grannie Liu and Ban-er were invited up on to the kang; Patience and Zhou Rui’s wife sat facing each other on its edge. A young maid poured tea for them.

Grannie Liu heard a steady clack-clack, very like the sound of someone working a flour-sifting chest, and could not help looking this way and that. Suddenly she noticed a box hanging on a pillar in the central room. Beneath it hung something like a weight from a pair of scales, swinging ceaselessly to and fro.

‘What a curious thing!’ she thought. ‘Whatever is it for?’

As she stared, there came a clang like a metal bell or a bronze chime. Taken unawares, she blinked in fright. Eight or nine more strokes followed in quick succession.

Just as she was about to ask, the young maids all began running about, saying, ‘The mistress is back.’

Zhou Rui’s wife and Patience rose hurriedly.

‘Just wait here,’ they told Grannie Liu. ‘We’ll come and fetch you when it’s time.’

With that they went out to meet her.

Grannie Liu waited without a sound, her head tilted to listen. Laughter came from a distance, and some ten or twenty women, their clothes and skirts rustling, gradually entered the central room and went into the room on the other side. Two or three more women came into the room where she sat, carrying large lacquered food-boxes, and waited. From the other side came the command, ‘Serve the meal.’ Little by little the crowd withdrew, leaving only the few who were serving the dishes.

After a long spell of utter silence, two people appeared carrying a low kang-table and set it on the kang beside her. It was thickly covered with bowls and dishes still full of fish and meat; only a few had been touched. Ban-er clamoured for meat as soon as he saw it, and Grannie Liu slapped him away. Just then Zhou Rui’s wife came over, all smiles, and beckoned to her. Understanding the signal, Grannie Liu brought Ban-er down from the kang and into the central room. Zhou Rui’s wife whispered to her for a little while before taking her into the other room.

A soft scarlet curtain scattered with flowers hung at the doorway from chased copper hooks. Beneath the south window stood a kang spread with a strip of scarlet felt. Against the wooden partition on the east were a backrest of chain-patterned brocade and a bolster. A large seat-cushion was covered in satin shimmering gold and green; beside it stood a carved-lacquer spittoon.

Xi-feng was dressed informally in a Zhaojun hood of autumn sable, encircled by a band of clustered pearls. She wore a peach-red jacket scattered with flowers, a dark-blue silk-tapestry cloak lined with grey squirrel, and a scarlet foreign-crêpe skirt lined with ermine. Bright with powder and rouge, she sat perfectly upright, stirring the ashes in her hand-warmer with a little pair of copper tongs. Patience stood at the edge of the kang holding a small tea tray decorated with filled-in lacquer, with a little covered teacup on it. Xi-feng neither took the tea nor raised her head, but went on stirring the ashes.

‘Why haven’t they been asked in yet?’ she said slowly.

As she spoke, she lifted herself to take the tea and saw that Zhou Rui’s wife had already brought two people in. They were standing on the floor before her.

She at once made as if to rise but, before actually doing so, greeted them with a face all smiles and reproached Zhou Rui’s wife for not telling her sooner. Grannie Liu had already bowed several times from where she stood.

‘My respects to you, Madam.’

‘Sister Zhou, quick, help her up. No more bowing,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Please sit down. I’m young and don’t know all our relations. I’m not sure which generation you belong to, so I daren’t venture on a form of address.’

‘This is the grannie I was telling you about,’ Zhou Rui’s wife hastened to explain.

Xi-feng nodded. Grannie Liu was now sitting on the edge of the kang, with Ban-er hiding behind her. No amount of coaxing could persuade him to come out and bow.

‘When relations seldom visit, they drift apart,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘Those who know the circumstances say you’ve taken a dislike to us and won’t come. But spiteful people who don’t know may think we look down on everyone.’

Grannie Liu hurried to invoke the Buddha.

‘We’re badly off. We can’t afford to make visits. If we came here, we’d only bring shame on you, Madam. Even your stewards would think we looked out of place.’

‘That’s a dreadful thing to say,’ Xi-feng replied, smiling. ‘We only live on our grandfather’s empty reputation and a poorly paid official post. What has any of us got? Just an empty shell left over from the old days. As the saying goes, “Even the imperial court has three branches of poor relations.” How much more must you and I?’

She asked Zhou Rui’s wife whether she had informed Lady Wang.

‘I was waiting for your instructions, ma’am.’

‘Go and see. If she has someone with her on business, leave it. If she’s free, tell her and see what she says.’

Zhou Rui’s wife assented and went. Xi-feng had someone bring a handful of fruit for Ban-er, and had just begun to ask a few casual questions when a number of married women servants with household duties arrived to report on business. Patience announced them.

‘I have a visitor,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Let them come back this evening. If anything is very urgent, bring them in and I’ll deal with it now.’

Patience went out and presently returned.

‘I’ve asked them all. There was nothing urgent, so I sent them away.’

Xi-feng nodded. Zhou Rui’s wife came back and addressed her.

‘Her Ladyship says she has no time today. If you keep them company, ma’am, it will be just the same. She thanks them for thinking of her. If they’ve simply come for a visit, very well. If there’s anything they want to say, they should tell you. It will be just the same.’

‘There’s nothing particular to say,’ said Grannie Liu. ‘We only came to see Her Ladyship and you, Madam, as relations ought.’

‘If there’s nothing, very well,’ said Zhou Rui’s wife. ‘But if there is, you need only tell Mrs Lian. It’s the same as telling Her Ladyship.’

As she spoke, Zhou Rui’s wife gave Grannie Liu a meaningful look. Grannie Liu understood. Her face flushed before she could speak. She wanted to say nothing, but then what had she come for today? She could only swallow her shame.

‘By rights, Madam, I shouldn’t speak of it on our very first meeting. But I’ve come such a long way to see you that I can hardly leave it unsaid.’

She had got no further when the pages at the inner gate announced, ‘The young master from the Ning mansion is coming in.’

‘No need to say any more,’ Xi-feng broke in hastily. Then she asked, ‘Where is Master Rong?’

Booted footsteps sounded all the way in, and a young man of seventeen or eighteen entered. He had delicate, handsome features and a fine figure, and wore light furs, a jewelled belt, splendid clothes and an ornate cap. Grannie Liu did not know whether to sit or stand, and there was nowhere to hide.

‘Stay where you are,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘This is my nephew.’

Only then did Grannie Liu settle awkwardly on the edge of the kang.

Jia Rong said with a smile, ‘Father sent me to ask a favour, Aunt. You know that glass screen for the kang your uncle’s wife gave you last time? We’re entertaining an important visitor tomorrow. Could we borrow it to set out for the occasion? We’ll send it straight back.’

‘You’re a day too late,’ Xi-feng said. ‘I gave it away yesterday.’

Jia Rong grinned and, kneeling on one knee on the edge of the kang, pleaded with her.

‘If you won’t lend it, Aunt, he’ll say again that I don’t know how to ask properly, and I’ll get another good beating. Have pity on your nephew!’

‘As though everything from our Wang family had to be better than anyone else’s!’ Xi-feng said, laughing.

‘You have just the same things at home. You’re never content once you’ve seen mine.’

‘Nothing there is as good as this,’ Jia Rong said with a smile. ‘Do be merciful!’

‘If you so much as chip it, look out for your skin!’

She told Patience to get the key to the upstairs rooms and send a few reliable people to carry it over. Jia Rong’s whole face lit up.

‘I’ll take the men myself. I won’t let them knock it about.’

He got up and went out.

Something else suddenly occurred to Xi-feng.

‘Rong, come back!’ she called through the window.

Several voices outside took up the call. ‘Master Rong, come back at once!’

Jia Rong hurried back and stood with his hands at his sides, waiting for her instructions.

Xi-feng merely went on sipping her tea, lost in thought for a long while. Then she smiled.

‘Never mind. Off you go. Come after supper, and we’ll talk then. I have someone here now, and I haven’t the energy.’

Jia Rong assented and slowly withdrew.

Only now did Grannie Liu recover her composure and begin again.

‘I’ve brought your nephew today for nothing else but this: his father and mother at home haven’t even anything to eat. And now it’s turned cold. However much we think, we can see no way out. So I had to bring your nephew and come to you.’

She nudged Ban-er.

‘What did your dad tell you at home? What did he send us here for? All you think about is eating fruit.’

Xi-feng had long since understood. Hearing how poorly she put it, she stopped her with a smile.

‘There’s no need to say more. I understand.’

Then she asked Zhou Rui’s wife, ‘Has Grannie had her breakfast, do you know?’

‘We set off for here first thing,’ Grannie Liu answered hurriedly. ‘When would we have had time to eat?’

Xi-feng at once ordered food to be brought.

Presently Zhou Rui’s wife had a meal for the visitors laid in the room on the east and came to take Grannie Liu and Ban-er in to eat.

‘Sister Zhou, see that they have plenty,’ Xi-feng said. ‘I can’t join them.’

They went into the east room. Xi-feng called Zhou Rui’s wife back and asked what Lady Wang had said when she reported to her.

‘Her Ladyship said they weren’t really of the same family. They only happened to share a surname, and their old gentleman had held office alongside her father in those days, so they’d casually claimed kinship. They haven’t visited much these last few years. When they came that time, though, they were not sent away empty-handed. It’s kind of them to come and see us today, and we mustn’t slight them. If there’s anything they want to say, you’re to decide what’s best, ma’am.’

‘That explains it,’ said Xi-feng. ‘I wondered how they could be relations without my having the faintest idea who they were.’

While they were talking, Grannie Liu finished her meal and came back leading Ban-er, licking and smacking her lips as she poured out her thanks.

‘Please sit down for a moment,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘Let me tell you something. I understand what you meant just now.

‘As relations, we really ought to have looked after you without waiting for you to come to our door. But there are so many things to attend to in this household, and Her Ladyship is getting on. It’s quite possible for something to slip her mind. As for me, I’ve only lately taken over some of the business, and I didn’t even know we had these relations. Besides, for all the grand show we make outside, a large household has difficulties of its own. People might not believe it if we told them. Still, you’ve come such a long way today, and it’s the first time you’ve asked me for anything. How could I let you go back empty-handed? It so happens that yesterday Her Ladyship gave me twenty taels of silver for clothes for my maids. I haven’t touched it yet. If you don’t think it too little, take that for the time being.’

When Grannie Liu first heard her speak of difficulties, she thought nothing was coming and her heart began to thump. Then she heard that she was to have twenty taels, and joy set her itching all over.

‘Ah, I know what difficulties are. But as they say, “Even a camel starved to death is bigger than a horse.” Whatever happens, a single hair plucked from your body is thicker than our waists!’

Finding her language coarse, Zhou Rui’s wife kept signalling with her eyes for her to stop.

Xi-feng saw, smiled and took no notice. She simply told Patience to fetch yesterday’s packet of silver and a string of cash as well, and bring them to Grannie Liu.

‘Here are twenty taels of silver. Let them provide a winter coat for the child for now. If you won’t take them, I shall know you’re displeased with me.

‘Use this cash to hire a carriage. Come again some day when you have nothing to do. That’s how relations ought to behave. It’s getting late, so I won’t keep you just for form’s sake. Give my greetings to everyone at home.’

As she spoke, she stood up.

Grannie Liu thanked her a thousand times, took the silver and cash, and followed Zhou Rui’s wife outside. Only then did Zhou Rui’s wife speak.

‘Good heavens, Grannie! Why did you forget how to talk as soon as you saw her? The first words out of your mouth were “your nephew”.

‘Don’t be offended by what I say, but even if he were her own nephew, you ought to have put it more gently. Master Rong is her proper nephew. Where did this other nephew spring from?’

‘Oh, Sister,’ Grannie Liu said, laughing, ‘I liked her so much the moment I saw her, I couldn’t find the words.’

Talking together, they went back to Zhou Rui’s home and sat for a little while. Grannie Liu wanted to leave a piece of silver for the children to buy fruit. Zhou Rui’s wife thought nothing of such a sum and firmly refused it. Grannie Liu could not thank her enough, and left again by the back gate. As the saying goes:

When fortune is at its height, a helping hand comes easily;

A deeply felt kindness binds more closely than kin or friends.
