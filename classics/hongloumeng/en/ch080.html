Chapter 80 Fair Caltrop suffers unjustly under a lustful husband’s cudgel; The Taoist Wang invents a cure for jealous wives

Chapter end

At this Jin-gui twisted her neck, curled her lip and gave two snorts through her nose. Clapping her hands, she laughed coldly.

‘Who ever heard of fragrant caltrop flowers? If you call those fragrant, where does that leave flowers with a proper scent? What utter nonsense!’

‘Not only caltrop flowers,’ Caltrop said. ‘Even lotus leaves and seed-pods have a fresh fragrance of their own. Of course it isn’t like the scent of flowers. But if you take the trouble to notice it on a still day or a quiet night, early in the morning or at midnight, it smells sweeter than any flower. Even caltrops, gorgon fruit, reed leaves and reed roots, after the wind and dew have touched them, have a freshness that clears your head and lifts your spirits.’

‘So according to you, orchids and osmanthus don’t smell nearly so nice?’

Carried away by her subject, Caltrop forgot the taboo and answered at once.

‘But the scent of orchids and osmanthus is something no other flower can compare with.’

Before she had finished, Jin-gui’s maid, whose name was Bao-chan, pointed at her face.

‘You’ll be the death of yourself!

‘How dare you actually say our young lady’s name!’

Caltrop suddenly remembered, and was overcome with embarrassment. Smiling apologetically, she hurried to make amends.

‘It slipped out, ma’am. Please don’t be angry.’

‘What does that matter?’ said Jin-gui, smiling. ‘You’re far too careful. But I have been thinking about the xiang in your own name, the part that means “fragrance”. It really isn’t suitable. I should like to replace it with another character. Would you agree to that?’

‘How can you ask, ma’am?’ Caltrop answered with a hasty smile. ‘My whole body belongs to you now. To ask whether I agree to a change of name is more consideration than I deserve. Whichever character you think best will do.’

‘That is all very well,’ Jin-gui said, ‘but I’m afraid Miss Bao-chai might take offence. “So the name I gave her isn’t good enough for you? You’ve scarcely arrived, and already you’re finding fault with what I’ve done!”’

‘There’s something you don’t know, ma’am,’ said Caltrop. ‘When I was first bought, I was meant to serve the old mistress. That was why Miss Bao-chai named me.

‘Once I began attending the master, I had nothing more to do with her. Now that you’re here, ma’am, there’s even less reason for her to concern herself with me. Besides, she’s far too sensible to mind a thing like that.’

‘In that case,’ said Jin-gui, ‘qiu, “autumn”, would certainly be better than xiang, “fragrance”. Caltrops and their flowers flourish in autumn. Surely that gives the name more reason behind it.’

‘Whatever you say, ma’am,’ Caltrop replied.

The change from xiang, ‘fragrance’, to qiu, ‘autumn’, was duly made. Under her new mistress, Caltrop was to answer to Lily1. Bao-chai took no exception to the change.

Xue Pan was by nature one of those who, having won Long, must reach out for Shu. Now that he had married Jin-gui, he noticed that her maid Bao-chan was rather pretty too, with a flighty manner he found attractive. He was constantly asking her for tea or water, deliberately teasing her. Bao-chan understood him well enough, but she was afraid of Jin-gui and dared not take liberties. She waited to see how her mistress would look upon it.

Jin-gui had a fair idea of what was going on. ‘I’ve been looking for an opening against Caltrop,’ she thought. ‘Since he’s taken a fancy to Bao-chan, I may as well give her up to him for now. He’s sure to neglect Caltrop then, and while he’s neglecting her I can deal with her. Afterwards, Bao-chan is my own girl: she’ll be easier to manage.’

Her plan settled, she waited for her chance.

One evening, when he had been drinking a little, Xue Pan again sent Bao-chan to pour him some tea. As he took the cup he deliberately squeezed her hand. She made a show of dodging him and snatched it away. Between them they lost their hold: the cup fell with a crash, splashing tea over clothes and floor. Embarrassed, Xue Pan pretended she had not held it properly.

‘You didn’t take it properly, sir,’ said Bao-chan.

‘That’s enough play-acting from the pair of you,’ Jin-gui said with a cold laugh. ‘Don’t imagine other people are fools.’

Xue Pan lowered his head and smiled without speaking. Bao-chan went out with a red face. When bedtime came, Jin-gui deliberately tried to send Xue Pan away to sleep elsewhere.

‘Then I shan’t have to look at those starving, greedy eyes.’

He only laughed.

‘If you want something, tell me,’ she said. ‘What’s the use of sneaking about?’

With wine to hide his blushes, Xue Pan seized his opportunity. He knelt on the quilt, caught hold of Jin-gui and smiled.

‘My sweet sister, if you’ll give me Bao-chan, you can do whatever you like.

‘If you asked for human brains, I’d get them for you.’

‘What nonsense!’ Jin-gui said, laughing. ‘If there’s someone you fancy, say so and take her into your room. It will spare other people the sight of your unseemly carryings-on. What could I possibly want?’

Delighted to have her consent, Xue Pan could not thank her enough. That night he did everything a husband could to please Jin-gui. Next day he stayed at home, pestering and coaxing, and grew bolder still.

After noon Jin-gui deliberately went out, leaving the two of them an opportunity. Xue Pan began pulling Bao-chan towards him. She had guessed well enough what was intended and half resisted, half yielded. They were just about to reach harbour.

But Jin-gui was deliberately waiting for this. Judging that they must now be too engrossed to draw apart, she sent for a little maid called Xiao-she. This child too had served her at home from an early age. Both her parents had died when she was small, leaving no one to look after her, and everyone had taken to calling her Xiao-she. She was employed chiefly on rough work. Jin-gui had now singled her out for a purpose.

‘Go and tell Lily to fetch my handkerchief from my room. You needn’t say I sent you.’

Xiao-she went straight to Caltrop.

‘Miss Caltrop, the mistress has left her handkerchief in her room. Wouldn’t it be a good idea to fetch it and take it to her?’

Caltrop had lately been subjected to one rebuff after another from Jin-gui. She could not understand why, and was doing everything in her power to recover her favour. At these words she hurried to the room for the handkerchief.

Quite unprepared, she blundered in upon them just as one was pressing and the other yielding. Her own face and ears flushed scarlet with shame. She turned and could not get away quickly enough.

Xue Pan considered that everything had been openly authorised. Apart from Jin-gui he had no one to fear, and had not even closed the door. Though a little embarrassed at Caltrop’s intrusion, he did not take it very much to heart. Bao-chan, however, was always quick to boast and determined to have the upper hand. Caught by Caltrop now, she wished the ground would open and swallow her. She pushed Xue Pan away and ran straight out, still pouring out angry complaints accusing him of rape and coercion.

Xue Pan had only just coaxed and wheedled her within his grasp when Caltrop broke it all up. His eager excitement turned to fury, all directed at Caltrop. Giving her no chance to explain, he came after her, spat twice and cursed her.

‘You damned whore! What brings you haunting the place at a time like this?’

Caltrop saw that trouble was coming and had already fled in a few quick steps. When Xue Pan went back to find Bao-chan, she had vanished. He could only go on cursing Caltrop in his rage.

By the end of supper he was thoroughly tipsy. When he went to bathe, the water was a little too hot and unexpectedly scalded his foot. He declared that Caltrop had meant to hurt him. Stark naked, he chased her and dealt her a couple of kicks and blows. Caltrop had never suffered such bitter humiliation before, but now that things had come to this there was nothing she could say. She could only grieve and lament her lot, and they went their separate ways.

Meanwhile Jin-gui had secretly come to an understanding with Bao-chan. Xue Pan was to spend that night with Bao-chan in Caltrop’s room and make her his concubine; Caltrop was ordered to come and sleep with Jin-gui. At first Caltrop was unwilling. Jin-gui accused her of finding her dirty, then of wanting an easy life and being afraid of having to wait on her during the night.

‘That master of yours has never seen anything of the world!’ she scolded. ‘Every woman he sees, he falls in love with. He takes possession of my girl and won’t even let you come to me. What is he trying to do? Drive me to my death, I suppose!’

Xue Pan heard this and, afraid that his affair with Bao-chan might come to nothing after all, hurried over to curse Caltrop again.

‘You don’t know when you’re well treated! Go at once, or I’ll beat you!’

Caltrop had no choice but to bring her bedding. Jin-gui told her to spread it on the floor, and again she had to obey. No sooner had she lain down than she was called to pour tea; next she must come and pummel Jin-gui’s legs. Seven or eight times that night she was summoned, never allowed a moment’s comfortable rest. Xue Pan, having obtained Bao-chan, prized her like a rare treasure and paid no attention to anything else.

Jin-gui seethed in secret. ‘Enjoy yourselves for a few days,’ she thought. ‘Wait till I’ve worked out how to deal with you. Don’t blame me then!’

While restraining herself, she continued plotting against Caltrop. After about half a month she suddenly began pretending to be ill, complaining of unbearable pain in her heart and an inability to move any of her limbs. Doctors were called, but their treatment did no good. Everyone said that Caltrop had upset her into this condition.

After two days of commotion, a paper effigy was suddenly shaken out of Jin-gui’s pillow. On it were written the eight characters of her birth date and hour. Five needles had been stuck into its heart and the joints of its four limbs. This threw everyone into greater confusion. The discovery was treated as sensational news and reported first to Aunt Xue. She immediately lost her head; naturally Xue Pan was more frantic still and proposed at once to beat everyone into a confession.

‘Why punish innocent people?’ said Jin-gui, smiling. ‘Most likely it’s some curse Bao-chan has worked.’

‘She’s scarcely had a moment to spend in your room lately,’ said Xue Pan. ‘Why blame someone innocent?’

‘Who else could it be?’ Jin-gui asked with a cold laugh. ‘Surely not myself!

‘There may be other people, but who would dare come into my room?’

‘Caltrop is with you every day now,’ said Xue Pan. ‘She’s bound to know. Question her with a beating first, and we’ll find out.’

Jin-gui laughed coldly. ‘Whoever you beat, would they admit it? If you ask me, we’d better pretend we know nothing and wash our hands of it. It doesn’t matter if they kill me. You can marry someone better, and welcome. If you’d speak honestly, the three of you simply find one of me too many.’

She burst into bitter sobs. Her words inflamed Xue Pan still further. Snatching up a door-bar, he rushed straight off to find Caltrop and, giving her no chance to speak, rained blows on her head and face, insisting that she had worked the curse.

Caltrop cried out that she was innocent. Aunt Xue ran up and shouted at him to stop.

‘You haven’t even found out what happened, and already you’re beating her! She’s served you all these years. When has she ever failed to be attentive or do her utmost? Why should she do such a wicked thing now?

‘Find out the rights and wrongs of it before you start using force!’

Hearing her mother-in-law speak like this, Jin-gui feared that Xue Pan would listen and change his mind. She began wailing more loudly than ever.

‘For over half a month you’ve kept my Bao-chan to yourself and wouldn’t let her come into my room. Only Lily has slept with me. I wanted to beat the truth out of Bao-chan and you were the first to protect her. Now you go off in a temper to beat the other one! Kill me and choose a rich, pretty wife in my place. Why go through all these tricks?’

These words made Xue Pan more frantic still. Aunt Xue heard the way Jin-gui used every sentence to hold her son in subjection, with one outrageous accusation after another, and hated her for it. Unfortunately her son had no backbone. He had grown used to submitting to his wife’s control. Now he had become involved with the maid, she called it taking her by force, while claiming for herself the credit of a gentle wife who let others share her husband. As for the harmful magic, no one knew who had really worked it. As the saying has it, even an honest magistrate cannot judge a family’s quarrels; here it was indeed beyond a parent to judge what went on between husband and wife.

With nothing else to be done, Aunt Xue turned in anger on Xue Pan.

‘You worthless wretch! A randy dog has more decency than you! Who would have thought you’d be fumbling at the maid your wife brought with her before anyone knew a thing about it? Now she can say you’ve taken her girl by force. How can you show your face outside? No one knows who did this, and without asking a single question you start beating people regardless. I know what you are: get something new and you throw the old away. All the care I took for you then was wasted! Even if she is no good, you’re not to beat her. I’ll send for a dealer this minute and sell her. Then your mind will be at rest.’

‘Pack your things and come with me,’ she told Caltrop. Then she sent someone off with orders: ‘Fetch a dealer at once. Let her go for whatever few taels she brings. We’ll pull out the thorn in the flesh, the nail in the eye, and everyone can live in peace.’

Seeing how angry his mother was, Xue Pan had already hung his head. But at these words Jin-gui cried out through the window.

‘Sell her if you like, Mother. There’s no need to drag one person in when you’re talking about another.

‘Am I supposed to be some jealous, sour creature who can’t bear anyone else in the house? What do you mean by “the thorn in the flesh, the nail in the eye”? Whose thorn? Whose nail? If I resented her in the least, would I have let him take my own maid into his room as well?’

Aunt Xue shook and choked with anger.

‘What sort of household manners are these? A mother-in-law speaks here and her daughter-in-law answers back through the window! And you call yourself the daughter of an old family! Shouting and bellowing at the top of your voice. What language is this?’

‘Stop, oh, stop!’ Xue Pan stamped in distress. ‘People will hear and laugh at us!’

Jin-gui decided that, having begun, she might as well go the whole way. She shouted still more wildly.

‘Let them laugh! That little concubine of yours bewitches me and tries to kill me, and I’m to worry about people laughing! Or else keep her and sell me. Everyone knows how rich you Xues are. Every time anything happens you use your money to grind people down, and your fine relations to bully them. You’d better get on with it. What are you waiting for?

‘If I’m no good, what made you come running to our house, blind as bats, begging and pleading? Now you’ve got the woman, and the gold and silver that came with her, and you’ve seized anyone with the least bit of a face as well. Time to squeeze me out!’

She rolled and writhed as she screamed and sobbed, striking herself. In his distress Xue Pan could neither reason with her nor soothe her, neither beat her nor beg her. He could only go in and out sighing, complaining of his bad luck.

Bao-chai had already persuaded Aunt Xue to come away indoors. Her mother merely sent orders that Caltrop was to be sold.

‘Our family has always bought people,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘I’ve never heard of our selling them. Anger must have muddled you, Mother. If anyone heard of it, wouldn’t we be laughed at? If my brother and his wife think her no good, leave her to wait on me. I’m short of someone myself.’

‘Keeping her will only mean more trouble,’ said Aunt Xue. ‘Better be rid of her altogether.’

‘It will come to the same thing if she’s with me,’ Bao-chai said, still smiling. ‘She won’t be allowed to go out to the front rooms.

‘She’ll have nothing more to do with that part of the house. It will be just as if she’d been sold.’

Caltrop had already run to Aunt Xue, weeping bitterly and pleading with her. She begged not to be sent away and offered to serve Miss Bao-chai. Aunt Xue could only abandon her intention.

From then on Caltrop did indeed go to Bao-chai and determinedly cut off all contact with the front rooms. Even so, she could not help grieving under the moon and sighing as she trimmed her lamp. She had always been frail. Though she had spent several years in Xue Pan’s room, a disorder of the blood had prevented her from conceiving. Now anger and sorrow were added to it; the strain, within and without, was more than she could bear, and a blood-depletion illness developed. Day by day she grew thinner and more feverish, with little appetite for food or drink. Doctors were called, but neither their consultations nor their medicines brought relief.

Jin-gui meanwhile created several more disturbances, leaving Aunt Xue and her daughter to shed tears in private and lament their fate. Two or three times Xue Pan had found courage in drink and stood up to her. If he raised a stick to beat her, she offered her body for him to strike as he pleased; if he took up a knife to kill her, she stretched out her neck. He could not actually bring himself to do it. After a wild scene he would have to let it drop. By now habit had made this the natural order: Jin-gui grew ever more imperious, Xue Pan ever less spirited. Caltrop was still there, but might as well not have been. It was not quite all Jin-gui could have wished, yet Caltrop no longer offended her eyes, and for the time being she left her alone.

Gradually she began picking on Bao-chan instead. But Bao-chan was not at all like Caltrop. She was dry tinder to a flame. Now that she and Xue Pan were of one mind, she had put Jin-gui out of hers. When her mistress began ill-treating her, she would not humble herself or give way an inch. At first they snapped and quarrelled; then Jin-gui’s temper rose to abuse and finally to blows. Bao-chan did not dare answer the abuse or strike back. Instead she abandoned herself to a wild tantrum, tossing her head and rolling about, threatening to kill herself. By day it was knives and scissors, by night ropes: there was no end to her carryings-on.

Xue Pan could not look after both women at once. He hovered between them, watching which way things would go. When the uproar became impossible, he went out and hid himself elsewhere. In her calmer, happier moments, Jin-gui gathered people round her to play cards and throw dice. She had always had a particular liking for gnawing bones. Every day chickens or ducks had to be killed; she gave the meat away to other people and kept only the bones, fried crisp in oil, to nibble with her wine. If she grew tired of eating or lost her temper, she let fly with a torrent of foul language.

‘Other bastards and whores can have their fun. Why shouldn’t I?’

Aunt Xue and Bao-chai simply took no notice of her.

Xue Pan could find no way out either. Day and night he could only regret having married this star of household calamity. For the time being none of them knew what to do. Soon there was no one, high or low, in either the Ning or the Rong household who did not know of it, and no one who did not sigh over it.

By now Bao-yu’s hundred days were over and he was going out again. He had been to see Jin-gui too.

‘There’s nothing fierce or strange about her appearance or her manner,’ he thought. ‘She’s a fresh flower, a tender willow, no different from my sisters and cousins. How can she have such a disposition? It’s the strangest thing imaginable.’

It troubled him. One day, when he went to pay his respects to Lady Wang, he found Ying-chun’s nurse there on a similar errand. She was saying how badly Sun Shao-zu behaved.

‘Miss Ying-chun does nothing but cry in secret and wipe away her tears. If only you could bring her home for a couple of days to take her mind off things.’

‘I’ve been meaning to send for her these last few days,’ Lady Wang said. ‘But one thing after another has gone wrong, and I forgot. Bao-yu went to see her the other day and told me about it when he came back. Tomorrow is an auspicious day. We’ll have her brought over.’

While they were talking, Grandmother Jia sent someone to find Bao-yu.

‘Tomorrow morning, first thing, you’re to go to Tianqi Temple to fulfil a vow.’

Bao-yu was longing for any chance to get out and about. The news delighted him so much that he did not close his eyes all night. Would morning never come?

Early next morning, washed, combed and dressed, he set out by carriage with two or three old nurses for Tianqi Temple outside the west gate of the city, to burn incense and fulfil the vow. Everything there had been made ready the day before.

Bao-yu was timid by nature and dared not approach grimacing images of gods and demons. Built under the previous dynasty, Tianqi Temple was a vast and imposing place, but after so many years it was very desolate. The clay images inside were all ferocious. He hurried through the burning of paper horses, money and provisions and withdrew to rest in the priests’ quarters.

After a meal, the nurses, Li Gui and the others accompanied him on a leisurely stroll round the grounds. When he grew tired he returned to the quiet room to rest. The nurses were afraid he would fall asleep, so they invited the old Taoist Wang, who was in charge of the temple, to come and talk to him. This old Wang specialised in peddling medicines, making money by treating people with outlandish remedies. There was a sign outside the temple at that very moment, advertising pills, powders, plasters and elixirs of every sort. He was also a frequent and familiar visitor to the Ning and Rong houses. Everyone there called him Wang One-Patch, a nickname implying that his plasters were so efficacious that a single application cured every ill.

When Wang One-Patch came in, Bao-yu was reclining on the kang, feeling sleepy. Li Gui and the others were trying to keep him amused.

‘Don’t go to sleep, Master Bao!’

They laughed at the sight of Wang One-Patch.

‘Just the man! Master Wang, you’re so good at old stories. Tell our young master one.’

‘Exactly,’ said Wang One-Patch with a smile. ‘Don’t go to sleep, young master. The little sinews in your belly might start making mischief.’

Everyone in the room laughed. Bao-yu too smiled, sat up and straightened his clothes. Wang One-Patch called to his disciples to make some good strong tea at once.

‘Our master doesn’t drink your tea,’ Tealeaf said. ‘He doesn’t even like sitting in this room. It smells of plasters.’

‘You young scamp!’ Wang One-Patch said, laughing. ‘The plasters never come into this room. I knew the young master was sure to be here today, so for three or five days I’ve had it scented with incense over and over again.’

‘That reminds me,’ said Bao-yu. ‘I keep hearing how good your plasters are. What exactly do they cure?’

‘If you want to know about my plasters, young master, that’s a long story. The fine points of the matter aren’t something I can explain in a word.

‘They contain a hundred and twenty ingredients, sovereign and minister in proper accord, visiting guests properly accommodated, warming and cooling drugs together, different prescriptions for rich and poor. Internally, they regulate the vital essence, replenish the qi, improve the appetite, nourish the sustaining and defending forces, soothe the spirit, calm the mind, drive out cold and heat, and dissolve undigested food and phlegm. Externally, they harmonise the blood vessels, loosen the sinews and channels, remove dead tissue, grow new flesh, expel wind and disperse poisons. Their effect is miraculous. Anyone who’s worn one knows.’

‘I don’t believe one plaster can cure all those illnesses,’ Bao-yu said. ‘Let me ask you about one particular complaint. Could your plaster cure that too?’

‘A hundred illnesses, a thousand afflictions: it works at once on every one. If it doesn’t, you may pull my beard and slap this old face. Pull my temple down, if you like! Just tell me what the trouble is.’

‘Guess,’ said Bao-yu, smiling. ‘If you can guess, you’ll be able to cure it.’

Wang One-Patch considered for a moment, then smiled.

‘That’s rather hard to guess. I’m afraid my plaster may not work so well after all.’

Bao-yu told Li Gui and the others, ‘Go outside and walk about for a while. With all these people in here, the room’s getting quite steamy and foul.’

Li Gui and the others went out to amuse themselves, leaving only Tealeaf. He held a burning stick of Sweet-dream incense. Bao-yu told him to sit beside him and leaned against him. Something occurred to Wang One-Patch. He came closer, all smiles, and spoke softly.

‘I think I’ve guessed. Has the young master begun to have affairs of the bedchamber? Is it something strengthening you want?’

Before he had finished, Tealeaf shouted, ‘You ought to be slapped for that, you old rascal!’

Bao-yu had not yet understood.

‘What did he say?’ he asked quickly.

‘Don’t listen to his nonsense,’ said Tealeaf.

Frightened, Wang One-Patch dared ask no more.

‘Tell me plainly, young master.’

‘What I want to know is, have you a plaster for women’s jealousy?’

Wang One-Patch clapped his hands and laughed.

‘Well, that settles it! Not only have I no prescription: I’ve never even heard of one.’

‘And you call that much of a remedy!’ said Bao-yu, smiling.

Wang One-Patch hurried to add, ‘I’ve never come across a plaster for jealousy, but there is a decoction that might cure it. It takes a little time, though. The effect won’t be as immediate as the shadow cast when you stand a pole upright.’

‘What decoction? How is it taken?’

‘It’s called Jealousy-curing Soup. Take one of the very best autumn pears, two mace of rock sugar, one mace of dried tangerine peel and three bowls of water. Cook it till the pear is done. Eat one of these pears early every morning. Keep on eating them and you’ll get better.’

‘That wouldn’t cost much,’ Bao-yu said. ‘I’m only afraid it wouldn’t work.’

‘If one dose doesn’t work, take ten,’ said Wang One-Patch. ‘If it doesn’t work today, take it again tomorrow. If it doesn’t work this year, go on taking it next year. In any case, all three ingredients moisten the lungs and improve the appetite without doing any harm. It’s nice and sweet, it stops a cough, and it tastes good.

‘Keep eating it till you’re over a hundred. Everyone has to die eventually, and once you’re dead, what is there to be jealous of? That’s when it works!’

Bao-yu and Tealeaf laughed and laughed, calling him a slick-tongued old ox-head.

‘It’s only idle talk to keep off the afternoon drowsiness,’ Wang One-Patch said with a smile. ‘Where’s the harm? If it makes you laugh, it’s worth something. To tell you the truth, even the plasters are a fraud. If I had real medicine, I’d take it myself and become an immortal. If I had anything genuine, would I come here to scrape a living?’

While they were talking, the auspicious hour arrived. Bao-yu was invited out to burn the paper money and provisions and distribute the blessings. The rites completed, he returned through the city gates and went home.

Ying-chun had been home for quite some time. The married women and other female attendants from the Sun household had been given supper and sent back. Now, in Lady Wang’s room, Ying-chun was weeping out the story of her sufferings. Sun Shao-zu, she said, was interested in nothing but women, gambling and drink.

‘He’s had his way with almost every married woman and maid in the house. Two or three times I tried to remonstrate, just a little, and he called me “something wrung out of a vinegar-soaked wife”. He says Father took five thousand taels of his silver and had no business spending it. He’s asked for it back two or three times without getting it. Then he points straight in my face and says, “Don’t put on your airs of mistress and wife with me. Your father spent five thousand taels of mine and sold you to me in payment. Give me any trouble and I’ll beat you and send you to sleep in the servants’ quarters.

‘“In your grandfather’s day, your family came running after ours, hoping to share in our wealth and standing. By rights I belong to your father’s generation. Now he’s forced me down a peg and sold me into the generation below. I ought never to have made this marriage. For no reason at all, I’ve been made to look like a fortune-hunter.”’

She sobbed as she spoke. Lady Wang and all the girls wept too. Lady Wang could only try to comfort her.

‘Now you’ve fallen in with such an unreasonable man, what can be done? At the time your uncle did try to persuade your father not to make this marriage. But he was determined and wouldn’t listen. He had set his heart on it, and now it has turned out badly after all. My child, this too is your fate.’

‘I can’t believe my fate should be so cruel!’ Ying-chun cried. ‘I lost my mother when I was little. Luckily I came over here to you, Aunt, and had a few peaceful years. And now it has all ended like this!’

Lady Wang went on trying to comfort her, and asked where she would like to stay.

‘Being parted from the girls so suddenly, I think of them in my sleep and dream of them all the time,’ Ying-chun said. ‘And I keep thinking of my rooms. If I could stay three or five days in my old place in the garden, I could die content. I don’t know if I’ll ever be able to stay there again.’

‘You mustn’t talk such nonsense,’ Lady Wang said hastily. ‘Young married couples quarrel. It happens to everyone. Why say such unlucky things?’

She sent people at once to put the rooms at Amaryllis Eyot in order, told the girls to keep Ying-chun company and cheer her, and warned Bao-yu:

‘You’re not to let a word of this reach your grandmother. If she hears anything about it, I shall know it came from you.’

Bao-yu obediently assented. That evening Ying-chun slept in her old rooms, and all the girls were more affectionate than ever. She stayed three days before going over to Lady Xing. First she took leave of Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang, then parted from the girls. All were heartbroken and could scarcely bear to let her go.

Only with the consolations and persuasion of Lady Wang, Aunt Xue and the others did they at last restrain themselves, and she went across. She had spent another two days with Lady Xing when Sun Shao-zu sent people to fetch her. Ying-chun did not want to go, but she feared his violence. She could only master her feelings and reluctantly take her leave. Lady Xing cared little about it. She asked neither whether husband and wife were happy together nor what troubles there might be in the household, merely going through the motions for appearance’s sake.
