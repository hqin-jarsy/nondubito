Chapter 23 Fine lines from The Western Chamber become words of teasing; Passionate songs from The Peony Pavilion awaken a girl’s heart

Chapter end

After Jia Yuan-chun returned to the palace from her visit to Prospect Garden, she ordered Tan-chun to make a careful copy, in sequence, of all the poems and inscriptions composed that day. She herself arranged them and assessed their merits, and ordered them to be engraved on stone in the garden, to commemorate this elegant occasion for ages to come. Jia Zheng therefore had skilled and celebrated craftsmen sought out everywhere to dress the stones and cut the inscriptions. Jia Zhen supervised the work with Rong, Ping and the others. Jia Qiang was also responsible for the twelve actresses, including Wen-guan, and their costumes and other theatrical equipment, and could not readily spare the time, so Jia Zhen summoned Jia Chang and Jia Ling to help supervise. One day the hot wax was applied and the lettering transferred in vermilion: work was under way. Of this we need say no more.

Now the twelve young Taoists of the Temple of the Jade Emperor and the twelve Buddhist novices of the Bodhidharma Hermitage had been moved out of Prospect Garden. Jia Zheng was intending to send them to live in various temples. It happened that Jia Qin’s mother, Mrs Zhou, who lived in the back street, had been considering how to obtain some charge, however small, for her son from Jia Zheng, so that he might make a little money to spend. Hearing of this matter, she came by sedan-chair to ask Xi-feng’s help. As she was not generally given to putting on airs, Xi-feng agreed. Having thought out what to say, she reported to Lady Wang:

‘We really mustn’t send these young monks and Taoists away. Her Highness may come out at any time and want them to perform their services. If we scatter them, it will only mean more trouble when they’re needed again. I think we should simply send them to our family temple, the Temple of the Iron Threshold. All we need do is send someone there once a month with a few taels for firewood and rice. The moment they’re wanted, we can go and fetch them. No trouble at all.’

Lady Wang heard her out and consulted Jia Zheng.

‘That reminds me,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘Yes, that is what we shall do.’

He sent for Jia Lian at once. Jia Lian was having a meal with Xi-feng when the summons came. Not knowing what it was about, he left his meal and was going off when Xi-feng caught hold of him.

‘Wait a moment and listen,’ she said, smiling. ‘If it’s anything else, I have nothing to say. But if it’s about the little monks, you must do as I tell you.’

She coached him in what he was to say.

‘I know nothing about it,’ Jia Lian said with a laugh. ‘If you’re so clever, go and tell him yourself.’

Xi-feng stiffened her neck, laid down her chopsticks and looked at him with a smile that was not quite a smile.

‘Do you mean that, or are you joking?’

Jia Lian laughed.

‘Yun, the son of Fifth Sister-in-law over by the west gallery, has asked me two or three times to find him something to manage. I agreed and told him to wait. At last something comes along, and you snatch it away.’

‘Don’t worry,’ said Xi-feng, smiling. ‘Her Highness has said she wants a great many more pines and cypresses planted in the north-east corner of the garden, and flowers and other plants below the tower. When that work comes up, I promise Yun shall have charge of it.’

‘Very well, if that’s how it is. Only last night I wanted to try things a different way, and you made such a fuss about it.’

Xi-feng gave a snort of laughter, spat at him, lowered her head and went on with her meal.

Jia Lian had already gone off laughing. When he reached the front and saw Jia Zheng, it was indeed about the young monks. He followed Xi-feng’s plan.

‘Qin seems to have come on remarkably well lately. Why not leave this in his charge? We can simply let him draw the monthly allowance at the same rate as when they were here.’

Jia Zheng had never paid much attention to such matters, and agreed to Jia Lian’s proposal. Back in their room, Jia Lian told Xi-feng, who immediately sent word to Mrs Zhou. Jia Qin came to see the couple and could not thank them enough.

Xi-feng did him another favour by coaxing Jia Lian to advance three months’ allowance. She had Jia Qin write out a receipt; Jia Lian approved the payment warrant and signed it, and an authorisation tally was sent out at once. The silver treasury paid out the three months’ allowance in full: two or three hundred taels of gleaming silver.

Jia Qin picked up a piece at random and tossed it to the men at the scales for their tea. He told a page to carry the money home and went to consult his mother. He promptly hired a large male donkey for himself to ride and several carts. At a side gate of the Rong mansion he called out the twenty-four young clerics, seated them in the carts, and set off straight for the Temple of the Iron Threshold outside the city. Nothing further need be said of this for the moment.

Let us return to Jia Yuan-chun. After arranging the Prospect Garden poems in the palace, she suddenly thought of the garden’s lovely scenery. Now that her visit was over, Jia Zheng would surely lock it up with reverent care and dare not let anyone disturb it. How desolate it would be! She had several sisters and cousins at home who could write both poetry and rhyme-prose. Why not tell them to live there, so that the girls need not languish in neglect, nor the flowers and willows lose their charm? Then she thought of Bao-yu. He had grown up among the girls, unlike his brothers and cousins. If she did not include him, he might feel lonely and become unhappy, which would only worry Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang. The best thing would be to tell him to live in the garden too. Having settled this in her mind, she sent the eunuch Xia Shou-zhong to the Rong mansion with an edict: Bao-chai and the other girls were to be free to live in the garden, with no prohibitions or locked gates to keep them out, and Bao-yu was to accompany them there to pursue his studies.

Jia Zheng and Lady Wang received the edict. After Xia Shou-zhong had left, they informed Grandmother Jia and sent people into the garden to clean and put the rooms in order, hanging curtains and setting up beds with their bed-hangings. The others heard the news calmly enough, but Bao-yu was beside himself with delight. He was discussing arrangements with Grandmother Jia, wanting this and asking for that, when a maid suddenly came in.

‘His Lordship is asking for Bao-yu.’

To Bao-yu it was like a thunderclap. His pleasure vanished at once; his face changed colour. Clutching Grandmother Jia, he writhed like a twisted sweet and would sooner have died than go. She had to comfort him.

‘My precious boy, go along. I’m here. He daren’t ill-treat you. Besides, you wrote that fine piece. Her Highness has told you to live in the garden, and I expect he has a few instructions for you, just to keep you out of mischief. Whatever he says, answer him nicely. That’s all you need do.’

As she soothed him, she called for two old nurses.

‘Take Bao-yu over carefully. Don’t let his father frighten him.’

The nurses promised. Bao-yu had no choice but to go, dragging himself along in steps scarcely three cun long. Jia Zheng happened to be in Lady Wang’s room discussing household matters. Golden, Cai-yun, Cai-xia, Xiu-luan, Xiu-feng and the other maids were standing under the eaves of the verandah. At the sight of Bao-yu they all smiled behind pursed lips. Golden caught hold of him.

‘I’ve just put scented rouge on my lips,’ she whispered with a smile. ‘Do you feel like tasting it now?’

Cai-yun pushed her away, laughing.

‘He’s worried enough already, and you’re still teasing him! Go in quickly, while His Lordship is in a good mood.’

Bao-yu edged through the doorway. Jia Zheng and Lady Wang were in the inner room. Aunt Zhao raised the curtain and Bao-yu bowed as he went in. His parents sat facing one another on the kang, talking. Below them stood a row of chairs occupied by Ying-chun, Tan-chun, Xi-chun and Jia Huan. When he entered, only Tan-chun, Xi-chun and Jia Huan got up.

Jia Zheng looked up. Before him stood Bao-yu, graceful and radiant, his beauty arresting. He glanced at Jia Huan, with his mean appearance and slovenly manners, and suddenly thought of Jia Zhu. Then he looked again at Lady Wang. This was the only son of her own she had left, and she had always cherished him like a treasure. His own beard was already turning grey. These thoughts together melted away eight or nine tenths of his usual dislike of Bao-yu and wish to punish him. After a long pause he said:

‘Her Highness says you spend your days running about outside and are growing ever more idle. You are now to be kept under supervision, studying and practising your writing in the garden with your sisters. Apply yourself properly. If you fail again to behave as you should, you had better look out!’

Bao-yu answered, ‘Yes, sir,’ several times in quick succession. Lady Wang drew him down to sit beside her. The other three sat down again.

Lady Wang stroked the back of Bao-yu’s neck.

‘Have you finished the pills you were given the other day?’

‘There’s one left.’

‘Have ten more fetched tomorrow. Every night at bedtime, let Aroma see that you take your medicine before you go to sleep.’

‘Ever since you gave the order, Mother, Aroma has remembered every evening and made sure I took it.’

‘Who is Aroma?’ Jia Zheng asked.

‘A maid,’ said Lady Wang.

‘Any ordinary name would do for a maid. Who had the perverse fancy of calling her that?’

Seeing his displeasure, Lady Wang tried to shield Bao-yu.

‘Her Old Ladyship named her.’

‘How would Her Old Ladyship know such a phrase? It must have been Bao-yu.’

Seeing that there was no concealing it, Bao-yu rose to reply.

‘In my reading I remembered a line by an old poet: “the flowers’ aroma steals upon one, telling of the warmth of day”. Since the maid’s surname is Hua, “flower”, the name came to me and I gave it to her.’

Lady Wang hurried to say, ‘Bao-yu, change it when you get back.

There’s no need for you to be angry over such a little thing, sir.’

‘There’s no harm in it, after all. Why change it? It simply shows that Bao-yu neglects his proper work and devotes his efforts to florid verses and amorous compositions.’

Then he snapped out, ‘You wicked little beast! Why are you still here?’

‘Go along,’ Lady Wang said quickly. ‘Her Old Ladyship is probably waiting for you to eat.’

Bao-yu answered obediently and withdrew slowly. Once outside he grinned at Golden, stuck out his tongue and was off like a wisp of smoke, taking the two nurses with him.

At the entrance to the passage hall he found Aroma leaning against the doorway. Seeing him return safe and sound, she broke into smiles.

‘What did he want you for?’

‘Nothing much. He was only afraid I’d get into mischief in the garden and wanted to give me some instructions.’

Talking as they went, he returned to Grandmother Jia and reported what had happened. Lin Dai-yu was there, and he asked her:

‘Where would you like to live?’

She had been turning that very question over in her mind. At his question she smiled.

‘I think Naiad’s House would be nice. I love those few stems of bamboo screening the winding balustrade. It’s more secluded than anywhere else.’

Bao-yu clapped his hands and laughed.

‘Exactly what I thought! I was going to tell you to live there. I’ll take the House of Green Delights. Then we’ll be close to each other, and both our places will be quiet and secluded.’

While they were discussing it, a messenger from Jia Zheng came to tell Grandmother Jia:

‘The twenty-second of the second month is an auspicious day for the young master and young ladies to move in.

People will be sent in over the next few days to allot the rooms and put them in order.’

Xue Bao-chai was assigned Allspice Court, Lin Dai-yu Naiad’s House, Jia Ying-chun the Brocade-adorned Tower, Tan-chun Autumn Studio, Xi-chun Knotweed Breeze Pavilion, Li Wan Sweet-rice Village, and Bao-yu the House of Green Delights. Each residence was given two additional old nurses and four maids, not counting its occupant’s own wet nurses and personal maids, and there were also servants specifically responsible for cleaning and tidying. On the twenty-second they all moved in. At once the flowers seemed to beckon to embroidered sashes, and the willows brushed scented breezes: the garden was no longer as lonely as before.

But enough of these details. Once Bao-yu was living in the garden, he was completely content and could think of nothing else to want. He spent every day with the girls and maids: reading, writing, playing the qin or chess, painting or composing poems. They traced patterns of luan-birds and embroidered phoenixes, played games with grasses and put flowers in their hair, hummed and sang softly, made riddles out of the parts of characters and guessed the number of counters in a closed hand. There was nothing they did not try, and he was very happy. He wrote several poems about the occasions of his daily life. They were not particularly good, but the feelings and scenes were true. Here are a few of them:

On a spring night

Rosy gauze and cloud-like curtains lie lavishly spread;

Across the lane the watch-clappers sound indistinctly.

A faint chill at my pillow, rain beyond the window;

Spring beauty before my eyes, the figure of my dreams.

For whom do brimming candles shed their tears?

Sorrowing flowers, one by one, reproach me.

The little maid is used to pampered idleness:

Clutching her quilt, she tires of all this talk and laughter.

On a summer night

Weary of stitching, the beauty dreams long and deep;

In its golden cage the parrot calls for tea.

At the bright window Musk uncovers a moon-bright palace mirror;1

In the room’s fragrant cloud Sandal savours imperial incense.

An amber cup tilts; lotus dew slips smoothly down;

The glass balustrade admits a cool breeze from the willows.

Fans of Qi silk stir in every waterside pavilion;

Evening toilette done, blinds rise on the crimson tower.

On an autumn night

In Crimson Rue Studio all clamour has ceased;

The cassia moon’s streaming light bathes the madder gauze.

Moss hides the grain of rocks that hold a sleeping crane;

Dew drifts from the well-side paulownia, wetting a roosting crow.

A maid comes with a quilt, unfolding golden phoenixes;

The girl at the rail comes in, unpinning jade flowers.

Thirst from wine keeps me awake in the still night;

Again I stir the aloeswood embers and ask for tea to be brewed.

On a winter night

The plum’s spirit, the bamboo’s dream: already the third watch;

Figured wool, a shuang-bird quilt, yet sleep eludes me.2

Pine shadows fill the courtyard; only cranes are seen;

Pear blossom carpets the ground, but no oriole is heard.

The girl in green sleeves feels her poetic fancies chilled;

The young master in golden sable finds the wine’s warmth slight.

I’m glad my maid has thought to try the tea:

She sweeps up fresh snow and sets it boiling at once.

Some worldly flatterers of the time, seeing that these poems were by a twelve- or thirteen-year-old young master of the Rong mansion, copied them out and praised them everywhere. There were also frivolous young men who took a fancy to the sensuous, seductive lines, wrote them on fans and walls, and recited and applauded them at every opportunity. People consequently began to come asking Bao-yu for poems and specimens of his calligraphy, commissioning paintings and requesting inscriptions. More pleased with himself than ever, he spent whole days on these extraneous pursuits.

Who would have thought that vexation could spring from such tranquillity? One day Bao-yu suddenly grew dissatisfied. Nothing was right, neither this nor that; indoors or out, he was always miserable. Most of the people in the garden were young girls, still in a world of unawakened, carefree innocence. They sat or lay down without reserve, laughed without a thought behind it: how could they know what was on Bao-yu’s mind? In his discontent he lost the inclination to stay in the garden and spent his time idling about outside, still in a daze. Seeing him like this, Tealeaf wanted to cheer him up. But every amusement he thought of was something Bao-yu had already tired of, and would give him no pleasure. There was just one thing Bao-yu had never seen. Having thought of it, Tealeaf went to a bookshop and bought a great many novels old and new, unofficial lives of Flying Swallow, He-de, Wu Ze-tian and Noble Consort Yang, and scripts of romance plays. He tempted Bao-yu to read them. Bao-yu had never seen books of this kind, and the moment he did, he felt he had found a treasure. Tealeaf warned him not to take them into the garden.

‘If anyone finds out, I’ll get more than I bargained for.’

But how could Bao-yu bear not to take them in? After much hesitation, he selected a few sets with the more subtle, carefully wrought writing, took them into the garden and put them above his bed, to read in secret when no one was there.

The coarse and over-explicit ones he hid in the outer study. One day, in the middle of the third month, he took a set of The Western Chamber after breakfast and sat on a rock beneath the peach trees by the Drenched Blossoms sluice-bridge. He opened it and began to savour it from the beginning. He had just reached ‘The falling red gathers in ranks’ when a gust of wind blew down most of the peach blossom from the branches, covering him, his book and the ground. He was about to shake it off, but feared that feet would trample it. Instead he gathered the petals in the front of his clothes, went to the pool and shook them into it. They floated and drifted on the water, and at last passed out through the Drenched Blossoms sluice.

When he returned, a great many blossoms were still on the ground. As he stood wondering what to do, a voice behind him said:

‘What are you doing here?’

He turned and saw Lin Dai-yu. She carried a flower hoe over her shoulder, with a bag for blossoms hanging from it, and held a blossom broom in her hand.

‘Good, good!’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Come and sweep these up and put them in the water. I’ve just put a great many in there.’

‘The water’s no good,’ said Dai-yu. ‘It looks clean here, but once it flows out where people live, all their filthy, stinking refuse is tipped into it, and the flowers will be ruined just the same. I have a grave for flowers in that corner. We can sweep them up, put them in this silk bag and bury them in the earth. In time they’ll simply turn to earth themselves. Isn’t that cleaner?’

Bao-yu was delighted.

‘Let me put down my book and help you.’

‘What book?’

At the question Bao-yu tried frantically to hide it.

‘Only the Doctrine of the Mean and the Great Learning.’

Dai-yu laughed. ‘You’re trying your tricks on me again.

You’d do much better to let me see it now.’

‘Dear cousin, I’m not afraid where you’re concerned. But once you’ve read it, whatever you do, don’t tell anyone else. It really is a wonderful book! You’ll forget all about eating once you’ve started.’

He handed it to her as he spoke. Dai-yu put down her flower-gathering things and took the book. She read from the beginning, enjoying it more and more, and in less time than it would have taken to eat a meal she had finished all sixteen scenes. The words startled her with their beauty and seemed to leave a lingering fragrance in her mouth. Even when she had finished, she remained lost in thought, silently committing the lines to memory.

‘Well, cousin,’ Bao-yu asked with a smile, ‘isn’t it good?’

‘It really is delightful,’ Dai-yu said, smiling.

Bao-yu smiled too. ‘I’m the one with “a body bowed by sickness and sorrow”, and you’re the one with “a beauty to overthrow cities and kingdoms”.’

Dai-yu flushed from her cheeks to her ears. Those brows that seemed to frown and yet not frown flew up; those eyes that seemed open and yet not open fixed him with a glare. Anger coloured her delicate cheeks, resentment her slender face. She pointed at him.

‘You wretch! How dare you talk like that! You bring in these lewd verses and seductive songs, then learn their filthy talk to insult me.

I’ll tell Uncle and Aunt.’

At the word ‘insult’, the rims of her eyes had already reddened. She turned to go. Bao-yu panicked and hurried in front of her to block her way.

‘Dear cousin, please forgive me just this once! I was wrong to say it. If I meant to insult you, may I fall into the pool tomorrow and be swallowed by a scabby-headed giant turtle and turn into a great big tortoise myself. And one day, when you’ve become a “Lady of the First Rank” and fall ill and die in your old age, I’ll go to your grave and carry your memorial stone on my back for the rest of my life.’

Dai-yu gave a sudden snort of laughter. Rubbing her eyes, she laughed and said:

‘So you can be frightened into this state too, yet still you keep talking nonsense. Pah! You’re just “a seedling that never flowers, a silver-looking spearhead made of pewter”.’

‘And what about that?’ Bao-yu said with a laugh. ‘I can go and tell on you too.’

‘You say you can remember anything after one reading,’ Dai-yu said, smiling. ‘Can’t I take in ten lines at a glance?’

Bao-yu put away the book.

‘Let’s get on and bury the flowers,’ he said with a smile. ‘No more of that.’

They gathered the fallen blossoms and had just finished burying them when Aroma came up.

‘I’ve looked everywhere, and at last I’ve tracked you down here! The Elder Master is unwell. The young ladies have all gone over to ask after him, and Her Old Ladyship wants you sent over too. Come back and change your clothes.’

Bao-yu quickly picked up the book, said goodbye to Dai-yu and went back with Aroma to change. We may leave them there. Dai-yu, seeing Bao-yu go and hearing that the other girls were all out too, felt lonely. She was about to return to her room. As she passed the corner of the wall of Pear-tree Court, she heard the clear, lingering notes of a flute and a voice singing with tender turns of melody. She knew it was the twelve girls rehearsing their plays. She had never much cared for the theatre and, paying little attention, walked on. Then two lines drifted into her ears, so distinctly that she missed not a word:

‘So vivid purple, lovely red have blossomed everywhere, Yet all are given over to broken wells and crumbling walls.’

The words stirred a deep, lingering feeling in Dai-yu. She stopped and listened closely. The singing went on:

‘In this fine hour, before this lovely scene, what can I do? Whose courtyard has the joys to gladden the heart?’

At these two lines she could not help nodding and sighing to herself. ‘So there is fine writing in plays too,’ she thought. ‘What a pity people only watch the performance. They may never appreciate what is in the words.’ Then she regretted letting her thoughts wander and missing the song. She listened again:

‘Only for you, my flower-fair love, And these years that flow like water…’

At these two lines her heart stirred and her senses reeled. Then came ‘You pity yourself in your secluded chamber’ and other lines. More entranced than ever, as if intoxicated, she could no longer stand. She sank on to a rock in the artificial hill and slowly savoured those eight Chinese characters, ‘my flower-fair love, these years that flow like water’. Suddenly she remembered a line she had recently seen in an old poem: ‘Water flows, flowers fade; neither has a heart.’ Then there was the lyric, ‘Water flows, flowers fall, spring is gone: heaven and the world below.’ And from The Western Chamber, which she had just read, came ‘Flowers fall, and the water flows red; ten thousand idle sorrows.’ All returned to her at once and gathered together in her mind.

As she pondered them, her heart ached, her thoughts grew dazed, and tears fell from her eyes. She could find no release, when suddenly she felt a tap on her back. She turned to look, and it was… For the answer, listen to the next chapter. As the couplet says:

For morning toilette and night embroidery she has no heart;

Before the moon and in the wind, she has sorrow enough.

Translator’s notes

  1. Musk’s Chinese name contains “moon”, and Sandal’s contains “cloud”. The lines play on both maids’ names. ↩
  2. Shuang is short for sushuang, a traditional bird name used here in the name of a sumptuous quilt. ↩
