Chapter 42 With gentle words the Lady of Allspice dispels a habit of suspicion; With polished wit the Naiad adds a lingering fragrance

Chapter end

The girls went back into the garden, ate their meal and dispersed. There is nothing more to tell of them for the moment.

Grannie Liu first took Ban-er to see Xi-feng.

‘We really must go home early tomorrow. It’s only been two or three days, but in that little while I’ve seen and tasted and heard things I never knew of in all my life. Her Old Ladyship and you, Madam, and all the young ladies, even the girls in each of the apartments, have been so kind to a poor old woman. When I get home I’ve nothing to repay you with. All I can do is buy some good incense and pray to the Buddha for you every day, that you may all live to a hundred. That will be my way of showing what I feel.’

‘Don’t be too pleased with yourself,’ said Xi-feng, smiling. ‘It’s all because of you that Her Old Ladyship has caught a chill. She’s lying down, saying she feels unwell. And our Da-jie has caught cold too. She’s got a fever.’

Grannie Liu sighed in concern.

‘Her Old Ladyship’s getting on in years. She’s not used to so much exertion.’

‘She’s never enjoyed herself as much as she did yesterday,’ said Xi-feng.

‘She often goes into the garden, but usually she just sits in one or two places and comes back. Yesterday, because you were here and she wanted to show you round, she walked over more than half of it. Da-jie went looking for me, and Her Ladyship gave her a piece of cake. Who would have thought that eating it out in the wind would bring on a fever?’

‘The little miss doesn’t often go into the garden, I expect,’ said Grannie Liu. ‘It’s a strange place to her, and little ones oughtn’t to go into strange places. She’s not like our children. Once they can walk, there isn’t a graveyard they won’t run about in. For one thing, the wind may have struck her. Then again, she’s so pure herself, and her eyes are pure too: she may have come upon some spirit. If you ask me, you ought to look it up in the book about spirits. She may have been troubled by one.’

This reminded Xi-feng of something. She told Patience to fetch The Jade Casket Almanac and sent for Sunshine to read it. He turned over the pages for a while, then read out:

‘“The twenty-fifth day of the eighth month. The sick person has encountered a flower spirit in the south-east. Take forty sheets of paper money in five colours and go forty paces to the south-east to send it away. Most auspicious.”’

‘Exactly!’ said Xi-feng, laughing. ‘What else would you find in the garden but a flower spirit? I expect Her Old Ladyship met one too.’

She ordered two sets of paper money to be brought and summoned two people, one to send the evil spirits away for Grandmother Jia and the other for Da-jie. Sure enough, the child was soon sleeping peacefully.

‘There’s no denying that you older people have seen more of life,’ said Xi-feng with a smile. ‘My Da-jie is always falling ill. I don’t know why.’

‘That does happen,’ said Grannie Liu. ‘Children brought up in rich households are generally too delicate. Naturally they can’t bear the least little hardship. And a child can’t stand having too much honour paid to her, either.

‘Love her a little less, Madam, and she’ll be all right.’

‘There’s something in that too. Now I think of it, she hasn’t got a name yet. You give her one. First, she can borrow a little of your long life; and then, you’re country people and, don’t be offended, you do have a harder time of it. A name given her by someone poor might just keep her troubles down.’

Grannie Liu thought for a moment, then smiled.

‘When was she born?’

‘That’s just it. Her birthday’s an unlucky one. It happens to be the seventh day of the seventh month.’

‘Why, that’s just right!’ Grannie Liu said eagerly. ‘Call her Qiao-ge. It’s what they call fighting poison with poison, or fire with fire. You must use my name for her, Madam. She’ll live to a hundred, I’m sure. Later on, when she’s grown up and everyone has a family and a livelihood of their own, if things should ever go against her, her troubles will turn to blessings and bad luck to good. It will all come from that word “Qiao”.’1

Xi-feng was naturally delighted. She thanked her warmly, then said with a smile:

‘Let’s only pray she fulfils your words.’

She called Patience and told her:

‘We’ve things to do tomorrow and may not have a moment to spare.

‘While you have time, get Grannie’s presents ready, so that she can leave comfortably first thing in the morning.’

‘You mustn’t go to any more expense,’ Grannie Liu protested. ‘I’ve been a trouble to you for several days already. If I take things away as well, I shall feel worse than ever.’

‘There’s nothing much,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Only ordinary things. Good or bad, take them with you. They’ll give the neighbours something to look at. You’ll have something to show for your trip to town.’

Patience came over.

‘Come and have a look in here, Grannie.’

Grannie Liu hurried after her into the next room. Half the kang was piled with things. Patience picked them up one by one to show her.

‘Here’s the bolt of blue-green gauze you asked for yesterday. Mrs Lian has added a piece of pale-blue solid-ground gauze for the lining. Here are two lengths of pongee, good for jackets or skirts. There are two bolts of silk in this bundle, so you can have new clothes for the New Year. This box has all sorts of pastries made in the palace, some you’ve tasted and some you haven’t. Take them home and put them out on plates when you have visitors. They’re rather better than the ones you can buy there. These are the two sacks you brought the fruit and vegetables in yesterday. This one now has two dou of non-glutinous rice from the imperial fields. You won’t find better for making porridge. The other has fruit from the garden and all sorts of dried fruit. This packet is eight taels of silver. All those are from Mrs Lian. These two packets have fifty taels each, a hundred altogether. They’re from Her Ladyship. She says you’re to use the money to set up a little business or buy a few mu of land, so that you won’t have to depend on relations and friends any more.’

Then she lowered her voice and smiled.

‘These two jackets and two skirts, the four head-cloths and this packet of embroidery thread are from me, Grannie.

‘The clothes aren’t new, but I haven’t worn them very much. If they’re not good enough for you, I wouldn’t presume.’

Every time Patience named an article, Grannie Liu invoked the Buddha. She had done so several thousand times already. Now, finding that Patience too was giving her all these things, and speaking so modestly about it, she invoked him again in her eagerness to reply.

‘What are you saying, miss? Me turn up my nose at such lovely things! Even if I had the money, I couldn’t buy things like these. Only I’m so ashamed. It doesn’t seem right to take them, but if I don’t, I’m throwing your kindness back in your face.’

‘Don’t talk as though we were strangers,’ Patience said, smiling. ‘I wouldn’t do this if I didn’t think of you as one of us. Take them without worrying. I’m going to ask you for something too. At New Year, just bring us some of your dried vegetables: those dried huitiaocai greens, cowpeas, flat beans, aubergines, strips of gourd, a little of each. Everyone here likes them, from the highest to the lowest. That will do. Nothing else at all. Don’t go to any unnecessary trouble.’

Grannie Liu promised, thanking her over and over again.

‘Now you just go and get your sleep,’ said Patience. ‘I’ll pack everything properly and leave it here. First thing tomorrow I’ll have the boys hire a cart and load it up. There’s nothing at all for you to worry about.’

Grannie Liu’s gratitude knew no bounds. She went back to thank Xi-feng again and again and take her leave, then spent the night in Grandmother Jia’s quarters. Early next morning she washed and combed her hair and was ready to say goodbye. As Grandmother Jia was unwell, everyone came to enquire after her, and someone was sent out to summon a doctor. Presently a serving-woman announced his arrival. The old nurses asked Grandmother Jia to sit behind the curtain.

‘I’m an old woman now,’ she said. ‘I could be the mother of any fellow like that. Am I to be afraid of him? Don’t draw the curtain. Let him examine me as I am.’

The women brought a small table, laid a little cushion on it and sent word for the doctor to be admitted.

Soon Jia Zhen, Jia Lian and Jia Rong appeared, bringing Doctor Wang with them. He did not venture on to the central path but kept to the side and followed Jia Zhen up to the steps. Two women were already holding up the portière; two more led the way inside. Bao-yu too came out to meet him.

Grandmother Jia sat upright on a couch in a jacket of pearl-curled lambskin faced with blue-green crinkled silk. Four little maids, still too young to wear their hair long, stood to either side with fly-whisks, a rinsing-bowl and other articles. Five or six old nurses were ranged on either hand like outspread wings. Behind the green gauze screen he could dimly make out a crowd of figures in red and green, wearing jewelled hairpins and pearls. Doctor Wang dared not raise his eyes. He stepped forward at once and paid his respects.

Seeing his sixth-rank robes, Grandmother Jia knew that he was an imperial physician, and greeted him with a smile.

‘How are you, Doctor?’

Then she asked Jia Zhen, ‘What is this gentleman’s surname?’

‘Wang,’ Jia Zhen and the others hastened to reply.

‘Wang Jun-xiao, who used to be head of the Imperial Medical Academy, was very good at taking pulses.’

The doctor bent low, his head bowed, and replied with a smile:

‘He was my paternal grandfather’s younger brother, Your Old Ladyship.’

‘Oh, was he indeed?’ said Grandmother Jia, smiling. ‘Then our families are old acquaintances.’

As she spoke, she slowly extended her hand and laid it on the little cushion. An old nurse hurriedly set a small stool before the table, a little to one side. Doctor Wang sat with one knee bent under him and, his head tilted, felt her pulse for some time. He then examined the other wrist, half rose with his head bowed and withdrew.

‘Thank you for your trouble,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Zhen, take him out and see that he has some tea.’

Jia Zhen, Jia Lian and the others replied with repeated assurances of obedience and escorted Doctor Wang to the outer study.

‘Her Old Ladyship has nothing else the matter with her,’ he said. ‘She has merely caught a little chill. There is really no need for medicine. Somewhat plainer food and a little extra warmth will put her right. I’ll leave a prescription here. If she feels like taking it, have one dose prepared according to the prescription. If she can’t be bothered, never mind.’

He drank his tea and wrote the prescription. Just as he was about to leave, the wet-nurse appeared carrying Da-jie.

‘Have a look at us too, Doctor,’ she said, smiling.

He rose at once. Leaving the child in her nurse’s arms, he supported her hand with his left and felt her pulse with his right. Then he touched her head and asked her to put out her tongue.

‘The little lady will scold me again for saying so,’ he said with a smile, ‘but all she needs is to be left quietly alone and go without a couple of meals. There’s no need for a decoction. I’ll send some pills over. Grind them up in ginger broth for her at bedtime and let her take it. That will do.’

With that he took his leave.

Jia Zhen and the others brought in the prescription, explained to Grandmother Jia what the doctor had said, left it on the table and went out. We need say no more of them.

Only when the doctor had gone did Lady Wang, Li Wan, Xi-feng, Bao-chai and the other girls emerge from behind the screen. Lady Wang sat for a little while, then returned to her own rooms.

Seeing that there was nothing more to be done, Grannie Liu came forward to take her leave of Grandmother Jia.

‘Come again when you have time,’ said Grandmother Jia. She called Faithful. ‘See Grannie Liu safely out, and look after her properly. I’m not well enough to see you off myself.’

Grannie Liu thanked her, said goodbye once more and went out with Faithful. In the servants’ room Faithful pointed to a bundle on the kang.

‘These are some clothes of Her Old Ladyship’s, given her on birthdays and festivals in past years. She never wears clothes other people have made for her, but it seems a pity to leave them lying there. They’ve never been worn, not even once. Yesterday she told me to get out two sets for you to take away. Give them to someone, or wear them yourselves at home. Don’t laugh at them. This box has the little pastries you asked for. And this packet has the medicines you spoke about the other day: Meihua Dianshe Pills, Zijin Tablets, Huoluo Pills and Cuisheng Baoming Pills. Each kind is wrapped in its own prescription, and they’re all inside the same bundle. Here are two purses for you as well. Keep them for fun.’

She loosened the drawstrings and took out two little silver pieces with brush, ingot and ruyi motifs to show her.2

‘You can have the purses,’ she added with a smile, ‘but leave these for me.’

Grannie Liu was already overjoyed and had invoked the Buddha several thousand times more. On hearing this she said:

‘Of course, miss. You keep them.’

Seeing that she had taken it seriously, Faithful put them back into the purses and laughed.

‘I’m only teasing you. I’ve plenty.

‘Keep them for the children at New Year.’

Just then a little maid came in with a Chenghua cup and handed it to Grannie Liu.

‘This is from Master Bao.’

‘How can such a thing happen to me?’ said Grannie Liu.

‘What good did I do in some former life, to be treated like this today?’

She took the cup.

‘The clothes you changed into when I had you take a bath the other day were mine,’ said Faithful. ‘If you don’t mind them, I have a few more you can have.’

Grannie Liu thanked her eagerly again. Faithful did indeed fetch two more garments and pack them for her. Grannie Liu wanted to go into the garden to thank Bao-yu and the girls and say goodbye, and to call on Lady Wang too.

‘There’s no need,’ said Faithful. ‘They aren’t seeing anyone just now. I’ll tell them for you later. Come again when you have time.’

She summoned an old serving-woman.

‘Get two boys from the inner gate to help Grannie carry her things out.’

The woman agreed and went with Grannie Liu to Xi-feng’s quarters to collect the rest. At the side gate she had the boys carry everything outside and saw Grannie Liu right into the cart. There we may leave her.

After breakfast Bao-chai and the others had again enquired after Grandmother Jia and were returning to the garden. Where the paths divided, Bao-chai called to Dai-yu:

‘Come with me, Little Pin-pin. There’s something I want to ask you.’

Dai-yu went with her to Allspice Court. Once they were in the room, Bao-chai sat down and smiled.

‘On your knees. I’m going to question you.’

Dai-yu had no idea why.

‘Look at Bao-chai! She’s gone mad!’ she said, laughing. ‘What do you want to question me about?’

Bao-chai gave a little scoffing laugh.

‘A fine young lady you are! Such a nicely sheltered girl! What are these things that come out of your mouth? You’d better confess.’

Still bewildered, Dai-yu kept laughing, though she was beginning to feel uneasy.

‘What have I ever said? You’re only trying to find something against me. Tell me what it is, then.’

‘Still playing the innocent!’ said Bao-chai with a smile. ‘What was it you said in the drinking game yesterday? I can’t imagine where you found it.’

Dai-yu thought back. Only then did she remember her carelessness of the previous day, when she had quoted those two lines from The Peony Pavilion and The Western Chamber. She blushed, went over and put her arms round Bao-chai.

‘Dear cousin,’ she said, smiling, ‘I didn’t realise. They just slipped out. You must teach me better. I won’t say them again.’

‘I don’t know them either,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘They sounded so strange when you said them that I wanted you to explain.’

‘Dear cousin, don’t tell anyone. I’ll never say them again.’

Seeing her scarlet with embarrassment and begging so earnestly, Bao-chai would not press her any further. She drew her down to sit and have some tea, then began to speak to her gently and without haste.

‘What sort of person do you think I am? I was naughty too. When I was seven or eight I was quite a handful. We’re a reading family, after all. My grandfather liked collecting books. There were a great many of us in the household then, boys and girls all together, and none of us wanted to read the proper books. Some of the boys liked poetry and some liked lyrics. We had everything: The Western Chamber, The Lute, A Hundred Yuan Plays, all those sorts of books. They read them on the sly, keeping it from us, and we read them on the sly, keeping it from them. Then the grown-ups found out. There were beatings and scoldings, and books were burnt, and only then did we give them up.

‘That’s why girls like us are better off not knowing how to read. Even a man is better off without books if they don’t teach him how to behave, let alone you or me. Writing poems and practising calligraphy aren’t really our business. For that matter, they aren’t really a man’s business either. A man should read to learn sound principles, help the state and govern the people. That would be something. But you don’t hear of men like that nowadays. Reading only makes them worse. The books do them harm, and it’s a pity, but they abuse the books as well. They’d be better off farming or trading. At least they wouldn’t do much damage that way.

‘You and I ought to concern ourselves with needlework, spinning and weaving. But here we are, knowing how to read. Since we do, we should at least choose proper books. The worst thing is to let miscellaneous books alter your nature. Once that happens, there’s no saving you.’

Dai-yu sat with her head lowered over her tea. In her heart she acknowledged the force of what Bao-chai said, and could only answer, ‘Yes.’

Just then Su-yun came in.

‘My mistress asks if you two young ladies will come and discuss something important.

‘The Second, Third and Fourth Young Ladies, Miss Shi and Master Bao are all there waiting.’

‘What can it be now?’ said Bao-chai.

‘We’ll find out when we get there,’ Dai-yu replied.

They went together to Sweet-rice Village and found everyone assembled there.

Li Wan smiled when she saw them.

‘Our society has hardly begun, and already someone’s trying to wriggle out. Our fourth young lady wants a year’s leave.’

‘It’s all because of what Grandmother said yesterday,’ said Dai-yu, laughing. ‘Asking her to paint the garden! Of course she jumped at the chance to take leave.’

‘You can’t blame Grandmother,’ Tan-chun said with a smile. ‘It all came from something Grannie Liu said.’

‘Of course!’ Dai-yu put in at once. ‘All because of something she said. What sort of grannie is she, anyway? Just call her Mother Locust and be done with it.’

Everyone laughed.

‘When Xi-feng has finished with a subject, you’d think there could be nothing left to say,’ said Bao-chai, smiling. ‘Luckily she can’t read and doesn’t know much about books, so hers are only ordinary street jokes. But there’s Little Pin-pin with that wicked tongue of hers. She uses the method of the Spring and Autumn Annals: takes the essence of a vulgar joke, cuts away the excess, gives it a polish and finds a comparison. Every phrase tells.3

‘Those three characters, “Mother Locust”, bring back the whole scene yesterday. How she thinks of them so quickly!’

‘Your commentary is quite a match for either of them,’ the others said, laughing.

‘I asked you here to decide how much leave we should give her,’ said Li Wan. ‘I offered a month, but she says it isn’t enough. What do you think?’

‘A year isn’t too much, if you look at it properly,’ said Dai-yu. ‘It took a year just to build this garden. Naturally it will take two to paint it. There’s the ink to grind, and the brush to dip, and the paper to spread out, and the colours to put on, and then there’s…’

By now they all knew she was teasing Xi-chun, and broke in, laughing:

‘What else is there?’

Dai-yu could no longer keep from laughing herself.

‘Then there’s painting it all, ever so slowly, just as it is. That must take two years!’

They clapped their hands and laughed without stopping.

‘That last bit is the best,’ said Bao-chai. ‘“Then there’s painting it all, ever so slowly.”

‘Yesterday’s jokes were funny enough, but there’s nothing in them when you think back. What Little Pin-pin has just said sounds very slight, yet the more you think about it, the more there is to enjoy. I’ve laughed so much I can hardly move.’

‘It’s all your praise that makes her so pleased with herself,’ Xi-chun said. ‘Now even I’m something for her to make fun of.’

Dai-yu promptly took her hand, laughing.

‘Tell me, are you only painting the garden, or are you putting all of us in too?’

‘It was only going to be the garden,’ said Xi-chun. ‘But yesterday Grandmother said that with no one in it, it would look like a plan of a house. She wants the people put in as well, like a picture of people at leisure. I can’t do those finely drawn buildings, and I can’t paint figures either. But I couldn’t very well refuse. That’s what I’m worrying about.’

‘The figures are easy enough,’ Dai-yu said. ‘It’s the insects you’re no good at.’

‘What nonsense!’ said Li Wan. ‘Where would insects come into this?

‘A bird or two, perhaps, to fill it out.’

‘You can leave out the other insects,’ Dai-yu said with a laugh, ‘but if yesterday’s Mother Locust isn’t in it, won’t the record be incomplete?’

They all laughed again. Dai-yu held both hands to her chest, laughing as she spoke.

‘Do hurry up and paint it. I’ve even got the inscription ready. We’ll give it a name: “Taking the Locust to a Feast”.’

This sent them into still louder laughter. They rocked backwards and forwards, until suddenly there was a thump. Something had fallen. They looked round in alarm and saw what had happened. Xiang-yun had been leaning with her whole weight over the back of a chair, laughing. The chair had not been standing squarely, and, before she knew it, their weight had shifted the wrong way. Both she and the chair had tipped sideways to the east. Luckily the wooden partition stopped them from reaching the floor. At the sight, the others laughed harder than ever. Bao-yu hurried to help her up, and only then did the laughter gradually subside.

Bao-yu gave Dai-yu a look. She understood and went into the inner room, lifted the mirror-cover and looked at herself. The hair at her temples was a little loose. She quickly opened Li Wan’s toilet-box, took out the small smoothing-brush and passed it over her hair a couple of times before the mirror. Having put everything back, she came out and pointed at Li Wan.

‘You’re supposed to lead us in our sewing and teach us how to behave. Instead you call us here to make a great noisy game of everything.’

‘Just listen to her!’ Li Wan said, laughing.

‘She starts all the mischief herself and sets everyone laughing, and then says it’s my fault. Really, I’m so cross I can only pray that one day you’ll have a dreadful mother-in-law and a whole set of her daughters, older and younger, every one a little monster. We’ll see whether you’re still so sharp then!’

Dai-yu was already blushing. She caught hold of Bao-chai.

‘Let’s give her a year’s leave.’

‘Listen to me,’ said Bao-chai. ‘I have a fair proposal. Our Lotus Pavilion girl can paint, but only a few freehand strokes. To make a picture of this garden, you need someone who already has a few landscapes composed in their mind. The garden itself is like a painting. The rocks and trees, the towers and houses, near and far, crowded or spread apart, are neither too many nor too few. They are just right as they are. But if you simply copy it all on to paper, it’s bound not to come off. You have to consider the space on the paper, what is near and what is far, how much to put in and how little, what is principal and what is secondary. Where something needs adding, add it; where something needs taking away, take it away; what should be hidden must be hidden, and what should show must show. Once you have a first sketch, you must look it over and weigh everything up before you have a proper design.

‘Then, secondly, all those towers and houses have to be drawn with ruled lines. One moment’s carelessness, and the railings lean, the pillars fall, the doors and windows turn upside down and the steps come apart. You might even have a table squeezed into a wall or a flowerpot sitting on a curtain. Then your picture really would be a joke.

‘Thirdly, when you put in the figures, you must think about their spacing and their different heights too. The folds of clothes, the ribbons on skirts, fingers and feet are especially important. One careless stroke, and you’ve given someone a swollen hand or a twisted leg. Getting the face coloured and the hair drawn is a small matter by comparison. I think the whole thing is very difficult. A year’s leave is too much, but a month is too little. Let’s give her six months and appoint Cousin Bao to help. Not because he knows enough to teach her to paint: that would only make matters worse. But whenever there’s something she doesn’t understand, or can’t decide where to put, he can take it outside and ask those gentlemen who know how to paint. That will make it easier.’

Bao-yu was the first to respond, delighted.

‘Exactly! Zhan Zi-liang is excellent at finely drawn buildings, and no one can touch Cheng Ri-xing for beautiful women. I’ll go and ask them now.’

‘I call you Busy over Nothing,’ said Bao-chai. ‘The moment I mention it, you’re off to ask. Wait till we’ve settled everything. What are you going to paint on, to begin with?’

‘We have Snow-wave paper. It’s large and takes ink well.’

Bao-chai gave a scoffing laugh.

‘Didn’t I say you were no use? Snow-wave paper is for calligraphy, or freehand painting, or Southern School landscapes, if you know how to do them. It takes ink and stands up to texture strokes. But use it for this, and it won’t take the colours properly; it’s hard to lay a wash on, too. You’ll get a poor painting and waste good paper. I’ll tell you what to do.

‘When the garden was being built, there was a detailed plan. It was only drawn by the workmen, but the placing and direction of everything are right. Ask Aunt Wang for it. Then ask Xi-feng for a piece of heavy silk the same size as the paper and have one of the gentlemen size it with alum. Get him to make a sketch from the plan, leaving things out and adding things where necessary, and put in the figures. You’ll have to let them mix the blues and greens and prepare the gold and silver paint too. You’ll also need to light a separate brazier, ready for melting the glue, drawing off the glue solution and washing brushes. Then you’ll need a large oil-painted work-table, with felt spread over it. You haven’t all the dishes or brushes you need either. You must get a fresh set of everything.’

‘When have I ever had all those painting things?’ said Xi-chun. ‘I just paint with whatever writing-brush comes to hand. Even for colours, I only have ochre, Guangdong indigo, gamboge and rouge, those four. Apart from that, there are two colour brushes, and that’s all.’

‘You should have said so before.

‘I still have some of those things,’ Bao-chai went on. ‘Only you had no use for them, and they’d have lain idle if I’d given them to you. I’ve been keeping them for you, and now that you need them I can let you have some. But you should save them for painting fans. It would be a pity to use them up on a picture as large as this. Today I’ll make you a list. Take it to Grandmother and ask for what’s on it. You probably don’t know everything that’s needed. I’ll dictate and Cousin Bao can write.’

Bao-yu had already got brush and inkstone ready. Afraid he might not remember everything clearly, he had meant to write it down. At Bao-chai’s words he took up his brush with delight and listened attentively.

She dictated:

‘Four No. 1 flat brushes; four No. 2 flat brushes; four No. 3 flat brushes; four large wash brushes; four medium wash brushes; four small wash brushes; ten large southern crab-claw brushes; ten small crab-claw brushes; ten beard-and-eyebrow brushes; twenty large colour brushes; twenty small colour brushes; ten brushes for painting faces; twenty willow-twig brushes; four taels of arrowhead vermilion; four taels of southern ochre; four taels of mineral yellow; four taels of mineral blue; four taels of mineral green; four taels of bamboo-cast gamboge; eight taels of Guangdong indigo; four boxes of shell white; ten sheets of rouge; two hundred packets of dachi gold leaf; two hundred packets of qingjin painting colour; four taels of Guangyun glue; four taels of purified alum.4

‘The glue and alum for sizing the silk are extra. Don’t worry about those. Just send the silk out and let them size it. We can wash, settle, separate and shake these pigments ourselves. We’ll have some fun and get something useful out of it, and I guarantee you’ll have enough colours to last a lifetime. Then you’ll need four extra-fine silk sieves; four coarse silk sieves; four dusting brushes; four mortars, large and small; twenty large coarse bowls; ten coarse dishes five cun across; twenty coarse white dishes three cun across; two portable braziers; four earthenware pots, large and small; two new porcelain jars; four new water buckets; four white cloth bags one chi long; twenty catties of light charcoal; one catty of willow charcoal; one wooden chest with three drawers; one zhang of solid-ground gauze; two taels of ginger; half a catty of sauce.’

‘One iron pan, one spatula,’ Dai-yu promptly added.

‘What are those for?’ Bao-chai asked.

‘Well, you want ginger and sauce and all these seasonings,’ said Dai-yu, smiling. ‘I’m getting you an iron pan so you can fry your colours and eat them.’

They all laughed.

‘What do you know about it?’ Bao-chai said, laughing too.

‘Those coarse dishes for the colours may well have to be heated over the fire. Unless you smear the bottoms with ginger juice and sauce and bake them beforehand, they’ll crack as soon as you put them over the heat.’

‘Oh, so that’s it!’ said the others.

Dai-yu studied the list a little longer, then caught Tan-chun’s arm and whispered with a smile:

‘Look at this. All these water jars and chests just to paint a picture! She must have got muddled and written down her dowry list as well.’

Tan-chun gave a little cry and could not stop laughing.

‘Cousin Bao-chai, why don’t you pinch her mouth? Ask her what she’s been making up about you.’

‘No need to ask,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘Would you expect ivory from a dog’s mouth?’

As she spoke she came over, pressed Dai-yu down on the kang and made to pinch her cheeks. Dai-yu laughed and begged for mercy.

‘Dear cousin, let me off! Little Pin-pin is young. She talks away without knowing what she ought to say. You’re older, you must teach me. If you won’t forgive me, who else can I ask?’

The others had no idea that there was more in this than they knew.

‘She sounds so pitiful,’ they laughed. ‘Even our hearts are melting. Do let her off.’

Bao-chai had only been playing. But when she heard Dai-yu bring in their earlier conversation about her indiscriminate reading, she could hardly go on rough-and-tumbling with her. She let her up.

‘You really are like an elder sister,’ Dai-yu said, smiling. ‘If it were me, I’d never let anyone off.’

Bao-chai pointed at her with a smile.

‘No wonder Grandmother dotes on you and everyone loves your cleverness. I’m growing very fond of you myself today. Come here and let me smooth your hair.’

Dai-yu obediently turned round, and Bao-chai smoothed it up with her hand. Watching beside them, Bao-yu thought this lovelier still. He could not help regretting that he had made Dai-yu brush back the hair at her temples earlier. He should have left it loose for Bao-chai to smooth now.

While he was lost in these fancies, Bao-chai said:

‘Now that it’s written, take it to Grandmother tomorrow. If we have the things in the house, well and good. If not, get some money and buy them. I’ll help you mix the colours.’

Bao-yu hastily put the list away.

They talked of this and that for a while longer. After the evening meal they went to enquire after Grandmother Jia again. There had been nothing seriously wrong with her, only tiredness and a slight chill. After a day of keeping warm and resting, and one dose of medicine to disperse the chill, she was well again by evening.

Translator’s notes

  1. The girl's protective pet-name uses ge, a boy-style address. Qiao links her seventh-night birthday with the lucky turn Grannie Liu wishes for her. ↩
  2. A rebus expressing 'May everything surely go as you wish': 'brush and ingot' sounds like 'certainly'. ↩
  3. The Spring and Autumn Annals was traditionally associated with Confucius and with judgements conveyed in very few words. Bao-chai applies that lofty method to Dai-yu’s pointed joke. ↩
  4. Dachi is the source’s designation for the gold leaf. Qingjin can denote a blue painting colour or a kind of gold leaf; its identity here is uncertain. Tie is rendered as ‘packets’, without assuming a fixed number of leaves. ↩
