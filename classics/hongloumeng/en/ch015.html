Chapter 15 Wang Xi-feng wields her influence at the Temple of the Iron Threshold; Qin Jing-qing finds pleasure at Steamed-bun Hermitage

Chapter end

Bao-yu looked up at Shui Rong, the Prince of Beijing. He wore a prince’s hat with pure white tassels and silver wings, a white mang robe patterned with five-clawed dragons shown full-face above waves and jutting rocks, and a red leather belt set with green jade. His face was like fine jade, his eyes like bright stars: a remarkably handsome figure. Bao-yu hurried forward to pay his respects, but Shui Rong at once reached out of his sedan-chair and caught him by the hand. He saw a boy wearing a silver coronet over his bound hair and a brow-band patterned with two dragons rising from the sea, a white mang robe with close-fitting sleeves and a silver belt studded with pearls. His face was like spring blossom, his eyes like drops of lacquer.

‘You quite live up to your name,’ Shui Rong said with a smile. ‘A treasure indeed, and precious as jade. Where is the precious thing you were born with in your mouth?’

At the question Bao-yu hastily took it from inside his clothes and handed it over. Shui Rong examined it closely, read out its inscription, then asked, ‘Does it really have miraculous powers?’

‘So it is said,’ Jia Zheng answered quickly, ‘but we have never put them to the test.’

Shui Rong went on praising its wonders as he straightened the coloured cord and put it back on Bao-yu himself. Then, holding the boy’s hand, he asked his age and what books he was reading.

Bao-yu answered each question. Finding his speech clear and his manner of speaking engaging, Shui Rong turned to Jia Zheng with a smile.

‘Your son is a dragon colt, a phoenix chick. Forgive me if I speak too freely before an elder of a family so long allied with mine, but one day “the young phoenix will sing more clearly than the old”. There is no measuring his prospects.’

Jia Zheng hastened to return the smile. ‘My poor boy could hardly deserve such lavish praise. Should Your Highness’s gracious blessing make it true, it would be a piece of good fortune for those of us who owe our standing to our forebears.’

‘There is only one thing,’ Shui Rong went on. ‘With such gifts, your son must naturally be the darling of his grandmother, his mother and the other ladies. Yet we young men are ill served by too much indulgence: it is all too easy to neglect our studies. I fell into that error myself once, and I imagine your son may not be altogether free of it. If he finds it hard to work at home, let him come often to my house. Though I have little ability myself, the distinguished men of our land are very kind to me: scarcely one comes to the capital without favouring me with a visit. Consequently quite a number of accomplished men gather under my roof. If your son comes to talk with them from time to time, his learning may improve day by day.’

Jia Zheng bowed his assent.

Shui Rong slipped a string of prayer beads from his wrist and handed it to Bao-yu.

‘This is our first meeting, and in my haste I have brought no suitable gift. Here is a string of fragrant jiling prayer beads which His Majesty himself bestowed on me the other day. Pray accept it as a small token of my good wishes.’

Bao-yu quickly accepted them, then turned and respectfully handed them to Jia Zheng. Father and son thanked the Prince together. Jia She, Jia Zhen and the others now came forward to ask him to turn his chair homewards.

‘The departed has ascended to the realm of immortals,’ Shui Rong said. ‘She is no longer one of the common mortals who toil here below, as you and I do. By the Emperor’s favour I have inherited a princely title which I do little to deserve, but how could I presume to go ahead of an immortal’s hearse?’

As he was determined not to yield, Jia She and the rest could only take their leave with further thanks. Returning to the procession, they ordered their men to silence the music. The whole great train streamed past before Shui Rong would allow his chair to turn for home. Of this we need say no more.

The Ning household’s funeral procession made a great stir along its route. Just before the city gate, more funeral canopies had been set up by colleagues and subordinates of Jia She, Jia Zheng, Jia Zhen and the others, whose households offered sacrifices as the coffin passed. When thanks had been given at each in turn, the procession left the city and took the road straight towards the Temple of the Iron Threshold. Jia Zhen brought Jia Rong to the elders to invite them to take their chairs or mount their horses. Those of Jia She’s generation got into their carriages and chairs, and those of Jia Zhen’s were about to mount.

Xi-feng was worrying about Bao-yu. Out in the country he might indulge himself, try to show off and refuse to listen to the servants; Jia Zheng did not concern himself with such small matters. If anything happened to the boy, how could she face Grandmother Jia? She therefore sent a page to fetch him. Bao-yu had no choice but to come to her carriage.

‘Dear brother,’ she said, smiling, ‘you are a precious creature, as fine and delicate as any girl. Don’t go playing the monkey on horseback like the rest of them. Come down and ride with me. We two sisters will be much better off together, won’t we?’

Bao-yu at once dismounted and climbed into her carriage. The two went on their way laughing and talking.

Before long two riders came galloping along as though flying over the ground. Both leapt down near Xi-feng’s carriage, put their hands to it and reported, ‘There is a place to stop here, ma’am, if you would like to rest and change.’

Xi-feng at once sent to ask Lady Xing and Lady Wang what they wished. The messenger came back with the answer: ‘Their Ladyships say they need not stop. You are to please yourself, ma’am.’

On hearing this, Xi-feng decided to rest before going on. The pages turned the carriage horses out of the crowd and hurried north. From inside the carriage Bao-yu urgently told them to invite Master Qin.

Qin Zhong was riding beside his father’s sedan-chair when Bao-yu’s page ran up to ask him to come for refreshments. Looking ahead, he saw Xi-feng’s carriage heading north, with Bao-yu’s horse led behind it, the saddle-cover laid over the saddle. Realising that Bao-yu was riding with Xi-feng, he turned his own horse and caught them up. They went in together through the gate of a farmstead.

The household servants had already driven all the farm men out. There were few rooms in the farmhouse, and the women had nowhere to withdraw to, so they had to be left where they were. How could the village girls and country women help wanting to look at Xi-feng, Bao-yu and Qin Zhong, with their fine looks and clothes, their manners and bearing?

Xi-feng went into the thatched hall and told Bao-yu and the others to go out and amuse themselves for a while. Understanding her, Bao-yu came out with Qin Zhong, and they wandered about with the pages. Everything used on the farm was new to them. Bao-yu marvelled at the spades, mattocks, hoes and ploughs, with no idea what they were for or what they were called. The pages told him each name and explained its use. Bao-yu nodded and sighed.

‘No wonder the old poet wrote:

“Who knows that in the meal upon the plate Each single grain has cost such bitter toil?”

‘This is just what he meant.’

As he spoke he came to another room, where he saw a spinning-wheel on the kang.

‘And what is this?’ he asked the pages.

They explained that too. Bao-yu went up and began turning it for amusement, quite delighted with himself. Just then a country girl of seventeen or eighteen ran in, crying out, ‘Don’t touch that! You’ll break it!’

The pages at once shouted her down and barred her way. Bao-yu quickly let go of the wheel and gave her an apologetic smile.

‘I’d never seen one before. I only wanted to try it.’

‘You wouldn’t know how to work it,’ the girl said. ‘Stand aside and I’ll spin for you to watch.’

Qin Zhong gave Bao-yu a secret tug and said with a smile, ‘Now here’s a young lady with quite a charm of her own.’

Bao-yu pushed him away, laughing. ‘You wretch! Say any more nonsense and I’ll hit you!’

The girl began spinning. Bao-yu was just about to speak when an old woman called from elsewhere, ‘Second Girl! Come here at once!’

The girl left the wheel and went straight off.

Bao-yu was left feeling flat and disappointed. Just then Xi-feng sent someone to call them both inside. Having washed her hands, changed her clothes and shaken out the dust, she asked whether they wanted to change too. Bao-yu did not, so she let the matter drop. The women servants brought in the teapot and cups, the tiered box of assorted refreshments and the various snacks they had brought for the journey. Xi-feng and the others took tea, waited while everything was packed away, then got up to return to the carriage.

Outside, Wang-er had prepared packets of gratuities and handed them to the owner of the farmstead. The farm women came to kowtow in thanks. Xi-feng paid no particular attention, but Bao-yu looked carefully: Second Girl was not among them. They got into the carriage and had not gone far when they saw her coming towards them, carrying her little brother and laughing and talking with several young girls. Bao-yu longed to get down and follow her, but he knew the others would never allow it. He could only follow her with his eyes. The carriage was light, the horses swift: in a moment she had vanished from sight.

They soon caught up with the funeral procession. Ahead of them, amid ritual drums and brass cymbals, banners, streamers and jewelled canopies, the monks from the Temple of the Iron Threshold had come out to receive the coffin. Before long they entered the temple, where further Buddhist rites were performed and the incense altar was set up afresh. The coffin was placed in a side chamber of the inner hall, and Jewel was lodged in an inner bedroom to keep it company.

Outside, Jia Zhen entertained all the friends and relations. Some stayed for a meal; others took their leave without eating. He thanked each for the trouble they had taken. The guests departed in groups, beginning with the dukes, then the marquises, earls, viscounts and barons. Not until the end of the wei hour had they all gone. Inside, Xi-feng attended to the women guests and saw to their reception. The ladies with imperial titles belonging to the highest officials’ households left first, and it was well past noon before the last of them had departed. Only a few of the closest relations remained: they would wait until the three days of rites for the resting coffin were over.

Lady Xing and Lady Wang knew that Xi-feng would be unable to return home, and were preparing to go back to the city. Lady Wang wanted to take Bao-yu with her, but he had only just reached the countryside and would not hear of leaving. He insisted on staying with Xi-feng. Lady Wang could do nothing with him; she left him in Xi-feng’s charge and went home.

The Temple of the Iron Threshold had originally been built by the Dukes of Ning and Rong. It still had an endowment of farmland to support its religious services, and provided a convenient place where the coffins of members of the family who died in the capital could rest temporarily. Accommodation for both the dead and the living had been arranged, so that those attending the coffins could stay there too.

By now, however, their descendants had grown numerous. Some were rich, some poor, and their dispositions differed as widely as stars that never meet. Those whose means were straitened and who were content with their lot stayed at the temple. Others, with money, influence and a taste for display, declared it inconvenient and insisted on finding rooms at a farmstead or nunnery elsewhere, where they could retire once the ceremonies and feasting were over. For Qin-shi’s funeral the other members of the clan were all lodging for the time being at the Temple of the Iron Threshold. Xi-feng alone found it inconvenient. She had sent ahead to Jing-xu, the nun at Steamed-bun Hermitage, to have two rooms cleared for her.

Steamed-bun Hermitage was in fact Water-Moon Hermitage. Its steamed buns were so good that they had earned it this nickname. It stood not far from the Temple of the Iron Threshold.

When the monks had finished their offices and the evening tea had been offered to the departed, Jia Zhen sent Jia Rong to ask Xi-feng to go and rest. Seeing that several other wives of her generation in the clan were still there to keep the women relations company, she took leave of them all and set out for Water-Moon Hermitage with Bao-yu and Qin Zhong. Qin Ye was old and frequently ill, and could not stay himself. He had merely told Qin Zhong to remain until the rites for the resting coffin were finished. Qin Zhong accordingly kept with Xi-feng and Bao-yu.

When they reached Water-Moon Hermitage, Jing-xu came out to welcome them with her two disciples, Zhi-shan and Sapientia. Everyone exchanged greetings. Xi-feng and her party went into the quiet rooms, changed and washed their hands. Seeing how much taller and prettier Sapientia had grown, Xi-feng said, ‘Why haven’t you and your disciples been to see us all this time?’

‘We really have had no time these last few days,’ Jing-xu answered. ‘There has been a son born in Mr Hu’s household. His wife sent us ten taels of silver to engage several nuns to recite the Blood-bowl Sutra for three days. We have scarcely had a moment free, so I have not been able to come and pay my respects to you, ma’am.’

Let us leave the old nun keeping Xi-feng company.

Bao-yu and Qin Zhong were amusing themselves in the hall when they saw Sapientia coming.

‘Here’s Sapientia,’ Bao-yu said with a smile.

‘What do you want with that creature?’ said Qin Zhong.

‘Don’t try your tricks on me,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘The other day, when there was nobody else in Grandmother’s room, what were you doing with your arms round her? And now you’re still trying to fool me!’

Qin Zhong laughed. ‘There’s no truth in that.’

Bao-yu smiled. ‘True or not, I shan’t bother you about it. Just tell her to pour me a cup of tea, and I’ll let you off.’

Qin Zhong laughed. ‘That’s a queer thing to say. Tell her yourself. Do you think she’ll refuse? Why must I ask?’

‘If I ask her, there’s no affection in the tea. It won’t be like the tea you ask her for: that will have affection in it.’

Qin Zhong had no choice but to say, ‘Sapientia, bring me a cup of tea.’

Sapientia had visited the Rong mansion since she was a small child. Everyone knew her, and she often played and joked with Bao-yu and Qin Zhong. Now that she was growing up and beginning to understand affairs of love, Qin Zhong’s handsome, graceful bearing had caught her fancy. He, in turn, was very taken with her pretty, winning ways. Though they had not yet been intimate, they were already drawn to one another.

The sight of Qin Zhong now filled Sapientia with delight, and she went to pour the tea. When she brought it back, Qin Zhong said with a smile, ‘Give it to me.’

‘Give it to me!’ cried Bao-yu.

Sapientia smiled with her lips pressed together. ‘Fighting over a cup of tea! Have I got honey on my hands?’

Bao-yu snatched it first. He was drinking and about to ask her something when Zhi-shan came to call Sapientia away to set out the tea dishes. Presently they invited the boys in for tea, fruit and cakes. Neither was interested in food of that sort. They sat for a little while, then went out to amuse themselves again.

Xi-feng too sat only a little while before returning to rest in the quiet rooms, with the old nun escorting her. Finding that there was nothing more to do, the women servants drifted away one by one to take their ease. Only a few trusted young maids who regularly attended her remained. The old nun took her opportunity.

‘There is a matter I was going to bring to Her Ladyship at the mansion. Might I first ask your advice about it, ma’am?’

Xi-feng asked what it was.

‘Amitabha! It began back when I first took my vows at Shancai Hermitage in Chang’an county. We had a benefactor there named Zhang, a very rich man. He had a daughter whose childhood name was Jin-ge. One year they all came to the hermitage to offer incense, and she happened to meet young Master Li, the younger brother of the prefect of Chang’an’s wife. He set his heart on marrying Jin-ge and sent someone to ask for her hand. But she had already accepted the betrothal gifts of the son of the former garrison commandant. The Zhang family were afraid that the commandant would object if they broke off the match, so they said she was already promised. Young Master Li would not accept that. He insisted on marrying the daughter.

‘The Zhang family could think of no way out, caught between the two sides. Then the commandant’s family heard of it. Without troubling to find out the rights and wrongs, they came and abused and insulted the Zhangs, asking how many families they meant to promise one girl to. They flatly refused to take back the betrothal gifts, and brought a lawsuit. The Zhangs were so distressed that they sent someone to the capital to find a way of getting help. Out of sheer anger, they were now determined to return the gifts. It occurred to me that Mr Yun, the military commissioner of Chang’an, is a very close friend of your household. If we could ask Her Ladyship to speak to His Lordship, and have him send a letter asking Mr Yun to put in a word with the commandant, I am sure the commandant would give way.

‘If it can be done, the Zhang family would gladly give all they possess to show their gratitude.’

Xi-feng smiled. ‘It’s no great matter, but Her Ladyship no longer concerns herself with that sort of thing.’

‘If Her Ladyship will not,’ the old nun said, ‘you could take it in hand yourself, ma’am.’

‘I am not short of money,’ Xi-feng said with a smile, ‘and I don’t do that sort of thing either.’

Jing-xu abandoned her hopes. After a long silence she sighed.

‘Of course. Only, the Zhangs already know I have come to ask your household for help. If nothing is done, they won’t understand that you have no time for it and have no interest in their gifts of thanks. It will look as though your household has not even the influence to manage this little thing.’

This stirred Xi-feng’s interest.

‘You have known me long enough. I have never believed in all that talk of the underworld, hell and retribution. Whatever it is, if I say it can be done, it can. Tell him to bring three thousand taels of silver, and I’ll see that he has his satisfaction.’

The old nun was beside herself with joy. ‘Yes, yes! That will be no difficulty.’

‘I am not like those go-betweens who pull strings for money,’ Xi-feng went on. ‘The three thousand is simply for the travelling expenses of the servants I send to speak for him, and to let them earn a little for their trouble. I don’t want a single coin of it myself. I could lay my hands on thirty thousand taels this very moment.’

The nun hastened to agree. ‘Then perhaps you would be so kind as to do it tomorrow, ma’am?’

‘Just look at how busy I am. Where can they do without me? Since I’ve promised you, naturally I’ll settle it as quickly as I can.’

‘Anyone else would be quite overwhelmed by a matter like this, but for you, ma’am, even a few more would hardly give your abilities room to show themselves.

‘But as the saying goes, “The able bear the heavier load.” Her Ladyship sees how well you manage everything, great and small, and leaves more and more to you. You must take care of your precious health, ma’am.’

These flattering remarks pleased Xi-feng more and more. Forgetting her fatigue, she went on talking.

Meanwhile Qin Zhong took advantage of the darkness and the absence of other people to look for Sapientia. He came to a room at the back and found her alone, washing the teacups. He ran up, put his arms round her and kissed her. Sapientia stamped her foot in distress.

‘What do you mean by this? Do that again and I’ll shout!’

‘My dear, I’m desperate,’ he pleaded. ‘If you refuse me again today, I’ll die here.’

‘What do you expect? Only when I’m out of this prison and away from these people will I agree.’

‘That’s easy enough. But distant water won’t quench thirst here and now.’

He blew out the lamp, leaving the room pitch-dark, carried Sapientia to the kang and lay with her. She struggled in every way to get free, but could not; unable to bring herself to call out, she had no choice but to yield.

Just as Qin Zhong was enjoying himself, someone came in and held them both down without a word. Not knowing who it was, they were too frightened to move. Then the intruder gave a snort and could no longer keep from laughing. Only when they heard his voice did they realise it was Bao-yu. Qin Zhong scrambled up.

‘What do you mean by this?’ he complained.

Bao-yu laughed. ‘If you’re going to object, I’ll shout.’

Sapientia slipped away through the darkness in shame. Bao-yu pulled Qin Zhong outside.

‘Will you still try to get the better of me?’

‘My dear fellow,’ Qin Zhong said with a smile, ‘just don’t shout and let everyone know. I’ll do anything you want.’

Bao-yu laughed. ‘No need to discuss it now. We’ll settle accounts properly when we go to bed.’

When it was time to undress and retire, Xi-feng slept in the inner room and Qin Zhong and Bao-yu in the outer one. The floor was covered with bedding for the household women who sat up on watch. Afraid that the Spiritual Jade might be lost, Xi-feng waited until Bao-yu was in bed, then had it brought to her and tucked it beside her own pillow.

What accounts Bao-yu settled with Qin Zhong is not known. It was not clearly witnessed or recorded; the matter remains in doubt, and I would not venture to invent it.

The night passed without further incident. Early the next morning, Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang sent people to see how Bao-yu was, with instructions that he should put on a couple more layers of clothing and, if there was nothing to keep him, come home. Bao-yu would not hear of going. Qin Zhong, moreover, was reluctant to leave Sapientia and urged him to ask Xi-feng to stay another day.

Xi-feng considered it. The important funeral arrangements were all in order, but there were still one or two small matters to settle. She could use these as a reason to stay another day: would that not be doing Jia Zhen a handsome favour as well? Secondly, she could finish Jing-xu’s business; thirdly, Bao-yu would have his wish, and Grandmother Jia would surely be pleased to hear of it. With these three advantages in mind, she said to Bao-yu, ‘All my work is done. But if you want to stay here and enjoy yourself, I suppose I must put myself out for one more day. Tomorrow, though, we really are leaving.’

Bao-yu called her his dear sister a thousand times over and begged, ‘Just one more day. We’ll go back tomorrow.’

So they stayed another night. Xi-feng had Lai Wang-er quietly informed of the matter the old nun had brought up the day before. He understood at once, hurried into the city and found the gentleman who handled the household’s correspondence. Pretending that Jia Lian had given the instructions, he had a letter written, then set off that very night for Chang’an county. It was only a hundred li away, and within two days everything had been arranged. The military commissioner, whose name was Yun Guang, had long been indebted to the Jia family. How could he refuse so small a request? He gave a letter in reply, and Wang-er returned. Let us leave that for the moment.

Xi-feng and the others stayed another day, then took leave of the old nun the following morning. Xi-feng told her to come to the mansion three days later for news. Qin Zhong and Sapientia could scarcely bear to part. Their secret promises and arrangements to meet need not be recounted in detail. At last they had to separate, full of regret. Xi-feng went to the Temple of the Iron Threshold once more to see how things stood.

Jewel insisted that she would not go home, and Jia Zhen had to assign women to stay with her. We shall return to this in the next chapter.
