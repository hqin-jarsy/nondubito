Chapter 50 At Reed Snow Lodge they vie in linking verses on the scene; At Warm Fragrance Retreat they fashion elegant lantern riddles

Chapter end

‘We must settle the order, so that I can write it down,’ said Bao-chai, and had them draw lots for their places.

‘In that case,’ said Xi-feng, ‘I’ll say a line to go at the beginning.’

‘Better still!’ they all said, laughing.

Bao-chai added the character Feng above the Old Farmer of Sweet-rice Village, and Li Wan explained the subject to her. After thinking for some time, Xi-feng laughed.

‘Don’t make fun of me. I’ve only one plain, ordinary line. What comes after that I haven’t the least idea.’

‘The plainer the better,’ they said, laughing. ‘Give us your line, then go and see to your proper business.’

‘I thought there must be a north wind when it snows,’ said Xi-feng. ‘I heard it blowing all last night, and that gave me a line: “All night the north wind blew hard.” Will that do?’

They exchanged amused glances.

‘It may be plain, but you can’t tell what’s coming next. That’s just how someone who knows about poetry would begin.

‘It’s not only good; it leaves so much room for those who come after. We’ll take it for our opening. Old Farmer, write it down quickly and carry on.’

Xi-feng, Aunt Li and Patience drank another couple of cups of wine and went away. Li Wan wrote:

All night the north wind blew hard;

Then added her own lines:

I open the door: snow still drifts.

Its pure white sinks into mud: how sad;

Caltrop continued:

Its precious jade covers the ground: how dear.

With purpose it lends dead grass splendour;

Tan-chun said:

Without intent it decks the withered reeds.

Village wine, well matured, commands a high price;

Li Qi said:

A rich year fills the granaries with grain.

Reed ash stirs and flies from the pitch-pipes;

Li Wen said:

Yang returns; the Dipper’s handle turns.

The cold hills have already lost their green;

Xiu-yan said:

No tide is heard along the frozen shore.

It hangs with ease on sparse-branched willows;

Xiang-yun said:

On torn plantain leaves it scarcely heaps.

Musk-scented incense melts in precious censers;

Bao-qin said:

Brocaded sleeves enclose gold-brown sable.

Its light outshines the mirror by the window;

Dai-yu said:

Its scent clings to the pepper on the walls.

The slanting wind persists and still persists;

Bao-yu said:

Clear dreams grow faint and indistinct.

Where plays the flute its ‘Plum Blossom’ tune?

Bao-chai said:

Whose house sends out a green-jade pipe’s song?

The great turtle fears earth’s axis sinking;

‘Let me go and see to warming your wine,’ said Li Wan, smiling.

Bao-chai told Bao-qin to continue, but Xiang-yun stood up and said:

As dragons fight, the massed clouds melt.

By a wild bank a lone boat turns home;

Bao-qin rose too:

The poet’s riding-whip points to Ba Bridge.

A gift of furs brings comfort to the garrison;

Xiang-yun would yield to no one, and none of the others was so quick. They all watched her straighten up, eyebrows raised, and say:

Added padding recalls those far on service.

In hollows, over mounds, judge smooth and perilous ground;

Bao-chai praised her repeatedly, then joined in herself:

We fear the swaying of the branches.

White upon white, it follows lightly at our steps;

Dai-yu hurried to continue:

Delicately, it dances with our waists.

Boiling taros makes a fresh delight;

As she spoke, she nudged Bao-yu and told him to add a line. He had been enjoying the sight of Bao-chai, Bao-qin and Dai-yu all fighting Xiang-yun far too much to think about linking verses. Only at Dai-yu’s nudge did he say:

Scattering salt belongs to an old song.

A reed-cloaked angler still moors his boat to fish;

‘Do get down,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing. ‘You’re no use. You’re only holding me up.’

But now Bao-qin was heard continuing:

No woodman’s axe sounds in the woods.

A thousand peaks rise, crouching elephants;

Xiang-yun hastened to follow:

One distant path coils like a snake.

These flowers take shape because the cold congeals them;

Bao-chai and the others hastened to applaud again. Tan-chun added:

Why should their colour fear the blight of frost?

Deep in the courtyard, sparrows start with cold;

Thirsty now, Xiang-yun was hurriedly drinking tea when Xiu-yan got in:

In the empty hills, an old owl cries.

Along the steps it follows rise and fall;

Xiang-yun hastily put down her cup and continued:

Upon the pool it floats and drifts at will.

Brightly it shines towards the clear dawn;

Dai-yu followed:

Thickly it whirls into the long night.

Truly we forget the cold three chi deep;

Xiang-yun laughed and promptly added:

This happy omen soothes the ninefold palace’s care.

Who comes to ask of one who lies stiff with cold?

Bao-qin too hurried in, laughing:

A guest on a wild outing finds glad welcome.

Heaven’s loom breaks bands of plain white silk;

Xiang-yun hurried on:

The sea’s mirage loses its merfolk gauze.

Before she could give another line, Dai-yu cut in:

Alone it faces terraces and pavilions;

Xiang-yun promptly continued:

In honest poverty, one thinks of basket and gourd.

Bao-qin showed no mercy either, but hurried in:

For brewing tea, the ice comes slowly to the boil;

Xiang-yun was thoroughly enjoying this. Laughing, she hastened to follow:

For warming wine, the leaves are hard to burn.

Dai-yu too laughed:

It buries the mountain monk’s broom as he sweeps;

Bao-qin too laughed:

It covers the qin the young boy bears.

Xiang-yun was bent double with laughter. She hurriedly recited a line, but the others asked, ‘What did you actually say?’ She shouted:

In a stone tower, the crane sleeps at ease;

Holding her chest with laughter, Dai-yu called out loudly:

In warm figured wool, we nestle with the cat.

Bao-qin hurried in again, laughing:

The moon’s cavern heaves with silver waves;

Xiang-yun hastened to follow:

The rosy-cloud city hides its crimson heights.

Dai-yu laughed and quickly said:

Steeped in plum blossom, its scent can be savoured;

Bao-chai laughed her approval, and hurried to continue:

Snow-drenched bamboo, tipsy, invites our play.

Bao-qin too came in quickly:

Now it wets a mandarin-duck sash;

Xiang-yun promptly followed:

Now it freezes on a kingfisher hairpin.

Dai-yu hurried on again:

Without wind, still softly, softly;

Bao-qin laughed and swiftly added:

Without rain, still soughing, soughing.

Xiang-yun had collapsed, helpless with laughter. Watching the three of them compete to get in first, the others had abandoned their own poems and could only laugh. Dai-yu kept pushing Xiang-yun to carry on.

‘So even you run out of talent! Let’s hear what else that tongue of yours can chew over!’

Xiang-yun merely lay in Bao-chai’s arms, laughing without stopping. Bao-chai pushed her upright.

‘If you’re so clever, use up every rhyme in the second group, xiao. Then I’ll admit you’ve beaten me.’

Xiang-yun sat up, laughing. ‘I wasn’t writing poetry at all. I was fighting for my life!’

‘Go on, then,’ they all laughed.

Tan-chun had long since realised that she would get no more lines in and had been writing out what they said.

‘We still haven’t brought it to a close,’ she observed.

At that, Li Wan took it up with a line:

To set down the pleasure of this morning,

Li Qi supplied the closing line:

Let verse wish blessings on our Shun and Yao.

‘Enough, enough,’ said Li Wan. ‘We haven’t used all the rhymes, but forcing in the ones that are left would only spoil it.’

They went through the poem carefully together. Xiang-yun’s lines far outnumbered anyone else’s.

‘All thanks to that piece of venison!’ they said, laughing.

‘Taken line by line, it holds together very well,’ Li Wan said with a smile. ‘Only Bao-yu has failed the examination again.’

‘I’m no good at linking verses,’ he said, laughing. ‘You’ll have to make allowances for me.’

‘We can’t make allowances at every meeting. First the rhymes are too difficult, then you go and get it wrong, then you can’t link verses. Today you must pay a forfeit. I noticed how lovely the red plum blossom was at Green Bower Hermitage. I want a branch for a vase, but Adamantina is such a tiresome person that I won’t have anything to do with her. Your forfeit is to fetch me a branch.’

Everyone agreed that this was both an elegant and an amusing penalty. Bao-yu was delighted to oblige and, accepting it, was about to leave when Xiang-yun and Dai-yu spoke together.

‘It’s terribly cold outside. Have a cup of hot wine before you go.’

Xiang-yun had already picked up the wine pot. Dai-yu passed her a large cup, and Xiang-yun filled it to the brim.

‘Once you’ve drunk our wine,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing, ‘your forfeit will be doubled if you don’t bring it back.’

Bao-yu quickly drank the cupful and set off through the snow. Li Wan ordered someone to go with him and take good care of him, but Dai-yu stopped her.

‘No need. Having someone with him would only spoil everything.’

‘Yes,’ Li Wan said, nodding.

She had a maid bring a slender, high-shouldered vase and fill it with water, ready for the plum blossom.

‘When he comes back, we ought to write about red plum blossom,’ she added, smiling.

‘I’ll do the first one,’ Xiang-yun said at once.

‘You certainly shan’t write any more today,’ said Bao-chai. ‘You snatched all the lines for yourself, and there was nothing left for anyone else. That’s no fun. When Bao-yu comes back, we’ll give him another forfeit. He says he can’t link verses; now he can write a poem on his own.’

‘Quite right,’ said Dai-yu, smiling. ‘I have another idea. Some of us didn’t get enough lines in just now. Why not choose the ones who had fewest, and let them write about the red plum blossom?’

‘Exactly,’ said Bao-chai.

‘We gave Cousin Xing and the two Li cousins no chance to show what they could do, and they’re our guests besides. Qin, our Pin-pin and Yun snatched plenty of lines between them. The rest of us should all keep out of it and leave these three to write.’

‘Qi isn’t much good at poetry either,’ Li Wan said. ‘Let Cousin Qin do it instead.’

Bao-chai could only agree.

‘Then we’ll use the three characters hong, mei, hua, “red plum blossom”, for the rhymes: one regulated eight-line poem of seven characters a line apiece.

‘Cousin Xing will take hong, “red”; your elder Cousin Li will take mei, “plum”; and Qin will take hua, “blossom”.’

‘But I won’t agree to letting Bao-yu off,’ said Li Wan.

‘I have a good subject to set him,’ Xiang-yun said quickly.

They asked her what it was.

‘“Calling on Adamantina to Beg Red Plum Blossom”. Wouldn’t that be fun?’

They all agreed that it would. She had hardly finished speaking when Bao-yu came in beaming, a branch of red plum blossom over his shoulder. The maids hurried to take it and put it in the vase. Everyone thanked him with smiles.

‘Enjoy it now,’ said Bao-yu. ‘You’ve no idea how much trouble it cost me.’

Tan-chun had already brought him another cup of warm wine. The maids came forward to take his rain-cape and hat and brush off the snow. Maids from all their rooms had brought more clothes; Aroma too had sent someone with a partly worn jacket of fox-underarm fur. Li Wan had a plate filled with the large steamed taros, and two more with red mandarins, yellow oranges, olives and other fruit, and sent them to Aroma.

Xiang-yun told Bao-yu the subject they had chosen and urged him to begin at once.

‘My dear sisters, let me choose my own rhymes,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Don’t prescribe them.’

‘Write it as you please,’ they all said.

As they talked, they admired the plum blossom. The branch stood only about two chi high, but a side bough thrust out across it, some five or six chi long. Smaller branches forked from it: some like coiled hornless dragons, some like stiff earthworms, some single and spare as brushes, others clustered thickly as a wood. The flowers opened in rouge-red, their scent surpassing orchids. Everyone praised them.

Meanwhile Xing Xiu-yan, Li Wen and Xue Bao-qin had all finished composing and written out their poems. The others read them in the order of the characters hong, mei, hua:

On Red Plum Blossom: the rhyme word hong, ‘red’ Xing Xiu-yan

Peach has not flowered, nor apricot blushed red;

Braving the cold, you greet the east wind first with smiles.

The soul flies to Yu Ridge: spring is hard to tell;

Rosy clouds veil Luofu: the dream cannot pass through.

Green Calyx adds her rouge; rich candles melt;

The white-clad immortal, tipsy, mounts a fading rainbow.

How could this be an ordinary colour?

Deep or pale, let it have its way in ice and snow.

On Red Plum Blossom: the rhyme word mei, ‘plum’ Li Wen

I care not to sing white plum, but sing the red;

Displaying splendour, first you open to a drunken gaze.

Each mark upon your frozen face is blood;

Your sour heart, without resentment, turns to ash.

An elixir swallowed by mistake has changed your very bones;

Slipping down from the Jade Pool, you have shed your former shape.

North and south of the river, spring shines bright;

Tell bees and butterflies: spare your idle doubts.

On Red Plum Blossom: the rhyme word hua, ‘blossom’ Xue Bao-qin

Sparse are the branches, lavish are the flowers;

Young girls in spring attire vie in their finery.

No snow remains by quiet court and winding rail;

By running water in empty hills, sunset clouds linger.

A secret dream, cold, follows the red sleeve’s flute;

Fragrance floats through immortal realms on a Crimson River raft.

In a former life you must have grown on the Jade Terrace;

Let difference of colour rouse no further doubt.

When they had read the poems, they praised them all with smiles, pointing out the last as the best. Bao-yu was astonished that Bao-qin, the youngest of them, should be so quick and gifted. Dai-yu and Xiang-yun poured a little cup of wine and offered her their congratulations together.

‘Each has its own merits,’ Bao-chai said, laughing. ‘You two have grown tired of teasing me every day, and now you’ve started on her.’

Li Wan asked Bao-yu, ‘Have you got yours yet?’

‘I had,’ he said quickly, ‘but those three frightened it out of my head. Let me think again.’

Xiang-yun took a brass fire-chopstick and struck the hand-warmer with it.

‘I’m beating the drum,’ she said, laughing. ‘If you haven’t finished when the drum stops, there’ll be another forfeit.’

‘I’ve got it,’ said Bao-yu, laughing.

Dai-yu picked up her brush. ‘You recite; I’ll write.’

Xiang-yun struck once. ‘The first roll is over!’

‘I’ve got it,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘Write it down.’

They heard him recite:

Before the wine jar’s opened, before a line is shaped,

Dai-yu wrote it down and shook her head with a smile.

‘A very ordinary opening.’

‘Quickly!’ Xiang-yun said again.

Bao-yu smiled:

I come to Penglai to seek spring and ask after winter.

Dai-yu and Xiang-yun both nodded, smiling. ‘There’s something in that.’

Bao-yu went on:

Not for the dew in the Bodhisattva’s vase I come,

But to beg plum blossom beyond the Moon Lady’s rail.

Dai-yu wrote it down, then shook her head again. ‘It just happens to fit.’

Xiang-yun hurried him on with a second drum-roll. Bao-yu continued, smiling:

Into the world I bear red snow through cold;

Leaving the dust, I cut fragrant purple cloud and bring it home.

Who pities the poet’s thin shoulder under this gnarled bough?

His clothes still carry moss from the Buddhist court.

Dai-yu finished writing. Xiang-yun and the others had only just begun to discuss the poem when several maids ran in.

‘Her Old Ladyship is coming!’

They hurried out to meet her, exclaiming with delight, ‘What a happy thought!’

They could see Grandmother Jia in the distance, wrapped in a great cloak, with a grey-squirrel hood to keep her warm, sitting in a small bamboo chair beneath an oiled blue-silk umbrella. Faithful, Amber and five or six maids in all, each carrying an umbrella, surrounded the chair. Li Wan and the others went forward, but Grandmother Jia had them stopped.

‘Stay where you are.’

When she reached them, she smiled.

‘I’ve come without telling your mother or Feng. There’s no harm in my sitting in this, even with the snow so deep. No need to make them come tramping through it.’

They agreed as they helped her out and took her cloak. On entering the room she exclaimed, smiling, ‘What beautiful plum blossom! You certainly know how to enjoy yourselves. I’ve come to the right place.’

Li Wan had already ordered a large wolfskin rug to be spread in the middle.

Grandmother Jia sat down.

‘Go on laughing and eating and drinking,’ she said with a smile. ‘The days are so short now I daren’t take a midday nap. I played cards for a while, then thought of you, and came to join in the fun.’

Li Wan brought her a hand-warmer. Tan-chun fetched a fresh cup and chopsticks, poured warm wine herself and offered it to Grandmother Jia. She took a sip, then asked what was on one of the plates. They hurriedly brought it over and told her it was quail preserved in wine lees.

‘That will do. Tear me off a scrap or two of the leg.’

Li Wan agreed at once, asked for water to wash her hands and tore off the meat herself.

‘Sit down again and talk,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Let me listen.’

She told Li Wan, ‘You sit down too. I want you to behave just as if I weren’t here, or I shall go away.’

Only then did they resume their seats in order, Li Wan moving to the very lowest place. Grandmother Jia asked what they had been doing, and they told her they had been writing poems.

‘Instead of poems,’ she said, ‘why not make some lantern riddles for us all to enjoy in the first month?’

They agreed. After a little more talk and laughter, Grandmother Jia said, ‘It’s damp here. Don’t sit too long; you mustn’t let the damp get into you.’

‘Fourth Sister’s rooms are warm. Let’s go and see her painting. Will it be ready for the New Year?’

‘How could it be ready by then?’ they said, laughing. ‘We’ll be lucky to have it by next year’s Dragon Boat Festival.’

‘This is too much! She’s taking longer than they took to build the garden.’

She got back into the bamboo chair. The others clustered round and followed, past Lotus Pavilion and into a narrow passage. At its eastern and western ends were gateways spanning the path, with stone tablets set into both the inner and outer faces of their gatehouses. They entered by the western gate: the tablet outside was carved with ‘Through the Clouds’, the one inside with ‘Across the Moon’. At the middle of the passage they went through the main south-facing entrance, and Grandmother Jia got out of her chair. Xi-chun had already come out to meet her.

An inner covered walk led to Xi-chun’s bedroom. Above its entrance were the three characters ‘Warm Fragrance Retreat’. Several servants lifted the scarlet felt curtain, and warmth and fragrance brushed their faces even before they entered. Grandmother Jia did not sit down, but asked where the painting was.

‘It’s turned cold,’ Xi-chun replied with a smile. ‘The glue has all thickened and set; it won’t flow smoothly. I was afraid the painting would look bad if I went on, so I put it away.’

‘I want it for the New Year,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘No making excuses to be lazy. Get it out and hurry on with it.’

Before she had finished, Xi-feng arrived in a purple lambskin jacket, smiling broadly.

‘Our old ancestor slipped away today without a word to anyone. What a search you’ve given me!’

Grandmother Jia was naturally delighted to see her.

‘I was afraid you’d get cold, so I wouldn’t let anyone tell you.

‘You really are a clever little imp, finding me out all the same. Strictly speaking, this isn’t what being dutiful consists of.’

‘Dutiful? That wasn’t why I came looking for you,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘I went to your rooms, and there wasn’t a sound, not a crow or a sparrow stirring.

‘I asked the little maids, but they wouldn’t tell me, so I had to come hunting in the garden. I was still wondering about it when two or three nuns arrived, and then I understood. They must have come with the New Year prayer papers, I thought, or to collect their annual allowance and incense money. With so many calls on our old ancestor at the year’s end, she must have gone into hiding from her creditors. I hurried to ask the nuns, and sure enough, I was right. I gave them their annual allowance straight away and sent them off. Now I’ve come to report that the creditors have gone, and you needn’t hide any longer.

‘There’s a beautifully tender pheasant ready. Do come to dinner; leave it any longer and it’ll be tough.’

All through this speech the others were laughing. Without even waiting for Grandmother Jia to answer, Xi-feng ordered the chair brought round. Laughing, Grandmother Jia took her hand and got in again. With everyone about her, she went out through the eastern gate of the passage amid talk and laughter.

All round them lay a world adorned in powder and built in silver. Suddenly they saw Bao-qin in her duck-feather cloak, standing on the slope waiting for them at a distance. Behind her a maid held a vase of red plum blossom.

‘So that’s where our two missing people are,’ they all said, laughing. ‘Waiting for us here! She’s been after plum blossom too.’

Grandmother Jia was delighted.

‘Look at her on that slope,’ she said eagerly, smiling. ‘With her looks, and that cloak, and the plum blossom behind her. What does it remind you of?’

‘Just like the picture by Qiu Shizhou in your room, “Two Beauties”,’ they all said, smiling.

Grandmother Jia shook her head, laughing.

‘Where is there a cloak like this in that picture? And the person in it could never be so lovely!’

Just then, from behind Bao-qin stepped someone in a scarlet broadcloth cloak.

‘Which girl is that?’ Grandmother Jia asked.

‘We’re all here,’ they said, laughing. ‘That’s Bao-yu.’

‘My eyes are getting worse and worse,’ she said with a laugh.

By now they had drawn near, and Bao-yu and Bao-qin it certainly was.

‘I’ve just been to Green Bower Hermitage again,’ Bao-yu told Bao-chai, Dai-yu and the others with a smile. ‘Adamantina is sending each of you a branch of plum blossom. I’ve already had them sent round.’

They all smiled and thanked him for his trouble.

Talking as they went, they passed out of the garden gate and returned to Grandmother Jia’s rooms. After dinner they talked and laughed for a while longer. Then Aunt Xue arrived.

‘Such heavy snow! I haven’t come over all day to ask how you were. Haven’t you felt like enjoying yourself today? It’s just the day to admire the snow.’

‘Who says I haven’t enjoyed myself?’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘I went to find the girls and had some fun with them.’

‘Last night I was thinking of borrowing the garden from my sister for the day,’ Aunt Xue said with a smile, ‘and laying on a couple of tables of plain food and wine so that I could invite you to enjoy the snow. But you retired early. My daughter told me you weren’t feeling very cheerful, so I didn’t dare disturb you today.

‘If I’d known, I certainly should have invited you.’

‘This is only the first snow of the tenth month,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘There’ll be plenty more snowy days. It isn’t too late for you to go to that expense.’

‘Indeed,’ said Aunt Xue, smiling. ‘At least you can count the sincerity of my good intentions.’

‘Careful you don’t forget, Aunt,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Weigh out fifty taels now and let me keep them for you. As soon as it snows, I’ll have the party ready. Then you needn’t worry, and you can’t forget.’

‘In that case, give her the fifty taels to keep,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘We’ll take twenty-five each. When it snows I’ll pretend to be out of spirits, and we’ll let the day slip by. You’ll have still less to worry about, and Feng and I will be that much better off.’

Xi-feng clapped her hands. ‘Perfect! Exactly what I had in mind.’

Everyone laughed.

‘Pah! Shameless creature,’ said Grandmother Jia, laughing. ‘No sooner do I hold out a pole than you climb up it!

‘You ought to have said your aunt is our guest and puts up with enough in our house; we should be entertaining her. How could we let her bear the expense? Instead of saying that, you have the face to ask her for fifty taels in advance. Aren’t you ashamed?’

‘Our old ancestor knows which way the wind is blowing better than anyone,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘First she tries Aunt out. If Aunt’s hand is open enough to produce fifty taels, she’ll go halves with me. Now she reckons it won’t work, so she turns on me to make herself look good, and comes out with all these generous sentiments. Very well, I won’t ask Aunt for any money. I’ll pay for the party myself on her behalf and invite you to it. Then I’ll wrap up another fifty taels as a dutiful offering to you, a fine for taking other people’s business on myself. Will that do?’

Before she had finished, everyone was doubled up with laughter on the kang.

Grandmother Jia returned to the subject of Bao-qin gathering plum blossom in the snow, saying that the sight had been lovelier than a painting. She went on to ask in detail about the eight characters of Bao-qin’s birth date and time, and about her family’s circumstances. Aunt Xue guessed that she was probably thinking of a match for Bao-yu. She herself would have welcomed it, but Bao-qin was already promised to the Mei family. As Grandmother Jia had said nothing openly, Aunt Xue could not take it upon herself to assume anything. She therefore told her, partly revealing and partly withholding:

‘It’s a pity the child has had so little good fortune. Her father died the year before last. She’s seen a great deal of the world since she was small, though. She went everywhere with her parents, all over the mountains. Her father loved enjoying himself. He had business in different places and took his family along, spending a year travelling about one province, then half the next year in another. They must have covered five or six tenths of the whole country. The year they were here, he promised her to Academician Mei’s son. Then, the very next year, he died, and her mother has a phlegm disorder.’

Xi-feng did not wait for her to finish, but sighed and stamped her foot.

‘What bad luck! I was just going to make a match for her, and now she’s promised to someone already.’

‘Who were you going to match her with?’ Grandmother Jia asked, smiling.

‘Never you mind, old ancestor. I’d made up my mind those two were meant for one another.

‘But now she’s promised, there’s no point saying anything. Better leave it unsaid.’

Grandmother Jia understood what Xi-feng meant. Hearing that Bao-qin was already engaged, she too let the subject drop. They chatted for a while longer, then dispersed. The night passed without anything to relate.

Next day the snow had cleared. After the meal, Grandmother Jia again instructed Xi-chun herself.

‘Cold or warm, go on painting. If it really can’t be ready by the New Year, so be it. The most important thing is to put in Qin as she was yesterday, with the maid and the plum blossom, exactly as they looked. Not a brushstroke wrong. Add them as soon as you can.’

Xi-chun found this difficult, but could only agree. Presently everyone came to watch her paint. She simply sat lost in thought.

‘Let her think it out by herself,’ Li Wan said to the others with a smile. ‘We can talk. Grandmother said yesterday we were to make lantern riddles. When we got home, Qi and Wen and I couldn’t sleep, so I made up two with answers from the Four Books. They each made up two as well.’

‘Just what we ought to be doing,’ they all said, laughing. ‘Tell us yours first and let us guess.’

Li Wan smiled:

Guanyin has no recorded family biography.

‘Find a phrase in the Four Books.’

Xiang-yun promptly answered, ‘“To rest in perfect goodness.”’

‘Think about the words “recorded family biography” before you guess,’ Bao-chai said, smiling.

‘Try again,’ said Li Wan with a smile.

‘Oh, I have it,’ Dai-yu said, laughing.

‘“Though good, there is no evidence.”’

‘That’s it!’ they all said, laughing.1

Li Wan gave them another:

A pond of green grass: what is the grass called?

‘It must be “They are rushes and reeds”,’ Xiang-yun said at once. ‘Surely it can’t be wrong this time?’

‘That one made you work!’ Li Wan said, laughing. ‘Wen’s is:

Water flowing out beside stone brings cold.

‘Find the name of someone from ancient times.’

‘Could it be Shan Tao?’ Tan-chun asked, smiling.

‘Yes,’ Li Wen said with a smile.2

‘Qi’s clue is the character ying (萤), “firefly”,’ Li Wan went on. ‘The answer is one character.’

They puzzled over it for a long time. At last Bao-qin smiled.

‘There’s more to this one than meets the eye. Could it be hua (花), the character for “flower”?’

‘Exactly,’ said Li Qi, smiling.

‘What have fireflies to do with flowers?’ the others asked.

‘It’s very clever,’ Dai-yu said, laughing. ‘Don’t fireflies come from grass changing?’

They saw the point, laughed and applauded.3

‘These are good,’ said Bao-chai, ‘but not what Grandmother meant. Better make riddles about simple everyday things, so that everyone can enjoy them, learned or not.’

They all agreed. ‘We ought to have some about ordinary, familiar things too.’

‘I’ve composed a lyric to “Rouged Lips”,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing. ‘It happens to be about an everyday thing. See if you can guess.’

She recited:

Parted from streams and ravines,

Playing in the dusty world,

What pleasure is there, really?

Fame and gain are empty still;

What comes behind can have no continuation.

They could not make it out. After thinking for some time, some guessed a Buddhist monk, others a Taoist priest, and others a puppeteer. Bao-yu laughed for a long while.

‘None of those. I’ve guessed it. It must be a performing monkey.’

‘That’s exactly it,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing.

‘The beginning is all right,’ they said, ‘but how do you explain the last line?’

‘What performing monkey hasn’t had its tail chopped off?’ Xiang-yun replied.

They all laughed. ‘Even your riddles have to be odd and mischievous.’

‘Aunt said yesterday how much of the world Cousin Qin had seen and how far she’d travelled,’ said Li Wan. ‘You really ought to make some riddles. Now’s the time to put that to use. Your poems are so good too. Why not make a few for us to guess?’

Bao-qin nodded with a smile and went off to think. Bao-chai had thought of one too, and recited:

Sandalwood carved, zi wood cut, tier upon tier:

Could a fine craftsman have piled them into shape?

Though wind and rain pass halfway up the sky,

When has the sound of a temple bell been heard?

The answer was a thing. While they were guessing, Bao-yu thought of one too and recited:

In heaven and the human world, all is remote and dim;

Past the joints of green-jade bamboo, take careful heed.

At phoenix notes and crane-borne messages, fix your gaze;

With sighs and breaths, make answer to high heaven.

Dai-yu too had one. She recited:

What need to bind the steed Green-ear with purple cord?

It races round the city, sweeps the moat with fearsome force.

At its master’s direction, wind and thunder move;

On the great turtle’s back, three mountains stand renowned.

Tan-chun had one too and was about to recite it when Bao-qin came over, smiling.

‘There are quite a few ancient sites among the places I’ve visited since I was small. I’ve now chosen ten and written ten poems recalling the past. They’re poor verses, but besides remembering former times, they hide ten everyday things. Cousins, please try to guess them.’

‘What a clever idea!’ they all said. ‘Why not write them out so that we can all read them?’
