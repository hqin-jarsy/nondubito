Chapter 47 The blockhead tyrant’s advances earn him a savage beating; The cold young gentleman flees to distant parts for fear of trouble

Chapter end

When Lady Wang heard that Lady Xing had come, she hurried out to meet her. Lady Xing still did not know that Grandmother Jia had heard about Faithful; she had come to find out how matters stood. She was barely inside the courtyard gate when several old women quietly told her, and only then did she learn the truth. She would have turned back, but those inside already knew she was there, and Lady Wang was coming out to welcome her. She had no choice but to go in. First she paid her respects to Grandmother Jia, who would not say a word to her. She herself felt ashamed and sorry. Xi-feng had already found a pretext to withdraw. Faithful, too, had gone back to her room to nurse her anger. Aunt Xue, Lady Wang and the others, afraid of adding to Lady Xing’s embarrassment, gradually withdrew as well. Lady Xing did not yet dare leave.

When no one else was left, Grandmother Jia spoke.

‘I hear you have come matchmaking for your husband. You certainly observe the Three Obediences and Four Virtues1, but there is such a thing as being too virtuous a wife! You have sons and grandsons all about you now, yet you are still afraid of him. You can’t so much as say a word or two to restrain him. You let him carry on just as he pleases.’

Lady Xing’s face was scarlet.

‘I’ve tried several times, but he won’t listen. There’s nothing you don’t know about him, Mother. I really had no choice.’

‘If he forced you to murder someone, would you do that too? Think for a moment. Your sister-in-law is an unassuming soul, and she is always ailing. What is there in this whole household that she doesn’t have to worry about? Your daughter-in-law helps her, certainly, but she is at one job after another all day, dropping the rake to take up the broom. I’ve cut down my own demands in every way. If there are things those two fail to see to, Faithful is a careful girl and gives a little thought to my affairs. Whatever I ought to have, she goes and gets it. Whatever needs adding, she finds a moment to tell them so.

‘If Faithful didn’t do that, with everything those two have to look after, indoors and out, great and small, how could they help overlooking a thing or two? Am I to start worrying about things myself at my age? Or to spend my days making out lists of what to ask you for? Of all the people who have come and gone in these rooms, she is the only one left with a few years behind her and some knowledge of my habits and my temper. And then the masters and mistresses all like her. She doesn’t trade on my favour to ask this lady for clothes and that young mistress for money. That is why, these past few years, whatever the business, everyone has trusted what she said, from your sister-in-law and your daughter-in-law down to the least of the servants. I’m not the only one who depends on her. She saves them both a great deal of worry too. With someone like her here, even if my daughters-in-law and granddaughters-in-law forget something, I don’t have to go without it, and there is nothing to make me angry. Now, if she goes, whom are you going to find to look after me? You might find me a perfect pearl of a girl, but if she couldn’t say what was needed she would be no use.

‘I was just going to send someone to tell your husband: whatever sort of girl he wants, I have money here. Let him spend eight or ten thousand buying her, if he likes. But this one girl he cannot have. Leave her to look after me for a few more years, and it will be just as good as his waiting on me day and night like a dutiful son. You’ve come at the right moment. You can tell him yourself. That will be better still.’

Then she called for a servant.

‘Ask Aunt Xue and the girls to come and talk to me. We were just enjoying ourselves. Why has everyone gone?’

The maids answered at once and went off. Everyone hurried back, except Aunt Xue, who said to the maid:

‘I’ve only just come away. What am I to go back for? Tell her I’ve gone to sleep.’

‘My dear, dear lady, my honoured ancestress! Her Old Ladyship is angry. If you don’t come, there’ll be no end to it. Do come, for our sakes. If you’re tired, I’ll carry you on my back.’

‘You little imp! What are you so frightened of? She’ll scold you a little and that will be the end of it.’

Nevertheless, she had to go back with the little maid. Grandmother Jia hastened to offer her a seat, then said with a smile:

‘Let’s play cards. Aunt Xue is out of practice too. We’ll sit together and not let Xi-feng get the better of us.’

‘Quite right,’ said Aunt Xue, smiling. ‘You must keep an eye on my hand for me.

‘Will it be just the four of us, or shall we have someone else?’

‘There are only four,’ said Lady Wang with a smile.

‘Another would make it livelier,’ said Xi-feng.

‘Call Faithful,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Let her sit next to me, below me. Aunt Xue’s eyes are growing dim. She can look after both our hands.’

Xi-feng sighed and turned to Tan-chun.

‘You girls can all read and write. Why don’t you learn to tell fortunes?’

‘What an extraordinary idea! Instead of putting your mind to winning a little money from Grandmother, you start thinking about fortune-telling.’

‘I want to find out how much I’m destined to lose today. Win, indeed! Look at them. I haven’t even taken the field, and they’ve laid ambushes on either side.’

Grandmother Jia and Aunt Xue both laughed. Presently Faithful came and sat next to Grandmother Jia, with Xi-feng next below her. A red felt cloth was spread out, the cards were shuffled, the order of the first draw was decided, and the five players took up their hands. They had been playing for a while when Faithful saw that Grandmother Jia’s hand was ready, needing only the two-circle card to complete it. She gave Xi-feng a secret signal. It was Xi-feng’s turn to play a card. Deliberately, she hesitated for some time, then said with a smile:

‘Aunt must be holding back the card I need. But unless I play this one, I can’t hold out any longer.’

‘I haven’t got your card,’ said Aunt Xue.

‘I shall check afterwards.’

‘Check all you like. Play yours first and let me see what it is.’

Xi-feng laid it down in front of her. Aunt Xue saw that it was the two-circle card and smiled.

‘I’ve no use for it, but I’m afraid it will complete Grandmother’s hand.’

‘Oh, I’ve played the wrong one!’ Xi-feng said quickly, smiling.

Grandmother Jia had already thrown her cards down with a laugh.

‘Don’t you dare take it back! Who told you to make a mistake?’

‘You see why I wanted my fortune told! I play the card myself, and then blame it on an ambush!’

‘Exactly. You ought to give yourself a slap on the mouth and ask yourself why.’ Grandmother Jia turned to Aunt Xue with a smile. ‘It’s not that I’m mean and want to win money. It’s the good luck of winning.’

‘Of course it is. Who could be fool enough to say you wanted the money?’

Xi-feng had been counting out her cash. At this, she hurriedly threaded the coins back onto their string and smiled at the others.

‘That puts me in my place.

‘So it isn’t the money you want to win, only the good luck! I really am mean, counting out cash as soon as I lose. I’d better put it away.’

Faithful usually shuffled for Grandmother Jia. In the midst of laughing and talking with Aunt Xue, the old lady noticed that Faithful had made no move.

‘What are you cross about? You won’t even shuffle for me.’

Faithful took up the cards with a smile.

‘Mrs Lian won’t pay.’

‘Won’t pay? Her luck’s about to turn, then.’

She told a little maid:

‘Bring her whole string of cash over here.’

The little maid actually took it and laid it beside Grandmother Jia.

‘Give it back to me!’ said Xi-feng, laughing. ‘I’ll pay you exactly what I owe.’

‘So you really are mean, Feng,’ Aunt Xue said with a smile. ‘It’s only a game.’

Xi-feng stood up, took Aunt Xue by the arm and turned to point at the wooden box where Grandmother Jia usually kept her money.

‘Look, Aunt. Who knows how much of mine has been played away into that box? Before I’ve had half a shichen’s play2 with this string, the money in there starts beckoning to it. Once this string has been called inside too, there’ll be no more need to play cards. Our old ancestress will have got over her anger, and she’ll find some proper work to send me off to do.’

Before she had finished, Grandmother Jia and all the others were laughing helplessly.

At that very moment Patience, afraid that the money would not last, brought another string.

‘Don’t put it in front of me,’ Xi-feng told her. ‘Put it over there with Grandmother’s. It will save trouble if it’s all called in together. No need to make the money in the box go to the trouble twice.’

Grandmother Jia laughed so hard that her cards scattered over the table. She pushed Faithful.

‘Quick! Tear that mouth of hers!’

Patience put the money down where she had been told, joined in the laughter for a while, then went back. At the courtyard gate she met Jia Lian, who asked her:

‘Where is Her Ladyship? Father has sent me to fetch her.’

‘With Grandmother,’ Patience answered quickly, smiling. ‘She’s been standing there all this time and hasn’t moved. You’d better leave well alone. Grandmother has been angry for half the day. It’s only just easing off, after all the trouble Mrs Lian has taken to amuse her.’

‘I’ll simply say I’ve come for Grandmother’s instructions: is she going to Lai Da’s on the fourteenth, so that we can get the sedan ready? I can fetch Mother and help to amuse her at the same time. Why not?’

‘If you ask me, you’d better not go. Everyone in the family has been in the wrong, even Lady Wang and Bao-yu. Now you want to go and take your turn too.’

‘It’s all over now. Surely she won’t start again? Besides, it has nothing to do with me. And Father told me himself to fetch Mother. If I send someone else and he hears of it, he’s in just the mood to seize on that and take it out on me.’

He went off as he spoke.

Patience saw that there was sense in what he said and followed him. Once inside the main room, Jia Lian softened his footsteps and peered into the inner room. There stood Lady Xing. Sharp-eyed Xi-feng saw him first and signalled to him not to come in, then signalled to Lady Xing. Lady Xing could not very well leave at once. Instead, she poured a bowl of tea and set it before Grandmother Jia. The old lady turned round. Caught unawares, Jia Lian failed to get out of sight in time.

‘Who is that outside?’ she asked. ‘It looked like a boy poking his head in.’

‘I thought I saw someone too,’ Xi-feng said hastily. ‘Let me go and look.’

She rose and went out as she spoke. Jia Lian hurried in, smiling ingratiatingly.

‘I came to ask whether you would be going out on the fourteenth, Grandmother, so that we can get the sedan ready.’

‘Then why didn’t you come in? What are you skulking about for?’

‘I saw you were playing cards and didn’t dare disturb you. I only wanted to ask my wife to come out so I could enquire.’

‘Are you so busy that it had to be this very moment? Couldn’t you ask her all you wanted when she got home? Since when have you been so considerate? Who knows whether you’ve come to carry tales or to spy? Creeping about like that, you gave me quite a fright. A fine low-bred lot you are! Your wife is playing cards with me. You’ve plenty of time yet. Go home and talk to Zhao Er’s wife again about how to deal with your own!’

Everyone laughed.

‘Bao Er’s wife, Grandmother,’ Faithful said, laughing. ‘You’ve dragged Zhao Er’s wife into it again.’

Grandmother Jia laughed too.

‘So it was. How should I remember all their hugging and carrying names?3 The very thought of these things makes me angry!

‘When I first came into this family, I was a great-grandson’s wife. Now I have a great-grandson’s wife of my own. Fifty-four years, from first to last. I’ve seen a few great shocks and dangers in my time, and all manner of strange goings-on, but never anything like this. Will you get out of here!’

Jia Lian did not dare say a word and hurried out. Patience stood outside the window, smiling quietly.

‘I told you, but you wouldn’t listen. You’ve walked straight into the net after all.’

As she spoke, Lady Xing came out too.

‘It’s all Father’s doing,’ said Jia Lian, ‘and now it’s being visited on you and me.’

‘You unfilial, low-bred wretch! May thunder strike you! Other sons will die for their fathers, and you’ve only had a few words said to you for nothing, yet here you are complaining.

‘Mind your behaviour. He’s been in a temper these last few days. Take care he doesn’t thrash you.’

‘Do go over, Mother. It’s a long time since he sent me to fetch you.’

With that, he escorted his mother out and across to the other house.

Lady Xing gave Jia She only a brief account of what had been said. He could do nothing, and he was ashamed as well. From then on he pleaded illness and did not dare face Grandmother Jia, sending Lady Xing and Jia Lian every day to pay his respects instead. He had to send people searching and enquiring everywhere for someone to buy. In the end he spent eight hundred taels of silver on a seventeen-year-old girl named Carmine, whom he took into his rooms. We need say no more of that.

Meanwhile, the card-playing went on for half the day and ended only when it was time for supper. The next day or two passed without event. Before long it was the fourteenth. Lai Da’s wife came before daylight to renew her invitation. Grandmother Jia was in good spirits and took Lady Wang, Aunt Xue, Bao-yu, the girls and the others to spend half the day in Lai Da’s garden. Though it could not rival Prospect Garden, it was spacious and very well kept. Among its springs and rocks, trees and groves, halls, towers and pavilions, there were several sights to astonish the eye.

In the outer hall were Xue Pan, Jia Zhen, Jia Lian, Jia Rong and a few of their nearer clansmen. The more distant relations had not come, nor had Jia She. The Lai family had also invited several serving officials and young men from established families to keep them company. One of these was Liu Xiang-lian. Ever since their previous meeting, Xue Pan had been unable to forget him. He had learnt that Xiang-lian was particularly fond of amateur acting, playing both male and female leads in romantic pieces. He had misunderstood this and wrongly taken him for a young man available for amorous favours. He had been eager to get to know him, only regretting that there was no one to introduce them. To meet him by chance today seemed more than he could have wished for.

Jia Zhen and the others had heard of his accomplishments too. When their faces were flushed with wine, they begged him to perform two pieces. Afterwards they moved their seats to sit with him, asking endless questions and talking of this and that.

Liu Xiang-lian came of an established family. He had made nothing of his studies, and both his parents had died while he was young. By nature frank and chivalrous, careless of trifles, he was passionately fond of spear-play and swordsmanship. Gambling, drinking, visiting the pleasure quarters, playing the flute or the zheng: there was nothing he would not do.

As he was young and handsome as well, people who did not know his background mistook him for a professional actor. Lai Da’s son, Lai Shang-rong, was an old friend of his and had invited him to help entertain the guests that day. Once the wine had gone round, the others were tolerable enough, but Xue Pan alone reverted to his old ways. Xiang-lian had long since grown displeased and wanted to slip away at the first opportunity and have done with it. Lai Shang-rong, however, would not let him go on any account.

‘Master Bao has just spoken to me again,’ he said. ‘You saw one another when he came in, but there were too many people about for him to talk. He told me to ask you not to leave when the party breaks up. He still has something to say to you. If you insist on going, let me call him out. You two can see each other before you go, and then it will be nothing to do with me.’

He told the pages to find an old woman in the inner quarters and quietly ask her to bring Master Bao out. In less time than it takes to drink a cup of tea, Bao-yu appeared. Lai Shang-rong smiled at him.

‘My dear uncle, I leave him in your hands. I must go and see to the guests.’

He went straight off.

Bao-yu drew Liu Xiang-lian into a small study beside the hall. They sat down, and he asked whether Xiang-lian had been to Qin Zhong’s grave lately.

‘Of course I have. The other day several of us went hawking. We were only two li from his grave when I remembered how much rain we’d had this summer and began to fear the mound wouldn’t hold. I slipped away from the others to look. Sure enough, a little more of it had given way. When I got home I managed to get together a few hundred cash. Early on the third day I went out and hired two men to put it right.’

‘That explains it,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Last month there were lotus seedpods in the pond in Prospect Garden. I picked ten and sent Tealeaf out to place them as an offering on his grave. When he came back I asked whether the rain had washed it away. He said not only had it not been washed away, it looked more freshly made than on his last visit. I thought some of his friends must have built it up again. How I hate being shut up at home every day, unable to decide the smallest thing for myself! I can’t make a move without someone knowing about it. If one person doesn’t stop me, another talks me out of it. I can say what I like, but I can’t do anything.

‘Even when I have money, I’m not allowed to spend it as I choose.’

‘There’s no need for you to worry about this,’ said Xiang-lian. ‘I’m out and about. It’s enough that you remember him. The first day of the tenth month will soon be here, and I’ve already put aside the money for visiting his grave. You know I’m as poor as can be. There’s nothing saved up at home, and if a few coins do come my way, they’re gone as soon as I get them. Better put this much aside while I can than find myself empty-handed when the day comes.’

‘That was just what I wanted to send Tealeaf to find you about. But you’re hardly ever at home. I know you drift about day after day, with no fixed place to be found.’

‘You needn’t look for me on that account either. Each of us can do what he can. In fact, I mean to go away before long, wander about for three or five years and then come back.’

‘Why?’ Bao-yu asked at once.

Liu Xiang-lian gave a cold laugh.

‘You don’t know what’s on my mind. You’ll understand when the time comes. For now, I must say goodbye.’

‘We so seldom meet. Why not stay till the party ends tonight and leave together?’

‘That esteemed cousin of yours, your aunt’s son, is still the same. If I stay, there may be trouble. Better for me to keep out of his way.’

Bao-yu thought for a moment.

‘In that case, you had better avoid him.

‘But if you really are going away, you must tell me first. Whatever you do, don’t go off without a word.’

Tears fell as he spoke.

‘Of course I shall come and say goodbye. Only don’t tell anyone else.’

Liu Xiang-lian rose to go.

‘Go back inside, all of you. There’s no need to see me out.’

He left the study as he spoke. He had scarcely reached the main gate when he found Xue Pan shouting there:

‘Who let little Liu go?’

Liu Xiang-lian’s anger flared. He longed to kill him with a single punch, but then reflected that a drunken brawl would put Lai Shang-rong in an awkward position. He forced himself, again and again, to hold back. Suddenly Xue Pan saw him coming. Delighted as though he had found a treasure, he lurched forward, caught hold of him and smiled.

‘Brother, where have you been?’

‘Out for a stroll. I’ll be back.’

‘My dear brother, all the pleasure goes when you do. Do stay a little. Be kind to me. Whatever important business you have, leave it to your big brother. There’s no hurry. With a brother like me, it’s easy enough for you to get office or make your fortune.’

At this intolerable behaviour, Xiang-lian felt both anger and shame. A plan occurred to him. Drawing Xue Pan aside where no one could see them, he smiled.

‘Do you really care for me, or are you only pretending?’

Xue Pan was almost beside himself with delight. He gave him a sidelong look and hurriedly answered with a smile:

‘Dear brother, how can you ask me such a thing?

‘If I’m pretending, may I drop dead before your eyes!’

‘In that case, this is not the place. We’ll sit a little longer. Then I’ll go first and you can follow me out to my lodgings. We can spend the whole night drinking on our own. I’ve two splendid boys there too, who’ve never been out into the world. You needn’t bring a single servant. There will be people ready to wait on you when you get there.’

The joy of hearing this half sobered Xue Pan.

‘Do you mean it?’

‘What! I offer you my true affection, and you don’t believe me!’

‘I’m no fool,’ Xue Pan said hastily, smiling. ‘Of course I believe you! But I don’t know the place. If you go first, where am I to find you?’

‘My lodgings are outside the north gate. Can you bear to leave home and spend a night outside the city?’

‘With you, what do I need a home for?’

‘Then I’ll wait for you on the bridge outside the north gate.

‘Let’s go back to the table and have some wine. Wait until you see I’ve left before you follow. Then they won’t notice.’

Xue Pan readily agreed. The two went back to their places and drank for a while. Xue Pan could scarcely contain himself. He kept looking at Xiang-lian, growing more delighted the more he thought about it. Pot after pot, without anyone urging him, he drank and drank again, until, before he knew it, he was very nearly dead drunk.

Xiang-lian rose and went out, slipping away when no one was watching. Outside the gate he told his page Xing-nu:

‘Go home ahead of me. I’m just going outside the city and then I’ll be back.’

With that he mounted his horse, rode straight through the north gate and waited on the bridge for Xue Pan. In less time than it takes to eat a meal, he saw Xue Pan riding towards him in the distance on a large horse, mouth open and eyes staring, his head turning this way and that like a rattle-drum. When he reached Xiang-lian’s horse, he was so intent on looking far ahead that he took no notice of what was close at hand and rode straight past. Half amused and half enraged, Xiang-lian gave his horse its head and followed.

As Xue Pan looked ahead, houses and people grew fewer and fewer. He wheeled his horse round to search again. To his surprise, as soon as he turned he saw Xiang-lian. It was like finding a rare treasure.

‘I knew you would never break your word!’ he said with a smile.

‘Hurry on,’ Xiang-lian answered, smiling. ‘If anyone sees us and follows, it will be awkward.’

He set off at a gallop, and Xue Pan followed close behind.

Seeing that there was hardly anyone ahead and that a stretch of reed marsh lay nearby, Xiang-lian dismounted and tied his horse to a tree. He smiled at Xue Pan.

‘Get down. Let’s swear an oath first. If either of us changes his mind later, or tells anyone, let the oath fall on him.’

‘A very good idea,’ said Xue Pan, smiling.

He hurriedly dismounted, tied his horse to a tree as well, then knelt.

‘If in time I change my mind, or tell anyone, may Heaven destroy me and Earth annihilate me!’

He had not finished when there came a thud. Something like an iron hammer struck the back of his neck. Everything went black, golden stars burst before his eyes, and he fell helplessly to the ground. Xiang-lian stepped up to look at him. Knowing that he was a clumsy fellow unused to being beaten, he used only three-tenths of his strength to give him several slaps across the face. In an instant the face was a fruit stall of colours. At first Xue Pan tried to struggle up, but two jabs from the toe of Xiang-lian’s boot knocked him down again.

‘It was supposed to be something we both wanted! If you didn’t want it, you only had to say so. Why trick me out here to beat me?’

As he spoke, he poured out a stream of abuse.

‘You blind fool! Take a good look at who Master Liu is! Instead of begging for mercy, you insult me! There’s no point in beating you to death. I’ll just teach you what I can do.’

He fetched his riding-whip and struck Xue Pan thirty or forty times, from his back down to his shins. More than half sobered now, Xue Pan could not bear the pain, and cries of ‘Ow! Ow!’ escaped him.

‘So that’s all you amount to!’ Xiang-lian said with a cold laugh. ‘I thought you weren’t afraid of a beating.’

As he spoke he lifted Xue Pan’s left leg and dragged him a few paces into the mire among the reeds, rolling him in muddy water from head to foot.

‘Do you know who I am now?’

Xue Pan did not answer. He lay face down, groaning. Xiang-lian threw down the whip and hammered him several times with his fists. Xue Pan rolled about and screamed.

‘My ribs are broken! I know you’re a respectable gentleman. I listened to what other people said and got it wrong.’

‘Leave other people out of it. Tell me what you have to say now.’

‘There’s nothing I can say now.

‘Only that you’re a respectable gentleman and I was wrong.’

‘You’ll have to speak more humbly than that before I let you off.’

‘My dear younger brother,’ Xue Pan groaned.

Xiang-lian gave him another punch.

‘Ow! My dear elder brother!’

Xiang-lian punched him twice more in succession.

‘Ow!’ Xue Pan cried hastily. ‘Good master, spare this blind fool who had no eyes to see! From now on I shall respect you and fear you.’

‘Drink a couple of mouthfuls of that water,’ said Xiang-lian.

Xue Pan frowned as he heard him.

‘That water is filthy. How could I swallow it?’

Xiang-lian raised his fist and struck.

‘I’ll drink! I’ll drink!’

Xue Pan had no choice but to bend his head down among the roots of the reeds and take a mouthful. Before he could swallow, he retched and brought up everything he had just eaten.

‘You filthy thing! Eat it all up, and I’ll let you off.’

Xue Pan knocked his head to the ground again and again.

‘Please earn yourself some unseen merit and spare me! I couldn’t eat that if you killed me.’

‘You’re stinking me out!’ said Xiang-lian.

He left Xue Pan where he was, led out his horse, found his stirrup and rode away. Only when Xue Pan saw that he had gone did he begin to feel safe. He bitterly regretted having mistaken the man.

He tried to struggle up, but the pain all over his body was unbearable.

Meanwhile, Jia Zhen and the others had suddenly missed the pair of them from the feast and searched everywhere without finding them. Someone said:

‘I think they went out through the north gate.’

Xue Pan’s pages lived in fear of him. He had forbidden them to follow, so who dared go looking for him? In the end Jia Zhen grew uneasy and sent Jia Rong out with the pages. Asking their way and following his trail, they went straight through the north gate. More than two li beyond the bridge, they suddenly saw Xue Pan’s horse tied up beside a reed-filled hollow.

‘Good!’ they all said. ‘Where there’s a horse, there must be a man.’

They went up to the horse together and heard someone groaning among the reeds. Hurrying over, they found Xue Pan with his clothes in shreds and his face swollen and cut, rolled in mud from head to foot, inside his clothes and out, like a muddy pig. Jia Rong had already guessed nine-tenths of what had happened. He hastily dismounted and had the others help Xue Pan out.

‘Uncle Xue,’ he said, laughing, ‘you make advances every day. Today you’ve made them all the way into a reed pit!

‘The Dragon King must have fallen for your charms too and wanted you for a son-in-law. You must have run into his horns.’

Xue Pan was so ashamed that he wished he could crawl into a crack in the earth. As for climbing onto a horse, that was out of the question. Jia Rong had to send someone to the settlement outside the city gate to hire a small sedan. Xue Pan got in, and they all went back into the city. Jia Rong still wanted to have him carried to the feast at the Lais’, but Xue Pan pleaded with him in every way, and told him not to tell anyone. Only then did Jia Rong agree to let him go home by himself. He returned to the Lais’ to report to Jia Zhen and describe the state in which he had found him. Jia Zhen, too, knew that Xiang-lian had given him the beating.

‘It will do him good to come off the worse for once,’ he said, laughing.

When the party broke up that evening, he went to enquire after Xue Pan, but Xue Pan was nursing himself in his bedroom and pleaded illness to avoid seeing him.

When Grandmother Jia and the others returned and went to their separate homes, Aunt Xue and Bao-chai found Caltrop with her eyes swollen from crying. Having learnt the reason, they hurried to see Xue Pan. Though there were marks of injury on his face and body, neither his sinews nor his bones had been damaged. Aunt Xue was torn between distress and fury. She scolded Xue Pan, then cursed Liu Xiang-lian. She wanted to tell Lady Wang and send men to find and seize Xiang-lian. Bao-chai hurried to dissuade her.

‘It’s nothing so very serious. They were drinking together, and it’s common enough for people to fall out over wine. If one of them is drunk, he may get a few extra blows. Besides, everyone knows how lawless our family is. You only feel this way because you’re distressed about him, Mother. It will be easy enough to get satisfaction. In three or five days, when Brother is better and can go out, Cousin Zhen, Cousin Lian and the rest won’t simply let it drop. They will naturally give a dinner and invite the man, and he can admit his fault and apologise to Brother in front of them all. But if you make a great affair of it now and tell everyone, you’ll only seem partial and overindulgent: you let him make trouble and provoke people, and then, the one time he happens to get the worse of it, you raise an army, using your relations’ influence to bully ordinary people.’

‘My child,’ said Aunt Xue, ‘you really have thought it through. I was too angry to think clearly.’

‘This is just what he needs,’ Bao-chai said with a smile.

‘He isn’t afraid of you and he won’t listen to anyone. He grows more unbridled every day. After two or three lessons like this, he may stop.’

Xue Pan lay on the kang cursing Liu Xiang-lian bitterly. He ordered the pages to tear down Xiang-lian’s house, beat him to death and take him to court. Aunt Xue stopped them. She merely said that Liu Xiang-lian had got carried away in his cups, and now that he was sober he was full of remorse and had fled for fear of punishment. Xue Pan heard her account.
