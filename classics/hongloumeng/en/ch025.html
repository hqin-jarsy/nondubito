Chapter 25 A sister and brother fall prey to the Five Ghosts through sorcery; In the Dream of the Red Chamber, the Spiritual Jade meets two adepts

Chapter end

Now Crimson, her mind wandering and her thoughts entangled in love, had drifted off to sleep. She met Jia Yun, who tried to take hold of her; turning to run, she tripped over the threshold and fell. The fright woke her, and only then did she realise it had been a dream. She tossed and turned after that, unable to sleep all night. At daybreak she got up. Several of the other maids came to fetch her to sweep the rooms and floors and carry water for washing. Without stopping to wash her face or comb her hair properly, she twisted up her hair anyhow before the mirror, washed her hands, tied a cloth round her waist and went to clean the rooms.

Bao-yu, meanwhile, had noticed Crimson the day before and had not forgotten her. He could have called for her by name to wait on him, but for two things: first, he was afraid of hurting Aroma and the others; and second, he did not yet know what sort of girl Crimson was. If she turned out well, all would be well; if she did not, it would be awkward to send her back.

These thoughts made him restless. He too rose early without washing or combing his hair, and sat lost in thought. Presently he lowered the window, and through its gauze screen could see clearly outside. Several maids were sweeping the courtyard, all of them rouged and powdered, with flowers and willow sprigs in their hair, but the girl from yesterday was nowhere to be seen. He slipped his shoes on and sauntered out, pretending to look at the flowers, glancing here and peering there. Raising his eyes, he thought he saw someone leaning against the railing beneath the covered walk at the south-west corner. Annoyingly, a crab-apple tree in front of him blocked the view. He moved a step farther and looked more closely. Sure enough, there was yesterday’s girl, lost in thought. He wanted to go up to her, but could find no good way of doing so. While he hesitated, Emerald came to hurry him in to wash his face, and he had to go. We may leave him there.

Crimson was still standing in her reverie when she saw Aroma beckon and had to go over to her.

‘Our watering can still hasn’t been put in order and brought back,’ Aroma said with a smile. ‘Go to Miss Lin’s and borrow theirs for us.’

Crimson agreed and set off for Naiad’s House. Just as she was crossing Green Haze Bridge, she looked up and saw screens stretched all along the upper slope. Only then did she remember that workmen were planting trees there that day. Turning to look, she saw a cluster of men digging in the distance, with Jia Yun seated on a rock of the artificial hill. She wanted to go over, but dared not. Dispiritedly she went on to Naiad’s House, fetched the watering can and returned, then lay down listlessly in her room.

The others supposed she was merely feeling a little unwell and paid no attention. A day soon passed. The next day was the birthday of Wang Zi-teng’s wife, who had sent invitations to Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang. Grandmother Jia was not feeling well, so Lady Wang decided not to go either. Aunt Xue went, however, with Xi-feng, the Jia girls, Bao-chai and Bao-yu. They did not return until evening.

As it happened, when Jia Huan came home from school, Lady Wang had told him to copy out the Vajra Mantra and recite it. He was now sitting on her kang, ordering the lamps to be lit and making a great show of his copying. One moment he called to Cai-yun to pour him a cup of tea, the next to Silver to trim a candle; then he complained that Golden was standing in his light. The maids, who had always disliked him, took no notice. Only Cai-xia was still on good terms with him. She poured a cup of tea and handed it to him, and, seeing Lady Wang occupied in conversation, spoke to him quietly.

‘Do behave yourself. Why must you make everyone dislike you?’

‘I know all about it,’ said Jia Huan. ‘Don’t try to fool me. You’re friendly with Bao-yu now and won’t have anything to do with me. I’ve seen it for myself.’

Cai-xia bit her lip and poked him in the forehead.

‘Ungrateful thing! You’re like the dog that bit Lü Dong-bin: you don’t know a kind heart when you find one.’

They were still talking when Xi-feng came in and paid her respects to Lady Wang, who began asking her all about the party: which ladies had been there, whether the plays were good, what the food had been like. They had exchanged only a few words when Bao-yu arrived. He came in and greeted Lady Wang with a few impeccably proper remarks, then had his brow-band removed, his robe taken off and his boots pulled away, and tumbled headlong into her arms. Lady Wang stroked and caressed his face and body, while he clung round her neck and chattered on.

‘My child, you’ve had too much wine again. Your face is burning,’ she said. ‘And still you keep wriggling about! You’ll make the wine go to your head in a moment. Won’t you lie quietly over there for a little while?’

She called for a pillow. At this Bao-yu slipped out of her arms and lay down behind her, then called Cai-xia to pat him. He joked and talked to her, but she answered coolly and scarcely attended to him; her eyes kept straying towards Jia Huan.

Bao-yu took her hand and smiled.

‘Dear sister, do pay me a little attention too.’

As he spoke he kept hold of her hand, but Cai-xia pulled it away, refusing him.

‘Go on like that and I’ll shout.’

Jia Huan could hear their teasing. He had long hated Bao-yu, and now the sight of him playing with Cai-xia made the malice within him harder than ever to contain. He dared not speak out, but had often plotted against him in secret, without finding a chance to act. Now that Bao-yu was so close, he decided to blind him with hot oil. Deliberately making it look like an accident, he gave the candle-lamp, brimming with oily liquid, a push straight at Bao-yu’s face.

Bao-yu cried out, startling everyone in the room. They hurriedly moved the standing lamp over from the floor and brought three or four more lights from the inner and outer rooms. In their light they saw oil all over his face and head. Frantic with distress and anger, Lady Wang ordered the maids to wipe him clean, while scolding Jia Huan. Xi-feng sprang up on to the kang to help attend to Bao-yu.

‘Third Brother’s still such a flustered chicken,’ she said, smiling. ‘I’ve always said you’re no good in grand company. Aunt Zhao ought to teach you better now and then.’

This reminded Lady Wang. She stopped scolding Jia Huan and called Aunt Zhao over instead.

‘You’ve brought this black-hearted, ill-bred little wretch into the world, and you won’t even keep him in order!

‘Time after time I’ve let things pass, and now you’ve grown so pleased with yourselves that you keep going further!’

Aunt Zhao had long harboured jealousy and resentment against both Xi-feng and Bao-yu, but had never dared show it. Now Jia Huan had caused fresh trouble and brought this abuse down on her. Not only must she swallow it in silence: she had to go and help tend Bao-yu as well. A line of blisters had risen on his left cheek; fortunately his eyes were quite untouched. At the sight, Lady Wang’s heart ached. Afraid, too, of what she could possibly say when Grandmother Jia asked about it next day, she turned in her agitation to rebuke Aunt Zhao once more. Then she comforted Bao-yu and ordered medicine to draw out the poison and reduce the swelling to be brought and applied.

‘It hurts a little, but it’s nothing serious,’ Bao-yu said. ‘If Grandmother asks tomorrow, just say I burnt myself.’

‘Even if we say that, she’ll still scold us for not watching you properly and letting you get burnt,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘There’s bound to be a scene whatever happens. You can say what you please tomorrow.’

Lady Wang ordered them to take Bao-yu carefully back to his rooms. Aroma and the others were thrown into a panic when they saw him. Dai-yu had felt out of sorts all day with Bao-yu away and no one to talk to. That evening she had already sent two or three times to ask whether he was back. Now at last he was, only to have been burnt. She hurried over and found him looking in a mirror, the whole left side of his face covered with medicine. Thinking the burn must be very serious, she hastened up, asking how it had happened and wanting to see. Bao-yu quickly covered his face and waved her away. He would not let her look, knowing how fastidious she was about cleanliness and how she disliked the sight of such things.

Dai-yu knew her own fastidiousness well enough, and understood that he was afraid she would find it dirty.

‘Let me see where you’ve burnt yourself,’ she said with a smile. ‘What is there to hide?’

As she spoke she came closer and, forcibly turning his head by the neck, took a look and asked how badly it hurt.

‘Not very much. A day or two’s rest will put it right,’ said Bao-yu.

Dai-yu sat for a while, then returned unhappily to her own rooms. Nothing more need be said of that night. Next day Bao-yu went to see Grandmother Jia. Although he declared that he had burnt himself and that no one else was to blame, she duly scolded his attendants all the same.

Another day passed, and Mother Ma, to whom Bao-yu had been entrusted as a godson for spiritual protection, came to pay her respects at the Rong mansion. The sight of him gave her a great start. She asked what had happened, and when told of the burn, nodded and sighed for a while, traced a figure with her finger on his face, and muttered prayers over him.

‘It’s certain to get better. This is only a passing stroke of misfortune.’

Then she turned to Grandmother Jia.

‘Your Old Ladyship, our venerable living Bodhisattva, can have no notion of the terrible things described in the Buddhist scriptures. From the moment a child is born into the family of a prince or a great minister, there are mischievous little demons following him unseen. Whenever they get a chance they give him a twist or a pinch, knock his bowl from his hand as he eats, or trip him up as he walks. That is why so many children in great families never live to grow up.’

‘Is there no Buddhist remedy for it?’ Grandmother Jia asked at once.

‘That’s easy enough,’ said Mother Ma. ‘You need only do more good works on his behalf, to bring him a good return.

‘The scriptures also tell of the Bodhisattva of Great All-Illuminating Light in the West, whose special charge is to shine into the darkness where evil spirits lurk. Any devout man or woman who makes offerings with a sincere heart can secure lasting health and peace for their children and grandchildren, with no more harm from frights, evil spirits or possessing ghosts.’

‘But how does one make offerings to this Bodhisattva?’ asked Grandmother Jia.

‘It costs very little. Besides the incense and candles, one need only provide a few catties of fragrant oil every day to keep a great votive lamp burning. The votive lamp is the Bodhisattva’s own manifested image, and must never be allowed to go out, day or night.’

‘How much oil does it take for a day and a night? Tell me plainly, so that I too can earn this merit.’

At this Mother Ma smiled.

‘There’s no fixed amount. Our benefactors, those living Bodhisattvas, give just as their hearts move them. Several princely consorts and titled ladies make such offerings at our temple. The dowager consort of the Prince of Nan’an’s household has made a great vow and gives generously: forty-eight catties of oil and one catty of wick-rushes every day. Her votive lamp is only a little smaller than a vat. The titled wife of the Marquis of Jintian gives on a somewhat smaller scale, only twenty-four catties of oil a day. Others give five catties, or three, or one. There is no fixed quantity.

‘Poor people in small households cannot afford so much. Even if they give only four taels of oil or half a catty, we still have to light a lamp for them.’

Grandmother Jia nodded thoughtfully.

‘There’s another thing,’ Mother Ma went on. ‘For one’s parents or honoured elders, it does no harm to give more. But when Your Old Ladyship is giving for Bao-yu, too much might be a bad thing. The young gentleman might not be able to bear it, and his blessings would be diminished instead. Besides, there’s no need to spend the household money so freely. If you wish to make an offering, seven catties at the most, or five at the least, will do perfectly well.’

‘In that case,’ said Grandmother Jia, ‘settle it at five catties a day. You can come and draw the whole month’s allowance at once.’

‘Amitabha, merciful great Bodhisattva!’ Mother Ma intoned.

Grandmother Jia then called in a servant and gave further instructions.

‘From now on, whenever Bao-yu goes out, give his pages a few strings of cash to take with them, so that he can give alms to any monks, Taoists or poor people he meets.’

Mother Ma sat a little longer, then wandered about paying her respects in the other courtyards and apartments.

Presently she reached Aunt Zhao’s room. After their greetings, Aunt Zhao told a little maid to pour her some tea. Mother Ma noticed a heap of odd scraps and offcuts of silk and satin on the kang, where Aunt Zhao was pasting shoes together.

‘Just when I’ve run out of material for shoe uppers!’ she said. ‘If you have any bits of satin, Mrs Zhao, whatever the colour, do let me have enough for a pair.’

‘Look through it yourself,’ Aunt Zhao sighed. ‘Is there a single decent piece there? Anything decent never reaches my hands. That’s all there is, good or bad. If you don’t mind it, choose a couple of pieces.’

Mother Ma duly chose two pieces and tucked them into her sleeve.

‘The other day I sent five hundred cash for an offering to the Medicine King,’ Aunt Zhao asked. ‘Did you receive it?’

‘I made the offering for you long ago,’ Mother Ma said.

Aunt Zhao sighed.

‘Amitabha! If only I had a little more to spare, I’d make offerings regularly. The will is there, but the means are not.’

‘Don’t worry,’ said Mother Ma. ‘Once young Huan grows up and gets himself an official post, you’ll be able to do all the good works you please.’

Aunt Zhao gave a derisive snort.

‘Enough, enough. Don’t speak of it again. You can see how things stand now. Is there anyone in this house that my boy and I can compare with?

‘You’d think they’d got a living dragon, not just Bao-yu. Still, he’s a child, and he has looks people like; if the grown-ups want to make more of him, I can put up with that. It’s this one I can’t stomach.’

She held up two fingers.

Mother Ma understood.

‘Mrs Lian?’

Aunt Zhao waved her hands in alarm, went to the door, lifted the curtain and looked out. Only when she had made sure there was no one about did she return and whisper:

‘Terrible, terrible! As for that woman, if she doesn’t carry every bit of this family’s property off to her own people, you can call me what you like.’

Mother Ma sounded her out.

‘You don’t have to tell me. Can’t we all see it? What amazes me is that you put up with it and let her do as she pleases. Very fine, I must say.’

‘Mother, what can we do but let her? Who would dare do anything to her?’

Mother Ma gave a snort. After a long pause she said:

‘Not that I’d want to say anything sinful, but you’re a helpless lot!

‘You can hardly blame anyone else. If you daren’t act openly, you could have found some way of dealing with her in secret. Why wait till things have come to this?’

Aunt Zhao found sense in these words and was secretly delighted.

‘What could one do in secret? I’ve had the thought myself, only I don’t know anyone with the skill. If you could show me a way, I’d reward you handsomely.’

Seeing that they had come to an understanding, Mother Ma deliberately drew back.

‘Amitabha! Don’t ask me. What do I know of such things? A sin, a sin!’

‘There you go again,’ said Aunt Zhao. ‘You’re always so ready to help people in distress. Surely you won’t stand by and watch them hound my boy and me to death? Or are you afraid I won’t reward you?’

Mother Ma smiled.

‘If you say I can’t bear to see the two of you ill-treated, that’s fair enough. But when you talk of rewarding me, you’ve got your sums wrong. Even supposing I did want a reward from you, what have you got that could tempt me?’

Aunt Zhao could hear her yielding.

‘How can someone as sharp as you suddenly turn so foolish? If your method really works and you finish those two off, surely this property will all be my Huan’s one day. What could you ask for then that you wouldn’t get?’

Mother Ma lowered her head. After a long pause she said:

‘Once it’s done, with nothing in writing, why should you bother about me?’

‘That’s easily settled.

‘I haven’t much in hand just now, but I’ve saved a few taels here and there, and I have some clothes and hairpins. Take something on account. For the rest, I’ll give you a written promise to pay, with any guarantor you like. When the time comes, you’ll get the full amount.’

‘You really mean that?’ asked Mother Ma.

‘How could I lie about such a thing?’

She called in an old woman she trusted and whispered a few words in her ear. The woman went out and soon returned with a bond made out for five hundred taels. Aunt Zhao put her handprint on it, then went to the cupboard, took out her private savings and showed them to Mother Ma.

‘Take this first for incense and candles and the expenses of the offerings. Will that do?’

At the sight of the heap of shining silver and the bond, Mother Ma agreed to everything without a further thought. She reached first for the silver, snatched it up and tucked it away, then secured the bond. Next she rummaged for some time inside her waistband, bringing out ten paper-cut ghosts with blue faces and white hair, and two paper figures. She handed them to Aunt Zhao and gave her instructions in a whisper.

‘Write the eight characters of their birth dates and hours on these two paper figures. Hide each figure, with five of the ghosts, in that person’s bed, and that’s all you have to do. I’ll perform the rites at home. They’re bound to work.

‘Above all, be careful, and don’t be afraid!’

They were still talking when one of Lady Wang’s maids came looking for Mother Ma.

‘Are you in here, ma’am? Her Ladyship is waiting for you.’

The two parted, and we may leave them there.

Since Bao-yu had burnt his face and stopped going out, Dai-yu had often been able to sit and talk with him. One day, after a meal, she read a couple of passages, but found no pleasure in them. She did some needlework with Nightingale and Snowgoose, but that only made her more restless. After leaning in the doorway for a while in thought, she wandered out to look at the tender bamboo shoots newly thrusting up below the steps, and before she knew it had passed through the courtyard gate. In the garden she could see no one in any direction: only the brightness of flowers and the shadows of willows, with birds calling and water murmuring in the stream.

She strolled towards the House of Green Delights. Several maids were dipping up water and clustering in the covered walk to watch a thrush take its bath. Hearing laughter indoors, she went in and found Li Gong-cai, Xi-feng and Bao-chai all there.

‘Here’s another one!’ they said, laughing as she entered.

‘Everyone’s here today,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘Who sent out the invitations?’

‘Where were you the other day when I sent a maid with two jars of tea for you?’ asked Xi-feng.

‘Oh, of course! I quite forgot. Thank you, thank you.’

‘Have you tried it? Did you like it?’

Before Xi-feng had finished, Bao-yu put in:

‘It ought to be all right, but I didn’t think much of it myself. I don’t know how the others found it.’

‘The flavour’s light,’ said Bao-chai, ‘but the colour isn’t very good.’

‘It came as tribute from Siam,’ said Xi-feng.

‘I didn’t find it very interesting either. It’s not as good as the tea I drink every day.’

‘I liked it,’ said Dai-yu. ‘I don’t know what sort of tastes you all have.’

‘If you really like it, take mine as well,’ said Bao-yu.

‘I have more, if you like it,’ Xi-feng said with a smile.

‘Really? Then I’ll send a maid to fetch it.’

‘No need. I’ll have someone bring it over. There’s something else I shall be asking you to do for me tomorrow. I’ll send that along at the same time.’

‘Do you hear that?’ Dai-yu said, laughing. ‘You only have to drink a little of their tea and they start giving you orders.’

‘I ask you a favour, and you come out with all this nonsense about tea and water!’ Xi-feng laughed. ‘Since you’ve accepted our family’s tea, why haven’t you come into the family as a bride?’

Everyone burst out laughing.1

Dai-yu blushed and turned her head away without a word. Li Gong-cai smiled at Bao-chai.

‘Our Second Sister-in-law really does have a way of making jokes.’

‘Making jokes!’ said Dai-yu. ‘Nothing but cheap, spiteful chatter to make people hate her.’

She gave a little spit of contempt.

‘Don’t be absurd!’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘What would you lack if you married into our family?’

She pointed to Bao-yu.

‘Look at him. Are his looks beneath you? His family? His standing? His wealth? Where’s the disgrace to anyone?’

Dai-yu rose to leave.

‘Little Pin-pin’s cross!’ Bao-chai called. ‘Come back and sit down. Going away will spoil it all.’

She rose to catch hold of her. They had just reached the doorway when Aunt Zhao and Aunt Zhou came in to see Bao-yu.

Li Gong-cai, Bao-chai, Bao-yu and the others invited the two women to sit. Xi-feng alone went on laughing and talking with Dai-yu, without so much as looking directly at them. Bao-chai was about to speak when a maid from Lady Wang’s rooms appeared.

‘Your uncle’s wife is here. Her Ladyship asks the ladies and young ladies to come out.’

Li Gong-cai immediately called to Xi-feng and the others to go with her. Aunt Zhao and Aunt Zhou hastily took their leave of Bao-yu too.

‘I can’t come out,’ Bao-yu said. ‘Whatever you do, don’t let my aunt come in here.’

Then he added:

‘Cousin Dai-yu, stay a moment. There’s something I want to say.’

Xi-feng turned back, smiling at Dai-yu.

‘Someone wants a word with you.’

She gave Dai-yu a push into the room and went off with Li Wan.

Bao-yu took hold of Dai-yu’s sleeve and could do nothing but grin. There was something in his heart, but he could not get it out. Dai-yu’s face grew redder in spite of herself, and she struggled to leave.

Suddenly Bao-yu gave a cry.

‘Oh, my head! What a pain!’

‘Serves you right. Amitabha!’ said Dai-yu.

‘I’m dying!’ he shouted.

He sprang three or four chi off the ground and began yelling and raving wildly. Dai-yu and the maids were terrified, and hurried to tell Lady Wang, Grandmother Jia and the others. Wang Zi-teng’s wife was there too. By the time they all arrived together, Bao-yu was brandishing knives and sticks, threatening to kill himself and throwing everything into turmoil. Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang shook so violently that their clothes trembled. Crying ‘My child!’ and ‘My own flesh!’, they broke into loud sobs.

The whole household was alarmed. Jia She, Lady Xing, Jia Zhen, Jia Zheng, Jia Lian, Jia Rong, Jia Yun, Jia Ping, Aunt Xue and Xue Pan, together with Zhou Rui’s wife and all the other married women-servants and maids, high and low, from the inner and outer quarters, came into the garden to see what was wrong.

The garden was instantly a tangle of confusion. While everyone was at a loss, Xi-feng came slashing her way in with a gleaming steel knife. Any chicken she saw she killed, any dog she saw she killed; when she saw a person she tried to kill them too. The panic grew worse. Zhou Rui’s wife quickly brought several strong, bold women to seize her. They wrested the knife away and carried her back to her rooms. Patience, Felicity and the others wept as though heaven and earth were falling apart. Jia Zheng and the rest were now in a quandary: whichever of the two they attended to, they could not leave the other.

The others’ agitation need hardly be described. Xue Pan alone was ten times busier than anyone else. He was afraid Aunt Xue would be knocked down in the crush, afraid Bao-chai would be seen, afraid Caltrop would be subjected to some indignity, for he knew that Jia Zhen and his like devoted their energies to women. He had more than he could manage. Then he caught a glimpse of Dai-yu’s graceful, delicate figure, and at once went weak with desire.

Everyone was talking at once. Some urged them to fetch a ritual exorcist to send the evil spirits away; others wanted a female spirit medium to perform a spirit dance; still others recommended Adept Zhang of the Jade Emperor Pavilion. The clamour of conflicting suggestions was endless. They tried every sort of medical treatment and prayer, consulted diviners and appealed to the gods, all without effect. It was close to sunset when Wang Zi-teng’s wife took her leave. Wang Zi-teng himself came the following day to ask after them.

Then came people from the young Marquis Shi’s household, Lady Xing’s brothers and all the other relations. Some brought talisman water, others recommended monks and Taoists, but nothing did any good. Bao-yu and his cousin’s wife grew steadily more confused and lost all awareness of their surroundings. They lay in bed, their bodies hot as live coals, saying every kind of wild thing. By night the older women, the married servants and the maids dared not go near them. Both were therefore carried into Lady Wang’s principal apartments, and Jia Yun was put in charge of the pages who took turns keeping watch at night. Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang, Lady Xing, Aunt Xue and the others would not move an inch away, but sat round them weeping helplessly.

Jia She and Jia Zheng were afraid that Grandmother Jia would ruin her health with crying. The lamps burnt night and day, the whole household was in distress, and neither man knew what more to do. Jia She still went everywhere in search of monks and Taoists. Jia Zheng, bitterly discouraged by their failure, tried to stop him.

‘Our children’s lot is Heaven’s decree. Human effort cannot force it. Their illness came without warning, and every treatment has failed. It seems Heaven means it to be so. We can only let them go.’

Jia She paid no attention and continued his frantic efforts, but to no avail. Three days passed. Xi-feng and Bao-yu lay in bed with scarcely a breath left in them. Everyone in the household was alarmed and said there was no hope. In haste they prepared the clothes and shoes in which the two were to be laid out. Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang, Jia Lian, Patience and Aroma wept more bitterly than anyone, forgetting food and sleep and declaring that they could not go on living. Aunt Zhao, Jia Huan and their like, of course, had what they wished for.

On the morning of the fourth day, Grandmother Jia and the others were weeping round Bao-yu when he opened his eyes.

‘From now on I shan’t be in your house any more. Get everything ready and send me on my way.’

To Grandmother Jia it was as though her heart and liver had been torn out. Aunt Zhao, standing beside her, offered consolation.

‘Your Old Ladyship mustn’t grieve too much. The young gentleman can’t be saved now. Better dress him properly and let him go back sooner, so that he needn’t suffer so much. If you keep clinging to him and his last breath won’t leave him, he’ll suffer and have no peace in the other world either.’

Before she had finished, Grandmother Jia spat in her face.

‘Rotten-tongued, good-for-nothing old woman! Who asked you to come chattering here? How do you know he’s suffering and has no peace in the other world? How do you know he can’t be saved? What do you stand to gain by wishing him dead? Don’t dream of it! If he dies, I’ll have your lives for his. Haven’t you always been egging them on to force him to write and study, till you’ve frightened the courage out of him? Doesn’t he run from his father like a mouse from a cat? Haven’t you strumpets been behind it all? Now you’ve driven him to death and got your wish. Which of you do you think I’ll spare?’

She wept as she cursed. Hearing this, Jia Zheng was more wretched than ever. He ordered Aunt Zhao away and came forward himself to soothe his mother as gently as he could. Just then someone came to report:

‘Both coffins are ready, sir. Will you come out and look at them?’

To Grandmother Jia this was oil on the fire.

‘Who has been making coffins?’ she cried, and kept shouting for the coffin-makers to be dragged in and beaten to death.

While the turmoil was at its height and no one could bring it to an end, the faint tapping of a wooden fish prayer-block was heard, followed by a chanted invocation:

‘Homage to the Bodhisattva Who Releases Karmic Wrongs! Are your loved ones afflicted, your household in ruin? Has danger befallen you, or have evil spirits taken possession? We are skilled in curing all such troubles.’

Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang could not wait a moment after hearing this. They ordered someone to hurry out and bring the visitors in. Jia Zheng felt ill at ease, but how could he oppose his mother? Besides, he wondered how the words could be heard so clearly this far inside the house. Finding it strange himself, he sent to have them admitted. When everyone looked up, they saw a scabby-headed monk and a lame Taoist. What did the monk look like?

His nose like a hanging gall-bladder, his eyebrows long,

His eyes like bright stars holding a precious light;

In ragged robe and straw sandals, he had no fixed abode,

Filthy, and with sores all over his head.

And what of the Taoist?

One foot rose high, the other dragged below,

His whole frame wet and trailing through the mire.

If, meeting him, you asked him where he lived:

In Penglai, west of the Weak Water.

‘At which temple do you two friends of the Way practise your devotions?’ asked Jia Zheng.

The monk smiled.

‘No need for further questions, sir. We heard of the affliction in your household and have come expressly to cure it.’

‘There are indeed two people under an evil influence,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘What talisman water have you to offer?’

The Taoist smiled.

‘You have a rare treasure in your own house. Why ask us for talisman water?’

Jia Zheng sensed a meaning in this and was stirred.

‘My son was born with a piece of precious jade, and the inscription says that it can dispel evil spirits. Yet it seems to have no power at all.’

‘You have no idea of that object’s wondrous uses, sir,’ said the monk. ‘It has now been beguiled by sensual pleasures, wealth and gain, and so has lost its power. Bring it out for us to recite over. That should put things right.’

Jia Zheng took the jade from Bao-yu’s neck and handed it to the two of them. The monk received it, held it aloft on his palm and sighed deeply.

‘Since we parted at Green Ridge Peak, thirteen years have passed in the blink of an eye! How swiftly time runs in the human world. The day when your earthly ties have run their course will come as quickly as the snap of a finger. How enviable was your former state:

Neither heaven constrained you nor earth held you bound,

In your heart was neither joy nor grief;

But once, refined, you gained your spiritual powers,

You went to seek out trouble in the human world.

How pitiful, the life you lead today:

Powder stains and rouge-marks dim your precious light,

Rich lattices cage pairs of mandarin ducks by night and day.

From one deep, drunken dream you must at last awake;

When karmic debts are paid in full, all can leave the stage!’

Having recited these lines, he stroked and handled the jade for a while, uttered some wild words, then handed it to Jia Zheng.

‘Its power has returned. It must not be profaned. Hang it from the lintel of the bedchamber and put both patients in that one room. Except for their own wives and mothers, no women must be allowed to intrude upon them. After thirty-three days, I guarantee they will be well, their illness gone and their former health restored.’

He turned to leave as he spoke. Jia Zheng hurried after them, still trying to speak, inviting the two to sit down for tea and wishing to offer a gift of thanks, but they were already gone. Grandmother Jia and the others kept sending people after them. There was not a trace to be found.

They could only do as they had been told. Both patients were placed in Lady Wang’s bedchamber, with the jade suspended above the door. Lady Wang kept watch herself and would let no one else in. By evening the two actually began to come round and said they were hungry. Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang were as delighted as if they had found a treasure. Rice water was quickly cooked and given to them; their strength gradually returned and the evil influence began to recede. Only then did the household breathe freely again.

Li Gong-cai, the three Jia girls, Bao-chai, Dai-yu, Patience, Aroma and the others were waiting for news in the outer room. When word came that the patients had taken rice water and regained their senses, Dai-yu was the first to speak.

‘Amitabha!’

Bao-chai turned and looked at her for a long while, then gave a sudden little laugh. No one understood.

‘Why are you laughing all of a sudden, Cousin Bao-chai?’ asked Xi-chun.

‘I was thinking how much busier the Buddha is than any of us,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘He has to expound the scriptures and preach the Law, and bring salvation to all living beings. Then Bao-yu and Cousin Feng fall ill, and with all the incense burnt and vows to be fulfilled, he has to grant blessings and avert calamity. Now they’re only just a little better, and he has Miss Lin’s marriage to arrange as well. Isn’t it funny how busy he is?’

Dai-yu blushed in spite of herself and spat in contempt.

‘You’re all wicked, every one of you! Heaven knows what deaths you’ll die. Instead of learning from decent people, you can only copy Cousin Feng’s cheap talk and rotten tongue.’

She flung aside the door-curtain and went out.
