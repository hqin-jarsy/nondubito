Chapter 10 The widow Jin swallows an insult for the sake of gain; Doctor Zhang examines an illness and traces its cause

Chapter end

Outnumbered, and ordered to do so by Jia Rui, Jin Rong had apologised and kowtowed to Qin Zhong. Only then would Bao-yu let the matter rest. When school was over and everyone had gone home, Jin Rong grew angrier the more he thought about it. ‘Qin Zhong is only Jia Rong’s wife’s younger brother,’ he said. ‘He isn’t a member of the Jia family. He’s allowed to study there, just as I am. He thinks he can look down his nose at everyone because Bao-yu is his friend. If that’s how he means to carry on, he ought at least to behave decently, so no one can say anything. He’s always up to something with Bao-yu, sneaking about as if the rest of us were blind and couldn’t see. And today he went after someone else, right under my eyes. What if there was a row? What have I got to be afraid of?’

His mother, née Hu, heard him grumbling on and on. ‘What are you working yourself up about now?’ she asked. ‘Do you know how hard it was for me to ask your aunt, and all the trouble she took before she could put in a word for you with Mrs Lian over at the Rong mansion? That’s how you got a place to study. Without their help, could we afford a teacher? Besides, they give you your tea and your meals at school. These two years you’ve been studying there have saved us a great deal in food alone. And you like to spend the savings on a nice bright new outfit. Then there’s Master Xue. Would you ever have got to know him if you hadn’t been at that school? Even when he hasn’t given much, one year with another, he’s helped us out with seventy or eighty taels over these two years.

‘If you make such a fuss now that you have to leave, let me tell you, finding another place like it will be harder than climbing to heaven! Be a good boy, amuse yourself for a bit and then go to bed. You’ll be far better off.’ Jin Rong swallowed his anger, and before long went off to sleep. Next day he went to school as usual.

We need say no more of that. His aunt had married Jia Huang, a member of the direct line in the Jia clan’s generation whose names bore the jade radical. There is no need to explain that not every member of the clan enjoyed the wealth and standing of the Ning and Rong households. Jia Huang and his wife had a small property to live on. They paid frequent calls at the two mansions to enquire after the family’s health, and knew how to make themselves agreeable to Xi-feng and You-shi. The help these two ladies gave them from time to time enabled them to get by. On this particular day the weather was fine and there was nothing to do at home, so Mrs Huang took an elderly woman servant with her and drove over to see her widowed sister-in-law and her nephew.

In the course of their chat, Jin Rong’s mother happened to bring up the previous day’s affair at the Jia school and told her sister-in-law everything, from beginning to end. No sooner had Mrs Huang heard this than her anger flared. ‘That little brat Qin Zhong is related to the Jias, is he? And isn’t our Rong related to them too?

‘People needn’t be quite so eager to curry favour with the powerful. And what a credit to themselves they are, with the things they get up to! Even Bao-yu has no business taking his side to such lengths. I’ll go over to the Ning mansion and see Mrs Zhen. Then I’ll have a word with Qin Zhong’s sister and ask her to judge between them.’

Jin Rong’s mother was beside herself with alarm. ‘It’s all my fault for letting my tongue run away with me and telling you,’ she said hurriedly. ‘Please, sister, don’t go. Never mind who was right and who was wrong. If there’s a row, how is he to keep his place? And if he loses it, not only can’t we afford a teacher, but there’ll be all his food to find as well.’ ‘Who can stop to think of all that?’ said Mrs Huang. ‘Wait till I’ve spoken and see what comes of it!’ She would not listen to her sister-in-law’s pleas, but told the old servant to see to the carriage, got in and set off for the Ning mansion. They drove through the carriage gateway, and she alighted at the little corner gate on the east. Going inside, she found Jia Zhen’s wife, You-shi. Here she did not venture to put on airs. She made warm and attentive enquiries after her health, exchanged a little small talk, and only then asked, ‘How is it we haven’t seen Mrs Rong today?’ ‘I don’t know what’s come over her lately,’ You-shi replied. ‘It’s more than two months since she had her period.

‘We had a doctor see her, but he said she wasn’t pregnant. These last couple of days, by the afternoon she’s too listless to move or even speak, and her sight swims. I told her, “There’s no need to stand on ceremony. Don’t come over morning and evening as you usually do. Just take good care of yourself. If any relations call, I’m here. Even if one of the elders finds fault, leave me to explain.” I’ve spoken to Rong as well. I told him, “You’re not to tire her or give her anything to be angry about. Let her rest quietly and she’ll get better. Whatever she fancies eating, come to me for it. If I haven’t got it, go and ask your Aunt Lian.

‘“If anything should happen to her, where would you find another wife with her looks and her disposition? You could search with a lantern and never find one.” With the way she conducts herself, which of our relations, which of the elders in the family doesn’t like her? So these last two days I’ve been dreadfully worried, quite beside myself. And then this morning her brother came to see her. The child has no sense. He could see his sister wasn’t well. Even if something had happened, he shouldn’t have told her. Never mind such a trifle as this, even if he’d suffered the most terrible injustice, he ought not to have told her.

‘It turns out there was a fight at school yesterday. Some boy who’s been allowed to study there, I don’t know who, had been bullying him. There was some dirty talk mixed up in it too, and he told his sister everything. You know what she’s like, Aunt. She may be all smiles and conversation when she sees people, and she knows how to behave, but she notices everything and takes it all to heart. Whatever she hears, she’ll turn it over for three days and five nights before she lets it go. It’s that worrying disposition of hers that has made her ill. Today, hearing that someone had bullied her brother, she was angry and upset. Angry with that pack of scoundrels he calls his friends, stirring up mischief and setting people against one another, and upset because her brother won’t behave or attend to his books, so that he gets into these rows at school. After hearing all this, she wouldn’t even eat her breakfast. When they told me, I went over and comforted her for a while, and talked to her brother too, to calm him down. I sent him off to the other mansion to find Bao-yu, and stayed till I’d seen her take half a small bowl of bird’s-nest soup. Only then did I come back.

‘Now, Aunt, wouldn’t you say I have reason to worry? And we haven’t even a good doctor. Whenever I think of her illness, it’s as if someone were sticking needles into my heart. Do you know of a good doctor anywhere?’

By the time she had heard all this, Mrs Huang, née Jin, had been so thoroughly frightened that the fiery determination to have things out with Qin-shi, which had filled her at her sister-in-law’s, had fled to the far side of the world. When You-shi asked whether she knew a good doctor, she hastened to reply. ‘We hear this and that, but I really haven’t heard anyone mention a good doctor. From what you say about Mrs Rong, though, it might still be a pregnancy. Don’t let them treat her at random, sister. It would be a dreadful thing if they mistook her condition.’ ‘Exactly,’ said You-shi. While they were speaking, Jia Zhen came in. Seeing the visitor, he asked You-shi, ‘Isn’t this Mrs Huang?’ Mrs Huang stepped forward to pay her respects. ‘Make our cousin stay for a meal,’ Jia Zhen told You-shi, and went on into the next room. Mrs Huang had come to speak to Qin-shi about Qin Zhong’s bullying her nephew. Now that she had heard Qin-shi was ill, she not only could not speak of it, but dared not even mention it.

Besides, Jia Zhen and You-shi had received her so kindly that her anger turned to pleasure. She stayed to chat a little longer before going home. After she had gone, Jia Zhen came back and sat down. ‘Did she have anything in particular to say today?’ he asked You-shi. ‘Not really. When she first came in, she looked as if something had put her out. But after we’d talked for a while and I’d told her about our daughter-in-law’s illness, she gradually looked calmer. Then you asked her to stay for a meal. Hearing how ill the girl is, she felt she oughtn’t to sit on and on, so she said a few more words and left. She didn’t ask for anything. But to come back to our daughter-in-law, the urgent thing is for you to find a good doctor to see her. Don’t let it drag on. What use are these doctors who come here now? Every one of them listens for what people want to hear, then repeats what they’ve said with a few learned phrases thrown in.

‘They’re attentive enough, I’ll grant them that. Three or four of them take turns coming to feel her pulse, four or five visits a day. They confer over a prescription, but the medicine does no good. All they achieve is to make her change her clothes four or five times a day and sit up to see them. It’s really of no benefit to the patient.’

‘Quite,’ said Jia Zhen. ‘But the child is being foolish too. Why keep taking her clothes off and changing them? If she catches cold and adds another illness to this one, what then? However fine her clothes may be, what are they worth beside her health? Even a new outfit every day would cost nothing worth speaking of. I was just coming in to tell you: Feng Zi-ying called a little while ago. He noticed I looked downcast and asked what was wrong. I told him our daughter-in-law had suddenly become very unwell, and without a good physician we couldn’t find out whether she was pregnant or ill, or whether there was any danger. That was why I’d been so anxious these last two days. Feng Zi-ying told me about a teacher he studied under as a boy, a man called Zhang You-shi. He says he’s a most learned scholar and has a profound knowledge of medicine as well, and can even tell whether a person will live or die. He’s come to the capital this year to purchase an official appointment for his son, and is staying with Feng Zi-ying. Perhaps it’s our daughter-in-law’s good fortune, after all, to have him here to bring her safely through this illness.

‘I sent a man off at once with my visiting card to ask him to come. If it’s too late for him today, I’m sure he’ll be here tomorrow. And Feng Zi-ying went straight home to ask him in person and make certain he came to see her.

‘Let’s wait till Mr Zhang has examined her.’ You-shi was delighted to hear this. ‘The old master’s birthday is the day after tomorrow,’ she said. ‘What are we going to do about it?’ ‘I’ve just been to pay my respects to him,’ said Jia Zhen, ‘and to ask him to come home so the whole family could offer their congratulations. He said, “I’m used to peace and quiet. I don’t want to get caught up in that whirl of squabbles at your place. If you insist that, because it’s my birthday, I must come and have everyone kowtow to me, you’d do a hundred times better to have a fair copy made of the Tract on Hidden Virtue I annotated some time ago and get it cut on printing blocks. Far better than having people knock their heads to me for no reason. If the family are coming over for a day or two around my birthday, you stay at home and entertain them properly. There’s no need to send me anything, and you needn’t come yourself on the day. If that troubles your conscience, you can kowtow to me now before you go. If you do come on my birthday and bring a whole crowd after you to disturb me, I shan’t let you off!” He said it over and over, so I certainly shan’t dare go.

‘Send for Lai Sheng and tell him to prepare two days of feasting.’ You-shi had Jia Rong called in. ‘Tell Lai Sheng to arrange two days’ feasting as usual, and make it generous. Then go over to the Rong mansion yourself and invite Grandmother Jia, Lady Xing, Lady Wang and your Aunt Lian to come and spend some time with us. Your father has heard of a good doctor today and has already sent to ask him here. He’ll certainly come tomorrow, I should think. You must give him a full account of your wife’s symptoms over these last few days.’

Jia Rong agreed to each instruction and went out. He met the servant who had been sent to Feng Zi-ying’s house to fetch the doctor, just back from his errand. ‘I went to Master Feng’s with the master’s visiting card to ask the gentleman to come,’ the servant reported. ‘He said, “Mr Feng has just spoken to me about it too. But I’ve been out paying calls all day and have only just got back. I’m quite exhausted. Even if I went over now, I couldn’t examine the patient’s pulse.” He said he’d rest for the night and be sure to come tomorrow. He also said, “My knowledge of medicine is slight. I really don’t deserve so warm a recommendation. But since Mr Feng and your master have both asked me, I can hardly refuse. Please explain this to your master for me. I’m not worthy of the honour of his visiting card.” So he made me bring it back. Would you pass on the message for me, Master Rong?’ Jia Rong went back inside, reported to Jia Zhen and You-shi, then came out again and sent for Lai Sheng to give him the instructions for the two days’ feasting.

Lai Sheng heard his orders and went to make the customary arrangements. We need say no more of that. At noon the next day, a servant announced that the invited Mr Zhang had arrived. Jia Zhen showed him into the main hall and asked him to sit down. When they had taken tea, he began: ‘Mr Feng told me yesterday of your character and learning, sir, and of your profound knowledge of medicine. I cannot tell you how much I admire you.’ ‘I am only a coarse, insignificant scholar, with very little knowledge or understanding,’ said Mr Zhang. ‘Mr Feng told me yesterday how graciously you and your family receive men of learning, however humble. When you were kind enough to send for me, how could I refuse? But I have no real attainments, and am all the more ashamed.’ ‘You are too modest, sir,’ said Jia Zhen. ‘Please go in and see my daughter-in-law. We are relying on your skill to set my mind at rest.’ Jia Rong accompanied him inside. When they reached Jia Rong’s rooms and he saw Qin-shi, he asked, ‘This is your wife?’ ‘Yes,’ Jia Rong answered. ‘Please sit down, sir. May I tell you about my wife’s illness before you feel her pulse?’ ‘I think it would be better if I took her pulse first.

‘This is my first visit to your house, and I know nothing of the case. Mr Feng was so insistent that I should come and see her that I could hardly do otherwise. Let me examine her pulse first, and you can see whether what I say is correct. Then tell me how the illness has developed over these last few days. We can consider a prescription together, and you can decide then whether to use it.’ ‘You are most skilful, sir,’ said Jia Rong. ‘I only wish we had met sooner.

‘Please feel her pulse and see whether she can be cured, so that my father and mother may be reassured.’ The married women servants brought a large cushion and drew back Qin-shi’s sleeve to expose her wrist. Mr Zhang laid his fingers on her right pulse, steadied his breathing to count its beats, and concentrated on examining it for half a ke. Then he changed to the left hand and did the same. When he had finished, he said, ‘Let us sit outside.’

Jia Rong accompanied Mr Zhang to the outer room, where they sat on the bed. An older woman brought tea.

‘Please have some tea, sir,’ said Jia Rong.

He drank with Mr Zhang, then asked, ‘From the pulse, sir, do you think she can be cured or not?’

‘Your wife’s pulse is deep and rapid at the left cun position, deep and hidden at the left guan, fine and weak at the right cun, and deficient and lacking vitality at the right guan. A deep, rapid pulse at the left cun means that deficient heart-qi has generated fire. A deep, hidden pulse at the left guan means that qi in the liver is stagnant and its blood deficient. A fine, weak pulse at the right cun means that qi in the lung channel is greatly deficient. A pulse deficient and lacking vitality at the right guan means that spleen-earth is constrained by liver-wood. When deficient heart-qi generates fire, one should expect irregular periods and sleeplessness at night.

‘When the liver has deficient blood and stagnant qi, there must be pain and swelling beneath the ribs, delayed periods and a sensation of heat within the chest. When qi in the lung channel is greatly deficient, there will be bouts of dizziness, spontaneous sweating during the yin and mao hours, and a feeling of being in a boat. When spleen-earth is constrained by liver-wood, there must be loss of appetite, weariness of spirit, and aching, enfeebled limbs. From the pulse, I should expect all these symptoms. If anyone takes it for a pulse indicating pregnancy, I am afraid I cannot agree.’ An elderly woman who attended Qin-shi closely stood beside them. ‘That’s exactly how it is,’ she said. ‘You’ve described it as if you had second sight, sir. There’s nothing left for us to tell you.

‘We have several doctors attending her now, but not one has been able to tell us things as clearly and surely as you. One says she’s pregnant; another says she’s ill. This one says there’s no danger; that one’s afraid of the Winter Solstice. There’s never a definite word. Please, sir, tell us plainly.’

Mr Zhang smiled. ‘Those gentlemen have certainly delayed the treatment of your mistress’s condition. If she had been given medicine when she first began menstruating, she would never have come to this. Indeed, by now she would have been quite well. As it is, the delay has brought her illness to this pass. It must be a misfortune she was destined to suffer. As I see it, there are still three parts in ten of hope for a cure. Let us see how she takes my medicine. If she can sleep at night, that will give me another two parts of confidence. Her pulse tells me that your mistress is strong-willed and exceptionally intelligent. When people are too intelligent, things often fail to satisfy them, and when that happens often, they think and worry too much. In this illness, anxiety has injured the spleen and liver-wood has grown too strong, so that the menstrual blood cannot come at the proper time. Ask about her periods in the past. The intervals will never have been regularly shortened; they must have been regularly prolonged. Am I right?’ ‘Indeed you are,’ the woman replied. ‘They’ve never been shorter. She’s been two or three days late, and even as much as ten.’ ‘Excellent!’ said Mr Zhang.

‘There is the source of the illness. If she had taken medicines to nourish the heart and regulate her periods earlier, how could it have come to this? Now she is clearly showing signs of deficient water and excessive wood. Let us try medicine and see.’ He wrote a prescription and handed it to Jia Rong. It read: Decoction to Replenish Qi, Nourish the Blood, Fortify the Spleen and Harmonise the Liver Ginseng, two qian; baizhu, two qian, stir-fried with earth; yunling, three qian; shudi, four qian. Guishen, two qian, rinsed with wine; baishao, two qian; chuanxiong, one and a half qian; huangqi, three qian. Processed xiangfu, two qian; vinegar-prepared chaihu, eight fen; huaishanyao, two qian, stir-fried; genuine ejiao, two qian, stir-fried with clam-shell powder. Yanhusuo, one and a half qian, stir-fried with wine; prepared liquorice, eight fen. To guide the decoction: seven Jian lotus seeds with the bitter centres removed, and two red dates. ‘Most skilful,’ said Jia Rong when he had read it. ‘There is one more thing I must ask, sir. Will this illness ultimately put her life in danger?’ Mr Zhang smiled. ‘You are a very discerning gentleman. An illness does not reach this stage overnight. Even with this medicine, much will depend on her good fortune in finding the right treatment. As I see it, she should come to no harm throughout this winter.

‘Once she is past the Spring Equinox, there will be hope of a full recovery.’ Jia Rong was an intelligent young man himself, and did not press him further. He saw Mr Zhang out, then took the prescription and the record of his findings to Jia Zhen. He repeated everything that had been said to both Jia Zhen and You-shi. ‘No doctor has ever spoken so plainly,’ You-shi said to Jia Zhen. ‘I imagine his medicine will be good too.’ ‘He’s not one of those men who has spent his life practising medicine to earn his keep,’ said Jia Zhen.

‘It was only because Feng Zi-ying is a friend of ours that he managed, with some difficulty, to persuade him to come. Now that we’ve found him, perhaps our daughter-in-law will get well. There’s ginseng in his prescription. Use the one catty of good ginseng we bought the other day.’ When Jia Rong had heard these instructions, he went out and sent someone to get the medicine, have it decocted and give it to Qin-shi. To learn how her illness fared after she had taken it, you must read the next chapter.
