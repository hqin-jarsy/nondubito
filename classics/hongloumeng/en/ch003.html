Chapter 3 Jia Yu-cun is restored to office in Jinling; Lin Dai-yu is taken into the Rong mansion

Chapter end

When Yu-cun hurriedly turned to look, the man proved to be an old colleague, Zhang Ru-gui, who had been impeached and dismissed in the same case as himself. Zhang was a native of this district and had been living at home since his dismissal. He had now heard that a memorial requesting the reinstatement of former officials had been approved in the capital, and was looking everywhere for connections and a way forward. Happening upon Yu-cun, he had hastened to congratulate him. They exchanged greetings, and Zhang Ru-gui told him the news. Yu-cun was naturally delighted. After a few hurried words they parted, each to return home. On hearing of this, Leng Zi-xing at once suggested that Yu-cun ask Lin Ru-hai to intercede with Jia Zheng in the capital. Yu-cun took his meaning, said goodbye and returned to his lodgings, where he immediately sought out the Court Gazette to make sure. The next day he discussed the matter with Ru-hai in person. ‘Heaven has arranged this very conveniently,’ said Ru-hai. ‘Since my wife’s death, my mother-in-law in the capital has been concerned that my little girl has no one to care for her and bring her up. She has already sent men and women servants, with boats, to fetch her. The child has not yet fully recovered, so they have not been able to leave.

‘I was just reflecting that I had done nothing to repay your kindness in teaching her. How could I fail to do my utmost for you when such an opportunity presents itself? Set your mind at rest. I have already made provision for this and written a letter of recommendation, asking my brother-in-law to give you every assistance. Only then shall I have done something, however little, to show my gratitude. As for any customary expenses, I have made that quite clear in my letter too. You need not trouble yourself about them.’ Yu-cun bowed repeatedly, thanking him without cease. He also asked, ‘May I inquire what office your distinguished relative now holds? I am afraid of being too hasty. I should not like to rush up to the capital and impose upon him.’ ‘My relations are of your own clan, if one goes by the genealogies,’ Ru-hai said with a smile. ‘They are grandsons of the Duke of Rong. The elder of my wife’s brothers has inherited the title of General of the First Rank. His name is She, his courtesy name En-hou. Her second brother, Zheng, whose courtesy name is Cun-zhou, is an assistant director in the Board of Works. He is modest, courteous and generous, with much of his grandfather’s character. He is not one of those frivolous men born to wealth who happen to hold office. That is why I have written to ask his help. Otherwise it would not only compromise your integrity: I should scorn to do it myself.’ Only now did Yu-cun feel sure of what Zi-xing had told him the day before. He thanked Lin Ru-hai once more.

Ru-hai went on, ‘We have chosen the second day of next month for my daughter’s journey to the capital. Would it not suit you both to travel together?’ Yu-cun readily agreed to everything, inwardly delighted. Ru-hai then arranged gifts and a farewell entertainment, all of which Yu-cun accepted.

Dai-yu, the young pupil, was only just recovering. At first she could not bear to leave her father, but her grandmother had sent word insisting that she come, and Ru-hai himself said, ‘Your father is nearly fifty. I have no intention of marrying again. You are often ill and still so young, with no mother to bring you up and no brothers or sisters to support you. If you go to your grandmother and your uncles’ family, with your girl cousins for company, I shall have less cause to worry about your care. Why do you still say you will not go?’ Only then did Dai-yu take leave of him, bowing through her tears,

and board the boat with her nurse and several elderly women from the Rong household. Yu-cun had a boat of his own and two young servants, and travelled in Dai-yu’s company. In due course they reached the capital and entered the imperial city. Yu-cun first put his clothes and cap in order, then took his boys to the gate of the Rong mansion and sent in a visiting card describing himself as a nephew of the same clan. Jia Zheng had already read his brother-in-law’s letter and promptly invited him in. Yu-cun was an imposing figure and spoke well. Jia Zheng had a particular regard for men of learning: he honoured talent without standing on his own rank, helped the weak and relieved those in distress, much as his forebears had done. With his brother-in-law’s request added to these considerations, he treated Yu-cun with exceptional favour and worked strenuously for him behind the scenes. When the memorial was submitted, it was a simple matter to secure his reinstatement, pending a vacancy. In less than two months the prefecture of Yingtian, at Jinling, fell vacant, and this post was obtained for him. Yu-cun took leave of Jia Zheng, chose a day and went off to assume office. We need say no more of that.

When Dai-yu left her boat and came ashore, a sedan chair and carts for her luggage had long been waiting, sent by the Rong household. She had often heard her mother say that her grandmother’s family was unlike other families. Even the third-ranking women servants whom she had seen in the last few days ate, dressed and spent in a manner far from ordinary. What must the house itself be like? She therefore watched every step and took care at every moment, unwilling to say one word too many or take a single unnecessary step, for fear of making herself ridiculous. Once seated in the chair and carried into the city, she looked out through its gauze window. The busy streets and crowded population were indeed unlike anything elsewhere. After they had gone on for some time, she suddenly saw two great stone lions crouching on the north side of the street, before a three-bay gateway ornamented with animal heads. Ten or more men in splendid caps and clothes sat in rows outside. The central gates were shut; people came and went only through the smaller gates to east and west. Above the main entrance a tablet bore five great characters: ‘Ning Mansion, Built by Imperial Command’.

‘This must be my grandfather’s elder branch of the family,’ Dai-yu thought. As she was thinking this, they went a little farther west and came to another three-bay gateway just like it. This was the Rong mansion. They did not use the main entrance, but went in through the small western gate. The bearers carried her about a bowshot inside, then set down the chair as they were about to turn a corner, and withdrew. The women following behind had already got out of their chairs and now came hurrying up. Three or four neatly dressed young servants of seventeen or eighteen took the bearers’ places and lifted the chair again. The women walked round it as it was carried to an inner gate with pendant-flower carving and set down.

The young servants withdrew. The women came forward to lift the curtain and help Dai-yu out. With her hand resting on one of the women’s, Lin Dai-yu passed through the flower-carved gate. Covered walks ran round both sides; in the centre stood a passage-hall, with a great marble screen on a rosewood stand. Beyond the screen was a small three-bay hall, and behind that the main courtyard of the inner apartments.

The principal range facing them had five bays, all carved beams and painted woodwork, with covered walks passing through the gables to side rooms on either hand. Parrots, hwamei and other birds hung in cages along them. Several maids in red and green were sitting on the terrace. The moment they saw the party they came forward, smiling. ‘Her Old Ladyship was only just asking about you. And here you are!’ Three or four competed to raise the door-curtain. Someone could be heard announcing, ‘Miss Lin has arrived.’

As Dai-yu entered, she saw an old lady with silver hair coming towards her, supported by two attendants, and knew at once that this was her grandmother. Before she could make her obeisance, the old lady had caught her in her arms and burst into loud weeping, calling her her heart and her darling flesh. Everyone standing in attendance covered their faces and cried. Dai-yu too wept without stopping. After a time the others gradually persuaded them to be calm, and only then could Dai-yu greet her grandmother formally. This was the old Lady Shi of whom Leng Zi-xing had spoken, the mother of Jia She and Jia Zheng. Grandmother Jia now introduced the others one by one. ‘This is your elder uncle’s wife. This is your second uncle’s wife. And this is your late Cousin Zhu’s wife, your sister-in-law.’ Dai-yu paid her respects to each in turn. ‘Ask the girls to come,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘Our visitor has only just arrived from far away. They need not go to their lessons today.’ The servants answered, and two went off.

Before long three girls arrived, surrounded by three nurses and five or six maids. The first was moderately tall, her figure a little full, her cheeks as smooth as freshly peeled lychees, her nose as creamy as goose fat. Gentle and quiet, she looked easy to approach. The second was tall and slender, with sloping shoulders, a narrow waist, an oval face, lovely eyes and finely shaped brows. Her eyes moved with spirit; there was a brilliance about her that made one forget the commonplace. The third had not yet grown to her full height and still looked quite a child. All three wore the same ornaments, skirts and jackets. Dai-yu rose quickly to greet them. They exchanged courtesies and learned who each other was, then all sat down again. The maids poured tea. They spoke only of how Dai-yu’s mother had fallen ill, the doctors and medicines, her death and the funeral arrangements. Grandmother Jia could not help grieving again. ‘Of all my children, your mother was the one I loved most. And now she has gone before me, and I could not even see her face. How can I look at you without feeling heartbroken?’ She held Dai-yu to her again and began to sob.

Everyone hurried to console her, until she was a little calmer. They could see that young as Dai-yu was, her speech and manner were far from common. Though her face and body seemed frail almost beyond endurance, there was a natural grace about her. They recognised that she suffered from a constitutional weakness and asked what medicines she regularly took, and why no prompt treatment had been undertaken. ‘I have always been like this,’ Dai-yu said. ‘I began taking medicine as soon as I could eat, and I have never stopped. Any number of famous doctors have written prescriptions and prepared medicines, but none has done any good. I am told that when I was three a scabby-headed monk came and asked my parents to give me up to the religious life. Of course they refused. He said, “If you cannot bear to part with her, I fear her illness will never be cured.

‘“To recover, she must never again be exposed to the sound of weeping. Apart from her parents, she must see no relations or friends of another surname. Only then can she live out her life in peace.” He was quite mad, talking such nonsense, and nobody paid him any attention. I am still taking ginseng pills to nourish the blood.’

‘Just as well,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘I am having pills made up here. They need only prepare an extra batch.’ Before she had finished, laughter sounded in the courtyard behind, and a voice called, ‘I’m late! I wasn’t here to welcome our visitor!’ Dai-yu wondered, ‘Everyone here has been holding their breath and speaking so quietly, so solemn and respectful. Who can this be, behaving with such abandon and so little ceremony?’

While she was thinking this, a woman came in through the rear doorway, surrounded by married women servants and maids. She was dressed quite differently from the girls. Brilliant in coloured embroidery, she might have been a goddess or an immortal. Gold filigree adorned her coiled hair, set with pearls and the Eight Treasures; her hairpin showed five phoenixes facing the rising sun, with hanging pearls. Round her neck she wore a pure-gold circlet of coiling hornless dragons. From her skirt hung a pea-green palace sash and rose-coloured stone pendants carved as paired fish beneath two crosspieces. Her close-fitting scarlet foreign-satin jacket was worked in gold with a hundred butterflies among flowers. Over it she wore a dark-blue coat lined with ermine and patterned in five-coloured silk tapestry, and below it an emerald-green skirt of foreign crêpe scattered with flowers. Her phoenix eyes tapered to sharp corners; her brows curved like willow leaves, lifting at the ends. She was slender and alluring. Her powdered face held a springtime warmth, its authority concealed; her laughter reached one before her red lips parted. Dai-yu rose at once to meet her. ‘You don’t know her,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘She’s our famous little rogue, our good-for-nothing. Down south they call such a person a “peppercorn”. Just call her “Peppercorn Feng”.’ Dai-yu was still unsure how to address her when the girls hurried to explain, ‘This is Cousin Lian’s wife.’ Though she had never met her, Dai-yu had heard her mother say that Jia Lian, the son of her elder uncle Jia She, had married a niece of her second aunt, Lady Wang. The girl had been brought up as a boy from childhood; her formal name was Wang Xi-feng.

Dai-yu smiled in response and paid her respects, calling her ‘Sister-in-law’. Xi-feng took her hand and looked her carefully up and down, then led her back to sit beside Grandmother Jia. ‘So there really are such beauties in the world!’ she said, smiling. ‘I had never seen one till today. And with that bearing, you would never take her for your daughter’s child, Grandmother. She looks like your own son’s daughter. No wonder you think and talk of her all day long.

‘Only it’s so pitiful that this dear little cousin should have such a hard lot. Why did Aunt have to die?’ She wiped her eyes with her handkerchief. ‘I had only just stopped, and here you are setting me off again,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘Your cousin has had a long journey, and she’s frail too. We had only just consoled her. Now, no more about that!’ Xi-feng immediately changed from sorrow to cheerfulness. ‘Of course! The moment I saw her, I could think of nothing else. I was so glad and so sad that I quite forgot you, Grandmother. I deserve a good beating!’ She took Dai-yu’s hand again. ‘How old are you, my dear? Have you been to lessons? What medicine are you taking? You mustn’t pine for home here. If there’s anything you’d like to eat or play with, just tell me. And if the maids or old women don’t behave, tell me that too.’ She turned to the women servants. ‘Has Miss Lin’s luggage been brought in? How many people came with her? Get two rooms in the servants’ quarters cleaned up at once, and let them have a rest.’

While they were talking, tea and fruit had been laid out. Xi-feng herself presented Dai-yu with both. Dai-yu then heard her second aunt ask Xi-feng, ‘Have the monthly allowances been paid?’ ‘They’ve all been paid. I was just taking some people up to the back storey to look for satin. We hunted for ages and couldn’t find the kind you mentioned yesterday. Perhaps you remembered it wrongly, ma’am?’ ‘It hardly matters whether there is any,’ Lady Wang said. Then she added, ‘You should have taken out a couple of lengths while you were there, to make clothes for your cousin. Remind me this evening to have someone fetch them. Don’t forget.’ ‘I had thought of that already,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Knowing she was due any day now, I’ve set some aside. Once you’ve been back and seen them, I can send them over.’ Lady Wang smiled and nodded without speaking.

When the tea and fruit had been cleared away, Grandmother Jia told two old nurses to take Dai-yu to pay her respects to her two maternal uncles. Jia She’s wife, Lady Xing, rose too. ‘It would be convenient for me to take my niece over,’ she said with a smile. ‘Yes, do go,’ Grandmother Jia replied, smiling. ‘You needn’t come back here.’ Lady Xing answered, ‘Yes,’ and took Dai-yu to say goodbye to Lady Wang. Everyone accompanied them as far as the passage-hall. Outside the flower-carved gate the young servants had already brought up a carriage with green curtains and a blue silk cover. Lady Xing took Dai-yu inside. The women lowered the curtains before ordering the young servants to lift and draw it to a wider space, where a docile mule was harnessed to it. They too went out through the small western gate, turned east past the main entrance of the Rong mansion and entered a great black-lacquered gateway, stopping before the ceremonial inner gate. Only after the young servants had withdrawn were the carriage curtains raised. Holding Dai-yu by the hand, Lady Xing led her into the courtyard. From the arrangement of the buildings and courtyards, Dai-yu judged that this must have been partitioned off from the Rong mansion’s garden.

After they had passed through three ceremonial gates, she found that the main rooms, side rooms and covered walks were all small and finely arranged, quite unlike the lofty splendour of the apartments she had just left. Trees and ornamental rocks stood everywhere in the courtyard. They entered the principal room, where a number of concubines and maids in splendid dress came to meet them. Lady Xing asked Dai-yu to sit down and sent someone to fetch Jia She from the outer study. Presently the messenger returned. ‘His Lordship says, “I have been unwell for several days. Seeing the young lady would only grieve us both, and for the moment I cannot bear it. Tell her not to be sad or homesick. With her grandmother and her aunts, it will be just as though she were at home. Her cousins may be dull girls, but their company will help to relieve her loneliness. If anything troubles her, she must say so, and not treat us as strangers.”’ Dai-yu stood up quickly and listened to the whole message. After sitting a little longer, she asked leave to go.

Lady Xing pressed her to stay for supper, but Dai-yu replied with a smile, ‘It is very kind of you to offer me supper, Aunt, and I ought not to refuse. But I have still to pay my respects to my second uncle. I am afraid it would be discourteous to go only after I had eaten. May I accept your kindness another day? I hope you will forgive me.’ ‘Of course,’ Lady Xing said, smiling.

She instructed two or three nurses to take good care of the young lady and escort her back in the same carriage. Dai-yu took her leave. Lady Xing saw her as far as the ceremonial gate, gave the servants a few more instructions, and watched the carriage go before returning inside.

Soon Dai-yu was back at the Rong mansion and alighted from the carriage. The nurses led her round to the east, through an east–west passage-hall, to a large courtyard inside the ceremonial gate behind the great south-facing hall. The principal building had five bays. Side rooms and low-roofed annexes joined it through the gables, with passages leading in every direction. Its imposing magnificence was quite different from Grandmother Jia’s apartments. Dai-yu recognised this as the principal inner residence. A broad paved walk led straight out to the main gate. On entering the hall, the first thing she saw when she looked up was a great blue tablet bearing nine dragons in gold. Three enormous characters read ‘Rong-xi Hall’. A small inscription behind them said, ‘Written and bestowed upon Jia Yuan, Duke of Rong, on such-and-such a day, month and year’, followed by the imperial seal, ‘Treasure of the Imperial Brush Amid Myriad Affairs’. On a great rosewood table carved with hornless dragons stood an ancient bronze tripod, some three chi high, with a blue-green patina. Above hung a large ink painting of a dragon, entitled ‘Awaiting the Water-clock to Attend Court’. On one side was a golden ape-shaped ritual vessel; on the other, a large glass drinking vessel. Below, sixteen nanmu folding chairs stood in two rows. A couplet, its engraved silver characters inlaid in ebony boards, read:

Pearls and jewels upon these seats shine with the sun and moon;

Robes of office before this hall set cloud and mist ablaze.

Below, in smaller characters, was written: ‘Respectfully inscribed by Mu Shi, hereditary Prince of Dong’an, a junior fellow countryman bound to you by generations of family friendship.’ Lady Wang did not ordinarily sit or rest in this main hall, but in the three-bay suite to its east. The old nurses now led Dai-yu through the eastern doorway. The great kang beneath the window was covered with a scarlet foreign woollen rug. A scarlet backrest with a pattern of coin-roundels and mang dragons stood against the wall, with dark-blue bolsters and a long olive-yellow mattress in the same design. On either side was a little plum-blossom-shaped table in foreign lacquer. On the left stood a King Wen-style tripod incense burner, with its spoon, tongs and incense box; on the right a slender ‘beauty’ vase of Ru ware, filled with flowers in season, as well as tea bowls, a spittoon and other things. Four chairs faced west below the kang, each draped in a pink floral cover, with four footstools beneath. A pair of tall stands flanked the chairs, also furnished with tea bowls and vases of flowers.

There is no need to describe the remaining furnishings in detail. The old nurses invited Dai-yu to sit on the kang. But there were two brocade cushions set opposite one another along its edge, and, judging the precedence of the places, she declined to mount it and sat instead on a chair to the east. The maids of these apartments hurried to bring tea. As Dai-yu drank, she observed their clothes and ornaments, their bearing and movements. They too were indeed unlike the servants of other households.

Before she had finished her tea, a maid in a red silk-twill jacket and a blue satin waistcoat with piped edges came in, smiling. ‘Her Ladyship asks Miss Lin to come and sit in the other room.’ The nurses led Dai-yu out and into a small three-bay range off the eastern covered walk. A low table stood across the kang in the main room, piled with books and tea things. Against the eastern wall, facing west, were a somewhat worn blue satin backrest and bolsters. Lady Wang sat at the lower place to the west, on a cushion with a backrest of the same worn blue satin. Seeing Dai-yu, she invited her to take the eastern place. Dai-yu felt certain that this was Jia Zheng’s seat. Beside the kang she noticed a row of three chairs draped with somewhat worn

covers patterned with splashes of ink, and sat on one of these instead. Only after Lady Wang had repeatedly taken her hand and urged her up onto the kang did she sit beside her. ‘Your uncle has gone to observe a fast today,’ Lady Wang said. ‘You can see him another time. There is just one thing I must tell you. Your three girl cousins are all very good. You will study, learn your characters and do needlework together, and if you sometimes play or joke, they will always give way to you. But there is one thing that worries me greatly. I have a son who is the very root of mischief, born to cause trouble,

‘the “Demon King” of this household. He went to the temple today to fulfil a vow and is not back yet. You’ll understand when you see him this evening. From now on you must take no notice of him. None of your girl cousins dares to provoke him.’

Dai-yu had often heard her mother speak of a boy cousin, the son of her second aunt, who had been born with a piece of jade in his mouth. He was extraordinarily unruly, hated studying, and liked nothing better than to spend his time among the women and girls. His grandmother doted on him, and no one dared discipline him. From Lady Wang’s words Dai-yu knew that this must be the cousin she meant. She smiled politely. ‘Do you mean the cousin who was born with the jade, Aunt? At home I often heard Mother speak of him. She said he was a year older than me and his childhood name was Bao-yu; although he was very wilful and mischievous, he was wonderfully affectionate towards his sisters and girl cousins. Now that I am here, I shall naturally be with the girls. The boys have their own rooms in another courtyard. How could I have occasion to provoke him?’ ‘You don’t understand,’ Lady Wang said, smiling. ‘He is not like other boys. From his earliest years your grandmother has spoilt him, bringing him up with the girls, and he is used to being indulged among them. On a day when they pay him no attention, he is rather quieter. Even if he feels neglected, he only goes outside the inner gate and takes it out on his two little pages where no one can see. A little muttering, and it is over.

‘But if the girls say one word more than usual to him that day, he is so pleased with himself that there is no end to the trouble he makes. That is why I am telling you to take no notice of him. One moment he is all honeyed words, the next he acknowledges neither heaven nor authority, and the next he talks like a madman or an idiot. Just don’t believe him.’

Dai-yu agreed to each of these instructions. A maid then came to announce, ‘Supper is being served at Her Old Ladyship’s.’ Lady Wang at once took Dai-yu through the rear doorway, west along the covered walk and out through a side gate into a broad north–south passage between walls. To the south stood a small three-bay hall, its back towards the outer approach, with a projecting covered porch; to the north was a great screen wall painted white. Behind it were a smallish gateway and a small set of rooms. Lady Wang pointed them out with a smile. ‘Those are your Cousin Feng’s rooms. You can come and find her here later. Whatever you need, just tell her.’ Four or five young servants, still with their hair tied in little children’s tufts, stood by the courtyard gate with their hands at their sides. Lady Wang led Dai-yu through another east–west passage-hall into Grandmother Jia’s rear courtyard. They entered by the back door, where many attendants were already waiting. Only when Lady Wang arrived were the table and chairs set in place. Jia Zhu’s wife, née Li, brought the rice; Xi-feng laid out the chopsticks; Lady Wang presented the soup.

Grandmother Jia sat alone on the couch facing the room, with four empty chairs, two on either side. Xi-feng hurried to draw Dai-yu to the first chair on the left, but Dai-yu was most reluctant to accept it. ‘Your aunts and your cousins’ wives don’t eat here,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘You are our guest. That is where you ought to sit.’ Only then did Dai-yu ask leave to be seated and sit down. Grandmother Jia told Lady Wang to sit. Ying-chun and the other two girls likewise asked leave before coming forward. Ying-chun took the first chair on the right, Tan-chun the second on the left, and Xi-chun the second on the right. Maids stood beside them holding fly-whisks, rinsing bowls and towels. Li and Xi-feng stood by the table, serving and inviting them to eat. Though many women servants and maids waited in the outer room, not so much as a cough was heard. The meal ended in silence. Maids brought each of them tea on small trays. Lin Ru-hai had taught his daughter to cherish her good fortune and preserve her health: after a meal she must be sure that every grain of rice had been swallowed and wait a while before drinking tea, so as not to harm the digestion. But Dai-yu had already seen many things here that differed from the customs at home and that she would have to follow. She must change her ways one by one. Accordingly she accepted the tea.

Someone promptly brought a rinsing bowl, and Dai-yu followed the others in rinsing her mouth. After they had washed their hands, more tea was brought. This was the tea for drinking. ‘You may go now,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘Let us talk at our ease.’ Lady Wang rose at once. After a few more casual remarks she took Xi-feng and Li away. Grandmother Jia asked Dai-yu what she was studying.

‘I have only just been studying the Four Books,’ Dai-yu said. She then asked what her cousins were reading. ‘Reading?’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘They only learn a few characters, so that they won’t be blind with their eyes open!’

She had scarcely finished when footsteps sounded outside. A maid came in, smiling. ‘Bao-yu is here!’ Dai-yu was wondering, ‘What sort of idle scamp is this Bao-yu? What sort of muddle-headed, naughty child?’

She would have been quite content not to meet the stupid creature at all. Before the maid had finished announcing him, however, a young gentleman had entered. His hair was held by a coronet of reddish gold set with gems; level with his brows lay a golden band worked with two dragons contending for a pearl. He wore a scarlet, tight-sleeved robe patterned in two shades of gold with a hundred butterflies among flowers, a palace sash of five-coloured silk tied in flower-knots with long tassels, and over it a dark-blue Japanese-satin coat with eight roundels in raised weave and a fringe of tassels. On his feet were little court boots of blue satin with white soles. His face was like the mid-autumn moon, his colour like a flower at spring dawn. The hair at his temples was trimmed as if with a knife, his brows drawn in ink; his cheeks were peach petals, his eyes autumn waters. Even in anger he would seem to smile; even a reproachful glance would hold affection. About his neck was a gold circlet of hornless dragons, and on a cord of five-coloured silk hung a beautiful piece of jade. Dai-yu was astonished at the sight of him. ‘How strange! It is as though I have seen him somewhere before. How can he look so familiar?’ Bao-yu greeted Grandmother Jia. ‘Go and see your mother,’ she told him, and he turned and went out.

When he returned a little later, he had changed his clothes and headgear. The short hair round his head was plaited into little braids tied with red silk, all gathered into the lock left to grow from infancy at the crown, and woven into a single long plait, black and shining as lacquer. A row of four large pearls ran from crown to tip, with a golden Eight Treasures ornament hanging at the end. He wore a somewhat worn pink long jacket scattered with flowers, and still had on his neck circlet, jade, the lock marking his dedication to a religious guardian, his protective charms and the rest. Below showed part of his pine-pollen-green flowered silk-twill trousers, ink-splashed stockings edged with brocade, and thick-soled scarlet shoes. His face looked more than ever as if it had been powdered, his lips as though touched with rouge. Affection moved in his glances; laughter was never far from his speech. All his natural charm was gathered in the tips of his brows, a lifetime’s countless feelings crowded into the corners of his eyes. His appearance was certainly superb, but what lay beneath it was harder to know. A later writer composed two lyrics to the tune ‘Moon over the West River’ which judged Bao-yu very aptly:

For no reason he seeks grief and resentment; At times he seems an idiot, at times mad. Granted, he was born with a handsome skin: Inside, nothing grows but weeds.

Good for nothing, ignorant of worldly business, Stupid and stubborn, afraid to study books; Wayward in conduct, perverse in disposition, What does he care for the world’s slander?

In wealth and honour he takes no joy in his proper work; In poverty he could not bear the desolation. Alas, he wastes the best years of his life: Neither state nor family can hope for anything from him.

First under heaven for incompetence, Without an equal, past or present, in unworthiness. A word to you, sons of silk and rich food: Do not model yourselves on this boy!

Grandmother Jia laughed. ‘Changing out of your clothes before you’ve even met our visitor! Go and greet your cousin.’ Bao-yu had already noticed the additional girl and guessed that she was his Aunt Lin’s daughter. He hurried over and bowed. When they had exchanged greetings and sat down again, he studied her features. She was unlike all the others: Two mist-veiled brows, drawn together, yet not quite frowning; Two dew-filled eyes, seeming glad, yet not quite glad. Sorrow shaped the two dimples in her cheeks; Illness lent its charm to all her slender body. Her tears glimmered, drop by drop; Her breath came in faint little gasps. At rest, a lovely flower reflected in water; In motion, a frail willow held by the wind. Her heart had one opening more than Bi Gan’s; Her frailty surpassed Xi Shi’s by three degrees. After looking at her, Bao-yu said with a smile, ‘I have met this cousin before.’

‘Nonsense again!’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘When could you ever have met her?’ Bao-yu smiled. ‘Even if I have never met her, her face is familiar. In my heart she counts as an old acquaintance. Why shouldn’t we treat today as a meeting after a long separation?’ ‘Better still! Better still!’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘Then you will get on all the better.’ Bao-yu moved over to sit beside Dai-yu and studied her again. ‘Have you done any reading, Cousin?’ ‘No,’ Dai-yu said. ‘I only went to lessons for a year. I know a few characters, that’s all.’ ‘What are the two characters of your name?’ Dai-yu told him. He asked her courtesy name. ‘I haven’t one.’

‘Let me give you a wonderful one,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘Nothing could be better than Pin-pin, “Frown-frown”.’ Tan-chun asked where he had found it. ‘The Comprehensive Survey of Persons Past and Present says, “In the west is a stone called dai, which can be used instead of ink for painting eyebrows.” And Cousin Lin’s brows look as though they are drawn together in a frown. Don’t those two characters make it doubly apt?’ ‘I’m afraid you’ve made that up again,’ Tan-chun said, laughing. Bao-yu laughed too. ‘Apart from the Four Books, there’s plenty that’s made up. Why am I the only one accused of inventing things?’ He asked Dai-yu another question. ‘Have you a jade too?’

No one understood what he meant. Dai-yu considered: ‘He has a jade himself, so he wants to know if I have one.’ ‘I haven’t anything like that,’ she answered. ‘It must be something very rare. How could everyone have one?’ At this Bao-yu instantly flew into one of his fits of madness. He tore off the jade and flung it down with all his strength. ‘What’s rare about it?’ he cried angrily. ‘It can’t even tell the better people from the worse, and they call it “spiritual”! Spiritual indeed! I don’t want the wretched thing!’ Everyone rushed in alarm to pick it up. Grandmother Jia clasped him to her in great distress. ‘You wicked boy!

‘If you’re angry, you can easily hit someone or scold them. Why must you throw away your very life?’ Bao-yu sobbed, his face streaked with tears. ‘None of the girls at home has one. I’m the only one, and I said it was no fun. Now this cousin has come, lovely as an immortal, and she hasn’t one either. That proves it’s no good.’ Grandmother Jia hurried to soothe him. ‘Your cousin did have one. But when your aunt died, she couldn’t bear to leave her little girl, and there was nothing else to be done, so she took your cousin’s jade with her. It served as a burial offering, to express your cousin’s devotion, and it let your aunt’s spirit feel that she could still see her daughter. That’s why your cousin says she hasn’t one. She doesn’t like to boast about herself. How can you compare yourself with her now? Put it on again and take good care of it. Mind your mother doesn’t hear of this!’ She took the jade from a maid and put it back on him herself. Bao-yu thought this explanation made excellent sense and raised no further objections. Just then Dai-yu’s nurse came to ask where she was to sleep.

‘For now, move Bao-yu out to the warm side-room with me,’ Grandmother Jia said, ‘and put Miss Lin inside the gauze-screened alcove. Once the rest of the winter is over, we can get rooms ready for them in the spring and make fresh arrangements.’ ‘Dearest Grandmother,’ Bao-yu said, ‘I shall be perfectly comfortable on the bed outside the gauze screen. Why move me out to disturb your peace?’ Grandmother Jia considered. ‘Very well.’ Each child was to have a nurse and a maid in attendance, with the others keeping watch in the outer room, ready to answer a call. Xi-feng had already sent over a flowered mauve bed-curtain, together with brocade quilts, satin mattresses and other bedding.

Dai-yu had brought only two attendants: Nurse Wang, who had nursed her from infancy, and a little maid of ten named Snowgoose, who had also been with her since she was very small. Grandmother Jia saw that Snowgoose was still thoroughly childish and Nurse Wang extremely old, and thought neither would be able to spare Dai-yu trouble or serve her as she wished. She therefore gave her one of her own second-ranking maids, called Ying-ge. In other respects Dai-yu was provided for on the same terms as Ying-chun and the others. Besides the nurse who had suckled each girl, there were four matrons to guide her conduct; besides the two personal maids who looked after her hair ornaments, washing and bathing, there were five or six little maids to sweep the rooms and run errands. Nurse Wang and Ying-ge now attended Dai-yu inside the green gauze screen. Outside, by the large bed, were Bao-yu’s wet-nurse, Nurse Li, and his senior maid, Aroma.

Aroma too had originally been Grandmother Jia’s maid, and her name had been Pearl. Doting on Bao-yu, Grandmother Jia had feared that none of his maids would give herself wholly to his service. She had always liked Aroma for her simple, good heart and the thoroughness with which she performed her duties, and had therefore given her to him. Bao-yu knew that her surname was Hua, ‘flower’, and had seen the phrase ‘the flowers’ aroma steals upon one’ in an old poem. He reported his wish to Grandmother Jia and changed her name to Aroma. Aroma had a certain single-mindedness of her own: when she served Grandmother Jia, the old lady alone filled her heart and eyes; now that she served Bao-yu, there was no one in her heart or eyes but Bao-yu. His perverse disposition, however, led her to remonstrate with him often and made her deeply anxious. That night, after Bao-yu and Nurse Li had gone to sleep, she saw that Dai-yu and Ying-ge were still awake inside. Having taken off her make-up, she slipped quietly in. ‘Why aren’t you resting yet, miss?’ she asked with a smile. ‘Please sit down, Sister,’ Dai-yu said at once. Aroma sat on the edge of the bed. ‘Miss Lin has been upset,’ Ying-ge said, smiling. ‘She was crying and wiping her eyes, saying, “I had only just arrived, and already I brought on your young master’s madness. If he had broken the jade, wouldn’t it have been my fault?” That’s what was troubling her. I’ve only just managed to comfort her.’ ‘Please don’t feel that way, miss,’ said Aroma. ‘You’ll probably see much stranger goings-on than that in future!

‘If you take his behaviour to heart and let it grieve you, I’m afraid there will be no end to your grieving. Please don’t fret over it!’ ‘I shall remember what you both tell me,’ Dai-yu said. ‘But where did that jade come from, after all? Does it have writing on it?’ ‘No one in the family knows where it came from,’ Aroma said. ‘There was already a hole in it. They say it was taken out of his mouth when he was born. Let me fetch it and you’ll see.’ Dai-yu stopped her at once. ‘No, never mind. It’s late now. Tomorrow will be soon enough.’ They talked a little longer, then at last went to bed.

Next morning, after getting up and paying her respects to Grandmother Jia, Dai-yu went to Lady Wang’s apartments. Lady Wang and Xi-feng were together, opening letters from Jinling. Two married women servants had also been sent with a message from Lady Wang’s brother and his wife. Dai-yu knew nothing of the circumstances, but Tan-chun and the others knew that they were discussing Xue Pan, their maternal cousin, the son of Aunt Xue in Jinling. Relying on his wealth and influence, he had beaten a man to death, and the case was now before the Yingtian prefecture. Their uncle Wang Zi-teng had heard the news and sent members of his household to tell Lady Wang’s family, with the intention of having the Xues called to the capital.
