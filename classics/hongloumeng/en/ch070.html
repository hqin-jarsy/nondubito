Chapter 70 Lin Dai-yu re-establishes the Peach-blossom Society; Shi Xiang-yun chances to compose a willow-catkin lyric

Chapter end

Now Jia Lian kept vigil at Pear-tree Court for seven days and nights, while Buddhist and Taoist priests performed services day after day without a break. Grandmother Jia sent for him and forbade him to take the coffin to the family temple. There was nothing he could do. He spoke to Shi-jue again, and a grave was marked out above You San-jie’s; the ground was broken and the burial took place. Only members of the clan, Wang Xin and his wife, and You-shi and her daughter-in-law attended the funeral. Xi-feng would have nothing to do with any of it, leaving him to make all the arrangements himself.

With the year’s end fast approaching, business of every kind was piling up. On top of it all, Lin Zhi-xiao brought in a list of eight unmarried pages, all twenty-five years old, who ought to marry and set up households. They were waiting to see whether any maids in the inner apartments were due to leave, so that they might ask to have wives allotted to them. Xi-feng read the list and went first to consult Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang. They discussed it together. Several maids were of an age to be married off, but each had some reason against it. First there was Faithful, who had sworn she would not go. Ever since that day, she had not spoken to Bao-yu, nor had she dressed in finery or worn elaborate ornaments.

Seeing how firm she was, no one liked to force her. Next came Amber, who was ill and could not be considered on this occasion. Cai-yun, too, had fallen out with Jia Huan recently and contracted an illness for which there was no cure. Only the older maids doing rough work in Xi-feng’s and Li Wan’s rooms were sent out; the others were not yet old enough. The men were told to find wives for themselves outside.

During this time, Xi-feng’s illness had left Li Wan and Tan-chun so busy managing the household that they had no leisure. Then came the New Year and its festivals, bringing a host of miscellaneous duties, and the poetry society was quite forgotten. Now it was mid-spring and there was time again. But Liu Xiang-lian’s cold withdrawal from the world, You San-jie’s death by the sword, You Er-jie’s death by gold and Liu Wu-er’s illness from distress had followed so closely upon one another that Bao-yu’s idle sorrows and unreasoning regrets kept mounting: before one weight was lifted, another was laid on him. His looks and manner became distraught and his speech often wandered, as though he were suffering from some disorder of the heart. Aroma and the others were alarmed but dared not tell Grandmother Jia. They could only try every means to coax him into laughter and play.

Early one morning he had just woken when he heard a ceaseless chatter of laughter in the outer room.

‘Quick, go and rescue her,’ Aroma said, laughing. ‘Skybright and Musk have pinned Wen-du-li-na down and are tickling her.’

Bao-yu hurriedly threw on his grey squirrel-fur coat and came out to look. None of the three had folded up her bedding or put on her outer clothes. Skybright, in a little spring-green yuan-silk jacket, red underclothes and red sleeping shoes, with her hair loose, was astride Xiong-nu. Musk wore a red damask chest-wrap with an old garment thrown round her and was digging at Xiong-nu’s ribs. Xiong-nu lay on her back on the kang in a close-fitting flowered jacket, red trousers and green stockings, kicking both legs and laughing till she could scarcely breathe.

‘Two big ones against one little one!’ Bao-yu cried, hurrying over with a laugh. ‘Let me lend a hand.’

He climbed on to the bed and began tickling Skybright. She was so ticklish that she let go of Xiong-nu at once and, laughing helplessly, grappled with Bao-yu. Xiong-nu seized her chance to pin Skybright down and tickle her under the arms.

‘Mind you don’t catch cold!’ Aroma said, laughing. The four of them tangled in a heap were a comical sight.

Just then Li Wan sent Bi-yue over.

‘My mistress left a handkerchief here last night. Has anyone seen it?’

‘Yes, yes, yes,’ said Swallow. ‘I picked it up off the floor. I didn’t know whose it was, so I’ve just washed it and hung it out. It isn’t dry yet.’

Seeing the four of them rolling about, Bi-yue laughed.

‘There’s always some life here. You’re all playing together and laughing your heads off first thing in the morning.’

‘There are plenty of you over there,’ Bao-yu said with a laugh. ‘Why don’t you play?’

‘Our mistress doesn’t play, and she keeps the two young aunts and Miss Qin quiet too. Now Miss Qin has gone to stay with Her Old Ladyship, so it’s lonelier still. Once this year is over, the two young aunts will both be leaving next winter. It will be lonelier than ever then. Just look at Miss Bao’s: only Caltrop has gone, and what a difference it makes! Miss Yun is left all on her own.’

While she was speaking, Kingfisher arrived with a message from Xiang-yun.

‘Master Bao, please come out quickly and see a lovely poem.’

‘Where did this lovely poem come from?’ he asked eagerly.

‘The young ladies are all at Drenched Blossoms Pavilion,’ Kingfisher said, smiling. ‘You’ll find out when you get there.’

Bao-yu hurried to wash and dress. When he came out, there indeed were Dai-yu, Bao-chai, Xiang-yun, Bao-qin and Tan-chun, with a poem in their hands. They greeted him with laughter.

‘Still not up at this hour! Our poetry society has been broken up for a year, and no one has done anything to bring it back. Now it’s early spring and everything is beginning anew. This is just the time to rouse ourselves and start it again.’

‘We first formed it in autumn,’ Xiang-yun said with a laugh. ‘No wonder it couldn’t flourish. Now all things have met their spring, which promises life and growth. Besides, this peach-blossom poem is so good that we ought to change the Begonia Society into the Peach-blossom Society.’

‘Very good,’ Bao-yu said, nodding, and eagerly asked to see the poem.

‘Let’s go and visit the Old Farmer of Sweet-rice Village now,’ the others said. ‘We can settle it all together and get it going.’

They rose and set off for Sweet-rice Village.

As they walked, Bao-yu read the poem on the sheet. It was entitled ‘Song of Peach Blossom’:

Beyond the peach-blossom curtain the east wind is gentle; Within the peach-blossom curtain she is loath to dress for morning.

Beyond the curtain, peach blossom; within the curtain, a girl: The girl and the peach blossom are not far apart.

The east wind would gladly lift the curtain at her window; The flowers would peep at her, but the curtain stays unrolled.

Beyond the curtain the peach flowers bloom as before; Within it, the girl is frailer than peach blossom.

Flowers know pity for her, and flowers grieve; Through the curtain the wind carries news.

Wind through the bamboo curtain, flowers filling the court: The spring colours before her deepen her hurt.

Moss idles in the courtyard, the door closed to no purpose; In slanting sun she leans alone against the rail.

Against the rail she weeps into the east wind; In madder skirts she steals to stand beside the peach blossom.

Peach blossom, peach leaves, all in a flutter; Flowers breaking in fresh red, leaves gathering green.

Mist wraps ten thousand trees, haze seals them in; A blur of red warms the tower and lights the wall.

Heaven’s loom burns through its mandarin-duck brocade; Drunk on spring, near waking, she shifts her coral pillow.

A maid brings water in a golden basin; In fragrant water a reflection dips, its rouge grown cold.

How alike they are, those vivid shades of rouge: The colour of the flowers, the tears upon her face.

Yet set her tears beside the peach blossom: The tears flow on and on, the flowers keep their charm.

Through tearful eyes she sees the flowers; the tears soon dry; Tears dry, spring ends, the flowers grow wan.

Wan flowers veil a wan girl; Flowers fly, she tires, and dusk comes all too soon.

One cuckoo’s cry, and spring is gone entirely; At the lonely curtained window, only an empty trace of moon.

Bao-yu did not praise it when he had finished. Instead, tears rolled down his cheeks. He knew it was Dai-yu’s work, and that was why he wept. Afraid the others would see, he hurriedly wiped his eyes.

‘How did you get hold of it?’ he asked.

‘Guess who wrote it,’ Bao-qin said with a smile.

‘The Naiad, of course,’ he replied, smiling.

‘As it happens, I wrote it.’

‘I don’t believe you,’ Bao-yu said with a laugh. ‘That tone and that voice are quite unlike the Allspice manner. That’s why I don’t believe it.’

‘Which only shows how little you understand,’ Bao-chai said, smiling. ‘Must Du Fu write “Twice the chrysanthemum clumps have bloomed with tears of other days” in every poem? He has lovely lines like “Red blossoms open; rain swells the plums” and “Water-weeds tug at the wind with long green ribbons” too.’

‘True enough,’ Bao-yu said, smiling.

‘But I know you, Cousin Bao-chai, would never let Qin write anything so full of grief. She may have the talent, but she would certainly never choose to write it. Cousin Dai-yu is different. She has known bereavement, and so she writes these sorrowful strains.’

At this, everyone laughed.

They had reached Sweet-rice Village by now. They showed the poem to Li Wan, whose praise and admiration need hardly be described. Then they discussed the society and settled everything. The next day, the second of the third month, they would begin again, changing the Begonia Society’s name to the Peach-blossom Society, with Lin Dai-yu as its president. They would all meet at Naiad’s House after the meal. Next they considered a subject.

‘Let everyone write a peach-blossom poem of a hundred rhymes,’ Dai-yu suggested.

‘That won’t do,’ Bao-chai said. ‘There have always been more poems on peach blossom than one can count. Whatever we wrote would be bound to fall into the usual mould. It couldn’t compare with this poem of yours in the old style. We must think of something else.’

Just then word came:

‘Your aunt has arrived. Will the young ladies come out and pay their respects?’

So they all went to the front of the house to see Wang Zi-teng’s wife and keep her company. After the meal they accompanied her into the garden and showed her round all its different parts. She stayed until after supper, leaving only when the lamps had been lit.

The following day was Tan-chun’s birthday. Yuan-chun had sent two little eunuchs early with several ornamental gifts, and of course everyone in the family had birthday presents for her. After the meal Tan-chun changed into formal dress and went round to pay her respects.

‘I’ve chosen an awkward time to start my society,’ Dai-yu said to the others with a laugh. ‘I quite forgot her birthday fell just now. Even if there’s no feast or play, we’re bound to spend the whole day with her, amusing Grandmother and Aunt Wang. How shall we find a moment to ourselves?’

They therefore put it off until the fifth.

One day, when the girls had finished attending the elders at breakfast, a letter arrived from Jia Zheng. Bao-yu paid his respects, then opened the letter addressed to Grandmother Jia and read it to her. It contained little beyond inquiries after her health and the statement that he expected to reach the capital in the middle of the sixth month. Jia Lian and Lady Wang opened and read the other family letters and business notes. Everyone was overjoyed to hear that he would be back in the sixth or seventh month.

Just at this time Wang Zi-teng’s daughter had been promised to the son of the Marquis of Baoning. The tenth of the fifth month had been chosen for the wedding, and Xi-feng was busy with the preparations, often staying away from home for three or five days at a stretch. That day Wang Zi-teng’s wife came to fetch her again, inviting the nephews and nieces as well to spend a pleasant day. Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang told Bao-yu, Tan-chun, Lin Dai-yu and Bao-chai to go with Xi-feng. None dared object; they went back to their rooms to change. The five took their leave and were away all day, returning when the lamps were lit.

Bao-yu had returned to the House of Green Delights and rested for half a ke when Aroma took advantage of the occasion to urge him to settle down a little and, when he had time, get his books in order against his father’s return.

Bao-yu counted on his fingers.

‘There’s plenty of time yet.’

‘Your books come first,’ Aroma said, ‘and your handwriting comes second. Even if you know your books by then, where are your written exercises?’

‘I’ve done quite a lot from time to time,’ he said with a smile. ‘Haven’t you kept them?’

‘Of course I’ve kept them. Yesterday, while you were out, I got them all together and counted them. There were only fifty or sixty sheets. Can those few sheets really account for three or four years’ work? If you ask me, from tomorrow you ought to put everything else out of your mind and hurry through a few sheets of copying every day to make up for it.

‘Even if you can’t produce something for every day, at least it ought to look reasonably convincing.’

At that, Bao-yu hurried to go through the work himself. There was really no passing it off.

‘From tomorrow I had better write a hundred characters a day,’ he said.

As they spoke, everyone settled down for the night. The next morning, once he had washed and dressed, he ground some ink by the window and began carefully copying a model in regular script. Grandmother Jia missed him and, assuming he was ill, hurriedly sent someone to inquire. Only then did Bao-yu go to pay his respects. He explained that he had been practising his handwriting. He wanted to use the early morning for that before doing anything else, which was why he had come out late.

Grandmother Jia was delighted.

‘From now on, keep to your writing and reading,’ she told him. ‘You needn’t come out at all. Go and let your mother know.’

Bao-yu went to Lady Wang’s rooms and explained.

‘Sharpening your spear on the eve of battle is no use,’ she said. ‘If you’d put all this anxious energy into a little writing and reading every day, what couldn’t you have finished? Now you’ll work yourself into an illness in your hurry.’

Bao-yu assured her that it would do him no harm.

Grandmother Jia, meanwhile, was also saying that she feared all this haste would make him ill.

‘There’s no need to worry, Grandmother,’ Tan-chun, Bao-chai and the others said with a smile. ‘We can’t learn his books for him, but we can do his writing. We’ll each copy a sheet for him every day and get him past this hurdle. Then his father won’t be angry when he comes home, and he won’t make himself ill with worry.’

Grandmother Jia was overjoyed.

Dai-yu, for her part, had realised that on his return Jia Zheng was sure to question Bao-yu about his studies. Bao-yu was so easily distracted that she feared he would suffer for it when the time came. She therefore merely pretended she could not be bothered with the poetry society, left it in abeyance and made no attempt to tempt him away with other interests.

Tan-chun and Bao-chai each copied a sheet of regular script for him every day. Bao-yu himself worked harder too, writing two hundred characters or three hundred as it happened. By the latter part of the third month he had gathered quite a store of written work. One day he was reckoning that another fifty sheets would be enough to get him through, when Nightingale came with a roll for him. He opened it and found sheet after sheet of aged youzhu paper covered in tiny regular script copied from Zhong and Wang1. The handwriting was very like his own.

Bao-yu was so delighted that he bowed to Nightingale with his hands clasped, then went in person to thank Dai-yu. Xiang-yun and Bao-qin each sent him a few sheets they had copied as well. The pile was still short of the work he ought to have done, but it would be enough to pass muster. With his mind at ease, Bao-yu went over the books he should have been studying several times.

He was hard at work every day when, as chance would have it, the sea surged over a stretch of the coast, devastating the people in several places. The local officials submitted a memorial, and an imperial order directed Jia Zheng to inspect the affected places and provide relief on his way home. By this reckoning he would not be back until the end of winter. On hearing that, Bao-yu put his books and writing aside once more and went back to his old idle wanderings.

It was now late spring. Xiang-yun had nothing to do and, seeing willow catkins dancing through the air, happened to compose a short lyric to the tune Ru meng ling, ‘Like a Dream’:

Can these be scraps of floss shed from embroidery? A half-raised curtain, a swirl of fragrant mist; A slender hand picks them out, Only to rouse the cuckoo’s cry, the swallow’s envy. Stay a while, Stay a while! Do not let spring’s loveliness depart.

Pleased with her composition, she wrote it neatly on a strip of paper and showed it to Bao-chai, then went to find Dai-yu. Dai-yu read it and smiled.

‘Good! It’s fresh and charming too. I couldn’t do it.’

‘In all our poetry meetings we’ve never written lyrics,’ Xiang-yun said with a laugh. ‘Why not call a meeting tomorrow and write some? A change of form would make it feel new, wouldn’t it?’

Dai-yu suddenly felt her interest stir.

‘That’s an excellent idea. I’ll send and ask them now.’

She ordered several kinds of fruit and sweetmeats to be made ready and sent people off in different directions to invite the others. She and Xiang-yun chose willow catkins as the subject and specified several tunes. They wrote them out and hung the sheet on the wall.

When the others arrived, they saw that the subject was willow catkins, to be treated in a choice of short lyric forms. They all read Xiang-yun’s poem too and spent some time praising it.

‘We’re not much good at lyrics,’ Bao-yu said with a laugh, ‘but we’ll have to cobble something together.’

They drew lots.2 Bao-chai drew Lin jiang xian, ‘Immortal by the River’; Bao-qin drew Xi jiang yue, ‘Moon over the West River’; Tan-chun drew Nan ke zi, ‘The Southern Bough’; Dai-yu drew Tang duo ling; and Bao-yu drew Die lian hua, ‘Butterfly Loves Flowers’. Nightingale lit a stick of Sweet-dream incense, and they all began to think.

Before long Dai-yu had her poem and wrote it out. Bao-qin and Bao-chai soon had theirs too. When the three had finished and were looking at one another’s work, Bao-chai said with a smile:

‘Let me finish reading yours first. Then you can see mine.’

‘Oh dear!’ Tan-chun cried, laughing. ‘Why is the incense burning so fast today? There are only three divisions left, and I’ve only got half a poem.’

She asked whether Bao-yu had finished. He had composed something, but disliked it so much that he had crossed it all out and meant to start afresh. When he looked back at the incense, it was nearly burnt away.

‘Then you’ve lost,’ Li Wan said, laughing. ‘As for our Plantain girl, write down your half and let us see it.’

Tan-chun hurriedly did so. When they looked, it was only half a lyric to Nan ke zi:

In vain the slender filaments hang, In vain the interlacing threads droop down; They are hard to bind, and hard to hold, Free to part for east and west and south and north.

‘That ought to be easy enough to go on with,’ Li Wan said with a smile. ‘Why not finish it?’

Bao-yu saw that the incense was finished. Willing to admit defeat rather than force out something merely to fill the paper, he laid down his brush and came to look at Tan-chun’s half. Its very incompleteness stirred his interest and set his thoughts moving. Taking up the brush, he continued:

Do not grieve when they fall away; When they come flying, I shall know. Orioles grieve, butterflies tire, in the late flowering time; Even to meet next spring means waiting out a year!

‘You couldn’t do your own proper share,’ the others said, laughing, ‘yet here you are with this! However good it is, it doesn’t count.’

Then they read Dai-yu’s Tang duo ling:

Powder falls on the Isle of a Hundred Flowers; Fragrance fades in Swallow Tower. Clump on clump, they chase in pairs and gather into balls. Adrift, as ill-starred lives drift too: Vainly entwined, And talking of romance. Even plants and trees know sorrow; In the bloom of youth, already white-haired! Ah, in this life, who casts away, who gathers in? Wed to the east wind, unheeded by spring: Go where you will; How bear to linger?

They all nodded and sighed over it.

‘It makes too much of sorrow. Good it certainly is.’

Next they read Bao-qin’s, to Xi jiang yue:

In Han gardens, only a scattering; On Sui embankments, adornments without end. Three months of spring’s endeavour given to the east wind: Bright moon and plum blossom, all a dream. How many courtyards hold fallen red petals? Whose curtained windows hold this fragrant snow? South of the river, north of the river, all alike; Only the parted bear a heavier grief!

‘There’s that grand sweep to her voice, after all,’ they said with a smile. ‘The lines beginning “How many” and “Whose” are the best of all.’

‘Still, it is rather too desolate in the end,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘Willow catkins are frivolous, weightless things, with neither roots nor ties. But my idea is to insist on speaking well of them. That’s the way to avoid the usual treatment. So I’ve put together a poem, though it may not be to your taste.’

‘Don’t be so modest,’ the others said, laughing. ‘Let us appreciate it for ourselves. It’s bound to be good.’

They began to read her Lin jiang xian:

Before white-jade halls I know the dance of spring; The east wind turns me with an even sweep.

Xiang-yun was the first to laugh in delight.

‘How good that is: “The east wind turns me with an even sweep.” That one line puts it above everyone else’s.’

They read on:

Swarms of bees and ranks of butterflies all tumble in confusion. When have I followed the waters away? Why must I fall to the fragrant dust? Ten thousand strands, a thousand threads, unchanged to the end, Let them gather, let them part, as they will. Fair spring, do not mock me for having no roots: Good wind, lend me your strength again and again, And bear me to the blue clouds!

They struck the table and cried out in admiration.

‘What strength there is in that reversal! This must certainly take first place. For lingering grief, we yield to the Naiad; for charm and tender feeling, to the Old Friend of Rosy Clouds. Little Xue and the Guest beneath the Plantain have failed today’s examination. They must pay a forfeit.’

‘We’ll pay ours, of course,’ Bao-qin said, laughing. ‘But what forfeit is there for someone who hands in a blank paper?’

‘Don’t be in such a hurry,’ said Li Wan. ‘We shall certainly make him pay dearly. It will be a warning for next time.’

She had not finished speaking when something struck the bamboo outside the window with a crash like a window-frame falling over. Everyone jumped. The maids went out to look, and one called from beyond the curtain:

‘A great butterfly kite has caught on the bamboo tops!’

‘What a fine kite!’ the maids cried, laughing. ‘Someone’s line must have broken. Get it down.’

Bao-yu and the others came out to see.

‘I know that kite,’ he said with a smile. ‘Jiao-hong from the Elder Master’s courtyard was flying it. Get it down and send it back to her.’

‘Is there only one kite like it in the whole world?’ Nightingale asked, laughing. ‘Must it be hers? I don’t care. I’m keeping it for now.’

‘So Nightingale has learnt to be mean too,’ said Tan-chun. ‘You have kites of your own. Aren’t you afraid of bad luck, picking up one someone else has let go?’

‘Exactly,’ Dai-yu said with a laugh. ‘Who knows whose ill luck it was carrying away? Throw it out quickly.

‘Bring ours out. We’ll send our ill luck away too.’

Nightingale at once told the little maids to take the kite to the old woman on duty at the garden gate. If anyone came looking for it, she could give it back.

At the mention of kite-flying, the little maids could hardly contain themselves. They hurried about, all hands at once, and brought out a kite in the shape of a beautiful woman. Some carried a high stool, some tied the forked line, others worked the reel. Bao-chai and the rest stood at the courtyard gate and told the maids to fly it from the open ground outside.

‘This one of yours isn’t very pretty,’ Bao-qin said with a laugh. ‘It’s not as good as Third Sister’s great soft-winged phoenix.’

‘Quite true,’ said Bao-chai, smiling. She turned to Ebony.

‘Fetch yours too, and let’s fly it.’

Ebony went off with a broad grin to get it.

Bao-yu’s enthusiasm flared up again. He too sent a little maid back to his rooms.

‘Fetch that big fish Mrs Lai gave me yesterday.’

The girl was gone a long time and came back empty-handed, smiling.

‘Miss Skybright let it go yesterday.’

‘But I haven’t flown it even once!’ Bao-yu protested.

‘She’s sent your ill luck away for you, anyhow,’ Tan-chun said, laughing.

‘Oh, very well. Fetch the big crab instead.’

Off went the maid, returning with several others carrying a beauty kite and its reel.

‘Miss Aroma says she gave the crab to Master Huan yesterday. This one has just come from Mamma Lin. Fly this instead.’

Bao-yu examined it carefully. The beautiful woman was made with great delicacy. Delighted, he told them to put her up. Tan-chun’s kite had arrived too, and Ebony and several little maids had already launched it from the hillside over there. Bao-qin sent for her own great red bat as well.

Bao-chai caught the enthusiasm and fetched one of her own, a string of seven wild geese. Up they all went. Only Bao-yu’s beauty would not rise. He blamed the maids for not knowing how to fly her and tried for a long time himself, but she would climb only as high as the roof before falling again. He grew so vexed that sweat stood on his forehead, while everyone laughed. In disgust he flung the kite on the ground and pointed at it.

‘If you weren’t a beauty, I’d stamp you to bits!’

‘The bridle isn’t right,’ Dai-yu said with a laugh. ‘Send it out and have someone put on another. Then it will fly.’

Bao-yu sent it off for a new bridle and fetched another kite to fly in the meantime. They all looked up. The kites were high in the sky now. Presently the maids brought out a great many line-riding kite messengers of every shape and kind, and they played with these for a while.

‘It’s pulling hard now, miss,’ Nightingale said with a laugh. ‘Come and fly it yourself.’

Dai-yu padded her hand with a handkerchief and gave the line a tug. The wind was indeed strong, and so was the pull. She took the reel and let it run with the kite. With a great whirring rush, all the line spun out at once. She offered it to the others to let go.

‘We each have our own,’ they said, laughing. ‘You go first.’

‘Letting it go is amusing,’ Dai-yu said with a smile, ‘but I haven’t the heart.’

‘That’s the whole pleasure of flying kites,’ Li Wan told her. ‘That’s why they call it sending ill luck away. You ought to let more go than anyone. Wouldn’t it be good if they carried off the very root of your illness?’

‘Our miss is getting meaner and meaner,’ Nightingale said with a laugh.

‘When has a year gone by without our letting several go? And now all of a sudden she begrudges it. If you won’t let it go, miss, I will.’

She took a little pair of Western silver scissors from Snowgoose and, leaving not a thread at the base of the reel, cut the line with a snip.

‘There!’ she said with a laugh. ‘This time it will take the whole root of your illness away.’

The kite swayed and floated steadily farther off. Soon it was no bigger than an egg; a moment later it was a black speck, and another moment it was gone. They all craned their necks and peered after it.

‘Lovely! Lovely!’

‘What a pity we don’t know where it will land,’ Bao-yu said. ‘If it comes down somewhere people live and a child finds it, that’s all right. But if it lands in the wilds with no one about, I shall feel its loneliness for it.

‘Come to think of it, I’ll let mine go so that the two can keep each other company.’

He too cut the line with the scissors and let his kite follow the first. Tan-chun was just about to cut her phoenix loose when she saw another phoenix in the sky.

‘I wonder whose that is,’ she said.

‘Don’t cut yours yet,’ the others said, laughing. ‘Look, it seems to be coming to tangle with it.’

As they spoke, the other phoenix drew nearer and nearer, until the two were tangled together. They were just going to reel theirs down when the people at the other end began reeling in too. Neither could get free. Then an openwork kite as big as a door, shaped like the character for joy, came towards them, its sounding attachment ringing high in the air like a bell.

‘Here’s another coming to tangle!’ they cried, laughing. ‘Don’t reel them in yet. Let the three get tangled together. That will be fun.’

Sure enough, the joy-character kite became entangled with both phoenixes.

All three sides reeled in and tugged at once. Suddenly the lines snapped, and all three kites went floating and swaying away. Everyone clapped and burst out laughing.

‘That was fun! But whose was that joy-character kite? What a mischievous thing!’

‘Mine’s gone, and I’m tired,’ Dai-yu said. ‘I’m going to have a rest.’

‘Wait until we’ve let ours go,’ Bao-chai said. ‘Then we can all leave together.’

They waited until the other girls had released theirs, and only then dispersed.

Dai-yu went back to her room and lay down to rest. To learn what followed, you must read the next chapter.
