Chapter 5 A visit to the Land of Illusion offers guidance through the Twelve Beauties; Over immortal wine, songs unfold the Dream of the Red Chamber

Chapter end

We have briefly related how the Xue family, mother and children, came to stay at the Rong mansion, and must leave them for the present. Let us turn instead to Lin Dai-yu.

Ever since her arrival at the Rong mansion, Grandmother Jia had lavished affection on her. In everything, from meals and sleeping arrangements to the ordering of her day, she was treated just like Bao-yu; the old lady’s own three granddaughters, Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun, had to take second place. The closeness and affection between Bao-yu and Dai-yu were quite unlike their relations with anyone else. By day they went about together and sat together; at night they rested in the same quarters. In word and thought they were in perfect accord, with scarcely a moment’s estrangement. But now Xue Bao-chai had suddenly appeared. Although only a little older, she had a composed, upright manner and a full, lovely face, and many thought Dai-yu no match for her. Bao-chai was easygoing, accommodating herself to people and circumstances, unlike Dai-yu, who held herself proudly apart and disdained anything she thought beneath her. Consequently she was far more popular with the servants. Even the little maids generally preferred to go and play with Bao-chai. This left Dai-yu feeling somewhat depressed and resentful, though Bao-chai was entirely unaware of it. Bao-yu was still a child. Moreover, with the simple-minded, contrary nature he had been born with, he felt the same towards all his brothers, sisters and cousins, making no distinction between close relations and distant ones.

Because he and Dai-yu shared Grandmother Jia’s quarters, he was a little more familiar with her than with the other girls. Familiarity brought greater intimacy; intimacy, in turn, inevitably brought the occasional reproach for falling short of perfection, the unexpected breach. On this particular day, for some reason or other, the two had disagreed. Dai-yu was once more crying alone in her room, while Bao-yu,

regretting his own rash words, went to make peace with her. Only then did she gradually relent. The plum blossom was now at its height in the garden of the Ning mansion to the east, and Jia Zhen’s wife, You-shi, arranged a party for Grandmother Jia, Lady Xing, Lady Wang and the others to enjoy it. That day she brought Jia Rong’s wife with her to deliver the invitation in person. Grandmother Jia and her party went over after breakfast and amused themselves in the Garden of Gathered Fragrance, taking tea first and wine afterwards. It was merely a small family gathering of the women from the Ning and Rong households; there were no other novelties or amusing incidents to record. Presently Bao-yu grew tired and wanted his midday nap. Grandmother Jia told his attendants to coax him gently to sleep and bring him back when he had rested. Jia Rong’s wife, Qin-shi, promptly answered with a smile, ‘We have a room all ready for Uncle Bao. Don’t worry, Great-grandmother. Just leave him to me.’ Then she turned to his nurse and maids. ‘Mamma, sisters, please bring Uncle Bao this way with me.’ Grandmother Jia had always known Qin-shi to be thoroughly capable. Delicate and graceful in appearance, gentle and even-tempered in her dealings, she was the old lady’s favourite among her great-grandsons’ wives. With Qin-shi seeing to Bao-yu, she naturally felt quite at ease.

Qin-shi led the whole party to an inner room in the main apartments. Bao-yu looked up and saw a picture mounted on the wall. The figures were beautifully painted, but its subject was The Burning Goosefoot Staff, and without even looking to see who the painter was, he began to feel displeased. Beside it hung a pair of scrolls: ‘A thorough knowledge of worldly affairs is learning; / A practised understanding of human dealings is literature.’ Once he had read those two lines, he would not stay, however exquisite the room and sumptuous its furnishings. ‘Let’s go out! Quickly!’ he cried. ‘If this won’t do, where can we go?’ Qin-shi said, laughing. ‘Unless you come to my room.’ Bao-yu nodded and smiled. ‘Who ever heard of an uncle sleeping in his nephew’s room?’ one of the old women objected. ‘Oh, dear me!’ Qin-shi laughed. ‘I hope he won’t be offended, but how old is he, that we should already worry about such things? Didn’t you see my brother when he came last month? He’s the same age as Uncle Bao, but put them side by side, and I rather think my brother would be the taller.’ ‘How is it I’ve never seen him?’ Bao-yu asked.

‘Bring him here for me to see.’ Everyone laughed. ‘With twenty or thirty li between us? How are we to fetch him here? You’ll have plenty of chances to meet.’ As they spoke, they reached Qin-shi’s room. At the doorway a faint, sweet fragrance came wafting towards them. Bao-yu’s eyes grew heavy, his limbs relaxed, and he kept saying, ‘What a lovely scent!’

Inside, he looked at the wall and saw Tang Bo-hu’s painting Spring Sleep among the Crab-apple Blossoms. On either side hung a couplet written by the Song scholar Qin Tai-xu:

A gentle chill confines the dream: spring is still cold;

Fragrance enfolds the sleeper: wine perfumes the air.

On the table stood the precious mirror that had once furnished Wu Ze-tian’s mirror chamber. To one side was the golden platter on which Flying Swallow had danced; in it lay the quince An Lu-shan had thrown, bruising Tai-zhen’s breast. Further in stood the couch on which Princess Shou-chang had slept below the Hanzhang Hall, hung with the strung-pearl curtains Princess Tong-chang had had made. ‘This is lovely!’ Bao-yu said again and again, smiling. ‘I dare say even an immortal could live in this room of mine,’ Qin-shi said with a laugh. With her own hands she spread a gauze coverlet washed by Xi Shi and moved into place a lovers’ pillow once held by Hong-niang. The nurses settled Bao-yu in bed and gradually withdrew, leaving only the four maids Aroma, Winsome, Skybright and Musk to keep him company. Qin-shi told the little maids to keep a good watch under the eaves in case the cats and dogs started fighting.

Bao-yu had scarcely closed his eyes before he drifted off to sleep. It still seemed that Qin-shi was before him, and he floated along behind her until they reached a place of red balustrades, white stone, green trees and clear streams, where human footsteps seldom came and no dust could reach. Delighted in his dream, he thought, ‘What an interesting place! I’d willingly spend my whole life here, even if it meant losing my home. It would be better than being beaten by my parents and teachers every day.’ While these idle thoughts were passing through his head, he suddenly heard someone singing beyond the hill:

Spring dreams disperse with the clouds;

Fallen flowers follow the flowing stream.

A word to all you girls and boys:

Why go in search of needless sorrow?

Bao-yu could tell that it was a woman’s voice. Before the song had ended, a figure emerged, moving with a light, slender grace quite unlike that of ordinary mortals. A descriptive verse bears witness:

She leaves the willow hollow, steps from her flowery bower. Where she walks, birds start in the courtyard trees; as she approaches, her shadow crosses the winding gallery. Her immortal sleeves stir: musk and orchid breathe around her; her lotus robe begins to move: her girdle pendants chime. Her dimples smile like peach blossom in spring beneath cloud-dark coils of hair; her lips part, red as cherries, her pomegranate-seed teeth hold fragrance. Her slender waist turns like snow dancing in a circling wind; pearls and kingfisher jewels gleam above a brow adorned with yellow. Among the flowers, now hidden, now seen, she is lovely in anger or joy; beside the pool she lingers, seeming to rise and fly. Her moth-fine brows smile and frown; she is about to speak, but has not spoken. Her lotus steps begin: she would pause, yet means to go. Admire the beauty of her being: clear as ice, lustrous as jade; admire her splendid robes, their patterns flashing bright. Love her face, as though nurtured on fragrance and carved of jade; praise her bearing, a phoenix soaring, a dragon in flight. Her unadorned fairness? Spring plum blossom opening in snow. Her purity? An autumn chrysanthemum mantled in frost. Her stillness? A pine growing in an empty valley. Her splendour? Rosy clouds reflected in a clear pool. Her elegance? A dragon moving through a winding lake. Her spirit? Moonlight striking a cold river. Xi Shi must feel ashamed beside her, Wang Qiang abashed. How wondrous! Where was she born, from what country does she come? Indeed, the Jasper Pool has no second such beauty, the Purple Palace no equal. Who can she be, so lovely as this?

Seeing that she was an immortal, Bao-yu hurried forward in delight and bowed with clasped hands. ‘Immortal sister, where have you come from, and where are you going? I don’t even know what this place is. Please, would you take me with you?’

The immortal smiled. ‘I dwell above the Heaven of Parting Sorrow, amid the Sea of Grief. I am Fairy Disenchantment, of the Land of Illusion, in the Cave of Dispersed Fragrance on Mount of Released Spring. I oversee the debts of love and desire among mortals, and govern women’s resentments and men’s infatuations in the dusty world. Lately certain tangled debts of passion have become bound up with this place, so I have come to examine the opportunities and spread the longing of lovers. Our meeting today is no accident either. My domain is not far away. I have little to offer: just a cup of immortal tea I picked myself, a jar of fine wine of my own brewing, a few singing girls practised in enchanted dances, and twelve newly written immortal songs called Dream of the Red Chamber. Will you come and visit it with me?’ Hearing this, Bao-yu forgot all about where Qin-shi might be and followed the fairy. They reached a stone archway bearing the great inscription ‘LAND OF ILLUSION’. On either side was a couplet:

When the false is taken as true, the true is also false;

Where nothing passes for something, something returns to nothing.

Beyond the archway stood a palace gate, bearing the inscription ‘SEA OF KARMIC DEBT, HEAVEN OF PASSION’.

Another couplet was written there in large characters:

Deep earth, high heaven: alas, love never ends, in any age;

Besotted men, resentful women: how hard to pay the debts of love!

‘So that’s how it is,’ Bao-yu thought after reading it. ‘But what is this love through all the ages? What are these debts of love? From now on I must find out about them.’ He merely thought it to himself, little knowing that he had already invited evil influences into the deepest recesses of his being. Following the fairy through a second gateway, he came to the side halls. All had plaques and couplets, too many to read at once. He noticed only a few: ‘Office of Infatuation’, ‘Office of Accumulated Resentment’, ‘Office of Morning Tears’, ‘Office of Night-time Grievances’, ‘Office of Spring Longings’ and ‘Office of Autumn Sorrow’. Having read them, he asked the fairy, ‘Could I trouble you to show me round these different offices? Would that be allowed?’ ‘These offices hold the records of the past and future of every woman under heaven,’ she said. ‘With your mortal eyes and earthly body, you cannot be permitted to know them beforehand.’ Bao-yu would not take no for an answer, and begged her again and again.

At last the fairy had to yield. ‘Very well. You may pay a brief visit to this one office.’ Overjoyed, Bao-yu looked up at its plaque: ‘OFFICE OF THE ILL-FATED’. The couplet on either side read:

Spring’s regrets and autumn’s sorrows are all of your own making;

For whom do flowerlike faces, moonlike beauties bloom?

Reading this, Bao-yu felt moved to sigh. Inside he saw a dozen or more large cupboards, all sealed with strips of paper bearing the names of the various provinces. He was so intent on finding the label for his own native place that he paid no attention to the others.

On a cupboard over to one side he saw a seal marked in large characters ‘MAIN REGISTER OF THE TWELVE BEAUTIES OF JINLING’. ‘What does “Main Register of the Twelve Beauties of Jinling” mean?’ he asked. ‘It records the twelve foremost women of your province,’ Disenchantment replied. ‘That is why it is called the Main Register.’ ‘People always say Jinling is enormous,’ said Bao-yu. ‘How can it have only twelve women? In our household alone, counting high and low, there are several hundred girls.’ Disenchantment gave a cold laugh. ‘There are indeed many women in your province. We have selected only those of consequence to enter here. Those in the two cupboards below rank next. As for the ordinary run of women, there are no registers for them.’ Looking at the two lower cupboards, Bao-yu saw that one was indeed labelled ‘SUPPLEMENTARY REGISTER OF THE TWELVE BEAUTIES OF JINLING’, and the other ‘SECOND SUPPLEMENTARY REGISTER OF THE TWELVE BEAUTIES OF JINLING’.

He first reached out to open the cupboard of the Second Supplementary Register. He took out a volume and opened it. On the first page was a picture, neither of people nor of a landscape: nothing but black clouds and murky mist washed across the paper in watery ink. A few lines were written there:

A moon after rain is seldom seen; bright clouds soon scatter.

Her heart stands higher than the sky; her station is lowly.

Her charm and cleverness provoke resentment.

Slander, more than anything, cuts short her years;

The loving young master grieves for her in vain.

Turning the page, Bao-yu saw a cluster of fresh flowers and a torn sleeping mat. Here too were a few lines:

Gentleness and obedience, all in vain;

In vain they call her cassia-sweet and orchid-fair.

Alas, an actor is the fortunate one;

Who knew the young master’s bond with her would fail?

Bao-yu could make nothing of it. Tossing the volume aside, he opened the cupboard of the Supplementary Register and took out another. In it he saw a flowering cassia tree, with a pool below: its water gone, its mud dried out, the lotus withered and its roots decayed. Behind the picture was written:

Her root beside the lotus, a single fragrant stem;

The fortunes of her life are sad indeed.

Once a lone tree springs from two patches of earth,

Her fragrant soul is sent back to its native home.

Bao-yu still did not understand. He tossed this volume aside too, and took out the Main Register. The first page showed two dead trees with a jade belt hanging from their branches, and a heap of snow with a golden hairpin beneath it. Four lines accompanied the picture:

Alas for the virtue that stopped the loom;

Alas for the wit that sang of willow fluff!

A jade belt hangs among the trees;

A golden hairpin lies buried in snow.

Again Bao-yu could make nothing of it. He wanted to ask, but knew she would never reveal the secret; he wanted to put the book down, but could not bear to. Turning on, he saw a bow with a citron hanging from it. A verse was written there:

For twenty years she has discerned the right from wrong;

Where pomegranate flowers open, light fills the palace chambers.

How can the three springs rival the first spring’s splendour?

Tiger and wild buffalo meet; she returns from the great dream.

The next picture showed two people flying kites, a great expanse of sea and a large ship. In the ship was a woman covering her face and weeping. There were four lines:

Her mind is keen, her aspirations high;

Born in a failing age, her fortune wanes.

At Clear Brightness, tears of parting, eyes turned towards the river;

A thousand li of east wind: even a dream is far away.

Next came a few wisps of flying cloud and a stretch of flowing water. The words ran:

What use are rank and wealth? In swaddling clothes she loses both her parents.

In a blink she mourns the setting sun: the Xiang’s waters pass, the clouds of Chu fly on.

The next picture showed a beautiful piece of jade fallen in the dirt. Its verdict read:

She longs to be pure, but when was she pure? She speaks of emptiness, yet may not be empty.

Alas for a nature of gold and jade, sunk at last in mire!

Next, suddenly, came a vicious wolf pursuing and leaping at a beautiful woman, as if to devour her. The writing read:

Child joined to thread: a wolf of Zhongshan;

Once you get your way, you run wild.

A flower-and-willow beauty from a noble chamber,

Within a year she enters the millet dream.

Next was an ancient temple, with a beautiful woman sitting alone inside, reading a sutra. The verdict ran:

Seeing through the three springs’ brief splendour,

She suddenly exchanges her old finery for a dark religious robe.

Alas, a marquis’s daughter from embroidered chambers,

Now sleeps alone beside a dim lamp and an ancient Buddha.

Next came an iceberg with a female phoenix on it. Its verdict read:

An ordinary bird comes, of all times, in the last days;

All know and admire her gifts.

First obedience, then command, then person and tree;

Weeping towards Jinling, her story grows more grievous still.

Next was a deserted village and a lonely wayside inn, with a beautiful woman spinning there. The verdict read:

When power fails, speak no more of rank;

When the family falls, count no more on kin.

Because she once chanced to help a woman named Liu,

By a lucky chance she meets a benefactor.

The next picture showed a pot of flourishing orchids. Beside it stood a beautiful woman wearing a phoenix coronet and a ceremonial cape. It too had a verdict:

Peach and plum in spring winds bear their finished fruit;

In the end, who can match that pot of orchids?

Goodness like ice and water is envied to no purpose;

In vain, it gives the others something to laugh about.

Next was a lofty mansion, with a beautiful woman hanging from a beam. Its verdict read:

Passion’s heaven and sea conjure a body of passion; when passion meets its counterpart, lust must prevail.

Do not say all the unworthy come from Rong: the first provocation to disaster lies in Ning.

Bao-yu wanted to read on, but the fairy knew how bright his natural gifts were, how quick his understanding, and feared that heaven’s secrets might be disclosed. She closed the volume and said with a smile, ‘Come and see the marvels with me. Why stay here puzzling over these riddles?’ In a haze, Bao-yu let the register go without quite knowing it and followed Disenchantment to the rear. There were pearl curtains, embroidered hangings, painted beams and carved eaves: words could not exhaust the light that played on crimson doors and floors of gold, or the snowy brilliance of jasper windows and palaces of jade. Immortal flowers and rare grasses filled the air with fragrance. It was a wonderful place indeed. Disenchantment called out, laughing, ‘Come quickly and welcome our honoured guest!’ Before she had finished, several immortals came out of the rooms, their lotus sleeves fluttering, their feather robes swaying, lovely as spring flowers and alluring as the autumn moon. The moment they saw Bao-yu, they reproached Disenchantment. ‘We wondered what “honoured guest” this could be, and hurried out to meet him! You said that at this very hour today our little sister Crimson Pearl’s living soul would come to visit, and we have been waiting for so long.

‘Why have you brought this filthy creature instead, to pollute our pure realm of girls?’ Hearing them, Bao-yu was so frightened that he wanted to retreat but could not, and truly felt himself unutterably unclean. Disenchantment quickly took his hand and said to her sisters, ‘You do not know the circumstances. Today I meant to go to the Rong mansion to fetch Crimson Pearl, but as I passed the Ning mansion I happened to meet the spirits of the Dukes of Ning and Rong. They charged me with this request: “Since our dynasty was established, our family has enjoyed distinction in generation after generation, passing wealth and honour down for a hundred years. But now our allotted fortune is spent, and nothing can turn it back. Though we have left many descendants, not one can carry on our work. Only Bao-yu, a grandson in the legitimate line, offers some hope. His disposition is perverse, his nature strange and unpredictable, but he is intelligent and quick-witted, and something might be made of him. Unfortunately our family’s term of fortune is due to end, and we fear there will be no one to guide him to the proper path. Since you have chanced to come, fairy, we beg you first to warn his stubborn, infatuated nature through matters of passion and desire, music and beauty. Perhaps he may break out of their beguiling circle and then enter the right way. That would be a blessing to us brothers.” Because they asked this of me, I took pity and brought him here.

‘First I let him study the lifelong records of the women of his household in all three ranks, upper, middle and lower, but still he did not awaken. So I have brought him here to experience further illusions of feasting, music and beauty. Who knows? Perhaps he will one day understand.’ With that, she led Bao-yu indoors. A thread of delicate scent reached him, but he had no idea what was burning, and could not help asking. Disenchantment gave a cold laugh. ‘How could you know it, when no such incense exists in the mortal world? It is made of the essence of rare flowers at their first budding in famous mountains and places of beauty, blended with oils from precious forests and jewelled trees. Its name is Marrow of All Flowers.’

Bao-yu could only admire it. They all sat down, and little maids brought tea. He found its fresh fragrance and unfamiliar flavour extraordinarily pure and delicious, and asked its name as well. ‘This tea comes from the Cave of Dispersed Fragrance on Mount of Released Spring,’ said Disenchantment. ‘It is brewed with dew that has lain overnight on immortal flowers and numinous leaves. Its name is A Thousand Reds in One Cavern.’ Bao-yu nodded appreciatively. Looking round the room, he saw that nothing was lacking: a precious qin, fine incense tripods, old paintings and new poems. He was still more pleased to find bits of embroidery floss spat out beneath the window and occasional powder stains among the dressing boxes. A couplet hung on the wall:1

A place of subtle mystery and spiritual grace;

A heaven where nothing can be done.

Bao-yu admired everything he read. He then asked the names of the immortals. One was the Fairy of Infatuated Dreams, another the Great Being of Devoted Love, another the Golden Maiden Who Draws Sorrow, and another the Bodhi of Passage beyond Grief. Each had a different religious name. Presently little maids came to arrange tables and chairs and lay out food and wine. Truly, precious nectar brimmed in glass goblets; rich jade liquor filled the amber cups.

There is no need to dwell on the abundance of the dishes. Finding the wine unusually fragrant, sweet and clear, Bao-yu could not resist asking about it too. ‘This wine is brewed from the stamens of a hundred flowers and the sap of ten thousand trees,’ Disenchantment told him, ‘with a ferment of qilin marrow and a starter of phoenix milk. That is why it is called Ten Thousand Beauties in One Cup.’ Bao-yu could not praise it enough.2

As they drank, twelve dancing girls came forward and asked which songs they should perform. ‘Give us the twelve newly composed songs of Dream of the Red Chamber,’ said Disenchantment. The girls assented, tapped their sandalwood clappers softly and gently played their silver-strung zithers.

They began to sing, ‘When primal chaos first broke open…’ They had sung only that one phrase when Disenchantment said, ‘These songs are not like the dramatic songs written in the mortal world, which must observe the conventions of male lead, female lead, painted face and supporting male roles, and the restrictions of the nine musical modes of north and south. Here one song may lament a person, another reflect on an event. A song comes to us, and we can set it straight to pipes and strings. Unless you have experienced these things yourself,

‘you cannot know their beauty. I doubt that you will fully understand these songs either. Unless you read the text before listening to the singing, it will be like chewing wax.’ She turned and told a little maid to fetch the original manuscript of Dream of the Red Chamber and hand it to Bao-yu. He took it, reading the words as he listened to the song:

[Dream of the Red Chamber: Prelude]

Since primal chaos first broke open, who was born a creature of love? It all springs from love’s passion for the moon and wind. In this helpless heaven, on this sorrowing day, in this lonely hour, let me try to give voice to my foolish heart. And so we perform this Dream of the Red Chamber, cherishing gold, mourning jade.

[A Life Mismatched]

They all proclaim the happy match of gold and jade; I think only of the former pledge of wood and stone. In vain I face the mountain recluse’s glittering snow; I cannot forget the lonely forest of that fairy beyond the world. Alas, only now do I believe that human happiness is never whole. Even were the tray raised level with the brows, still my heart would find no peace.

[Brows Knit in Vain]

One is a flower of the immortal garden, one a flawless piece of precious jade. If no strange destiny unites them, why have they met in this life? If a strange destiny unites them, why must their heart’s desire dissolve at last? One sighs to no purpose; one wastes all care in vain. One is the moon in water, one a flower in a mirror. How many tears can there be in those eyes, to endure the flow from autumn to winter’s end, from spring into summer?

Bao-yu found the song rambling and groundless, and could see no merit in it. Yet its plaintive melody could quite dissolve his soul and intoxicate his spirit. So he neither examined its meaning nor asked where it came from, but simply let it ease his boredom for the moment. Then he read on:

[Resentment at Impermanence]

Joy in splendour just at its height; resentment that impermanence has come again. With open eyes, all things must be abandoned; drifting, drifting, the fragrant soul is spent. I look towards home: the road is long, the mountains high. So I seek my parents in their dreams to tell them: ‘Your child’s life has entered the Yellow Springs. My dear ones, step back and get free while there is time!’

[Kin Torn Apart] A sail in wind and rain, a journey of three thousand li; home and kin, all left behind. Fearing tears will harm their declining years, I tell my parents, ‘Do not worry about your child. Hardship and advancement have always had their appointed course; can meeting and parting be without their destined ties? From now on we shall live in separate places; let each keep safe and well.

‘I am going. Do not let me hold you bound.’

[Sorrow within Joy]

In swaddling clothes, alas, both parents lost. Even dwelling amid fine silks, who knows the care that cherishes a child? Fortunately, she was born open-hearted, bold and generous; never did the private feelings of lovers entangle her heart. She was like a moon after rain and a clear fresh wind, shining through a hall of jade. Matched with a handsome, gifted husband, fair as an immortal, she won a union that would last as long as heaven and earth, surely enough to make amends for childhood’s hardships. Yet in the end the clouds disperse from Gaotang, the Xiang River’s waters run dry. Such are the destined ebb and flow of this dusty world. Why grieve to no purpose?

[No Place in the World]

Her nature lovely as an orchid, her abundant gifts an immortal’s equal; born solitary, unlike anyone else. You call the eating of meat and fish rank, and find silk finery vulgar and repellent. Yet you do not know: the loftier you stand, the more they envy; the purer you are, the more the world dislikes you. Alas, she will grow old by a dim lamp in an ancient hall, wasting the fading spring of a painted face in crimson chambers. In the end there is still the grime of the dusty world, against her heart’s desire. As though flawless white jade were sunk in mud. Why then should princes and young gentlemen lament that no destined bond was theirs?

[A Welcome Enemy]

Wolf of Zhongshan, heartless beast, forgetting entirely how it all began! Nothing but arrogance, extravagance and debauchery, craving carnal pleasure. He regards that noble beauty as a common willow, degrades a duke’s daughter as though she were the lowest of the low. Alas for her lovely, fragrant soul, drifting away within a year.

[Awakening from Empty Flowers]

See through the three springs: what then can red peach blossom and green willow do? Extinguish the splendour of youth; seek a heart free of passion, at peace with heaven. What talk of peach trees blooming high in heaven, of countless apricot stamens among the clouds! In the end, who endures beyond autumn? Look: among the white poplars, people sob; beneath the green maples, ghosts chant. And withered grass, stretching to the sky, hides the graves. Truly, poor yesterday, rich today, people toil; flowers flourish in spring and fade in autumn, worn away. Who can escape life’s barriers and death’s ordeals? They say a precious tree in the West is called the Sala, and on it grows the fruit of everlasting life.

[Undone by Cleverness]

Every device calculated, clever beyond measure, she calculates away her own dear life. Before death her heart is broken; after death her spirit is empty and free. A wealthy house, its people at peace: still there comes the family’s ruin, its members scattering, each to flee alone. All for nothing, half a lifetime’s anxious heart, like a dream adrift at the third watch. A crash, as a great mansion falls; a gathering darkness, as a lamp burns low. Ah! All that joy, suddenly grief and bitterness. Alas for this world: nothing can be settled!

[Blessings Left Behind]

Blessings left behind, blessings left behind: a benefactor met by chance. Fortunate in her mother, fortunate in her mother, who stored up hidden merit! Let all people take this counsel: help the distressed, support the poor. Do not be like my cruel uncle and treacherous elder cousin, who love silver and forget their own flesh and blood! Multiplication, division, addition, subtraction: heaven above keeps the account.

[Late Splendour]

Affection in a mirror, and worse, distinction in a dream! How swiftly lovely youth is gone. Speak no more of embroidered curtains and lovers’ quilts. Even wearing a pearl coronet and a phoenix-patterned robe cannot offset the impermanence of life. Though they say none should suffer poverty in old age, there is still a need to store hidden merit for one’s descendants. Head held high beneath an official’s hat and tassels; a golden seal hanging bright at the waist; rank and stipend mounting in imposing splendour: then darkness, and the road to the Yellow Springs draws near. Where are the generals and ministers of old? Only empty names remain for later people to admire.

[The End of Good Things]

Spring ends at the painted beam; fragrant dust falls. Skilled in love, possessed of a moonlike face: these become the roots of the family’s ruin. The ancestral calling falls to pieces, all from Jing; for the household’s destruction, Ning bears the first blame. Old karmic wrongs always spring from passion.

[Finale: Birds Return to Their Woods]

Those in office, their family estates decline; the wealthy and honoured, their gold and silver scattered. Those who have shown kindness escape from death; the heartless receive their plain reward. Those owing life have paid with life; those owing tears have exhausted them. Wrong repaying wrong is no light matter; partings and reunions were all ordained before. To learn why life is short, ask of a former life; even wealth and honour in old age are truly a lucky escape. Those who see through it retreat into the Buddhist gate; the deluded throw their lives away for nothing. Like birds returning to the woods when all the food is gone, they leave the great earth white and bare, clean at last!

When they had finished, they were about to sing the supplementary songs. Seeing how little interest Bao-yu showed, Disenchantment sighed. ‘The foolish child still has not awakened!’ Bao-yu hastily stopped the singers from going on. Feeling dazed and confused, he pleaded drunkenness and asked to lie down. Disenchantment had the remains of the feast removed and took him to a fragrant, embroidered bedchamber, furnished with splendours such as he had never seen.

Still more astonishing, a young woman was already inside. In the rich freshness of her beauty she resembled Bao-chai; in her graceful charm and slender bearing she was like Dai-yu. He had no idea what this meant. Suddenly Disenchantment spoke. ‘In so many wealthy, distinguished households in the mortal world, the moonlit pleasures behind green windows and the misty splendour of embroidered chambers have all been defiled by debauched young wastrels and loose women. More hateful still, how many frivolous libertines through the ages have excused themselves by saying, “I love beauty but am not lustful,” or pleaded, “I have passion, but not lust.” These are all words to dress up wrongdoing and conceal ugliness.

‘To love beauty is already lust; to know passion is greater lust still. The meetings on Mount Wu, the pleasures of cloud and rain, all come from delighting in someone’s beauty and then becoming attached to their affection. What I value in you is that you are the foremost creature of lust in all the world, ancient or modern.’

Bao-yu was so alarmed that he answered hastily, ‘You are mistaken, fairy. My parents are always scolding me for being lazy about my books. How could I dare to incur the name of “lustful” as well? Besides, I’m still young. I don’t know what “lust” is.’ ‘No,’ said Disenchantment. ‘Though lust has one underlying principle, its meanings differ. Those who delight in lust in the ordinary world merely enjoy good looks, singing and dancing, endless flirting, lovemaking at every hour. They wish all the beauties in the world could serve their momentary pleasure. Such creatures are fools whose excess goes no deeper than the skin. You, however, were born with a strain of devoted feeling that we call “lust of the imagination”. This can be understood in the heart, but not passed on by mouth; grasped in spirit, but not conveyed in words. You alone now possess this quality. It can make you a good companion in the women’s quarters, but out in the world you will inevitably seem impractical and strange, mocked by every tongue, glared at by every eye. Having met your ancestors, the two Dukes of Ning and Rong, and heard their heartfelt entreaty, I cannot bear that you should bring distinction only to our world of women and be rejected by society. That is why I have brought you here: to intoxicate you with numinous wine, steep you in immortal tea, warn you through marvellous songs, and then give you my younger sister in marriage. Her childhood name is Jian-mei, “Beauty Combined”, and her courtesy name Ke-qing.

‘Tonight is auspicious; you may marry at once. You need only learn that even this immortal bedchamber in an illusory realm offers no more than this. How much less, then, can the mortal world offer? Henceforth you must free yourself from these entanglements, awaken from your former passions, attend to Confucius and Mencius, and devote yourself to the work of governing the world.’ She then privately instructed him in the business of cloud and rain, pushed him into the room, closed the door and went away.

Still dazed, Bao-yu followed Disenchantment’s instructions, and the intimacies of lovers took their course, which cannot be fully recounted. By the next day he and Ke-qing were bound to each other in tender feeling and gentle words, unable to bear parting. As they went out hand in hand to amuse themselves, they suddenly came to a place overgrown with thorns and brambles, where wolves and tigers ran together. A black stream blocked their path, with no bridge across it. While they hesitated, Disenchantment came running up behind them. ‘Do not go any further! Turn back at once!’

Bao-yu stopped hurriedly. ‘What is this place?’ ‘This is the Ford of Delusion,’ Disenchantment said. ‘It is ten thousand zhang deep and stretches a thousand li. No boat can cross it. There is only a wooden raft: Layman Wood steers it and Attendant Ash poles it. They accept no gold or silver in thanks, but ferry across only those with whom they have a destined bond.

‘You have happened to wander here today. If you were to fall in, you would gravely betray all the earnest warnings I gave you.’ Before she had finished, the waters of the Ford of Delusion thundered, and a host of yakshas and sea-demons came to drag Bao-yu down. Sweat poured off him like rain; in terror he cried out, ‘Ke-qing, save me!’ Alarmed, Aroma and the other maids hurried to hold him. ‘Don’t be afraid, Bao-yu! We’re here!’

Now Qin-shi was outside the room, telling the little maids to keep a good watch in case the cats and dogs fought. Suddenly she heard Bao-yu call her childhood name in his dream. She wondered, ‘No one here has ever known my childhood name. How does he know it, to call it out in his sleep?’ As the saying goes:

In one deep dream, with whom am I made close?

Of lovers through the ages, I alone am so besotted.
