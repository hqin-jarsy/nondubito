Chapter 60 Jasmine powder takes the place of rose medicinal powder; Rose essence brings a gift of fuling frost

Chapter end

Now Aroma asked Patience what was keeping her in such a bustle.

‘Things no one would ever have thought of,’ Patience said with a smile. ‘They’re funny enough in the telling. I’ll tell you in a few days. Nothing’s clear yet, and I haven’t a moment to spare.’

Before she had finished, one of Li Wan’s maids came in.

‘Is Sister Patience here? My mistress is waiting for you. Why haven’t you come?’

Patience turned and hurried out, calling back with a smile, ‘Coming, coming!’

‘Now her mistress is ill, she’s become a sweet bun herself,’ Aroma and the others laughed. ‘Everyone’s snatching for her, and no one can get hold of her.’

We shall leave Patience to her business.

Bao-yu called Swallow.

‘Go with your mother to Miss Bao’s rooms and say something pleasant to Oriole. You can’t just offend her and leave it at that.’

Swallow agreed and went out with her mother. Bao-yu called after them through the window:

‘Don’t say it in front of Miss Bao. You might only get Oriole a scolding.’

Mother and daughter answered him and set off, chatting as they went.

‘I’m always trying to tell you, Mother, but you won’t believe me,’ said Swallow. ‘Why must you go on till you’ve made yourself look foolish?’

‘Get along with you, little hussy!’ her mother said, laughing. ‘As the saying goes, “No experience, no wisdom.” I know now. And here you are calling me to account as well!’

‘Mother, if you behave yourself and stay in these rooms long enough, there’ll be all sorts of advantages,’ Swallow said with a smile. ‘Let me tell you something. Bao-yu’s always saying that one day he’ll ask Her Ladyship to let everyone in his rooms go free, whether we were born in the household or brought in from outside, every one of us, and leave us and our parents to do as we please. Now tell me, isn’t that one good thing?’

Delighted, her mother asked at once, ‘Is that really true?’

‘Who would make up a lie like that? What for?’ said Swallow.

The old woman went on invoking the Buddha all the way. When they reached Allspice Court, Bao-chai, Dai-yu, Aunt Xue and the others were at their meal. Oriole went to make tea, and Swallow and her mother went straight over to her. Smiling apologetically, they said they had spoken rudely just now, begged her not to take offence, and explained that they had come expressly to ask her pardon. Oriole hastened to offer them seats, smiling, and poured tea for them. Mother and daughter said they had things to do and took their leave.

Suddenly Rui-guan came running after them.

‘Mamma! Sister! Wait a moment!’

She came up and handed them a paper packet. It was rose medicinal powder, she said, for them to take to Parfumée to put on her face.

‘You do make a fuss over trifles!’ Swallow said, laughing. ‘As if there weren’t any for her where she is! You have to go to all this trouble to send her another packet.’

‘What she has is hers. What I give her is mine. Do take it back for me, dear sister.’

Swallow had to accept it. When she and her mother returned, Jia Huan and Jia Cong had just come to ask after Bao-yu and were only now going in.

‘I’ll go in by myself,’ Swallow told her mother. ‘You needn’t come.’

Her mother obeyed. From then on she did whatever Swallow said and dared no longer be obstinate. As Swallow came in, Bao-yu knew she was there to report back and forestalled her with a nod. She understood, said nothing, stood a moment and then went out again, giving Parfumée a look as she did so. Parfumée followed her out, and only then did Swallow quietly tell her about Rui-guan and hand over the powder.

Bao-yu could find nothing to talk about with Jia Cong and Jia Huan, so he asked Parfumée with a smile what she was holding. She at once handed it to him to see, explaining that it was rose medicinal powder for a spring skin eruption.

‘How thoughtful of her!’ Bao-yu said, smiling.

Jia Huan craned his neck for a look. He caught a delicate fragrance, bent down and pulled a piece of paper out of his boot-top, then held it out.

‘Dear Brother, give me half,’ he said with a grin.

Bao-yu could hardly refuse. But because it was a gift from Rui-guan, Parfumée was unwilling to let anyone else have it. She hurriedly stopped him, smiling.

‘Don’t touch that. I’ll fetch some more.’

Bao-yu understood and quickly wrapped it up again.

‘Fetch it quickly,’ he said with a smile.

Parfumée took the packet and put it safely away, then went to her dressing case to find the powder she ordinarily used. When she opened it, the box was empty. She was puzzled. There had been some left that morning. How could it have gone? She asked the others, but they all said they did not know.

‘Why bother asking about that now?’ said Musk. ‘Someone in these rooms must have run short for a moment. Give them anything. How would they know the difference? Get rid of them quickly, so that we can eat.’

At this Parfumée wrapped up some jasmine powder and brought it over. As soon as Jia Huan saw it he reached to take it, but she quickly tossed it onto the kang. He had to pick it up from there himself. He tucked it inside his clothes, said goodbye and left.

With Jia Zheng away, and Lady Wang and the others away as well, Jia Huan had been pretending to be ill for several days to get out of school. Now, delighted with his powder, he went to find Cai-yun. She happened to be talking to Aunt Zhao.

‘I’ve got a packet of the good kind too,’ he told Cai-yun with a grin. ‘I’m giving it to you for your face. You always say rose medicinal powder is better for the skin eruption than the silver powder you buy outside. Have a look. Is this it?’

Cai-yun opened the packet and gave a snort of laughter.

‘Who did you get it from?’

Jia Huan told her what had just happened.

‘They’ve made a fool of you, you country bumpkin!’ she said, laughing. ‘This isn’t the medicinal powder. It’s jasmine powder.’

Jia Huan looked. It was indeed a little pinker than the first lot, and when he sniffed it the fragrance was strong.

‘This is good too,’ he said with a smile. ‘Medicine or face powder, it’s all the same. Keep it for your face. As long as it’s better than what you buy outside, that’s good enough.’

Cai-yun could only put it away.

‘As if they’d give you anything good!’ said Aunt Zhao.

‘Who told you to go begging for it? How can you blame them for making a fool of you? If it were me, I’d take it back and fling it in her face. Just now, while some of them are off paying court to a corpse and others are lying stiff in their beds, make a proper scene. Don’t let anyone have any peace. That would be revenge, at least. Do you suppose they’ll dig this up two months from now and question you about it? Even if they do, you’ll have something to say for yourself. Bao-yu’s your elder brother, so you daren’t offend him, of course. But can’t you even question the cats and dogs in his rooms?’

Jia Huan hung his head.

‘Why make more trouble?’ Cai-yun quickly put in. ‘Whatever’s happened, bear with it a little.’

‘Keep out of it,’ Aunt Zhao told her. ‘It’s nothing to do with you anyway.

‘Now we’ve got right on our side, it would do us good to give those shameless whores a proper scolding.’

She pointed at Jia Huan.

‘Pah! You spineless little wretch! All you’re fit for is to let those brats ride over you. If I so much as say a word to you, or happen to give you the wrong thing by mistake, you can turn your head away, bulge your veins, glare at me and stamp and slam things about in front of your own mother. But when those little cunts make a fool of you, you put up with it! And you expect the servants in this household to be afraid of you one day? You haven’t a bloody scrap of spirit. I’m ashamed for you.’

Jia Huan was ashamed and angry, but still did not dare to go. He flung out his hands.

‘You’re so good at talking, but you daren’t go yourself. You send me to make the trouble. Suppose they tell the school and I get beaten? Of course, it won’t hurt you! Every time you egg me on, and when there’s trouble and I get beaten and scolded, you hang your head like everyone else. Now you’re trying to set me against those little girls again. If you’re not afraid of Third Sister, go yourself. I’ll give you credit if you dare.’

That last remark went straight to his mother’s sore spot.

‘She crawled out of my guts!’ she shouted. ‘Am I to be afraid of her too?

‘Things in this house are coming to a pretty pass!’

She snatched up the packet as she spoke and flew off towards the garden. Cai-yun tried desperately to stop her, but could not, and could only retreat into another room. Jia Huan slipped out through the ceremonial gate and went off to amuse himself.

Aunt Zhao went straight into the garden, blazing with anger, and ran into Mamma Xia, Ou-guan’s godmother, coming towards her. Seeing the furious look on her face, Mamma Xia asked:

‘Where are you going, ma’am?’

‘Just look at this house!’ said Aunt Zhao. ‘Even the little painted whores from the theatre, who’ve only been here two or three days, have learnt to weigh you up and serve you according to what they think you’re worth. If it were anyone else, I wouldn’t be so angry. But to be made a fool of by those little prostitutes! What does that make us?’

This was just what Mamma Xia wanted to hear. She hurriedly asked what had happened, and Aunt Zhao told her how Parfumée had slighted Jia Huan by passing off face powder as the medicinal sort.

‘My dear ma’am, have you only just found out?’ said Mamma Xia. ‘That’s nothing. Only yesterday they were burning paper money in this very place, all on their own authority, and Bao-yu actually stepped in to protect them. Other people can’t so much as bring anything in without being told it won’t do, it’s unclean, it’s unlucky. And burning paper money isn’t unlucky? Think about it, ma’am. Apart from Her Ladyship, who in this house is above you?

‘You don’t stand up for yourself. If you did, who would dare not be afraid of you? Now, the way I see it, these little painted whores haven’t any proper standing. It won’t matter much if you offend them. You’ve got these two things to go on. Make an example of them, and I’ll be there as your witness. Show them your authority, and afterwards you’ll be able to insist on proper respect in other things too. Even the mistresses and young ladies can hardly blame you for the sake of those little painted whores.’

Hearing this, Aunt Zhao felt more justified than ever.

‘I didn’t know about the paper-burning. Tell me exactly what happened.’

Mamma Xia gave her a detailed account of the earlier affair.

‘You just go and speak your mind,’ she added. ‘If it comes to a row, we’ll be there to help you.’

This pleased Aunt Zhao still more. Her courage strengthened, she went straight to the House of Green Delights.

As it happened, Bao-yu had heard that Dai-yu was at Allspice Court and had gone over there. Parfumée was eating with Aroma and the others. At the sight of Aunt Zhao they all got up, smiling, and invited her to join them.

‘Have some food, ma’am. What’s brought you here in such a hurry?’

Aunt Zhao made no answer. She walked up and scattered the powder in Parfumée’s face, pointing at her as she cursed:

‘Little whore! You were bought with my money to learn to sing, nothing but a common painted prostitute! Even the lowest grade of servants in our family is above you. So you know how to change the dish according to the guest, do you? Bao-yu wants to give something, and you get in his way. Was he asking you for anything? You give Huan this to fool him. Do you suppose he doesn’t know the difference? Whatever else they may be, they’re brothers, and both equally your masters. Who are you to look down on him?’

Parfumée could not endure such words. Crying, she answered:

‘I only gave him this because there was none of the medicinal powder left. If I’d said there wasn’t any, I was afraid he wouldn’t believe me. Isn’t this good too?

‘I may have learnt to act, but I’ve never sung outside the household. I’m a young girl. What do I know about painted prostitutes or whatever you call them? There’s no call for you to come here cursing me, ma’am. I wasn’t bought by your family. As the saying goes, “Mei-xiang and her sworn sister: servants both!”’1

‘Don’t talk nonsense!’ Aroma said, hastily pulling at her.

In a fury, Aunt Zhao came up and slapped her face twice. Aroma and the others hurried between them, pulling Aunt Zhao back and trying to soothe her.

‘Don’t take a child so seriously, ma’am. Leave us to talk to her.’

After two slaps Parfumée was hardly going to submit. She threw herself about and rolled on the floor, wailing and making a scene.

‘You think you’re fit to hit me? Take a look at yourself before you raise your hand! You’ve beaten me. How can I go on living now?’

She hurled herself against Aunt Zhao’s breast and dared her to hit her. The others tried to calm her and pull her away. Skybright quietly tugged at Aroma.

‘Leave them alone. Let them fight, and see how they get out of it. There’s no rule here now but whoever makes the most trouble. You can hit people, I can hit people. Where will it end if everyone starts doing that?’

Outside, the people who had followed Aunt Zhao heard the uproar with private satisfaction. Each was invoking the Buddha and saying, ‘So this day has come at last!’ Some of the older women who bore grudges were equally pleased that Parfumée had been beaten.

Ou-guan and Rui-guan were playing together when Kui-guan, the girl who had played major painted-face roles and now served Xiang-yun, and Dou-guan, who served Bao-qin, heard the news. They hurried to find the other two.

‘If someone can bully Parfumée, we’ll look fools too. We must all risk it and make a proper uproar. That’s the only way to get our own back.’

The four were children at heart. Thinking only of their friendship and the wrong done to their companion, they paid no heed to anything else and ran together into the House of Green Delights. Dou-guan charged head first and very nearly knocked Aunt Zhao down. The other three crowded in too, crying at the tops of their voices, tugging with their hands and butting with their heads until Aunt Zhao was surrounded. Skybright and the others laughed and made a pretence of pulling them apart. Aroma was frantic: she would catch hold of one only to have another break away.

‘Are you trying to get yourselves killed?’ she kept saying. ‘If you’ve been wronged, tell someone. How can you behave so outrageously?’

Now it was Aunt Zhao who did not know what to do. She could only curse at random. Rui-guan and Ou-guan held an arm each, one on either side, while Kui-guan and Dou-guan pressed their heads against her from front and back.

‘Just beat all four of us to death, then!’ was all they would say.

Parfumée lay stretched rigid on the floor, crying until she fainted.

There seemed no way of ending the affair, but Skybright had already sent Swallow to inform Tan-chun. You-shi, Li Wan and Tan-chun now arrived with Patience and a number of married servants and hurriedly ordered the four girls to stop. Asked what had happened, Aunt Zhao glared, the veins standing out, and was too angry to give a coherent account. You-shi and Li Wan did not answer her. They merely kept ordering the four girls to desist.

Tan-chun sighed.

‘What a little thing to be so angry about, Aunt! I have something I wanted to discuss with you. No wonder the maid couldn’t find you. Here you’ve been, working yourself into a rage. Come along with me.’

You-shi and Li Wan both smiled.

‘Please come to the hall, Aunt. We can talk there.’

Aunt Zhao had no choice but to go out with the three of them, though she was still complaining as she went.

‘Those little maids are only there for our amusement,’ Tan-chun told her. ‘If you like them, you can laugh and talk with them. If you don’t, you can ignore them. Even when they behave badly, it’s no more than a scratch or a bite from a cat or dog. If you can overlook it, do. If you can’t, send for the women in charge and have them speak to the girls and punish them. Why lower yourself, shouting and bellowing till all dignity is gone? Look at Aunt Zhou. Who ever bullies her? She doesn’t go looking for quarrels either. Do go back to your rooms and cool down, Aunt. Don’t listen to those scoundrels egging you on. You only make yourself a laughing-stock, stupidly doing their dirty work for nothing. However furious you are, bear with it these few days. Her Ladyship will deal with it when she comes back.’

This left Aunt Zhao without a word to say. She could only go back to her rooms.

Tan-chun turned angrily to You-shi and Li Wan.

‘At her age, she still never does anything to make people respect her. What does she mean by it? Is such a thing worth a quarrel, throwing every shred of dignity away? She’ll listen to anyone and never think for herself. This is more of those shameless servants’ contriving. They’ve found a fool to work off their anger for them.’

The more she thought about it, the angrier she grew, until she ordered an inquiry into who had stirred up the trouble. The married servants had to promise to see to it, but once outside they looked at one another and laughed.

‘How are we to find a needle in the sea?’ they all said.

They could only summon Aunt Zhao’s attendants and the people in the garden for questioning. Everyone denied knowing anything. At a loss, they reported to Tan-chun:

‘We can’t find out at once. We’ll keep asking quietly. Whoever we find has been saying things they shouldn’t, we’ll report them all for punishment.’

Tan-chun’s anger gradually subsided and she let the matter rest. It happened, however, that Ai-guan came privately to tell her:

‘It’s all Mamma Xia. She’s always against us, making up stories and causing trouble. The other day she accused Ou-guan of burning paper. Luckily it was Bao-yu who’d told her to burn it, and he said so himself, so she had nothing more to say. Today, when I went to deliver the handkerchief for you, miss, I saw her with Aunt Zhao. They were whispering together for ages and only went away when they saw me.’

Tan-chun could see there was something in this. But she was also sure that the girls were all in league, and they were an exceedingly mischievous set to begin with. She merely acknowledged what Ai-guan had said and would not take it as established fact.

Now Mamma Xia had a granddaughter, her daughter’s child, called Chan-jie, who served in Tan-chun’s quarters, often buying things and fetching people for the maids in her rooms. All the girls were fond of her. That day after the meal Tan-chun went to the hall to attend to business, leaving Ebony to mind the rooms. Ebony told Chan-jie to go out and ask a young serving-boy to buy some cakes.

‘I’ve only just swept that great garden,’ Chan-jie said. ‘My back and legs are aching. Can’t you send someone else?’

‘Who else am I to send?’ Ebony said with a smile. ‘Be quick and go. I’ll tell you something for your own good. When you get to the back gate, you can warn your grandmother on the way.’

She told her what Ai-guan had said about Mamma Xia. Chan-jie hurriedly took the money.

‘So that little hussy wants to play tricks on people too! I’ll go and tell her.’

Off she went. At the back gate she found the kitchen people enjoying a quiet interval, all sitting on the steps chatting. Her grandmother was among them.

Chan-jie sent one of the women out to buy the cakes. Then, cursing as she talked, she told Mamma Xia what she had just heard. Mamma Xia was both angry and frightened. First she wanted to find Ai-guan and challenge her, then she wanted to go to Tan-chun and protest her innocence. Chan-jie quickly stopped her.

‘What are you going to say when you get there? How did you come to hear about it? That would only stir up more trouble. I’m telling you so you can be on your guard. There’s no need to rush off this minute.’

They were still talking when Parfumée appeared. Holding onto the courtyard gateway, she called with a smile to Liu’s wife in the kitchen:

‘Mrs Liu, Master Bao says he wants something cool and sour among the vegetable dishes for supper. Only don’t put sesame oil in and make it greasy.’

‘All right,’ Liu’s wife said, smiling. ‘How is it they’ve sent you today with such an important message? If you don’t mind the dirt, why not come in and have a look round?’

Parfumée had just stepped in when a woman arrived carrying a plate of cakes.

‘Who’s bought hot cakes?’ Parfumée said teasingly. ‘Let me try one first.’

Chan-jie took the plate at once.

‘These were bought for someone else. You wouldn’t care for anything like this.’

‘Miss Parfumée, do you like these?’ Liu’s wife put in quickly, smiling. ‘I’ve some here that I only just bought for your sister. She hasn’t eaten them. They’re put away, quite clean and untouched.’

She brought out a plate and handed it to Parfumée.

‘Wait while I go and make you a nice cup of tea.’

She went back inside, poked up the fire and set the tea to heat.

Holding a hot cake, Parfumée challenged Chan-jie to her face.

‘As if I wanted your cakes! Isn’t this a cake too? I was only joking. I wouldn’t eat yours if you kowtowed to me.’

She broke the cakes in her hand into pieces, tossing them at the sparrows for fun.

‘Don’t begrudge them, Mrs Liu,’ she called, laughing. ‘I’ll buy you two catties to make up for it.’

Chan-jie stared in fury, then gave a cold laugh.

‘Lord Thunder has eyes too. Why doesn’t he strike anyone who does such wicked things? And now she’s trying to provoke me! How could I compare with you? You’ve people bringing you tribute, people volunteering to be your slaves, sucking up to you in hopes of a good word to help them on.’

‘Come, girls, that’s enough,’ the women said. ‘You can’t see each other without squabbling.’

Some of the quicker-witted women, seeing them exchange words and fearing another scene, got to their feet and slipped away. Chan-jie herself did not dare say too much to Parfumée and left, muttering.

Seeing that everyone had gone, Liu’s wife hurried out to Parfumée.

‘Did you mention what we were talking about the other day?’

‘I did. I’ll bring it up again in a day or two. And now that Zhao woman, who’ll never die, has picked another quarrel with me.

‘Has your girl had the rose essence I sent the other day? Is she any better after all?’

‘She’s had every drop,’ said Liu’s wife. ‘She loved it more than anything, but she was ashamed to ask you for more.’

‘That’s nothing,’ said Parfumée. ‘I’ll ask for some more and bring it to her.’

Liu’s wife had a daughter who was only sixteen that year. A kitchen servant’s daughter though she was, her looks were of a kind with those of Patience, Aroma, Nightingale and Faithful. She was the fifth in her family and so was called Wu-er. Having always been frail and sickly, she had never been given a post. Lately her mother had noticed that there were plenty of maids in Bao-yu’s rooms and the duties were light. She had also heard that he meant to let them all go free one day. She therefore wanted to have her daughter put on the staff there.

At first she could see no way of bringing it about. But she had served at Pear-tree Court, where her attentive care for Parfumée and the other girls had been better even than that of the other godmothers. They in turn were very good to her family. She had now spoken to Parfumée and begged her to put in a word with Bao-yu. Bao-yu had agreed, but lately, with his illness and so many other things going on, he had not yet been able to raise the matter.

Enough of what had gone before. Parfumée now returned to the House of Green Delights and reported to Bao-yu.

He had heard Aunt Zhao’s uproar and been much distressed. To speak would not do, and to say nothing would not do either. He had therefore waited until the quarrel was over. Only after learning that Tan-chun had persuaded her to leave had he come back from Allspice Court. He had spent some time comforting Parfumée before everyone was quiet again.

Now, on returning, she told him she wanted some more rose essence for Liu Wu-er.

‘There’s some left,’ he said at once. ‘I hardly take any. Give her all of it.’

He told Aroma to fetch it. Seeing how little remained in the bottle, he gave Parfumée bottle and all.

Parfumée carried it over herself. Liu’s wife had brought her daughter into the garden for a little diversion. They had walked for a while round a remote corner and were now back in the kitchen, drinking tea and resting their feet. Parfumée brought a small glass bottle about five cun high. Held against the light, it showed less than half a bottle of liquid as red as rouge. Mother and daughter took it for the Western grape wine Bao-yu drank.

‘Quick, get the warming pot and some boiling water,’ they said. ‘Do sit down.’

‘This is all that’s left,’ Parfumée said, smiling. ‘You can have the bottle too.’

Only then did Wu-er realise that it was rose essence. She quickly took it, thanking Parfumée again and again.

‘Are you any better?’ Parfumée asked.

‘I’ve a little more spirit today, so I came in for a walk. There’s nothing much to see round the back, though, only great rocks and trees and the backs of the buildings. I haven’t seen any of the real beauty spots.’

‘Why didn’t you go round to the front?’ Parfumée asked.

‘I wouldn’t let her,’ her mother said. ‘The young ladies don’t know who she is. If someone who took a dislike to her saw her, there’d be more talk. Once you’ve helped her into a place in one of the rooms, there’ll be people enough to take her round. She may even grow tired of walking here.’

‘What’s there to be afraid of?’ Parfumée said with a laugh. ‘I’m here.’

‘Oh, my dear miss!’ Liu’s wife said quickly. ‘We can’t afford to be so bold. We’re not like you.’

She poured more tea. Parfumée would not drink such tea. She merely rinsed her mouth with a sip and left.

‘My hands are full here,’ Liu’s wife said. ‘Wu-er, see her out.’

Wu-er accompanied her outside. Seeing no one about, she caught hold of Parfumée.

‘Have you really spoken about me?’

‘Do you think I’d lie to you?’ Parfumée said with a smile. ‘I hear there are actually two places in the rooms that haven’t been filled. One was Crimson’s. Mrs Lian asked for her and hasn’t sent anyone in exchange. The other was Zhui-er’s, and that hasn’t been filled either. It’s hardly too much to ask to have just you. Only Patience keeps telling Aroma that whenever something means moving people or spending money, it’s best to put it off another day if you possibly can. The Third Miss is looking for someone to make an example of. She’s even turned down two or three things in Patience’s own rooms. Now she’s trying to find something wrong in ours and hasn’t managed it. Why go walking into the net? If she says something and turns it down, the refusal will stick, and it’ll be hard to get it changed. Better wait till things have cooled down and Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship have some peace. However big the matter, tell the old lady first and it’s sure to be settled.’

‘That may be so,’ said Wu-er, ‘but I’m too impatient to wait.

‘If I could be chosen now, first it would give Mother something to be proud of, so she wouldn’t have brought me up for nothing. Second, there’d be the extra monthly allowance, and things would be easier at home. Third, I’d be happier, and perhaps I’d even get well. Even if I had to see a doctor and take medicine, it would save the family money.’

‘I understand it all,’ said Parfumée. ‘Don’t you worry.’

They parted, and we shall leave Parfumée to go her way.

Wu-er went back, and she and her mother talked gratefully of Parfumée’s kindness.

‘I never expected to get anything like this,’ her mother said. ‘Precious stuff it may be, but too much of it is very heating. If we poured some out and gave it away, that would be a fine favour too.’

‘Who to?’ Wu-er asked.

‘Your uncle’s boy. He’s had a fever since yesterday, and he’s been longing for something like this. I’ll take him half a small cup.’

Wu-er said nothing for a while. She let her mother pour out half a cup, then put the rest, still in the bottle, away in the kitchen cupboard.

‘If it were up to me, I wouldn’t give him any,’ she said with a cold smile. ‘Suppose someone starts asking questions. There’ll be another affair.’

‘If we’re to be frightened of everything like that, where will it end?’ her mother said. ‘We work hard enough. It’s only right we should get a few things out of the household. It’s not as if we stole it.’

She went straight out to her elder brother’s home. Her nephew was lying down. At the sight of what she had brought, her brother, his wife and their son were all delighted.

Cool water was fetched from the well at once, and the boy drank a bowl with the essence mixed into it. He felt refreshed, his head and eyes cool and clear. The half-cup that remained was covered with paper and set on the table.

Just then several young servants from the household, friends of her nephew, came to ask after him. Among them was a young fellow called Qian Huai, a nephew of Aunt Zhao’s. His parents kept the accounts at the storehouse, and he himself had been assigned to accompany Jia Huan to school. With some money and influence behind him, and still unmarried, he had long admired Liu Wu-er’s good looks. He had told his parents he wanted her for his wife, and intermediaries and a matchmaker had repeatedly been sent to press his suit. Her parents were willing enough, but Wu-er was determined not to agree. She had never said so outright, yet her behaviour had made it plain, and they had not dared give their consent.

Lately, with her mind on a place in the garden, she had put the match still further out of mind. She meant to wait until she was released in three to five years, then choose a husband outside for herself. The Qian family, seeing her attitude, had let it go. Qian Huai, however, was both angry and ashamed at not being able to get her. He had vowed in his resentment to contrive to make her his wife. Nothing else would satisfy him.

He had come with the others that day to visit the sick boy, not expecting to find the boy’s aunt there. Liu’s wife, suddenly faced with a whole party and Qian Huai among them, made the excuse that she could not spare the time, rose and prepared to leave.

‘Why, Auntie, you haven’t even had any tea,’ her brother and his wife said. ‘It was kind of you to remember us.’

‘I’m afraid they’ll be calling for the meal inside,’ she said with a smile. ‘I’ll come and see my nephew when I’ve more time.’

Her sister-in-law took a paper packet out of a drawer and carried it out with her. When they reached the corner of the wall, she handed it to Liu’s wife.

‘Your brother was on duty at the gate yesterday,’ she said, smiling. ‘On this five-day rota it’s been so quiet he hasn’t had a single extra bit of money. Only yesterday an official from Guangdong came to call and brought two little baskets of fuling frost for the masters.

‘Besides those, he gave the men at the gate another basket as a gate gift, and this is what your brother got as his share. That part of the country abounds in thousand-year-old pines and cypresses. So they take the essence of this fuling and mix it with medicine, and somehow make it into this wonderfully fine white frost. They say the best way is to mix it with human milk and take a cupful first thing every morning. Nothing could be more nourishing. Cow’s milk is next best. If there’s nothing else, plain boiled water will do.

‘We thought it would be just the thing for our niece. I sent a little maid round with it earlier in the day, but she said the door was locked and our niece had gone inside the mansion too. I’d meant to come and see her and bring it myself. Then I thought, with the masters away and everywhere under such strict watch, and no duty to take me in, why should I go running about for no good reason? Besides, there’s talk these last two days of the whole place being in an uproar. If I got dragged into it, I’d pay dearly for it. You’ve come just at the right moment, sister. Take it back yourself.’

Liu’s wife thanked her for her kindness, said goodbye and set off back. She had only just reached the side gate when a young serving-boy said with a grin:

‘Where have you been? They’ve sent for you inside again and again. Three or four of our lads have gone looking for you and haven’t come back yet. And where have you come from now? This isn’t the way to your house. You’ve got me wondering!’

‘You little monkey …’ Liu’s wife retorted, laughing.
