Chapter 2 Jia Min dies in Yangzhou; Leng Zi-xing describes the Rong mansion

Chapter end

A poem says:

The outcome of this game is hard to tell; Incense spent and tea gone, still we linger. To read the signs of rise and fall before our eyes, Ask the onlooker who watches coolly.

Now Feng Su, hearing the officers calling for him, hurried out and asked their business with an obliging smile. They only shouted, ‘Fetch Mr Zhen out at once!’

‘My name is Feng, gentlemen, not Zhen,’ he said, still smiling. ‘My son-in-law’s name was Zhen, but he left to enter religion a year or two ago. Could he be the man you want?’

‘We know nothing about your Zhens and your Fakes,’ the officers said. ‘Our prefect sent us to ask. If he’s your son-in-law, we’ll take you along to explain it to His Honour yourself. Save us running about.’ Without allowing Feng Su another word, they crowded round and hustled him away. Everyone in the Feng household was frightened, not knowing what this might mean.

It was about the second watch that night before Feng Su came home, delighted with himself. Everyone pressed him to explain.

‘Our newly appointed prefect is a man named Jia Hua, originally from Huzhou. He used to be a friend of my son-in-law. He passed our door just now and saw that maid Jiao-xing buying thread, so he supposed my son-in-law had moved in here. I explained everything. His Honour was quite distressed and sighed over it. Then he asked after my granddaughter. I told him she had been lost while looking at the lanterns. “Never fear,” he said. “I shall send my men out and have every effort made to find her.” We talked for a while, and when I left he actually gave me two taels of silver.’

Mrs Zhen could not hear this without sorrow. Nothing more happened that night.

Early next morning Yu-cun sent two packets of silver and four lengths of brocade to thank Mrs Zhen. He also sent Feng Su a private letter, asking him to approach Mrs Zhen on his behalf: he wanted Jiao-xing as a secondary wife. Feng Su was so beside himself with joy he all but shat and pissed himself. Eager to curry favour, he urged his daughter so insistently that she agreed, and under cover of night Jiao-xing was carried in to the prefect in a single small chair. Yu-cun’s delight need hardly be described. He sent Feng Su a packet of a hundred taels of silver and gave Mrs Zhen many other gifts, telling her to take good care of herself while they sought news of her daughter. Feng Su went home, and there we may leave him.

This maid Jiao-xing was the very one who had looked back at Yu-cun that year. A casual glance had brought all this about: an extraordinary turn of fate she could never have foreseen. Who would have thought her destiny and her luck would serve her so well? Within a year of going to Yu-cun she unexpectedly bore him a son. Another six months passed; his lawful wife suddenly fell ill and died, and Yu-cun formally raised Jiao-xing to the position of principal wife. As the saying goes:

One chance move, made in error,

Raised her above the rest.

After Shi-yin had given him the money that year, Yu-cun had set off for the capital on the sixteenth. When the great examination came round, he did unexpectedly well, passing as a jinshi. Selected for a provincial appointment, he had by now risen to be prefect here.

Able as he was, he was not free from greed and harshness. Confident of his talents, he treated his superiors with contempt, and his fellow officials all looked askance at him. Before a year was out, his superior found an opening and drew up a memorial impeaching him for ‘a deceitful and crafty disposition, the unauthorised prescription of ceremonial rules, and the pursuit of a reputation for purity and uprightness while secretly associating with men who are wolves and tigers, bringing troubles upon the district and making the people’s lives unbearable’, among other charges. The emperor was furious and ordered his dismissal. When the notice arrived from the ministry, every official in the prefecture rejoiced. Yu-cun was deeply ashamed and bitter at heart, but showed not a trace of resentment. He went on smiling and joking as though nothing had happened. After handing over his duties, he sent the money he had accumulated in office, together with his family and dependants, back to his native place. Once all was properly arranged, he set off alone, with the wind on his shoulders and moonlight in his sleeves, to visit the famous sights of the empire. His travels happened to bring him to the Huaiyang region. There he heard that Lin Ru-hai had been appointed to oversee the salt revenue that year. This gentleman’s surname was Lin, his personal name Hai and his courtesy name Ru-hai. He had placed third in the previous palace examination and had now risen to be an officer of the Imperial Library. A native of Gusu, he had been specially appointed by the emperor as Salt Inspector and had been in his new post a little over a month.

An ancestor of Lin Ru-hai had held a hereditary marquisate; Ru-hai was now the fifth generation. Originally the title had been granted for three generations only. But the munificence and virtue of the present sovereign so far surpassed those of earlier reigns that an additional favour had allowed Ru-hai’s father to inherit it for a fourth generation. Ru-hai himself had entered official life through the examinations. His family belonged to the hereditary nobility, with its bells and bronze vessels, but it also had a tradition of learning. Unfortunately, the Lin clan had few branches and few descendants. There were several households, but these were only collateral relations of Ru-hai’s, with no close kin in his direct line. He was now forty. His only son, a boy of three, had died the previous year. He had several concubines, but no son was allotted to him by fate, and nothing could be done about it. His lawful wife, a lady of the Jia family, had borne him a daughter, known from infancy as Dai-yu, who was just five. Having no son, husband and wife treasured her. She was bright and delicate-looking, too, and they wished her to study and learn a few characters, merely to stand in, as it were, for the son they were not raising, and ease their sorrow at the emptiness about their knees.

Yu-cun had caught a chill and lain ill at an inn for nearly a month before beginning to recover. Worn out in body and running short of travelling money, he was looking for a suitable place where he could settle for a while. Fortunately, two old friends lived in the district. Hearing that the Salt Inspector wanted a tutor, he asked for their help and secured the post, as a means of providing himself with a home for the time being.

The beauty of it was that he had only one girl to teach, with two maids studying beside her. She was very young and extremely frail, and no fixed amount of work was required of her, so his duties were light. Almost another year had passed when his pupil’s mother fell ill and died. The girl had attended her with broth and medicine and now observed her mourning with the utmost grief. Yu-cun was about to leave the post and look elsewhere, but Lin Ru-hai wished his daughter to continue her studies during mourning, and kept him on. Lately, however, grief had overwhelmed the girl. Always frail and sickly, she had suffered a return of an old illness and had missed lessons for several days. Yu-cun had nothing to do. Whenever the weather was sunny and mild, he would go out for a walk after his meal.

One day he happened to go beyond the city walls, intending to enjoy the country scenery. Wandering on, he came to a place of encircling hills and winding water, thick woods and luxuriant bamboo. A temple stood half hidden there. Its gateway and approach were falling in, its walls crumbling. Over the entrance a board bore the words ‘Temple of Understanding’; beside the door hung an old, damaged couplet:

With more than enough to leave behind, you forget to draw back your hand;

With no road left before you, you think of turning round.

Yu-cun read it and thought: ‘The words are plain enough, but the meaning goes deep. I have visited famous mountains and great temples without ever seeing a saying like this. Perhaps someone here has taken a tumble and come through it. Why not go in and find out?’

He went in. There was only a decrepit old monk cooking gruel, and Yu-cun took little interest in him. When he did ask him a question or two, the old monk proved deaf and muddle-headed, toothless and slow of speech; his answers had nothing to do with the questions.

Yu-cun grew impatient and went out again. He thought he would have a few cups at the village tavern to round off his enjoyment of the countryside, and strolled towards it. Just as he was entering, one of the drinkers rose with a loud laugh and came to meet him.

‘What a meeting! What a meeting!’

Yu-cun looked and recognised a man known as Leng Zi-xing, an antiques dealer in the capital whom he had met there in former days. Yu-cun had always praised him as a capable man who could accomplish things; Zi-xing, for his part, profited by Yu-cun’s reputation as a scholar. They therefore found plenty to talk about and got on very well.

‘When did you come here, my dear fellow?’ Yu-cun asked with a smile. ‘I had no idea. What a lucky chance to meet you today!’

‘I came home at the end of last year,’ said Zi-xing. ‘Now I am on my way back to the capital, and stopped here to have a word with a friend. He was kind enough to keep me for a couple of days. I have no pressing business, so I’m staying a little. I shall set off about the middle of the month. My friend is busy today. I happened to stroll out here and thought I would rest my feet.’

‘Who could have expected such a lucky meeting?’

As he spoke he invited Yu-cun to sit with him, and ordered fresh wine and dishes. They drank at leisure and talked of what had happened since their last meeting.

‘Any news in the capital lately?’ Yu-cun asked.

‘Nothing much. Though there has been one little oddity in the family of your distinguished kinsmen.’

Yu-cun laughed. ‘None of my clan live in the capital. Whom can you mean?’

‘You share a surname,’ said Zi-xing, smiling. ‘Doesn’t that make you one clan?’

Yu-cun asked which family he meant.

‘The Jias of the Rong mansion. Surely that would bring no discredit on your family?’

‘Oh, that family!’ Yu-cun said with a smile. ‘As it happens, our humble clan has quite a number of members. Since Jia Fu of the Eastern Han, its branches have multiplied and spread through every province. Who could trace them all? The Rong branch does belong to the same genealogy as ours, but with all their splendour we could hardly presume on the connection. So by now we have grown still further apart and scarcely know each other.’

Zi-xing sighed. ‘Don’t say that, sir. Both houses, Ning and Rong, are in decline now. They are not what they were.’

‘There used to be a great many people in those two mansions. How have they declined so soon?’

‘Ah,’ said Leng Zi-xing, ‘that is a long story.’

‘Last year,’ said Yu-cun, ‘I went to Jinling to see the remains of the Six Dynasties. One day I entered the Stone City and passed their ancestral mansions. The Ning mansion lay to the east, the Rong mansion to the west; together they took up more than half the street.’

‘The great gates were quiet and deserted, but over the enclosing walls I could see halls and towers, all still lofty and imposing. Even the trees and rocks in the gardens behind had a rich, flourishing look. How could that be a family in decline?’

Leng Zi-xing laughed. ‘And you a jinshi! You understand nothing of it. As the old saying has it, “A hundred-footed creature does not fall stiff even when dead.” They may not flourish as they did, but they still look very different from an ordinary official’s household. There are more mouths every day, and more affairs to attend to. From the masters down to the servants, plenty enjoy the wealth and honour, but not one does any planning. Yet they cannot bring themselves to cut down the expense of their daily show. The outer structure has hardly begun to totter, but inside the purse is nearly empty. Even that is a small matter. There is something worse. Who would have thought that in a house of bronze vessels and dinner bells, a family of books and poetry, the sons and grandsons would grow worse with every generation?’

Yu-cun was astonished. ‘How could a family so steeped in learning and propriety neglect its children’s education? I cannot speak for other houses, but Ning and Rong are particularly good at bringing up their sons.’

Zi-xing sighed. ‘Those are the very two I mean. Let me tell you.’

‘The first Duke of Ning and the first Duke of Rong were brothers born of the same mother. Ning was the elder and had four sons. After his death Jia Dai-hua inherited his rank. Dai-hua himself had two sons. The elder, Jia Fu, died at eight or nine, leaving only the younger, Jia Jing, to inherit. Nowadays Jing thinks of nothing but the Tao. He loves firing his elixirs and refining mercury; nothing else concerns him. Luckily he had a son in his early years, called Jia Zhen. So intent was the father on becoming an immortal that he handed his rank over to the son. He will not return to his native place, but stays outside the capital, muddling about with Taoist priests. This Master Zhen has a son in his turn, Jia Rong, who is just sixteen this year. Old Mr Jing leaves everything alone. As for Master Zhen, he has no mind for books: pleasure is all he cares about. He has turned the Ning mansion upside down, and nobody dares control him.

‘Now listen to the Rong side. That is where the strange thing I mentioned happened. After the Duke of Rong died, his eldest son Jia Dai-shan inherited. His wife was a daughter of the Marquis Shi’s family, another house of hereditary distinction in Jinling. They had two sons: the elder Jia She, the younger Jia Zheng.’

‘Dai-shan has long been dead, but his widow is still alive. The elder son, Jia She, inherited the rank. The younger, Jia Zheng, was devoted to books from childhood and was his grandfather’s favourite. He had meant to make his way through the examinations. But when Dai-shan’s dying memorial reached the emperor, His Majesty, out of regard for his late servant, not only ordered the eldest son to succeed him but asked how many other sons there were, and summoned them at once. As an additional favour he gave Mr Zheng the title of secretary and sent him to a ministry to learn its business. He has now been promoted to assistant director.

‘Mr Zheng’s wife, a lady of the Wang family, first bore a son called Jia Zhu. He qualified as a licentiate at fourteen; before he was twenty he had married, fathered a son, and died of an illness. Her second child was a girl, born on New Year’s Day. That was remarkable enough, but later she had another boy, and this was stranger still: he came out of the womb with a piece of lustrous, many-coloured jade in his mouth, with a good many characters written on it. So they named him Bao-yu, “Precious Jade”.’

‘Wouldn’t you call that something new and strange?’

‘Strange indeed,’ said Yu-cun, smiling. ‘He may have no ordinary origin.’

Zi-xing gave a cold laugh. ‘That is what everyone says. His grandmother treasured him from the start. On his first birthday Mr Zheng wanted to test what his future inclinations would be, and set out a great collection of all sorts of things for him to choose from. He would touch none of them, but reached straight for the rouge and powder, hairpins and bracelets. His father was furious. “He’ll be nothing but a wine-bibber and a lecher!” After that he took a great dislike to him. Old Lady Shi alone still cherished him as her very life.

‘And here is another odd thing. He has grown to seven or eight now, and though he is extraordinarily naughty, a hundred other children together could not match his intelligence and quickness. Even his childish talk is strange. “Girls’ flesh and bones are made of water,” he says. “Men’s are made of mud. When I see girls, I feel fresh and clear; when I see men, their muddy stink comes pressing in on me.” Isn’t that laughable? He is certain to grow into a sex-mad devil!’

Yu-cun’s expression abruptly hardened. He hurried to stop him.

‘No! What a pity you do not understand where such a person comes from. I suspect that Mr Zheng too has mistaken him for a lecherous monster. Unless one has read widely and learnt about the world, laboured at investigating things and extending knowledge, and striven to apprehend the Way and explore its mysteries, one cannot understand.’

Seeing how seriously he took it, Zi-xing at once asked him to explain.

‘Of all the people Heaven and Earth produce,’ said Yu-cun, ‘there are two exceptional kinds: the supremely benevolent and the supremely wicked. The rest do not differ greatly. The supremely benevolent are born in answer to an auspicious turn of fortune; the supremely wicked in answer to a time of calamity. Those born of fortune bring order to the world; those born of calamity bring danger. Yao, Shun, Yu, Tang, Kings Wen and Wu, the Dukes of Zhou and Shao, Confucius and Mencius, Dong Zhong-shu and Han Yu, Zhou Dun-yi, the Cheng masters, Zhang Zai and Zhu Xi: all were born in answer to fortune. Chi-you, Gong-gong, Jie, Zhou, the First Emperor, Wang Mang, Cao Cao, Huan Wen, An Lu-shan and Qin Hui: all were born in answer to calamity. The supremely benevolent set the world in order; the supremely wicked throw it into turmoil.’

‘Clarity, illumination, spirit and refinement are the upright force of Heaven and Earth, with which the benevolent are endowed. Cruelty and perversity are its evil force, with which the wicked are endowed. In our present dynasty, with its flourishing fortune and enduring blessings, in this peaceful age of effortless rule, those endowed with the clear, luminous, spiritually refined force are everywhere, from the court above to the countryside below. What remains of that refined force wanders with nowhere to go, and becomes sweet dew and gentle breezes, spreading moisture and harmony throughout the four seas. The evil force of cruelty and perversity cannot range abroad in the bright light of day. It congeals and fills deep ravines and great chasms instead. A gust of wind may stir it, or a cloud drive it on; a slight movement or response is awakened, and a thread or half a wisp escapes by accident. If the spiritually refined force happens to pass at that moment, the upright cannot tolerate the evil, and the evil in turn resents the upright. Neither will yield. Like wind, water, thunder and lightning meeting underground, they can neither die away nor make room for each other. They must clash and burst forth before their strength is spent. Such a force, too, must therefore be bestowed on human beings; only when it has discharged itself completely can it disperse. Men or women born by chance with this mixed endowment can neither rise to be benevolent men and gentlemen nor sink to be the greatly vicious and wicked.’

‘Set them among a hundred million people, and their intelligence, quickness, spirit and refinement will put them above them all; but in their perversity, waywardness and disregard for common human feeling, they will rank below them all. Born into a rich and noble house, they become fools for love, creatures of passion. Born into a poor but learned family, they become lofty recluses. Even if born into a humble household with scant blessings, they can never become common runners or sturdy serving-men, content to be driven and ruled by mediocrities. They must become extraordinary actors or celebrated courtesans. Think of Xu You, Tao Qian, Ruan Ji, Ji Kang, Liu Ling, the Wang and Xie families, Gu Hu-tou, the Last Emperor of Chen, Emperor Ming-huang of the Tang, Emperor Hui-zong of the Song, Liu Ting-zhi, Wen Fei-qing, Mi Nan-gong, Shi Man-qing, Liu Qi-qing and Qin Shao-you in earlier times; or, more recently, Ni Yun-lin, Tang Bo-hu and Zhu Zhi-shan. Then there are Li Gui-nian, Huang Fan-chuo, Jing Xin-mo, Zhuo Wen-jun, Hong-fu, Xue Tao, Cui Ying and Chao-yun. Change their circumstances, and they would all be the same kind of people.’

‘Then by your account,’ said Zi-xing, ‘“Success makes a prince; failure makes a bandit.”’

‘Precisely,’ said Yu-cun. ‘And there is something else you do not know. In the two years since my dismissal I have travelled through the provinces and met two unusual children. So as soon as you mentioned this Bao-yu, I could guess that he was very probably of this kind. There is no need to look far. Do you know the Zhen family in Jinling, whose head is imperial commissioner and president of the Institute of Benevolence in Jinling province?’

‘Who doesn’t? The Zhens and the Jias have long been related by marriage and are old family friends besides. They are extremely close. I myself have had dealings with the Zhens for a good while.’

‘Last year, when I was in Jinling,’ Yu-cun said with a smile, ‘someone recommended me as a tutor to that family. Once inside, I found that, exalted as their position was, they were rich yet mindful of propriety. It was a rare household to teach in. But this one pupil, though only beginning his letters, cost me more trouble than a student preparing for the examinations.’

‘His talk was more ridiculous still. “I must have two girls beside me while I study,” he said. “Then I can recognise the characters and understand them. Otherwise my mind is all muddled.” He would often say to the pages who attended him, “The word ‘girl’ is supremely noble and supremely pure. Even the sacred names of Amitabha Buddha and the Primordial Lord of Heaven cannot equal it in honour. You must never profane it with your filthy mouths and stinking tongues. Remember that! Whenever you wish to say it, you must first rinse your mouths with clean water and fragrant tea. If you slip up, I’ll have your teeth knocked out and your cheeks pierced!” His cruelty, impatience, wilfulness and foolish infatuation were extraordinary in every way. Yet the moment lessons ended and he went indoors to see the girls, he became gentle and peaceable, intelligent and polished: quite another person. His father had beaten him almost to death several times for it, but nothing would change him. Whenever the pain of a beating grew unbearable, he would start crying out, “Sister! Little sister!”’

‘Later I heard the girls indoors teasing him. “Why do you keep calling for your sisters when the beating gets bad? Do you want us to plead for you and get you off? Aren’t you ashamed?” His answer was marvellous. “When the pain is terrible,” he said, “I thought that just saying ‘sister’ or ‘little sister’ might perhaps ease it. And when I tried it, I really did feel no pain. That was how I discovered the secret. Whenever the pain is at its worst, I call out for my sisters over and over.” Isn’t it ridiculous? His grandmother’s blind indulgence made matters worse. Time and again, for her grandson’s sake, she insulted his tutor and reproached her son. So I resigned and left. I am now teaching in the household of Lin, the Salt Inspector. You see, children of that sort will never preserve what their forefathers established or follow the guidance of their teachers and elders. The pity is that the girls in his family are so remarkable.’

‘The three girls now at the Jia house are good, too,’ said Zi-xing. ‘Mr Zheng’s eldest daughter, Yuan-chun, has been chosen for her goodness, filial devotion, talent and virtue to serve as a lady clerk in the palace.’

‘The second young lady, Ying-chun, is Mr She’s daughter by a concubine. The third, Tan-chun, is Mr Zheng’s daughter by a concubine. The fourth, Xi-chun, is Master Zhen’s full sister, from the Ning house. Old Lady Shi is so fond of the granddaughters that they all live with her and study together. I hear they are all excellent.’

‘One particularly good thing about the Zhens,’ said Yu-cun, ‘is that they choose their daughters’ names on the same pattern as the boys’. They do not set them apart with such pretty characters as “spring”, “red”, “fragrance” and “jade”, as other families do. Why should the Jias enjoy following that stale fashion?’

‘That is not quite it,’ said Zi-xing. ‘The eldest girl was born on the first day of the first month, so she was called Yuan-chun, “First Spring”, and the others merely followed her in using “spring”. The women of the previous generation were named on the same pattern as their brothers. There is a case in point: your present employer Mr Lin’s wife was the full sister of Messrs She and Zheng of the Rong house. Before she married she was called Jia Min.’

‘If you doubt it, make some inquiries when you go back.’

Yu-cun slapped the table and laughed. ‘So that is why! Whenever my pupil comes to the character min in her reading, she pronounces it mi instead. She does it every time. When she writes it, she leaves out a stroke or two. I had wondered about that. Now you tell me this, there can be no doubt of the reason. And no wonder the girl speaks and behaves so differently from other girls nowadays. I had thought her mother must have been an unusual woman to have such a daughter. Now that I know she is a granddaughter of the Rong house, there is nothing surprising in it. Sadly, her mother died only last month.’

Zi-xing sighed. ‘There were four sisters in that generation, and she was the youngest. Now she too is gone. Not one of the older generation’s sisters remains. We can only wait and see what husbands these younger girls will find.’

‘Quite so,’ said Yu-cun. ‘Now, you said Mr Zheng has the son born with jade in his mouth, and a young grandson left by his eldest son. Has Mr She no son at all?’

‘After Mr Zheng’s jade boy was born, a concubine bore him another son. I don’t know what sort he is. At any rate, he has two sons and a grandson living now; what will become of them remains to be seen. Mr She has two sons as well. The elder, Jia Lian, is about twenty. He strengthened an existing family tie by marrying the niece of Mr Zheng’s wife, Lady Wang: her brother’s daughter. They have been married two years. Master Lian has purchased the title of sub-prefect. He has no taste for books either, but he is quick and adaptable in worldly affairs and can talk well enough. So he lives at his uncle Mr Zheng’s house and helps manage the household business. But since he married, everyone from top to bottom has praised his wife, and Master Lian has fallen a bowshot behind. She is extremely good-looking, brisk and fluent in speech, and remarkably shrewd and calculating. You would not find her equal in ten thousand men.’

Yu-cun smiled. ‘Which shows I was not wrong.’

‘For all we know, these people we have been discussing may belong to that same class, endowed with both the upright and the evil forces.’

‘Evil or upright,’ said Zi-xing, ‘you’ve been so busy reckoning up someone else’s affairs, you ought to have a cup of wine.’

‘True,’ said Yu-cun. ‘Though in all this talking I have actually drunk a few cups too many.’

Zi-xing laughed. ‘Other people’s gossip is the perfect relish for wine. What harm in a few extra cups?’

Yu-cun looked out of the window. ‘It’s getting late. Mind they don’t shut the city gates on us. We might walk slowly back and go on talking.’

They rose and settled their bill. Just as they were about to leave, someone called from behind them: ‘Brother Yu-cun, congratulations! I have come especially to bring you good news.’

Yu-cun hurriedly turned to look. To learn who it was, listen to the next chapter.
