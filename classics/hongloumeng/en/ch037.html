Chapter 37 A Begonia Society takes shape by chance at Autumn Studio; Chrysanthemum themes are devised by night at Allspice Court

Chapter end

That year Jia Zheng was again appointed to a provincial educational commission, and chose the twentieth of the eighth month for his departure. On that day he paid his respects at the ancestral shrine and took leave of Grandmother Jia before setting out. Bao-yu and the other young men of the family accompanied him as far as the Pavilion of Parting Tears. Once Jia Zheng had left, there was much outside the household that we cannot record. We shall speak only of Bao-yu, wandering through the garden day after day as the whim took him, truly wasting his time and adding empty days to his years. One day, when he was at a loose end, Ebony came in with a sheet of floral notepaper for him.

‘Of course! I’d forgotten,’ Bao-yu said. ‘I was just saying I must go and see Third Sister, and here you are. Is she any better?’

‘She’s quite well now,’ said Ebony. ‘She isn’t taking medicine today. It was only a slight chill.’

Bao-yu unfolded the paper and read:

Your younger sister Tan respectfully submits this to her second brother’s writing table:

The other evening, when the rain had newly cleared and the moonlight shone as though washed clean, I could not bear to go to bed and lose so rare a prospect. Though the water-clock had marked the third watch, I still lingered by the paulownia railings. Unprepared for the assault of wind and dew, I fell ill, unfit even to gather firewood. Yesterday you honoured me with a visit and affectionate counsel, then sent your maid repeatedly to inquire after me, besides bestowing fresh lychees and a specimen of Zhen-qing’s calligraphy. How deep your sympathy, how generous your care!

Now, in the quiet hours spent leaning at my desk or resting on my bed, I have been thinking of the men of former times. Even amid the assaults and rivalries of fame and profit, they would set aside a little plot of hills and water, summon friends from afar and welcome those nearby, throw away their carriage-pins or cling to their shafts to detain them, intent on gathering two or three kindred spirits there. They would raise a platform for letters or found a society of poets. What began in a passing impulse became a delightful story for all time.

Though your sister has no talent, I too am fortunate enough to dwell among rocks and springs, and I admire the accomplishments of Xue and Lin. It is a pity that these wind-swept courts and moonlit pavilions have yet to entertain a gathering of poets. With apricot blossom before our curtains and peach blossom beside the stream, perhaps we too might send the wine-cup flying as we sing. Who says that the great talents of the Lotus Society must be confined to bearded men? Let the elegant gatherings of East Mountain yield a place to us who wear rouge and powder. If you will come rowing through the snow, your sister will sweep the fallen flowers to receive you. Respectfully submitted.1

Bao-yu clapped his hands in delight.

‘What a splendid idea of Third Sister’s! I’ll go and discuss it with her at once.’

He set off as he spoke, with Ebony following. They had just reached Drenched Blossoms Pavilion when the old woman on duty at the garden’s rear gate came towards them with a note in her hand. Seeing Bao-yu, she went up to him.

‘Master Yun sends his respects. He’s waiting at the back gate and asked me to bring you this.’

Bao-yu opened it and read:

Your unworthy son Yun respectfully wishes his honoured father every blessing and the best of health.

Ever since Heaven bestowed upon me the favour of being received as your son, I have thought day and night of some way to show my filial devotion, yet have found no opportunity. While buying flowers and plants recently, thanks to the good fortune you confer upon me, I became acquainted with many gardeners and came to know many celebrated gardens. By chance I saw a rare variety of white begonia. I tried every means, but could obtain only two pots. If you regard me as your own son, pray keep them for your enjoyment. The weather being hot, I fear that a visit might inconvenience the young ladies in the garden, and so I dare not present myself. I respectfully send this letter and bow to wish you well.

Written on his knees by your son Yun.

Bao-yu smiled when he had read it.

‘Has he come alone? Is anyone with him?’

‘There are two pots of flowers as well,’ the woman replied.

‘Go out and say I’ve read his note. It was thoughtful of him to remember me. Just have the flowers brought to my rooms.’

He went on with Ebony to Autumn Studio, where Bao-chai, Dai-yu, Ying-chun and Xi-chun were already gathered.

‘Here’s another!’ they all said, smiling as he entered.

‘At least I’m not entirely commonplace,’ Tan-chun said with a laugh. ‘The idea happened to occur to me, so I wrote a few notes to see what would happen. Who would have thought a single summons would bring everyone?’

‘It’s a pity we’ve left it so late,’ Bao-yu said. ‘We ought to have had a society long ago.’

‘Form as many societies as you please,’ said Dai-yu, ‘but don’t count me in. I wouldn’t dare.’

‘If you wouldn’t dare, who would?’ Ying-chun laughed.

‘This is a serious undertaking,’ Bao-yu said. ‘We must all put our hearts into it, instead of each insisting on giving way to the next.

Whatever ideas you have, say them, and we’ll discuss them together. Cousin Bao-chai, you suggest something. Cousin Dai-yu, let’s hear from you too.’

‘What’s the hurry?’ said Bao-chai. ‘We aren’t all here yet.’

Before she had finished, Li Wan came in, smiling.

‘How elegant! If you’re founding a poetry society, I nominate myself to preside. I had the very same idea back in the spring, but then I thought: I can’t write poetry, so why should I meddle? I forgot about it and never said anything. Since Third Sister is so keen, I’ll help her set it going.’

‘If we really are to have a poetry society,’ Dai-yu said, ‘we’re all venerable poets now. We must first get rid of these “sister” and “brother-in-law” and “sister-in-law” names. They’re far too commonplace.’

‘Exactly,’ said Li Wan. ‘Why don’t we each choose a literary name? It would be much more elegant to address one another that way. I’ve decided on the Old Farmer of Sweet-rice Village. No one else may have it.’

‘I’ll be the Recluse of Autumn Studio,’ Tan-chun said, laughing.

‘Recluse, master of this or that: they aren’t really suitable, and they’re cumbersome,’ Bao-yu said. ‘There are plenty of paulownias and plantains here. Why not name yourself after one of those?’

‘I have it!’ Tan-chun laughed. ‘I’m fondest of the plantains. I’ll call myself the Guest beneath the Plantain.’

Everyone found this pleasingly original.

‘Quick, take her away and stew her flesh to eat with our wine,’ Dai-yu said with a laugh.

No one understood.

‘There’s an old story of “a deer covered with plantain leaves”,’ she explained. ‘If she calls herself the Guest beneath the Plantain, isn’t she a deer? Hurry up and make us some dried venison.’

They all burst out laughing.

‘Don’t be so quick with your clever insults,’ Tan-chun laughed back. ‘I’ve already thought of a lovely name that suits you perfectly.’

She turned to the others.

‘In ancient times E-huang and Nü-ying wept on the bamboos and stained them with their tears. That’s why the spotted kind is also called the bamboo of the Xiang Princesses. Now she lives at Naiad’s House, and she’s so fond of crying that, one day, when she’s pining for our brother-in-law Lin, all her bamboos will be spotted too.

From now on we need only call her the Naiad.’

They all clapped and applauded the name. Lin Dai-yu lowered her head and said nothing.

‘I thought of a good one for our eldest Miss Xue long ago,’ Li Wan said, smiling. ‘It has just three characters too.’

Xi-chun and Ying-chun both asked what it was.

‘I’m conferring on her the title of Lady of Allspice. What do you think?’

‘An excellent title,’ said Tan-chun with a laugh.

‘What about me?’ asked Bao-yu. ‘You must think of one for me too.’

‘You have one already,’ Bao-chai said, smiling. ‘Busy over Nothing. Those three characters suit you perfectly.’

‘You can keep your old name, King of Flowers of the Crimson Grotto,’ said Li Wan.

Bao-yu laughed.

‘That was something I did when I was little. Why bring it up now?’

‘You have more than enough names,’ said Tan-chun. ‘Why do you need another? Whatever we choose to call you, just answer to it.’

‘I’d better give you one after all,’ Bao-chai said. ‘It’s the most commonplace name imaginable, but it suits you very well. Wealth and honour are hard enough to come by, and so is leisure. You can hardly ever have both, yet you’ve managed it. We might as well call you the Gentleman of Wealth and Leisure.’

‘I’m not worthy of it, not worthy of it,’ Bao-yu laughed. ‘Call me whatever you like.’

‘What names shall we give Second Sister and Fourth Sister?’ Li Wan asked.

‘We can hardly write poetry,’ said Ying-chun. ‘What would we want names for?’

‘Even so, you ought to have them,’ said Tan-chun.

‘She lives at Amaryllis Eyot, so let her be Amaryllis,’ Bao-chai said. ‘Fourth Sister is at Lotus Pavilion. We can simply call her Lotus Pavilion.’

‘That will do very well,’ said Li Wan. ‘But I am the eldest here, so you must all do as I suggest. I guarantee you’ll like it. There are seven of us founding this society, and Second Sister, Fourth Sister and I can’t write poetry. You must excuse the three of us and give us each some other duty.’

‘We’ve chosen names, and you’re still calling us the old ones!’ Tan-chun laughed. ‘We might as well never have bothered. We ought to make a rule fining anyone who gets it wrong.’

‘Let’s establish the society before we settle the fines,’ said Li Wan. ‘I have plenty of room. We’ll meet at my place. Though I can’t write poetry, if all these poets will tolerate an unlettered visitor, I’ll be your hostess, and some of their elegance will rub off on me.

If you elect me president, I naturally can’t manage by myself. I shall need two vice-presidents, and will invite the two learned scholars Amaryllis and Lotus Pavilion: one to set the subjects and rhymes, the other to make fair copies and oversee the proceedings. Nor must it be an absolute rule that the three of us never write anything. If the subject and rhymes happen to be easy, we can each try a poem. But you four must be obliged to write. On those terms we can begin. If you won’t agree, I wouldn’t dare to follow in such illustrious company.’

Ying-chun and Xi-chun were not naturally inclined to poetry, and with Bao-chai and Dai-yu there to outshine them, this proposal suited them perfectly. Both declared it excellent. Tan-chun and the others understood their feelings, and, seeing how pleased they were, could scarcely press them. They had to agree.

‘Very well, then,’ Tan-chun said with a laugh. ‘But I can’t help finding it funny. Here I’ve thought of this perfectly good idea, only to put the three of you in charge of me!’

‘In that case, let’s go to Sweet-rice Village,’ Bao-yu said.

‘Always in a hurry,’ said Li Wan. ‘Today we’re only discussing it. Wait till I invite you.’

‘We ought to decide how often to meet,’ Bao-chai said.

‘Too often, and it would stop being fun,’ said Tan-chun.

‘Two or three times a month would be enough.’

Bao-chai nodded.

‘Twice a month will do. We should fix the dates and meet whatever the weather. Besides those two days, if anyone feels inspired to hold an extra meeting, we can go to their place or they can bring it here. That would give us plenty of freedom and make it more enjoyable.’

Everyone thought this an even better suggestion.

‘Only, since the idea was mine,’ said Tan-chun, ‘I must be hostess the first time. Otherwise all my enthusiasm will have come to nothing.’

‘Then why not hold the first meeting tomorrow?’ Li Wan asked.

‘Today is better than tomorrow. This very moment will do perfectly. You set the subject, Amaryllis can choose the rhymes, and Lotus Pavilion can supervise.’

‘If you ask me,’ said Ying-chun, ‘we needn’t leave the subject and rhymes to any one person. Drawing lots would be fairer.’

‘As I was coming over,’ Li Wan said, ‘I saw them carrying in two pots of white begonias. They were lovely flowers. Why not write about those?’

‘We haven’t even admired them yet, and we’re to write poems already?’ said Ying-chun.

‘They’re only white begonias,’ said Bao-chai. ‘Why must we see them before we write? The poems and rhyme-prose of the ancients were simply ways of giving expression to their thoughts and feelings. If they had all waited to see things for themselves, we wouldn’t have so many poems now.’

‘In that case, let me choose the rhymes,’ said Ying-chun.

She went to the bookcase, took down a volume of poetry and opened it at random. The poem she found happened to be a regulated poem of eight seven-character lines. She passed it to the others to see: they would all have to write in that form. Closing the book, she said to a little maid:

‘Say any word that comes into your head.’

The girl was leaning against the door and said, ‘Door.’

‘Then it will be mén, “door”,’ Ying-chun laughed. ‘The thirteenth rhyme group, yuan. And the first rhyme-word must be mén.’

She called for the box of rhyme-tokens, pulled out the drawer for the thirteenth group and told the same little maid to pick four tokens at random. The girl took out pén, “pot”; hún, “soul”; hén, “trace”; and hūn, “dusk”.2

‘Pot and door will be rather difficult,’ Bao-yu said.

Scribe laid out four identical sets of paper and brushes, and they all fell silent to think. Dai-yu alone stroked the paulownia, gazed at the autumn scene, or laughed and joked with the maids. Ying-chun told a maid to light a stick of Sweet-dream incense. These sticks were only about three cun long and as thin as a rush wick; they burnt quickly and so provided the time limit. Anyone who had not finished when the incense burnt out would be fined.

Tan-chun was the first to have her poem ready. She wrote it down herself, made some alterations, and handed it to Ying-chun.

‘Have you got yours, Lady of Allspice?’ she asked Bao-chai.

‘I have, but it isn’t good.’

Bao-yu paced up and down the covered walk with his hands behind his back.

‘Listen, they’ve both finished,’ he said to Dai-yu.

‘Never mind me.’

Seeing that Bao-chai had already made her fair copy, Bao-yu cried:

‘This is terrible! There’s only one cun of incense left, and I’ve only got four lines.’

He turned to Dai-yu again.

‘The incense is almost finished. Why do you keep crouching on that damp ground?’

She paid him no attention.

‘I can’t worry about you now,’ he said. ‘Good or bad, I’d better write mine out.’

He went to the table and began.

‘We’re going to read the poems now,’ Li Wan said. ‘Anyone who hasn’t handed in their poem by the time we finish will certainly be fined.’

‘The Old Farmer of Sweet-rice Village may not be good at writing, but she’s a fine reader and the fairest judge of all,’ said Bao-yu. ‘You must decide which are best. We’ll all accept your judgement.’

‘Of course,’ the others agreed.

They looked first at Tan-chun’s manuscript, which read:

On White Begonias With the prescribed rhyme-words: door, pot, soul, trace, dusk

Slant sunlight and cold grass surround the layered gates; After the rain, green moss covers the pot.

Its spirit is jade, yet purer than jade can be; Its flesh and bones are snow, enough to steal the soul.

A fragrant heart, so small, so tender and so frail; At the third watch, moonlight traces its lovely form.

Do not say this white-robed immortal will take wing: Full of affection, it shares my songs at dusk.

Next they read Bao-chai’s:

I prize its fragrant beauty, and close the door by day;

My own hands bring a pitcher to water the mossy pot.

Rouge washed away, a form stands on the autumn steps; Ice and snow summon a soul to the dew-wet stones.

Only at its palest does its richer beauty show;

How could jade bear such sorrow and show no trace?

To repay the White Emperor, it offers purity alone;

Silent, graceful, it stands as the day sinks into dusk.

‘Just what one would expect of the Lady of Allspice,’ Li Wan said with a smile.

Then they looked at Bao-yu’s:

Pale autumn beauty shines through gate on gate; At seven stem-joints, clustered blooms fill the pot with snow.

Tai-zhen steps from her bath, her form all ice; Xi Shi clasps her heart, with jade for a soul.

The dawn wind cannot scatter a thousand points of grief; The night’s rain adds another lingering trace of tears.

Leaning alone on painted rails, it seems to brood; Cloth-beating rings out clear; sad flutes attend the dusk.

After everyone had read it, Bao-yu praised Tan-chun’s poem. Li Wan was just about to commend the distinction of Bao-chai’s when she turned to hurry Dai-yu.

‘You’ve all finished, then,’ Dai-yu said.

She took up her brush, wrote the whole poem at a stroke and tossed it to the others. Li Wan and the rest read:

The Xiang-bamboo blind half raised, the door half closed;

Ice crushed to make the earth, and jade to make the pot.

At this line Bao-yu was the first to applaud.

‘Where could she have thought of that?’ was all he could say.

They read on:

Three parts of whiteness stolen from the pear-blossom; One slender wisp of soul borrowed from the plum.

Now the others too could not help exclaiming in admiration.

‘She really does have a mind unlike anyone else’s.’

They went on to read:

In her moon-cave the immortal sews her white silk sleeves; In an autumn chamber a grieving girl wipes away the traces of her tears.

To whom can she confide her silent, bashful tenderness? Weary, she leans into the west wind; night has darkened.

When they had finished, they all declared this the best.

‘For grace and originality, certainly,’ said Li Wan. ‘But for depth and restraint it must still yield to Allspice’s.’

‘That’s a fair judgement,’ said Tan-chun. ‘The Naiad should take second place.’

‘The Young Master of Green Delights comes last,’ said Li Wan. ‘Do you accept that?’

‘Mine wasn’t good to begin with,’ Bao-yu answered. ‘It’s the fairest possible judgement.’

Then he smiled.

‘Only, Allspice’s and the Naiad’s might bear a little more consideration.’

‘You asked me to judge, and it’s no business of yours,’ said Li Wan. ‘One more word from anyone, and there’ll be a fine.’

Bao-yu had to give in.

‘From now on,’ Li Wan continued, ‘I fix the second and sixteenth of every month as our meeting days. The subjects and rhymes will be for me to decide. In between, if you feel like it, choose other days and hold extra meetings. I won’t interfere, even if you meet every day of the month.

Only, on the second and sixteenth you must come to me.’

‘But we must give the society a name,’ Bao-yu said.

‘A commonplace one won’t do,’ said Tan-chun, ‘and neither will something so original that it’s odd and far-fetched. Since we happen to have begun with poems on begonias, let’s call it the Begonia Society. It may be commonplace, but as there really was such an occasion, that needn’t matter.’

They discussed it a little longer, took some wine and fruit, then dispersed, some to their own rooms, others to see Grandmother Jia or Lady Wang. We have nothing further to relate of them just now.

Aroma had seen Bao-yu hurry off with Ebony after reading the note, without knowing what it was about. Later she saw the women from the rear gate bring in two pots of begonias. She asked where they came from, and the old woman told her what had happened with Bao-yu. Aroma had the pots set in place and invited the women to sit in the servants’ room. She went to her own room, weighed out six qian of silver and wrapped it up. Then she brought this, together with three hundred cash, to the two women.

‘The silver is for the lads who carried the flowers. Buy yourselves some wine with the cash.’

The women got up, beaming, with a thousand thanks and protests that they could not possibly take it. Only when Aroma insisted and refused to take it back did they accept.

‘Are there any lads on duty outside the back gate?’ Aroma asked.

‘There are four every day,’ they hastened to reply, ‘kept there to run errands for the inner rooms. What would you like done, miss? We’ll tell them.’

‘What errand would I have?’ Aroma said with a smile. ‘Master Bao wants to send someone to the young marquis’s house today with some things for Miss Shi. Since you happen to be here, tell the lads at the back gate to hire a carriage as you go out. Come here for the money afterwards. Don’t let them go blundering about at the front.’

The women agreed and went off.

Aroma returned to the room to get a dish for the things she was sending Shi Xiang-yun, but found its slot on the shelves empty. Turning, she saw Skybright, Ripple, Musk and the others sitting together at their needlework.

‘Where has that banded white agate dish gone?’ she asked.

They looked at one another, but none could remember. After a while Skybright laughed.

‘It went to Miss Tan-chun with the lychees. It hasn’t come back yet.’

‘We have plenty of ordinary dishes for sending things,’ said Aroma. ‘Why did it have to be that one?’

‘I said exactly the same thing,’ Skybright answered. ‘He said the fresh lychees would look prettier on it. When I took them over, Miss Tan-chun thought so too, and asked us to leave the dish with them. That’s why I didn’t bring it back. Look, those two “linked-pearl” vases from the very top shelf haven’t come back either.’

‘Talking of the vases reminds me of something funny,’ Ripple said, laughing. ‘When Master Bao has a fit of filial devotion, he gives twice the proper measure. The other day he saw the osmanthus in the garden and cut two sprays, meaning to put them in a vase for himself. Then it suddenly struck him that these were fresh flowers, just opened in our own garden, and he couldn’t presume to enjoy them first. He insisted on taking that pair of vases down, filled them with water and arranged the flowers himself, then had someone carry them while he went in person to present one to Her Old Ladyship and one to Her Ladyship.3

His fit of filial devotion brought good fortune even to the person who went with him. It happened to be my turn to carry them that day. Her Old Ladyship was so delighted she didn’t know what to do. She told everyone she saw, “It’s Bao-yu who really thinks of me. He remembers me even over a spray of flowers. And people complain that I’m too fond of him!” You know she hardly ever speaks to me ordinarily. I don’t seem to take her fancy. But that day she actually told someone to give me a few hundred cash. She said I was a poor little thing, so slight and delicate. A piece of good fortune I’d never have dreamt of! A few hundred cash is nothing; being singled out like that is another matter. When we got to Her Ladyship’s, she was going through her boxes with Mrs Lian, Aunt Zhao, Aunt Zhou and quite a number of others. They were looking for the brightly coloured clothes she used to wear when she was young. I didn’t know who they were for.

As soon as she saw the flowers, she stopped looking for clothes and came to admire them. And with Mrs Lian there to join in, praising Master Bao’s devotion and how well he knew what was proper, there were two cartloads of compliments, true or not. Before all those people, Her Ladyship felt it reflected credit on her and silenced every critic. She was more pleased than ever, and gave me two of the garments that were ready to hand. The clothes themselves were nothing: we get clothes every year anyway. But it isn’t every day you get such a lucky chance.’

‘Pah! You little fool, you’ve never seen anything!’ Skybright said with a laugh. ‘The best were given to someone else. You only got the leftovers, and you think it was an honour!’

‘Whoever the good ones went to, it was still a kindness from Her Ladyship,’ said Ripple.

‘I wouldn’t have taken them,’ Skybright said. ‘If they’d been left over from someone elsewhere, well and good. But when we all belong to this room, is one of us any better than another? Give her the good ones and me the leavings? I’d rather have nothing. Even if I offended Her Ladyship, I wouldn’t swallow an insult like that.’

‘Who here got them?’ Ripple asked quickly.

‘I was ill for a few days and went home, so I don’t know. Be a good sister and tell me.’

‘If I told you, would you take yours back to Her Ladyship now?’ Skybright asked.

‘Don’t be silly,’ Ripple laughed. ‘I only want to hear, for the fun of it. Even if they were left over from a dog in this room, I’d be grateful to Her Ladyship. The rest is no concern of mine.’

The others all laughed.

‘What a neat way of abusing her! They did go to that spotted foreign lapdog.’

‘You foul-mouthed creatures,’ Aroma said, laughing. ‘The moment you have nothing to do, you make me the butt of your jokes. Heaven knows what deaths you’ll all die!’

‘So it was you, Sister!’ said Ripple. ‘I truly didn’t know. Let me beg your pardon.’

‘Stop your nonsense,’ Aroma said with a smile. ‘What matters is that one of you should fetch the dish.’

‘We ought to fetch the vases when there’s a moment, too,’ said Musk. ‘The one at Her Old Ladyship’s is all right, but there are too many people handling things in Her Ladyship’s rooms.

The others might leave it alone, but if Aunt Zhao’s lot see something from here, they’ll never rest till they’ve spitefully damaged it. Her Ladyship doesn’t pay much attention to such things. Better bring it back soon.’

Skybright threw down her needlework.

‘You’re right about that. I’ll go and fetch it.’

‘Let me get it,’ said Ripple. ‘You go for your dish.’

‘No, I insist on going,’ Skybright laughed. ‘You’ve had all the lucky chances. Can’t I have one for once?’

‘All that’s happened is that Ripple got some clothes once,’ Musk said with a laugh. ‘Why should they happen to be sorting clothes again today, just when you turn up?’

Skybright gave a cold laugh.

‘Even if I don’t find them sorting clothes, Her Ladyship may notice how conscientious I am and spare two taels a month for me out of her allowance. Who knows?’

Then she laughed again.

‘Don’t play your little tricks with me. There’s nothing I don’t know about.’

And out she ran.

Ripple went out with her and made her own way to Tan-chun’s, where she collected the dish. Aroma had the gifts ready. She called an elderly woman who served in their quarters, old Nurse Song.

‘Go and wash and comb your hair properly, then come back in your outdoor clothes. I’m sending you to Miss Shi with some things.’

‘Just give them to me and tell me what to say, miss,’ the old woman replied. ‘Then I can get ready and go straight off.’

Aroma brought over two small filigree boxes. She opened the first, which held two kinds of fresh produce, red water-caltrops and gorgon fruit. In the other was a dish of cakes made from the new chestnut flour and steamed with osmanthus sugar. She went on:

‘Say: “These are all from this year’s crop in our own garden. Master Bao sends them for you to try. The other day you admired this agate dish, so please keep it for your enjoyment. In the silk bundle is the needlework you asked me to do last time. Please excuse its roughness and make do with it.”

Give her our respects and Master Bao’s good wishes. That’s all.’

‘Might Master Bao have anything else to say?’ Nurse Song asked. ‘You’d better ask him, miss, or we may find we’ve forgotten something afterwards.’

‘Did you see him at Miss Tan-chun’s just now?’ Aroma asked Ripple.

‘They’re all there talking about founding some poetry society, and writing poems,’ Ripple answered. ‘I don’t suppose he has any message. You can go.’

The old woman took the things out and went to change. Aroma reminded her:

‘Go by the back gate. There’ll be a lad and a carriage waiting.’

Nurse Song left, and we need say no more of her for the moment.

When Bao-yu returned, he first hurried to admire the begonias, then went into his room and told Aroma about the poetry society. Aroma in turn told him that she had sent Nurse Song to Shi Xiang-yun with the gifts. Bao-yu clapped his hands.

‘We forgot her! I knew there was something on my mind, but I couldn’t think what. Thank goodness you reminded me. I must invite her. What pleasure would there be in a poetry society without her?’

‘It isn’t anything urgent, only an amusement,’ Aroma urged. ‘She hasn’t your freedom, and she can’t decide things for herself at home. If you tell her, she’ll want to come and may not be allowed. If she can’t, she’ll keep fretting about it. You’ll only make her unhappy for nothing.’

‘That doesn’t matter,’ said Bao-yu. ‘I’ll ask Grandmother to send for her.’

As they were speaking, Nurse Song returned. She delivered Xiang-yun’s thanks and her message to Aroma for all the trouble she had taken, then added:

‘She asked what Master Bao was doing. I said you and the young ladies were founding some poetry society and writing poems.

Miss Shi said you were writing poems without even telling her to come. She was terribly impatient.’

Bao-yu got up at once and went to Grandmother Jia’s, insisting that someone must be sent for Xiang-yun immediately.

‘It’s late today,’ Grandmother Jia told him. ‘We’ll send first thing tomorrow.’

Bao-yu could only give up and return in low spirits.

Early next morning he went back to Grandmother Jia’s to urge her to send for Xiang-yun. It was not until the afternoon that she arrived, and only then did he feel at ease. As soon as they met he told her the whole story and was about to show her the poems, but Li Wan and the others stopped him.

‘Don’t show her the poems yet. Tell her the rhymes first. Since she’s late, her first penalty must be to write a poem matching them. If it’s good, we’ll admit her to the society. If it isn’t, she must pay a further penalty by entertaining us before we consider it.’

‘You forgot to invite me,’ said Shi Xiang-yun. ‘I ought to be fining you! Give me the rhymes. I may have no talent, but I shall have to do my best and make a fool of myself. If you’ll let me join, I’ll even sweep the floor and burn the incense.’

Her good humour delighted them more than ever. They all wondered reproachfully how they could have forgotten her the day before, and hurried to tell her the rhymes. Xiang-yun was too eager to pause over polishing and revising. She went on talking to everyone, but in her mind the answering poems were already complete. She wrote them out with whatever brush and paper came to hand, then said with a smile:

‘I’ve written two to match your rhymes. I don’t know whether they’re good or bad. I’ve only done as I was told.’

She handed them over.

‘We thought we’d exhausted everything in our four,’ the others said. ‘We couldn’t have written another, and you’ve produced two! How can there be so much to say? You’re bound to have repeated us.’

As they spoke, they began to read. The two poems ran:

I

Yesterday an immortal descended at the capital’s gates;

A pot was planted full of jade from Lantian.

The Frost Maiden, by nature, is partial to the cold; No tale of Qian-niang, yet a soul slips free.4

From what far land does autumn’s shade bring forth this snow?

Rain leaves upon it traces of the night just past.

Happily the poets never tire of singing: How could they leave it lonely, morning through dusk?

II

Sweet herbs along the steps lead to a vine-hung gate; A corner by the wall suits it as well as a pot.

So fond of purity, the flower can find no mate; So prone to autumn grief, the heart soon loses its soul.

A jade-white candle drips its wind-borne tears away; The crystal blind breaks up its moonlit image.

To Chang-e I would speak my hidden feelings, But the empty corridor lies dark in the night.

They marvelled as they read, praising each successive line.

‘Now those really do begonia poems justice,’ they all said. ‘We truly deserve to have a Begonia Society.’

‘Will you fine me by making me hostess tomorrow?’ Shi Xiang-yun asked. ‘May I be the first to invite an extra meeting?’

‘Better still!’ they replied.

They then showed her the previous day’s poems and discussed them together. In the evening Bao-chai invited Xiang-yun to spend the night at Allspice Court. By lamplight Xiang-yun planned how to entertain the society and choose its themes.

Bao-chai listened to her for a long time, but none of her plans would do.

‘If you call a meeting, you have to be the hostess,’ she told her. ‘It may only be an amusement, but you must think it through. It has to be manageable for you and give no offence to anyone, or there’ll be no pleasure in it. You can’t decide things for yourself at home. You only have those few strings of cash each month, and they scarcely cover your own expenses. If you start spending on something as unnecessary as this and your aunt hears of it, she’ll have still more to complain of. Besides, even if you spent it all, it wouldn’t pay for entertaining us.

You can hardly go home and ask for more on that account. Or were you going to ask for money here?’

At these words Xiang-yun saw the difficulty and began to hesitate.

‘I’ve already thought of a way,’ Bao-chai went on. ‘There’s a man at our pawnshop whose family’s fields produce exceptionally fine, fat crabs. He sent us several catties the other day. More than half the people here, from Grandmother down to those in the garden, are fond of crabs. Aunt was saying just the other day that she meant to invite Grandmother into the garden to admire the osmanthus and eat crabs, but she’s had other things to do and hasn’t invited her yet. For now, say nothing about the poetry society. Simply invite everyone. When they’ve gone, we can write as much poetry as we please. I’ll speak to my brother and ask for several baskets of the biggest, fattest crabs, fetch a few jars of good wine from the shop, and provide dishes of fruit for four or five tables. That would save trouble and make a lively party for everyone.’

Xiang-yun was deeply grateful and impressed. She warmly praised Bao-chai for thinking of everything.

‘I’m speaking entirely for your sake,’ Bao-chai added with a smile. ‘You mustn’t take offence and think I’m looking down on you, or all our affection will have been wasted. If you won’t take it amiss, I can tell them to make the arrangements.’

‘Dear cousin, now you’re the one taking things the wrong way!’ Xiang-yun laughed. ‘However foolish I may be, I’d scarcely be human if I couldn’t tell kindness from unkindness. If I didn’t look on you as my own elder sister, I wouldn’t have told you all those private troubles at home last time.’

Bao-chai called an old woman servant.

‘Go out and tell my brother to send several baskets of big crabs like the ones we had the other day. We’re inviting Her Old Ladyship and my aunt to admire the osmanthus tomorrow after the meal.

Tell him he must be sure not to forget. I’ve already given the invitations today.’

The old woman went out to deliver the message and returned. There is nothing further to say of her.

Bao-chai turned again to Xiang-yun.

‘The themes shouldn’t be too ingeniously novel, either. Think of those odd, far-fetched subjects in the old poets, and those impossibly difficult rhymes. If the subject is over-elaborate and the rhymes too hard, there can never be a good poem. It only shows a petty spirit. Certainly poetry must avoid hackneyed phrases, but it mustn’t strain too hard after the unfamiliar. The first thing is a fresh conception; the words will then be fresh of themselves. After all, none of this amounts to very much. Spinning and needlework are our proper occupations. In an idle moment, reading a few chapters of something truly useful to us would be a better way to spend our time.’

Xiang-yun merely assented. Then she smiled.

‘I was thinking: yesterday they wrote about begonias. What if we write about chrysanthemums now?’

‘Chrysanthemums suit the season,’ said Bao-chai, ‘but so many people have written about them already.’

‘That’s what I thought. I’m afraid of being trite.’

Bao-chai considered.

‘I have it. Let the chrysanthemums be the guests and the human beings the hosts. We’ll devise several titles, each of two characters: one for the thing itself and one of broader application. The concrete word will be ju, “chrysanthemum”; the other we’ll take from the general-purpose category.

That way we shall both celebrate the chrysanthemum and describe what people do. No one has done it before, so we can’t be trite. Each subject will embrace both scene and object, and be fresh without being petty.’

‘That’s very good,’ said Xiang-yun, smiling. ‘But what sort of broader words should we use? Think of one to show me.’

Bao-chai thought for a moment and smiled.

‘Chrysanthemum Dreams would do.’

‘How good!’ Xiang-yun laughed. ‘I have one too. Would Chrysanthemum Shadows do?’

‘Well enough, though people have written that one before. If we have a number of subjects, it can go in among them. I’ve thought of another.’

‘Tell me quickly.’

‘What about Questioning Chrysanthemums?’

Xiang-yun slapped the table in delight.

‘I’ve got another too. What about Seeking Chrysanthemums?’

Bao-chai found that interesting as well.

‘We might as well think of ten. Let’s write them down and go on.’

They ground ink and dipped a brush. Xiang-yun wrote while Bao-chai dictated, and before long they had made up ten.

Xiang-yun read through them and smiled.

‘Ten don’t quite make a set. Let’s make it twelve and have it complete, like an album of paintings and calligraphy.’

Bao-chai thought of two more, bringing the number to twelve.

‘Now that we’ve gone this far,’ she said, ‘we might as well put them in order.’

‘Better still,’ said Xiang-yun. ‘We shall have a whole chrysanthemum album.’

‘First comes Remembering Chrysanthemums,’ Bao-chai began. ‘Since remembering them doesn’t bring them to us, we seek them: Seeking Chrysanthemums comes second. Once found, they are planted: Planting Chrysanthemums is third. When they flower abundantly, we sit before them and admire them: Facing Chrysanthemums is fourth. Still not content with sitting before them, we cut some and set them in a vase to enjoy: Chrysanthemums in a Vase comes fifth. But with the flowers in their vase and no poem about them, even they seem to lack colour. So the sixth is Singing of Chrysanthemums. Once they have entered verse, we must let brush and ink do their part as well: Painting Chrysanthemums is seventh. After all this busy devotion, we still don’t know exactly what is so wonderful about them, and cannot help asking: Questioning Chrysanthemums is eighth. If the chrysanthemums could answer, we should be beside ourselves with delight: Wearing Chrysanthemums is ninth. That exhausts what people do, but not what we can write about the flowers. Chrysanthemum Shadows and Chrysanthemum Dreams follow as the tenth and eleventh. The last leaf will be Fading Chrysanthemums, gathering up all the splendour of the earlier themes.

There we have the lovely sights and occupations of all three autumn months.’

Xiang-yun copied the subjects in the order Bao-chai had given, read them through once more, and asked:

‘What rhymes shall we prescribe?’

‘I’ve never liked prescribing rhymes,’ Bao-chai replied. ‘If there’s a good poem to be written, why bind it hand and foot with a rhyme? Let’s not copy that petty school. We’ll set the subjects and leave the rhymes free. The whole point is to enjoy the good lines that may occur to us, not to make things difficult for anyone.’

‘Quite right,’ said Xiang-yun. ‘Everyone’s poetry will be the better for it. But there are only five of us. Surely we aren’t each to write twelve poems on these twelve subjects?’

‘That would be asking far too much,’ Bao-chai said.

‘Make a fair copy of the titles and put them on the wall tomorrow. All the poems must be regulated verse of eight seven-character lines. Once they’ve seen the list, everyone can choose whichever subject they like. Anyone who feels equal to all twelve may write them all; anyone who can’t manage even one needn’t write one. Let talent and speed take the honours. Once all twelve subjects have been covered, no one may come along afterwards with another poem. We’ll simply fine the latecomer.’

‘That will do,’ said Xiang-yun.

With their plans settled, the two put out the lamp and went to bed. To learn what happened, listen to the next chapter.
