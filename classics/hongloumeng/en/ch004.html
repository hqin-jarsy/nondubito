Chapter 4 An ill-fated girl meets an ill-fated young man; The Gourd Temple monk muddles a muddled case

Chapter end

Now Dai-yu and the other girls went to Lady Wang’s rooms, where they found her discussing household business with a messenger from her brother and his wife. There was also talk of a homicide case involving the girls’ aunt’s family. Seeing how much Lady Wang had to attend to, they withdrew and went instead to the rooms of their widowed sister-in-law, Mrs Li.

This Mrs Li was Jia Zhu’s wife. Though Zhu had died young, he had fortunately left a son, Jia Lan, now just five years old and already attending school. Mrs Li too came from a distinguished official family of Jinling. Her father, Li Shou-zhong, had been Chancellor of the Imperial Academy, and there was not a man or woman in the clan who did not study books and recite poetry. Once Li Shou-zhong became head of the family, however, he maintained that ‘A woman’s virtue lies in having no accomplishments.’ When his daughter was born, therefore, he did not have her taught very much. Three or four works, such as the Four Books for Women, the Lives of Exemplary Women and the Collected Lives of Worthy Ladies, would suffice: she need only learn to recognise a few characters and remember a few virtuous women of earlier dynasties. Spinning and weaving, drawing water and pounding grain were what mattered. Accordingly, he named her Li Wan and gave her the courtesy name Gong-cai.

Thus Li Wan, though widowed in the bloom of youth and living amid fine food and silks, was like dead wood and cold ashes. She took notice of nothing, heard nothing, and knew only the service of her elders and the upbringing of her son. Beyond these duties, she kept her young sisters-in-law company at their needlework and reading. Though Dai-yu was a guest beneath another’s roof, she now had these girls with her every day. Apart from her elderly father, there was nothing more she need trouble about.

Let us turn for the moment to Yu-cun. He had been appointed to fill the vacant prefecture of Yingtian, and scarcely had he taken up his post when a report of a homicide came before him. Two families had tried to buy the same maid; neither would give way, and the quarrel had ended in a fatal beating. Yu-cun summoned the complainant for examination.

‘The man they beat to death was my master,’ the man said. ‘He bought a girl one day, never knowing she had been kidnapped and was being sold by her kidnapper. The fellow had already taken our money. Our young master said that the third day from then was an auspicious day, and he would wait till then to bring her home. But the kidnapper secretly sold her a second time, to the Xues. We found out and went to seize the seller and recover the girl. The Xues are one of the tyrants of Jinling. They used their power to intimidate us, and a gang of their brutal servants beat my young master to death. Both the murderer and his servants have fled without a trace. Only a few people who were not involved are left. I have been lodging complaints for a year, and no one will see justice done. I beg Your Honour to arrest the killers, root out these evil men and protect the widowed and the orphaned. The dead man’s gratitude for your boundless kindness would know no end!’

Yu-cun flew into a rage.

‘What damned nonsense is this? Beat a man to death and simply walk away, never to be caught?’

He was about to issue a warrant ordering his runners to arrest members of the murderer’s clan at once and question them under torture until they revealed where he was hiding. A general warrant for his arrest would also be circulated. Just as he was going to issue the order, however, an usher standing beside the tribunal desk caught his eye and signalled to him not to do so. Greatly puzzled, Yu-cun stayed his hand. He dismissed the court and retired to a private room, where he sent all his attendants away except the usher.

The man hurried forward to pay his respects.

‘Your Honour has been rising in rank and growing richer all this time,’ he said with a smile. ‘Have eight or nine years been enough to make you forget me?’

‘Your face is certainly very familiar. I simply cannot place you for the moment.’

The usher smiled. ‘Great men do have short memories, sir! Have you forgotten the very place you started from? Can you not remember the Gourd Temple?’

Yu-cun started as though struck by thunder. Only then did it come back to him. This usher had been a little novice in the Gourd Temple. After the fire he had had nowhere to live. He had thought of entering another temple to continue his religious life, but could not bear its bleak austerity. An usher’s work, he thought, would be lighter and offer more company and bustle. So, while still young enough, he had grown his hair again and taken up the occupation.

Yu-cun had never dreamed it could be he. He hastily took him by the hand and smiled.

‘An old friend, then!’

He invited him to sit down so that they could talk. The usher dared not accept.

‘One must never forget the friends of one’s poor days,’ Yu-cun said, smiling. ‘You and I are old friends. Besides, these are my private rooms. If we are to have a long talk, you cannot possibly remain standing.’

Only then did the usher thank him for the seat and perch sideways on its edge.

Yu-cun asked why he had signalled to stop the warrant.

‘Now that Your Honour has received this distinguished appointment in our province,’ the usher said, ‘surely you have had a copy made of the provincial “Charm for Protecting Officials”?’

‘What is this “Charm for Protecting Officials”?’ Yu-cun asked at once. ‘I know nothing of it.’

‘That will never do!’ said the usher.

‘How can you expect to last in office if you do not even know that? Every local official nowadays keeps a private list of the great gentry families in his province, the richest, noblest and most powerful of them all. It is the same in every province. If you do not know who they are and happen to offend one of them, you may lose more than your rank. You may not even keep your life. That is why we call it the “Charm for Protecting Officials”. How could Your Honour touch the Xues we have just been speaking of? There is nothing difficult about the case itself. It has dragged on like this only because no one wants to offend their connections.’

As he spoke, he took a handwritten copy of the charm from his pouch and handed it to Yu-cun. It consisted of popular sayings about the great local clans and families of high officials.

The sayings were neatly set out, each with notes below giving the rank and title of the family’s founding ancestor and the branches descended from him. The Stone once copied this list too. Here is what was written on it:

The Jias are no sham: Their halls are white jade, their horses gold.

Three hundred li of Epang Palace Cannot house the Shi clan of Jinling.

When the Eastern Sea lacks a white-jade bed, The Dragon King comes to ask the Wangs of Jinling.

A splendid fall of snow for a year of plenty: Pearls are dirt to them, and gold is iron.

Before Yu-cun had finished reading, the sound announcing a visitor was heard, and someone reported, ‘Mr Wang has come to call.’ Yu-cun hurriedly put on his formal robes and cap and went out to receive him. Not until the time it took to eat a meal had passed did he return to ask further questions.

‘These four families are all linked by marriage,’ the usher explained. ‘Harm one and you harm them all; when one prospers, they all prosper. They support one another, cover up for one another, and see that everything is looked after. The Xue accused of this killing is the “snow” in that line about the year of plenty. Nor does his family depend only on the other three. They have plenty of old family friends and relatives of their own, both in the capital and elsewhere. Whom is Your Honour going to arrest now?’

Hearing this, Yu-cun smiled and asked, ‘If that is so, how are we to settle the case?

‘I suppose you also know very well where the murderer is hiding?’

The usher smiled. ‘To tell Your Honour the truth, I know more than where the murderer is. I know the kidnapper too, and I knew the dead buyer very well indeed. Let me tell you the whole story. The fellow who was killed was a local minor landowner’s son called Feng Yuan. Both his parents died when he was a child, and he had no brothers. He lived alone on the little property they had left him. By the time he was eighteen or nineteen he was passionately fond of men and detested women. It must have been some unhappy debt from a former life: he happened to see this kidnapper offering the girl for sale and took a fancy to her at first sight. He made up his mind to buy her as a concubine and swore he would have no more dealings with men,

‘nor take another woman after her. That was why she was to enter his household three days later. Who could have known the kidnapper would secretly sell her again to the Xues? He meant to pocket both families’ money and escape to another province. But he failed to get away. Both families caught him and beat him within an inch of his life. Neither would take its money back; each insisted on having the girl. Young Master Xue was hardly one to yield. He shouted to his men to lay into them, and they beat young Feng to a pulp. Feng was carried home and died three days later. Now, this young Master Xue had already fixed the day for going up to the capital. He happened to come upon the girl two days before he was due to leave, and meant to buy her and take her with him. Then all this trouble broke out.

‘Once he had had Feng beaten and seized the girl, he simply took his family and went on his way as though nothing had happened. There were kinsmen and servants here to deal with the matter. It was not as if a little business like this were worth his running away from.

‘But leave that aside. Who do you suppose the girl being sold was, sir?’

‘How should I know?’ Yu-cun said with a smile.

The usher gave a cold laugh. ‘In a manner of speaking, sir, she is one of your great benefactors. She is the daughter of Mr Zhen, who lived beside the Gourd Temple. Her name is Ying-lian.’

Yu-cun was astonished. ‘So it was she! I heard that she was kidnapped when she was five. Has she only now been put up for sale?’

‘That sort of kidnapper specialises in stealing children of five or six,’ said the usher. ‘He keeps them somewhere out of the way until they are eleven or twelve, then takes them to another district and sells them according to their looks. We used to play with Ying-lian and amuse her every day. Seven or eight years have passed, and she must be twelve or thirteen now. She has grown much prettier, but her features are still much the same. Anyone who knew her would recognise her easily. Besides, she had a red birthmark the size of a grain of rice between her eyebrows. She was born with it. That was how I recognised her.

‘As it happened, the kidnapper had rented some rooms of mine. One day when he was out, I questioned her. But he had beaten her till she was terrified of him, and she dared not tell me a thing. She would only say that he was her own father, and was selling her because he had no money to pay his debts. I tried coaxing her again and again. Then she cried and said only, “I cannot remember when I was little!” That left me in no doubt.

‘The day young Feng came to look at her and paid over the silver, the kidnapper got drunk. She sighed to herself, “At last I must have paid for all my sins!” But when she heard that Feng had ordered her to be brought home three days later, she grew troubled again.

‘I could not bear to see her like that. When the kidnapper went out, I sent my wife to comfort her. “Young Master Feng insists on waiting for a lucky day to fetch you,” she told her. “That shows he does not mean to treat you as a maid. Besides, he is a very handsome, charming young man, and comfortably off. He has always hated women; yet now he is paying such a price for you. You can imagine what that means for your future. You have only two or three days to wait. Why be so unhappy?” That eased her mind a little, and she thought she had found a home at last.

‘Who could have dreamed things would turn out so badly? The very next day the man sold her again, to the Xues. It might have been all right if he had sold her to anyone else. But this young Master Xue, the one they call the “Stupid Tyrant”, is the most wilful, overbearing creature in the world, and spends money like dirt. So he had them thrashed to pieces and Ying-lian was dragged away by force. I do not even know whether she is alive or dead. Poor Feng had all that happiness for nothing. Before he could realise a single hope, he had lost both his money and his life. Is it not pitiful?’

Yu-cun sighed too. ‘Their burden of past sins had brought them to this unhappy pass. There was nothing accidental about it.

‘Otherwise, why should Feng Yuan have set his heart on Ying-lian of all people? After all those years of torment at the kidnapper’s hands, she had at last found a way out, with someone who loved her too. It would have been a happy thing if they could have come together. Instead, this had to happen.

‘The Xues may be richer and grander than the Fengs, but, from the sort of man he is, I imagine he has any number of concubines and indulges himself without restraint. He may be very far from Feng Yuan, who gave his heart to one woman only. A bond of love as fleeting as a dream, and two ill-fated young people brought together! But let us leave them. What I need to know is how best to decide this case.’

The usher smiled. ‘Your Honour used to be so clear-headed and decisive. How is it that you cannot make up your mind today? I hear it was through the Jia and Wang families that you obtained this new appointment. Xue Pan is a relative of the Jias. Why not go with the current, do them a thorough good turn and settle the case? Then you will have something to say when you next call on the Jias and the Wangs.’

‘There is much in what you say,’ Yu-cun replied. ‘But a man’s life is involved. His Majesty’s great kindness in reinstating me and entrusting me with office has given me a second life. This is the very time when I ought to devote every effort to repaying him. How can I set the law aside for private interests? I truly could not bring myself to do it.’

The usher gave a cold laugh. ‘Your Honour’s principles are excellent. Only they will not work in the world as it is today.

‘Have you not heard the old sayings, “A man of mettle acts as the times require”, and “The wise gentleman seeks good fortune and avoids disaster”? If you do as you say, you will not serve the court, and you will not be safe yourself. You had better think it over very carefully.’

Yu-cun kept his head lowered for a long time. At last he spoke.

‘What would you do?’

‘I have already thought of an excellent plan,’ the usher said. ‘When Your Honour takes your seat in court tomorrow, make a great show of authority. Issue documents and warrants for arrests. Naturally, the actual killer will not be found. The complainant is bound to insist that some of the Xue clansmen and servants be arrested and questioned under torture. I shall arrange things behind the scenes and have them report that Xue Pan has died of a sudden illness. His clansmen and the local people can jointly submit a written guarantee of the fact. Your Honour need only claim to be skilled at spirit-writing and summoning immortals. Have an altar set up in court, and invite soldiers and civilians alike to watch. Then announce: “The immortal has given his verdict through the writing-planchette. Feng Yuan and Xue Pan were brought together by a debt from a former life. Having met on a narrow path with no room to pass, they were bound to settle it. Xue Pan has now succumbed to a nameless illness. Pursued by Feng’s ghost, he has died. All the trouble was caused by a certain kidnapper, a man of such-and-such a name from such-and-such a place. He is to be dealt with according to law; no one else need be implicated.” Something of that sort. I shall privately instruct the kidnapper to make a confession agreeing with it.

‘Once people see that the immortal’s pronouncement agrees with the kidnapper’s confession, they will naturally believe the rest of it too. The Xues have money enough. Your Honour may order them to pay a thousand taels, or five hundred, towards the Feng family’s funeral expenses. There is no one of any consequence among the Fengs. All they want is money, and once they get it, I imagine they will have nothing more to say. What does Your Honour think of the plan?’

‘No, that will not do,’ Yu-cun said with a smile. ‘Let me think it over again. Perhaps there is a way to silence the talk.’

It was already late by the time they had finished their discussion, and there is nothing more to tell of that evening. The next day Yu-cun took his seat in court and summoned all those named in the case. He examined them closely and found that, as he had been told, there were few people left in the Feng household, and they were merely using the case to obtain a larger sum for funeral expenses.

The Xues, relying on their power and connections, had refused to yield, which was why the affair had been turned this way and that without a settlement. Yu-cun now bent the law to favour his patrons and disposed of the case in a muddle. The Fengs received a substantial sum for funeral expenses and had little more to say. As soon as he had decided the case, Yu-cun hurriedly wrote two letters, one to Jia Zheng and the other to Wang Zi-teng, Commander of the Metropolitan Garrison. They said little more than, ‘Your nephew’s affair has been settled. There is no need for further concern.’

The whole arrangement had originated with the former novice of the Gourd Temple, now an usher. Yu-cun, however, feared that he might tell people about his earlier days of poverty and low estate, and was deeply uneasy. In the end he found some fault with the man and had him sent far away into penal exile. Only then was he satisfied.

But we need say no more of Yu-cun for the present. Let us return to the young Master Xue who had bought Ying-lian and had Feng Yuan beaten to death. He too was a native of Jinling, born into a family with a tradition of scholarship. He had lost his father as a child, however, and his widowed mother, cherishing him as her only son, had spoilt and indulged him until he grew up good for nothing. The family possessed a fortune reckoned in millions, and currently received funds from the imperial privy treasury to purchase miscellaneous supplies.

The young man’s formal name was Xue Pan, and his courtesy name Wen-long. Even at five he had been extravagant in his tastes and arrogant in his speech. He had attended school, but knew little more than a few characters, and spent his days at cockfights and horse races, or roaming the hills and enjoying the scenery. Though an imperial purveyor, he knew nothing at all of business or practical affairs. Old ties with his grandfather and father kept his name nominally on the Board of Revenue’s books and allowed him to draw the allotted funds. Everything else was attended to by the clerks and the old family servants.

His widowed mother, née Wang, was a younger sister of Wang Zi-teng, the present Commander of the Metropolitan Garrison, and a sister by the same mother of Lady Wang, Jia Zheng’s wife at the Rong mansion. She was only about forty, and Xue Pan was her only son. She also had a daughter, two years younger than Xue Pan, whose childhood name was Bao-chai. The girl’s skin had a smooth, lustrous bloom, and her manner was composed and graceful. Her father had adored her and had her taught to read, and she was ten times the scholar her brother was. After her father’s death, seeing that her brother could do little to comfort their mother, she gave up making books her chief concern and turned instead to needlework and household affairs, hoping to ease her mother’s worries and spare her some labour.

Now the present sovereign, who honoured poetry and ritual and sought out talent, had recently bestowed an extraordinary favour. In addition to the selection of imperial consorts, the names of daughters from distinguished official families were all to be submitted to the appropriate board, so that some might be chosen as study companions for princesses of the imperial and princely houses, with appointments as Ladies of Talent and Ladies of Instruction. There was another consideration too. Since Xue Pan’s father had died, the managers and clerks of the family’s businesses and supply agencies in the provinces, seeing how young and inexperienced Xue Pan was, had seized the opportunity to cheat him. Several of the businesses in the capital were dwindling away as well.

Xue Pan had long heard that the capital was the most splendid and prosperous place in the world, and had been thinking of going there. He took this opportunity to do so: first, to escort his sister there for the selection; second, to visit relatives; and third, to settle the old accounts in person at the Board and arrange for new advances. His real purpose was to see the sights of the imperial city.

He had accordingly packed his baggage and valuables well beforehand, together with local specialities and other gifts for relatives and friends. Just as an auspicious day had been chosen and his departure fixed, he happened to encounter the kidnapper selling Ying-lian a second time. Struck by her unusual beauty, he was determined to buy her. When the Fengs came to claim her, he relied on his superior strength and ordered his brutal servants to beat Feng Yuan to death. Then, having entrusted every detail of the household’s affairs to his clansmen and a few old servants, he set off on his long journey with his mother and sister. To him a homicide case was child’s play. He thought there was nothing that a little filthy money would not settle.

We need not count the days they spent on the road. As they were nearing the capital, they heard that his maternal uncle Wang Zi-teng had been promoted to Commander-in-Chief of Nine Provinces and ordered to leave the capital on an inspection of the frontiers.

Xue Pan was secretly delighted.

‘I was just worrying that my own mother’s brother would be there to keep me in order,’ he thought, ‘and I would not be free to spend and enjoy myself as I pleased. Now he has been promoted and sent away! Heaven does give a man what he wants.’

He broached the matter with his mother.

‘We have several houses in the capital, but none of us has lived there for ten years or more. The caretakers are sure to have been letting them out on the quiet. We ought to send a few men ahead to clean up and put things in order.’

‘Why make such a fuss?’ his mother said. ‘The first thing we ought to do on reaching the capital is visit our relatives and friends. We can stay either with your uncle or with your aunt’s husband. Both houses have very convenient accommodation. Why not settle in at one of them first, and then have our own house put in order at leisure? It would be much less trouble.’

‘But my uncle has just been promoted to a post outside the capital. His household will be busy getting ready to leave. Surely it would be rather tactless for the whole lot of us to descend on them just now.’

‘Even if your uncle is moving away, there is still your aunt’s husband,’ his mother replied. ‘And for years now both your uncle’s and your aunt’s households have been sending letters and messages inviting us to come. Now that we are here, your uncle may be busy leaving, but your aunt at the Jias’ is sure to press us to stay. If we rush about getting our own house ready, will people not take offence? I know what you are thinking. Living with your uncle or your aunt’s husband would put a check on you. You would rather live on your own and do just as you please. Very well, then. Go and choose a house for yourself. Your aunt and I have not seen each other for years, and I want to spend a few days with her. I shall take your sister and stay at her house. How does that suit you?’

Xue Pan knew from this that he could not get his way. He had to tell the men to take them straight to the Rong mansion.

Lady Wang had already heard how Jia Yu-cun had intervened to settle Xue Pan’s case, and her mind was now at ease. Then came the news of her brother’s promotion to a frontier post. She was feeling rather lonely at the thought that there would be one fewer member of her own family to see. A few days later a servant suddenly announced, ‘Your sister has brought the young master and young lady to the capital. The whole family is outside, getting out of the carriages.’

Delighted, Lady Wang hurried out to the main hall with the girls and daughters-in-law to welcome Aunt Xue and bring them in. The sisters were meeting again in their later years: there is no need to describe their mingled grief and joy, their tears and laughter as they spoke of their long separation. Lady Wang hastened to take them to pay their respects to Grandmother Jia, and the various presents and local specialities were offered. When everyone in the household had greeted them, a welcoming feast was quickly prepared.

Xue Pan had already paid his respects to Jia Zheng, and Jia Lian took him to see Jia She, Jia Zhen and the others. Jia Zheng sent a message in to Lady Wang:

‘Your sister is getting on in years, and our nephew is young and knows nothing of the world. If they live outside, I fear someone may make trouble for them. There are a dozen rooms standing empty in Pear-tree Court, in the north-east corner of our grounds. It would be an excellent arrangement to have them cleaned and invite your sister and the two young people to stay there.’

Before Lady Wang could urge them to stay, Grandmother Jia too sent to say, ‘Ask your sister to make her home here, so that we can all be closer.’

Aunt Xue had wanted just such an arrangement, which would allow her to keep a tighter hold on her son. If they lived separately, she feared he would give his temper free rein and get into trouble again. She therefore thanked them warmly and accepted. In private, however, she made one point clear to Lady Wang:

‘You must leave all our daily expenses and provisions to us. That is the only way we can go on comfortably together.’

Lady Wang knew that her sister’s family could well afford them, and agreed. From then on Aunt Xue and her children lived in Pear-tree Court.

Pear-tree Court had been the old Duke of Rong’s retreat in his later years. It was a neat little place of a dozen rooms or so, complete with a hall at the front and living quarters behind. A separate gate opened on to the street, and the Xue servants used it to come and go. In the south-west corner a side gate led into a narrow passage, which emerged on the east side of Lady Wang’s principal rooms. Every day, either after a meal or in the evening, Aunt Xue would come over to chat with Grandmother Jia or spend time with Lady Wang. Bao-chai passed her days with Dai-yu, Ying-chun and the other girls, reading, playing chess or doing needlework, and was very happy.

Xue Pan had originally been unwilling to live in the Jia household, fearing that his uncle would keep him in order and give him no freedom. But his mother was determined to stay, and the family pressed them so warmly that he had to remain for the time being. Meanwhile, he sent men to prepare one of his own houses, intending to move there later. Yet before he had been there a month he had become well acquainted with half the sons and nephews of the Jia clan. Every one of its idle young rakes was delighted to make his acquaintance. There was a drinking party today, an outing to see the flowers tomorrow; then came gambling parties and visits to prostitutes, until by degrees there was nothing they would not do. Under their influence Xue Pan grew ten times worse than before.

Jia Zheng had sound methods of bringing up his sons and firm rules for his household. But in the first place, the clan was too large, with too many members, for him to supervise all this. In the second, its present head was Jia Zhen, the eldest grandson of the Ning branch, who had also inherited its rank. Clan affairs were his responsibility. Thirdly, Jia Zheng had more than enough public and private business to attend to, and was by nature a man of easy detachment who set little store by mundane affairs. In his hours away from official work he merely read or played chess, paying little attention to anything else. Moreover, Pear-tree Court was two ranges of buildings away and had its own street gate, through which anyone could come and go at will. The young men were therefore quite free to enjoy themselves as they pleased, and Xue Pan gradually abandoned all thought of moving out.
