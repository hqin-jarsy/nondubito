Chapter 43 A birthday purse is made up for an idle day’s amusement; For an affection not laid to rest, a pinch of earth serves as incense

Chapter end

Now Lady Wang saw that Grandmother Jia had merely caught a little chill that day in Prospect Garden. It was no serious illness: the doctor had been called, and two doses of medicine had put her right. Relieved, Lady Wang sent for Xi-feng and told her to prepare the things that were to be sent to Jia Zheng. While they were discussing this, a messenger came to summon them to Grandmother Jia. Lady Wang at once took Xi-feng over with her.

‘Are you feeling a little better now?’ Lady Wang asked again.

‘Much better today. I tasted the young pheasant broth you sent just now. It had quite a flavour. I ate two pieces of the meat as well, and felt all the better for it.’

‘That was Xi-feng’s offering to you,’ said Lady Wang, smiling. ‘She has a truly devoted heart. You haven’t wasted all the affection you’ve lavished on her.’

Grandmother Jia smiled and nodded.

‘It was good of her to think of it. If there’s any uncooked meat left, have another couple of pieces fried for me, nice and salty. They’ll give some flavour to my porridge. Good as that broth is, it doesn’t go with thin rice porridge.’

Xi-feng promptly assented and sent someone to the kitchen with the order. Grandmother Jia turned back to Lady Wang with a smile.

‘This is all I sent for you about. The second is Feng’s birthday. The last two years I’d been meaning well beforehand to give her a birthday party, but each time some important business came up just as the day arrived, and it was allowed to pass. This year everyone is here, and I don’t suppose anything will happen. Let us all have a proper day’s enjoyment.’

‘I was thinking about it too,’ said Lady Wang. ‘Since you feel inclined, why don’t we settle it now?’

‘Whenever we’ve had a birthday before, no matter whose it was, we’ve all sent separate presents. It’s become rather commonplace, and somehow it makes us seem like strangers. I’ve thought of a new way today. It will be more friendly, and we can have a little fun out of it too.’

‘Whatever you think best is what we shall do,’ Lady Wang said at once.

‘I thought we might copy the small households and each put in a share,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Whatever it comes to, that’s what we’ll spend. Wouldn’t that be fun?’

‘That would be very nice,’ Lady Wang said, smiling. ‘Only how are we to arrange the contributions?’

Grandmother Jia grew more delighted still. She hurriedly sent people to invite Aunt Xue, Lady Xing and the others, and to fetch the girls and Bao-yu. Zhen’s wife from the other mansion was to come too, along with Lai Da’s wife and the other married servants of standing who had charge of household affairs.

Seeing Grandmother Jia in such good spirits, the maids and older women caught her excitement. They hurried off in different directions, some to invite, some to pass the word. In less time than it took to eat a meal, old and young, high and low, were crowded thickly into the room. Only Aunt Xue sat facing Grandmother Jia. Lady Xing and Lady Wang occupied the two chairs by the door. Five or six of the girls, including Bao-chai, sat on the kang; Bao-yu sat nestled in front of his grandmother. The floor was packed with people standing. Grandmother Jia promptly ordered a few small stools to be brought for Lai Da’s mother and several other elderly servants of standing. It was the Jia family’s custom to accord old servants who had attended the parents of the present generation greater consideration than the younger masters and mistresses. Thus You-shi and Xi-feng remained standing, while the three or four old women, including Lai Da’s mother, begged leave to sit and took the little stools.

Smiling, Grandmother Jia repeated her proposal. Who among them would refuse to join in the fun? Besides, some were fond of Xi-feng and quite willing to do it; others were afraid of her and eager for a chance to ingratiate themselves. All could afford it. At the first mention, therefore, they cheerfully agreed.

‘I’ll give twenty taels,’ Grandmother Jia began.

‘I shall follow your example,’ said Aunt Xue, smiling. ‘Twenty taels from me too.’

‘We daren’t put ourselves on a level with you,’ said Lady Xing and Lady Wang. ‘We must come a step lower. Sixteen taels each will do.’

‘Then we must come a step lower still,’ You-shi and Li Wan said with a smile. ‘Twelve taels each.’

‘You’re a widow without a livelihood,’ Grandmother Jia hastily told Li Wan. ‘How could we drag you into paying for this? I’ll pay your share.’

‘Don’t get carried away, Grannie,’ Xi-feng said quickly, laughing. ‘Do the accounts before you take on anything more. You’ve already got two other shares to pay, and now you’re taking on another twelve taels for my elder sister-in-law. You’re enjoying saying it now; in a little while you’ll think it over and feel the pinch. Afterwards you’ll be saying, “All that money spent on Feng!”

‘Then you’ll think of some clever trick to wheedle three or four shares out of me to make it up on the quiet, and I shan’t know what’s happened.’

Everyone laughed.

‘Well, what would you have me do?’ Grandmother Jia asked, laughing too.

‘My birthday hasn’t come yet, and already I’ve had so much honour I’m quite uncomfortable with it. I’m not being asked to part with a single cash, and I feel dreadful putting all these people to such trouble. Why don’t I pay my elder sister-in-law’s share? Then on the day I shall only have to eat a little more, and I shall have had all the good of it.’

Lady Xing and the others thought this quite right, and Grandmother Jia consented.

‘There’s one other thing,’ Xi-feng went on, smiling. ‘Our Old Ancestress is giving twenty taels for herself, as well as Cousin Lin’s share and Brother Bao’s. Aunt is giving twenty for herself and Cousin Bao-chai’s share too. That’s fair enough. But our two ladies are only giving sixteen taels apiece, and neither is paying for anyone else. Their own shares are smaller to begin with. That doesn’t seem altogether fair. Our Old Ancestress is being done out of her money!’

‘My Feng is the one who takes my part!’ Grandmother Jia said at once, laughing. ‘Quite right!

‘If it hadn’t been for you, they’d have taken me in again.’

‘Just give the two children to the two ladies, one apiece,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Whatever contribution is fixed, let each lady pay one of their shares.’

‘That’s perfectly fair,’ Grandmother Jia said quickly. ‘We’ll do that.’

Lai Da’s mother rose hastily, smiling.

‘Well, this is turning things upside down! I feel indignant on Their Ladyships’ behalf. A son’s wife on one side, a brother’s daughter on the other, and instead of taking her mother-in-law’s and aunt’s part, she sides with someone else! The daughter-in-law has become a stranger, and the brother’s daughter has turned into an outsider’s niece.’

Grandmother Jia and all the others burst out laughing.

‘If the young mistresses give twelve taels,’ Lai Da’s mother went on, ‘then we ought to come a step below them.’

‘That won’t do,’ said Grandmother Jia.

‘You ought to be a step lower, it’s true, but I know you few are all rich women. Your rank may be lower, but you have more money than they do. You must pay the same as they do.’

The old women hastened to agree.

‘The girls need only make a token contribution,’ Grandmother Jia continued. ‘Let each give one month’s allowance.’

She turned and called Faithful.

‘You get a few of your people together too. Talk it over and bring your contributions.’

Faithful assented. Before long she returned with Patience, Aroma, Cai-xia and several younger maids. Some gave two taels, some one.

‘Aren’t you giving your mistress a birthday celebration yourself?’ Grandmother Jia asked Patience. ‘Why are you joining in this?’

‘I’ve made my own little arrangement for her separately,’ Patience replied with a smile. ‘This is the general fund, and I ought to give a share to that too.’

‘Now that is a good girl,’ said Grandmother Jia.

‘That’s everyone, high and low,’ Xi-feng said, smiling. ‘There are still the two aunts, though. We ought to ask if they want to contribute.

‘It’s only proper to include them. Otherwise they’ll think we’re looking down on them.’

‘Of course!’ Grandmother Jia said quickly. ‘How did we come to forget them? They may be busy. Send a maid to ask.’

A maid had already gone. After a while she returned.

‘They’ll give two taels each.’

‘Bring the brushes and inkstone,’ Grandmother Jia said happily. ‘Let us work out exactly how much there is.’

You-shi meanwhile was scolding Xi-feng under her breath.

‘You insatiable little bitch! All these mothers-in-law and aunts putting up money for your birthday, and you’re still not satisfied. Why drag those two poor miserable creatures into it?’

‘Don’t talk nonsense,’ Xi-feng murmured back with a smile. ‘I’ll settle with you when we get out of here. Why should they be miserable? If they have any money, they’ll only pour it away on someone else. Better to make them contribute to our fun.’

By now the calculation was finished. They had collected more than a hundred and fifty taels in all.

‘A day’s plays and feasting won’t use all that,’ said Grandmother Jia.

‘With no outside guests and not many tables, it will pay for two or three days,’ said You-shi.

‘For one thing, the plays cost us nothing. That’s a saving to start with.’

‘Let Feng say which troupe is best, and send for them,’ said Grandmother Jia.

‘We know our own troupe’s performances by heart,’ said Xi-feng. ‘Why don’t we spend a little and have an outside company for a change?’

‘I’m putting Zhen’s wife in charge of this,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Feng is not to have a single thing to worry about. It will only count if she can enjoy a whole day.’

You-shi assented. They talked a little longer, then, seeing that Grandmother Jia was tired, gradually took their leave.

After seeing Lady Xing and Lady Wang off, You-shi and the others went to Xi-feng’s rooms to discuss the birthday arrangements.

‘You needn’t ask me,’ said Xi-feng. ‘You have only to take your cue from the old lady’s face.’

‘You little creature, what a run of luck you’re having!’ You-shi said, laughing. ‘I wondered what business they were sending for us about, and it was nothing but this. As if paying weren’t enough, I’m to have all the worry too. How are you going to thank me?’

‘Don’t talk such rubbish. I didn’t send for you. What have I to thank you for? Afraid of a little trouble, are you? Go straight back to the old lady and ask her to appoint someone else.’

‘Just look how pleased with herself she is! I advise you to keep a little in reserve. Fill it too full and it spills over.’

They talked a while longer before parting.

The silver was sent to the Ning mansion the next day. You-shi had only just risen and was washing and doing her hair. She asked who had brought it.

‘Mrs Lin,’ the maids replied.

You-shi told them to call her in. A maid went to the servants’ rooms and fetched Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife. You-shi had her sit on the footstool and, busy with her toilette, asked:

‘How much is there in this packet?’

‘This is the money from those of us below stairs. We put it together and brought it over first. Her Old Ladyship’s and Their Ladyships’ shares aren’t here yet.’

Just then the maids announced that Her Ladyship and Aunt Xue at the other mansion had sent people with their contributions.

‘Little hussies!’ You-shi scolded, laughing. ‘You remember every word that doesn’t matter. Yesterday the old lady happened to be in good spirits and deliberately chose to play at pooling our pennies like some small household. You’ve remembered that, and now it comes out of your mouths as solemn business! Hurry up and bring it in. See that the people have tea before you send them away.’

The maids assented and promptly brought in two packets. Bao-chai’s and Dai-yu’s shares were included.

You-shi asked whose money was still missing.

‘Her Old Ladyship’s, Her Ladyship’s, the young ladies’ and the maids’,’ Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife replied.

‘And what about your elder mistress’s?’

‘When you go over, ma’am, you’ll find the money is all being paid out through Mrs Lian. It’s all included.’

By now You-shi had finished dressing and ordered her carriage. Presently she reached the Rong mansion and went first to Xi-feng, who had already sealed up the silver and was just about to send it off.

‘Is it all there?’ You-shi asked.

‘Every bit. Hurry up and take it away. If it gets lost, I’m not responsible.’

‘I don’t quite trust you. I’d better count it in front of you.’

She did indeed count it against the list. Only Li Wan’s contribution was missing.

‘I knew you were up to some damned trick! Where’s your elder sister-in-law’s money?’

‘Isn’t there enough for you there?’ Xi-feng said, laughing.

‘One share short won’t matter. If you run short, I’ll give it to you then.’

‘You made a fine show in front of everyone yesterday, and today you’re trying to wriggle out of it with me. I won’t have it. I’ll simply go and ask the old lady.’

‘So that’s how hard you can be! Next time anything comes up, I’ll insist on every item being just so, and don’t you complain.’

‘So you can be frightened too! If it weren’t for all the attention you usually pay me, I certainly wouldn’t let you off.’

As she spoke, You-shi took out Patience’s contribution.

‘Patience, come here. Put your money away. If there isn’t enough, I’ll make up your share.’

Patience understood her meaning.

‘Please use it for now, ma’am. If there’s any left over, giving it to me afterwards will come to the same thing.’

‘Is your mistress the only one allowed to play tricks? Mayn’t I do a kindness?’

Patience had no choice but to take it.

‘Your mistress is so particular over every penny,’ You-shi went on. ‘What does she mean to do with all this money she gets together?

‘If she can’t spend it, she can take it into her coffin to spend there.’

Still talking, she set off for Grandmother Jia’s. She paid her respects and exchanged a few words, then went into Faithful’s room to discuss the arrangements. On how best to please Grandmother Jia, she deferred entirely to Faithful’s advice. They settled everything between them. As she was leaving, You-shi gave Faithful back her two taels as well.

‘We shan’t be able to spend even what we have.’

She went straight out and called on Lady Wang for a little talk. When Lady Wang went into the prayer room, she returned Cai-yun’s contribution too. Xi-feng was not there, so she presently gave Aunt Zhou and Aunt Zhao their money back as well. Neither dared accept it at first.

‘You poor things, where would you find money to spare? If Feng hears of it, I’ll answer for it.’

At that, they accepted with a thousand thanks. You-shi went straight out, got into her carriage and returned home. Of that we need say no more.

In the blink of an eye it was the second day of the ninth month. Everyone in the Garden had heard that You-shi had arranged a splendid entertainment. There were to be not only plays but acrobatic performances and storytellers, men and women alike. They were all preparing for a day of fun.

‘Today is a regular meeting day of our society,’ Li Wan reminded the girls. ‘Don’t forget. Bao-yu hasn’t come either. I suppose he’s so taken up with the excitement that he’s thrown all our quiet refinements aside.’

She sent a maid to see what he was doing and ask him to come at once. The maid was away some time, then returned.

‘Miss Hua says he went out first thing this morning.’

They were all astonished.

‘He can’t possibly have gone out.

‘The girl’s muddled. She doesn’t know what she’s saying.’

They sent Ebony instead. Presently she returned.

‘He really has gone out. They say a friend has died, and he’s gone to pay a visit of condolence.’

‘Certainly not,’ said Tan-chun. ‘Whatever it is, there is no reason for him to go out today. Tell Aroma to come. I’ll ask her.’

Even as she spoke, Aroma appeared. Li Wan and the others all began:

‘Whatever business he had today, he ought not to have gone out. First, it’s Mrs Lian’s birthday. The old lady is making such an occasion of it, and everyone in both households is joining in. Yet off he goes! And second, this is the first regular meeting of our society. He hasn’t asked for leave. He’s simply gone off on his own!’

‘He told me last night,’ Aroma said with a sigh. ‘He had something important to do at the Prince of Beijing’s first thing this morning, and he’d come straight back. I tried to persuade him not to go, but he wouldn’t listen. Then this morning he asked for plain clothes. Perhaps some favourite concubine in the Prince’s household has died. I really don’t know.’

‘If that’s the case, he ought to go,’ said Li Wan and the others. ‘But he should have come back by now.’

They began discussing it among themselves.

‘Let us get on with our poems, and fine him when he comes back.’

They had hardly said this when a messenger arrived from Grandmother Jia to fetch them, and they all went to the front of the mansion.

Aroma explained Bao-yu’s absence. Grandmother Jia was displeased and sent someone to bring him back.

Bao-yu, as it happened, had a private matter on his mind. The day before, he had instructed Tealeaf:

‘I’m going out early tomorrow. Have two horses ready and wait at the back gate. No one else is to come. Tell Li Gui I’ve gone to the Prince of Beijing’s. If anyone wants to look for me, he’s to stop them. There’s no need to come looking. He need only say they’ve kept me there and I shall certainly be back soon.’

Tealeaf could make nothing of it, but had to pass on the message as instructed. Sure enough, early that morning he was waiting with two horses at the back gate of the Garden. At daybreak Bao-yu came through the side gate, dressed from head to foot in plain clothes. Without a word he swung himself into the saddle, bent forward and went jolting off along the street. Tealeaf could only mount and whip his horse to catch up.

‘Where are we going?’ he called urgently from behind.

‘Where does this road lead?’ Bao-yu asked.

‘It’s the main road out of the north gate. There’s nothing but lonely country beyond. Nothing to amuse you there.’

Bao-yu nodded.

‘A lonely place is just what I want.’

He gave his horse another cut. Already it had rounded two corners and passed out through the city gate.

More at a loss than ever, Tealeaf could only keep close behind. They rode seven or eight li without stopping. As the houses grew fewer, Bao-yu at last reined in and looked back.

‘Is there anywhere here that sells incense?’

‘There’s incense to be had. What sort do you want?’

‘Other kinds won’t do,’ Bao-yu said after thinking. ‘It must be sandalwood, yun and jiang.’

‘Those three will be hard to find,’ Tealeaf said, smiling.

Bao-yu was at a loss. Seeing his difficulty, Tealeaf asked:

‘What do you want it for? I’ve often seen loose incense in that little sachet of yours, Master Bao. Why don’t you have a look?’

This reminded Bao-yu. Reaching back, he pulled a sachet from his robe and felt inside. There were indeed two tiny fragments of chensu incense. He was delighted, though he thought it rather lacking in reverence. On second thoughts, something he had carried on his own person was better than anything bought. He then asked about an incense burner and charcoal.

‘Now that is out of the question,’ said Tealeaf. ‘Where would you find those in the middle of the country? If you wanted all these things, why didn’t you say so before? We could have brought them so easily.’

‘Idiot! If I could have brought them, we shouldn’t have had to tear off as if our lives depended on it.’

Tealeaf thought for a while, then smiled.

‘I’ve an idea, Master Bao. I don’t know what you’ll think of it. I don’t suppose this is all you’ll want. There may be other things as well, and we can’t go on like this. Another two li along this road brings us to the Water Spirit Convent.’

‘The Water Spirit Convent is here?’ Bao-yu asked eagerly. ‘Better still! Let us go.’

He urged his horse on, looking back at Tealeaf as he rode.

‘The nun from there often visits us. If we go and ask to borrow an incense burner, she’s sure to agree.’

‘We support their worship, of course she will. But even if it were a temple where nobody knew us, they wouldn’t dare refuse. There’s just one thing: I’ve always noticed you disliked this convent more than any other. How have you taken such a fancy to it today?’

‘What I’ve always hated is the way ignorant people go worshipping gods and building temples without knowing why. Some rich old fellow, or some rich foolish woman, hears of a god and builds a temple to worship it in, without even knowing who the god is. They hear something out of an unofficial history or a storybook and take it for truth. This Water Spirit Convent, for instance, is called that because it houses the goddess of the Luo River. They don’t realise there never was a goddess of the Luo River in ancient times. Cao Zi-jian made her up. Yet these fools have had a statue made and worship it.

‘Today, though, it suits what is in my heart, so I’m borrowing it for once.’

They were already at the gate. The old nun was so astonished to see Bao-yu that he might have been a living dragon dropped from the sky. She hurried forward to greet him and told an old Taoist to take the horses. Bao-yu went in. He did not bow to the goddess’s image, but studied it admiringly. Clay though it was, it truly had the movement of one ‘light as a startled wild goose, graceful as a swimming dragon’, and the beauty of ‘a lotus rising from green waves, the sun shining on morning clouds’. Tears fell from his eyes before he knew it. The old nun brought him tea.

Bao-yu asked to borrow an incense burner. The nun was gone some time and returned with incense, offerings and paper spirit-offerings all ready as well.

‘I don’t need any of those,’ said Bao-yu.

He told Tealeaf to carry the burner into the back garden. There they looked for a clean place to put it, but could find none.

‘What about the platform by that well?’ Tealeaf suggested.

Bao-yu nodded. They went to it together and set the burner down. Tealeaf stood to one side. Bao-yu took out his incense and lit it, made an abbreviated obeisance with tears in his eyes, then turned and told Tealeaf to take the burner away.

Tealeaf said he would, but instead of picking it up he hastily dropped to the ground and kowtowed several times, praying aloud:

‘I, Tealeaf, have served Master Bao these several years. There’s nothing in his heart I don’t know about, except today’s offering. He hasn’t told me about that, and I daren’t ask. I don’t know the name of the spirit receiving it, but I imagine she must be a sister such as the world has only one of and heaven cannot match, a lady of the utmost intelligence and grace. My master cannot put what is in his heart into words, so let me pray for him. If your fragrant soul has feeling, if your sweet spirit knows affection, then though the worlds of the living and the dead divide you, you are kindred hearts. You might still come and see Master Bao from time to time.

‘In the world below, watch over him, so that in his next life he too may be a girl and keep you all company. Never let him be born again as one of these filthy bearded creatures.’

He kowtowed several more times before getting up. Bao-yu had begun to laugh before he finished. He gave him a kick.

‘Stop talking nonsense. Someone will hear and laugh at us.’

Tealeaf rose and picked up the burner. As he walked beside Bao-yu, he said:

‘I’ve told the nun you haven’t eaten yet and asked her to put together whatever she has. Try to eat a little, Master Bao. I know there’s a great feast at home today, with all the excitement that goes with it, and that’s why you’ve come away. A quiet day here will do all that respect requires, anyhow. But going without food simply won’t do.’

‘If I’m not having the plays and the wine,’ Bao-yu said, ‘there’s no reason I shouldn’t have a little plain vegetarian food.’

‘That’s more like it,’ said Tealeaf. ‘And there’s something else. Now that we’ve come out, there are people worrying about us. If nobody were worried, what would it matter if we went back to the city late? But if people are worried, you really ought to go back home.

‘First, Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship can be easy in their minds. Second, you’ve paid your respects here, and that’s all that’s needed. Even if you go home and watch the plays and drink wine, that isn’t something you’ve gone out of your way to do. You’re only keeping your elders company out of filial duty. If you let them worry just for the sake of this, even the spirit you’ve just made an offering to won’t be at peace. What do you think of that, Master Bao?’

‘I can guess what you mean,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘You’re thinking that you’re the only one who came out with me, and you’re afraid of getting the blame when we go back. That’s why you’re persuading me with such grand arguments. I only came to pay my respects before going back to the wine and the plays. I never said I wouldn’t go into the city all day. Now I’ve done what I wished, we can hurry back and put everyone’s mind at rest. Won’t that fulfil both obligations?’

‘Better still,’ said Tealeaf.

They went into the convent hall, where the nun had indeed laid a table with vegetarian dishes. Bao-yu ate a little, without much attention to what it was, and Tealeaf ate too. Then they mounted and took the same road back. From behind, Tealeaf kept warning him:

‘Ride carefully, Master Bao. This horse hasn’t been ridden much. Keep a firm hold of the reins.’

Even as he spoke they were back inside the city. They went in by the rear gate as before and hurried to the House of Green Delights. Aroma and the others were not there, only a few old women left to watch the rooms. At the sight of him their faces broke into smiles.

‘Blessed Buddha, here you are at last! Miss Hua has been frantic. They’re at the feast now. You must hurry over, Master Bao.’

Bao-yu hastily took off his plain clothes and found some fine ones to change into himself. He asked where the feast was being held. The old women told him it was in the large new reception hall.

He set off straight for it. Already he could faintly hear singing and pipes. As he reached the far side of the passage-hall, he saw Silver sitting alone beneath the eaves, crying. When she saw him she wiped her tears away.

‘Here comes the phoenix! Hurry in. A little longer and they’d have turned the place upside down.’

‘Guess where I’ve been,’ Bao-yu said with a coaxing smile.

Silver gave no answer, but went on wiping her eyes. Bao-yu hurried into the hall to see Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the rest. Everyone really did receive him as though a phoenix had arrived. He hastened to offer Xi-feng his birthday respects. Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang both scolded him for not knowing how to behave.

‘Running off by yourself without a word to anyone! How can we allow that? Do it again, and when your father comes home we shall certainly tell him to beat you.’

They went on to abuse the pages for obeying him in everything, going wherever he chose without reporting it. At the same time they asked where he had been, whether he had eaten anything, and whether anything had frightened him.

‘One of the Prince of Beijing’s favourite concubines died yesterday,’ Bao-yu merely replied. ‘I went to offer my condolences. He was crying so much I couldn’t leave him and come straight back, so I stayed a little longer.’

‘Go out on your own again without telling us first, and I’ll certainly have your father beat you,’ said Grandmother Jia.

Bao-yu assented. She then wanted the pages who had gone with him beaten, but everyone hastened to plead for them.

‘You needn’t worry any more. He’s come back. We can all set our minds at rest and enjoy ourselves.’

It was her earlier anxiety that had made Grandmother Jia so fierce. Now he was back, she was more than happy: how could she still be angry? She let the matter drop. Indeed, fearful that he might feel ill, or had not eaten enough wherever he had been, or had had a fright on the road, she began coaxing him in every possible way. Aroma had already come to attend to him. They all went back to watching the play.

That day’s play was The Thorn Hairpin. Grandmother Jia, Aunt Xue and the others were so moved that they wept; some sighed, and some scolded. To learn what followed, read the next chapter.
