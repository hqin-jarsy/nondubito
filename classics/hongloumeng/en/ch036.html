Chapter 36 Embroidered mandarin ducks attend a dream’s omen in Crimson Rue Studio; Love’s allotted shares become clear at Pear-tree Court

Chapter end

After returning from Lady Wang’s, Grandmother Jia was naturally delighted to see Bao-yu improving day by day. Afraid that Jia Zheng might send for him again, she summoned the head of his personal pages.

‘In future, if your master wants Bao-yu to meet visitors or entertain guests, don’t come in with the message. Tell him I have said this: first, the beating was too severe, and he will need several months of proper nursing before he can get about; second, his stars are unfavourable. Now that offerings have been made to them, he must see no outsiders. He is not to go beyond the inner gate until the eighth month is over.’

The head page received her instructions and withdrew.

Grandmother Jia also sent for Nurse Li, Aroma and the others and told them to pass this on to Bao-yu, so that he need not worry. Bao-yu had always been reluctant to converse with gentlemen and officials, and particularly detested the ceremonial hats and robes, the visits of congratulation and condolence, and all such comings and goings. This pronouncement suited him better still. Not only did he shut out relatives and friends alike; even his morning and evening attendance on his elders was left very much to his own convenience. He spent his days wandering or lying about the garden, merely calling on Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang early each morning before coming back. Yet he was often perfectly willing to run errands for the maids, and so passed his time in ample leisure. If Bao-chai or anyone like her took an opportunity to offer him guidance, he would grow angry instead.

‘A girl as pure and unspoilt as that, and now she too must learn to fish for fame and buy herself a reputation, joining those traitors to the country, those office-hungry ghosts! It all began with the ancients making trouble for no reason, setting up their doctrines and fine phrases to instruct the filthy bearded creatures who came after them. And I have the misfortune to live when even the jewelled chambers of girls have caught the infection. What a betrayal of all the finest spirit of heaven and earth that went into making them!’

The calamity thus spread to the ancients: apart from the Four Books, he actually burnt the other books.

Seeing him behave so wildly, the others gave up talking to him about these serious matters. Dai-yu alone had never, from childhood onwards, urged him to make a career or a name for himself, and for this he held her in profound esteem.

But enough of that. To return to Wang Xi-feng: since Golden’s death, several families of servants had suddenly taken to bringing her little offerings, calling to pay their respects and flattering her. She was puzzled, unable to make out what they meant by it. After yet another gift arrived, she asked Patience with a smile that evening, when they were alone, ‘Those families don’t usually have much to do with me. Why have they suddenly become so friendly?’

Patience gave a cold laugh. ‘Can’t you work even that out, ma’am? My guess is that their daughters are all maids in Her Ladyship’s rooms. There are four senior girls there who get a tael of silver a month; the rest only get a few hundred cash. Now Golden’s dead, they must be after that nice little one-tael opening.’

Xi-feng laughed. ‘Of course! Of course! You’ve put me on to it. What a greedy lot they are. They’ve made plenty of money, and none of the hard jobs ever comes their way. They might be satisfied with putting up a daughter to cover their own obligations, but no, they must have this as well. Very well, then. It isn’t often their money finds its way to me, and they’ve asked for it themselves. Whatever they send, I’ll take. I know what I’m going to do.’

Having settled that to her satisfaction, Xi-feng let the matter drag on until the families had sent all the gifts she thought sufficient. Only then did she find an opportunity to speak to Lady Wang. One day at noon, Aunt Xue and her daughter, Dai-yu and the others were having something to eat in Lady Wang’s room, and Xi-feng took her chance.

‘Since Silver’s elder sister died, ma’am, you’ve been one maid short. If there’s a girl you think suitable, please let me know, so that we can arrange the monthly payments for next month.’

Lady Wang considered. ‘As far as I’m concerned, what is all this about a fixed number? Why must there be four or five? Enough to do the work is enough. We might simply do without another.’

‘In principle, ma’am, you’re quite right,’ said Xi-feng, smiling. ‘But it’s the old allowance. Even other households have two girls on that rate, and here you would be, not taking your full number. Besides, saving one tael won’t make much difference.’

Lady Wang thought again. ‘Very well. Draw the allowance as usual, but don’t fill the place. Give the tael to her younger sister Silver.

‘Her sister served me all that time and came to no good end. There is only the younger one left with me now. Letting her have a double share would hardly be excessive.’

Xi-feng assented, then turned to look for Silver. ‘Congratulations! Congratulations!’ she said, smiling.

Silver came forward and kowtowed.

‘There was something I meant to ask you,’ Lady Wang went on. ‘How much do Aunt Zhao and Aunt Zhou receive each month now?’

‘The fixed allowance, ma’am, two taels each. Aunt Zhao has another two taels for Huan, making four altogether, with four strings of cash besides.’

‘And are they given the full amount?’

Xi-feng thought this a strange question. ‘Of course they are!’ she said quickly.

‘The other day I thought I heard someone complaining that a string of cash was missing. Why was that?’

Xi-feng hastened to explain, smiling. ‘The concubines’ maids used to receive a string each every month. Last year the men outside discussed it and decided to halve the allowance for each maid in those households, making it five hundred cash a head. Each concubine has two maids, so that is where the missing string comes from. They can’t blame me for it. I’d be glad to give it to them, but if the men outside hold it back, am I supposed to make up the difference? The money only passes through my hands. What comes in goes out; I have no say in it. I did ask two or three times for those allowances to be restored, but they said that was all there was under that heading, and there wasn’t much more I could say. As things are, I pay them every month without so much as a day’s delay. When they used to draw it from outside, was there ever a month without a fuss? Did they ever once get it smoothly?’

Lady Wang let the matter drop. After a while she asked, ‘How many maids on one tael are there in the old lady’s rooms?’

‘Eight,’ said Xi-feng.

‘Only seven are actually there now. The eighth is Aroma.’

‘Exactly,’ said Lady Wang. ‘Bao-yu has no one-tael maid of his own. Aroma is still reckoned as belonging to the old lady’s household.’

‘Aroma was always the old lady’s maid,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘She was only given to Bao-yu to use. Her tael still comes out of the old lady’s allowance for maids. It simply wouldn’t do to cut it now on the grounds that she belongs to Bao-yu. If we gave the old lady another maid, then we could discontinue Aroma’s allowance there. If we don’t discontinue it, we ought to add a maid on the same rate to Huan’s rooms, to keep things fair and even. As for the seven senior maids, Skybright, Musk and the others, at one string a month each, and the eight little maids, Melilot and the rest, at five hundred cash each, that was the old lady’s own decision. Who can get angry or resentful about that?’

‘Listen to Feng!’ Aunt Xue laughed. ‘Like a cartload of walnuts tipping out. And her accounts are so clear, her reasoning so fair!’

‘Aunt, have I said anything wrong?’ Xi-feng asked, smiling.

‘Not a thing. Only wouldn’t it be less tiring if you said it a little more slowly?’

Xi-feng was about to laugh, but checked herself and waited for Lady Wang’s instructions.

After thinking for some time, Lady Wang said to Xi-feng, ‘Tomorrow choose a good maid and send her to the old lady in Aroma’s place, and discontinue Aroma’s allowance there. Take two taels of silver and one string of cash out of my own twenty-tael monthly allowance and give them to Aroma. From now on, whatever Aunt Zhao and Aunt Zhou receive, Aroma is to receive too. Only her share must all come out of mine. There is no need to draw on the household funds.’

Xi-feng agreed to each instruction, then gave Aunt Xue a smiling nudge. ‘You heard that, Aunt! What did I always tell you? Today it’s come true.’

‘It should have been done long ago,’ Aunt Xue said.

‘Her looks go without saying. But that open, capable way she has, and the firmness and determination beneath her pleasant manner with people: those are rare qualities.’

‘You have no idea how good that child Aroma is,’ Lady Wang said, her eyes filling. ‘Ten times better than my Bao-yu! If he is truly blessed, and can have her to look after him all his life, that will be enough.’

‘If that’s how you feel, why not have her bridal face-threading done and openly install her in his rooms?’ Xi-feng asked.

‘That would not do,’ said Lady Wang. ‘For one thing, they are both young. For another, his father would not allow it. And thirdly, while Bao-yu sees her as a maid, even when he is being wilful he will still listen to her advice. Once she becomes his concubine, she will not dare press him quite so hard on the things she ought to. Leave it undefined for the present. We can speak of it again in two or three years.’

Some time passed after this. Seeing that there was nothing more to be said, Xi-feng withdrew. She had barely reached the verandah when she found several married servants with household duties waiting to report to her.

‘What have you been discussing all this time, ma’am?’ they asked, smiling. ‘You must be terribly hot.’

Xi-feng rolled her sleeves up several times and stood with a foot on the threshold of the side gate. ‘There’s a cool draught through here. I’ll catch a little of it before I go.’ Then she told them, ‘You wonder what kept me talking so long? Her Ladyship remembered things from two hundred years ago and wanted to ask me about them. Could I refuse to answer?’

She gave a cold laugh. ‘From now on I really will do a few spiteful things. Let them complain to Her Ladyship; I’m not afraid. Their hearts must be clogged with grease, the filthy low creatures! May their tongues rot, may they die a wretched death! They can stop their damned dreaming. There are days ahead when the whole lot will be cut off.

‘A little taken off their maids’ money, and they blame us! They might remember they’re servants themselves. What right have the likes of them to two or three maids?’

Still cursing, she went off to choose a maid and report to Grandmother Jia. Of that we need say no more.

Meanwhile, Lady Wang and the others finished their watermelon, chatted a little longer and dispersed. Bao-chai, Dai-yu and the rest returned to the garden. Bao-chai invited Dai-yu to go with her to Lotus Pavilion, but Dai-yu said she wanted a bath at once, so they parted. Walking on alone, Bao-chai went into the House of Green Delights on her way, hoping a little conversation with Bao-yu would dispel the afternoon drowsiness. To her surprise, the courtyard was utterly still. Even the two cranes beneath the plantains were asleep. She followed the covered walk to the rooms. On the bed in the outer room the maids lay asleep, sprawling in every direction. Passing the ornamental lattice partition, she entered Bao-yu’s room. He was asleep on his bed, with Aroma sitting beside him at her needlework. A fly-whisk with rhinoceros-horn mounts lay nearby.

Bao-chai went up and whispered with a smile, ‘You do take such care! There can’t be any flies or mosquitoes in here. What do you need a whisk for?’

Aroma, taken unawares, looked up with a start. Seeing Bao-chai, she hastily laid down her sewing and rose, whispering with a smile, ‘Oh, miss, I didn’t know you’d come in. You startled me.1

‘You wouldn’t know, miss, but although there aren’t any flies or mosquitoes, there’s a sort of tiny insect that gets through the holes in the gauze. You can’t even see it. Once you’re asleep it bites, and it’s just like being nipped by an ant.’

‘No wonder,’ said Bao-chai. ‘The water is so close behind the house, and there are all those scented flowers, and the rooms are fragrant too. Those insects grow in the hearts of flowers. They come flocking wherever they smell a scent.’

As she spoke, she looked at Aroma’s sewing. It was a stomach-cover of white figured silk lined with red, embroidered with mandarin ducks playing among lotuses: red flowers, green leaves and ducks in many colours.

‘Oh, what lovely bright work!’ said Bao-chai. ‘Who is it for, to be worth so much trouble?’

Aroma pursed her lips towards the bed.

‘At his age, does he still wear one?’ Bao-chai asked, smiling.

Aroma smiled. ‘He wouldn’t wear one before. That’s why I’ve taken such pains to make it beautiful: when he sees it, he won’t be able to resist. In this hot weather he’s so careless when he goes to sleep. If I can coax him into wearing it, we needn’t worry so much if he isn’t properly covered at night. You think I’ve spent time on this? You haven’t seen the one he’s wearing now.’

‘What patience you have!’

‘I’ve been at it a long time today, with my head down. My neck’s terribly stiff.’ Aroma smiled. ‘Be a dear, miss, and sit here a moment. I’ll just go out and stretch my legs.’

She went. Bao-chai was so absorbed in examining the work that, without thinking, she lowered herself into the very place Aroma had just left. Finding the embroidery really delightful, she could not help picking up the needle and putting in a few stitches for her.

Now Dai-yu had met Shi Xiang-yun, who proposed that they go and congratulate Aroma. They came into the silent courtyard, and Xiang-yun turned aside to look for Aroma in the side rooms. Dai-yu went to the window and looked through its gauze. Bao-yu, in a silvery-pink gauze tunic, lay at ease on the bed, while Bao-chai sat beside him sewing, with the fly-whisk at her side. At the sight, Dai-yu quickly drew back out of view and pressed a hand over her mouth to keep from laughing. She beckoned to Xiang-yun. Seeing her like this, Xiang-yun supposed there must be something new to see and hurried over for a look. She too was about to laugh, but suddenly remembered how kindly Bao-chai always treated her, and covered her mouth. Knowing Dai-yu’s merciless tongue, she was afraid she would make some cutting joke, so quickly drew her away.

‘Come on. I’ve just remembered Aroma said she was going to the pond at midday to wash some clothes. She must have gone there. Let’s go and find her.’

Dai-yu understood perfectly. She gave a couple of dry laughs, but had to go with her.

Bao-chai had embroidered only two or three petals when Bao-yu suddenly began shouting angrily in his sleep.

‘How can you believe what monks and Taoists say? What is this marriage of gold and jade? I say it’s a marriage of wood and stone!’

Bao-chai was left staring. Just then Aroma came back.

‘Not awake yet?’ she asked, smiling.

Bao-chai shook her head.

‘I’ve just met Miss Lin and Miss Shi,’ Aroma went on. ‘Did they come in?’

‘I didn’t see them come in.’ Bao-chai smiled at her. ‘Didn’t they tell you anything?’

Aroma smiled. ‘Only their usual jokes. What serious thing would they have to say?’

‘It wasn’t a joke. I was just going to tell you myself, but you went hurrying off.’

She had not finished speaking when someone arrived from Xi-feng to summon Aroma.

‘That’s what it’s about,’ said Bao-chai, smiling.

Aroma had to wake two of the maids. She left the House of Green Delights with Bao-chai, then went on to Xi-feng’s. Sure enough, Xi-feng told her about the new arrangement and directed her to kowtow to Lady Wang, but said she need not go to Grandmother Jia. Aroma was quite embarrassed. She hurried back after seeing Lady Wang and found Bao-yu awake. When he asked why she had been sent for, she gave him only a vague answer. Not until the house was quiet that night did she tell him.

Beside himself with delight, he said, smiling, ‘Now let’s see whether you go home! You went home once, and when you came back you said your brother wanted to buy you out. You said you had no settled place here, and what would it all come to in the end? You frightened me with all those cold, heartless words, as though we were strangers. From now on, I’d like to see who dares take you away.’

Aroma gave a cold laugh. ‘You’d better not talk like that. From now on I belong to Her Ladyship. If I want to leave, I don’t even have to tell you. I need only ask her, and go.’

‘Even supposing I behaved badly and you asked Mother’s leave and went,’ Bao-yu said, smiling, ‘once people heard you’d left because I was no good, you wouldn’t be very happy about it either.’

Aroma smiled. ‘Why shouldn’t I be? If you turned thief and robber, would I have to follow you? And if there were nothing else, there would still be death. Live a hundred years, you have to die all the same. Once the breath is gone, you neither hear nor see any more, and that’s that.’

Bao-yu hurriedly put a hand over her mouth. ‘Enough, enough! Don’t say things like that.’

Aroma knew his peculiar nature well. He disliked flattering, auspicious talk as hollow and untrue, yet such unreserved truths made him sorrowful. Regretting her rashness, she quickly smiled and turned the conversation, asking him only about the things he most enjoyed discussing. First came spring breezes and autumn moons, then the soft bloom of powder and the lustre of rouge, then the goodness of girls. When the talk came round to girls dying, Aroma hurriedly stopped herself. Bao-yu was in full flow. Seeing her fall silent, he smiled.

‘Who doesn’t die? What matters is to die well. Those filthy bearded creatures know only this: a civil official dies remonstrating with his sovereign, a soldier dies in battle. These, they say, are the two deaths by which a man wins honour and keeps his integrity. How much better not to die at all! There has to be a benighted ruler before the official can remonstrate. He thinks only of winning a name, throws away his life, and where does that leave his sovereign? There has to be a war before the soldier can fight. He throws away his life, thinking only of the glory he will win in battle, and where does that leave his country?

‘Neither is a proper death.’

‘Loyal ministers and good generals only die when they have no choice,’ Aroma said.

‘The generals merely rely on hot-blooded courage. They have no plans, no strategy. They lose their lives through their own incompetence. Is that having no choice? The civil officials are worse still. They have read a couple of lines and let them foul their minds. Let the court show the slightest fault and they babble out their advice, thinking only of a reputation for loyalty and sacrifice. Up comes a surge of that filthy spirit, and they rush to their deaths. Is that having no choice? They ought to know that the sovereign receives his mandate from Heaven. If he were neither wise nor benevolent, Heaven and earth would never entrust all those weighty affairs to him. Clearly, those who die like that are buying a reputation; they know nothing of the greater duty. Take me, now. If I were truly fortunate, this would be the time for me to die, while all of you are here. If the tears you shed for me could flow into a great river, float my body away and carry it somewhere lonely, where not even a crow or sparrow ever came, and I could dissolve on the wind and never be born human again, then I should have died at the right time.’

Faced with this sudden outpouring of wild talk, Aroma said she was sleepy and paid him no more attention.

Only then did Bao-yu close his eyes and go to sleep. By the next day the matter had been put aside.

One day, tired of wandering everywhere, Bao-yu thought of the songs in The Peony Pavilion. He read them through twice, but still felt unsatisfied. Having heard that Ling-guan, who played young female roles among the twelve girls at Pear-tree Court, sang them better than anyone, he made a point of going out through the side gate to find her. Bao-guan and Yu-guan were in the courtyard. When they saw him, both smilingly offered him a seat.

‘Where is Ling-guan?’ he asked.

‘In her room,’ they told him.

He hurried in. Ling-guan was lying alone against a pillow. When he entered, she did not stir.

Bao-yu was so used to playing with the other girls that he assumed Ling-guan would be just like them. He went over and sat beside her, then coaxed her with a smile to get up and sing the suite beginning ‘Gossamer drifts in the clear air’. To his surprise, the moment he sat down she raised herself and moved away.

‘My voice is hoarse,’ she said gravely. ‘When Her Imperial Highness sent for us the other day, I didn’t even sing for her.’

Now that she was sitting upright, Bao-yu looked more closely and recognised the girl who had been tracing ‘qiang’ beneath the roses. He had never been shunned like this before. Flushing with embarrassment at her reception, he could only leave. Bao-guan and the others, puzzled, asked what had happened. He told them and was going out when Bao-guan said, ‘Wait just a little. When Master Qiang comes, if he asks her to sing, she certainly will.’

Puzzled by this, Bao-yu asked, ‘Where has Qiang gone?’

‘He’s just gone out. Ling-guan must want something again, and he’s off finding a way to get it for her.’

Bao-yu thought this remarkable. He had been standing there only a little while when Jia Qiang came in from outside, carrying a birdcage with a miniature stage fastened to it and a bird inside. He was hurrying eagerly in to find Ling-guan, but had to stop when he saw Bao-yu.

‘What sort of bird is it?’ Bao-yu asked. ‘Can it carry flags and run about a stage?’

‘A Jade-crowned Golden Bean,’ said Jia Qiang, smiling.

‘What did you pay for it?’

‘One tael and eight qian of silver.’

As he spoke, he invited Bao-yu to sit down, then went into Ling-guan’s room.

Bao-yu had now lost all interest in hearing songs. He wanted to see how Jia Qiang and Ling-guan behaved together. Jia Qiang went in smiling.

‘Get up and look at this toy.’

Ling-guan sat up and asked what it was.

‘I’ve bought you a bird to play with, so you won’t be bored every day with nothing to cheer you up. I’ll show you how it works.’

With a little grain he coaxed the bird to scamper about the stage, carrying masks and flags in its beak.

‘How amusing!’ the other girls all laughed.

Ling-guan alone gave a couple of cold laughs and lay down again in a temper. Jia Qiang kept smiling ingratiatingly and asking whether she liked it.

‘Your family brings perfectly good people here and shuts them up in this prison to learn that wretched trade. Isn’t that enough? Now you must get a bird and make it do exactly the same thing. You’ve plainly brought it here to mimic us and make fun of us, and then you ask me if I like it!’

Jia Qiang was alarmed. He hastily swore oaths on his own life.

‘What scented grease got into my head today?’ he went on. ‘I spent a tael or two on it to amuse you. I never thought of that. All right, all right! I’ll set it free, and may that spare you illness and misfortune.’

He did indeed release the bird, then pulled the cage to pieces.

But Ling-guan went on. ‘A bird may not be a person, but it too has an old bird waiting in its nest. How could you have the heart to catch it and make it do that wretched work? I coughed up two mouthfuls of blood today. Her Ladyship sent for the doctor to see me, and instead of asking him properly about it for me, you bring this thing here to make fun of me. No one cares about me, no one looks after me, and I have to be ill as well!’

She began to cry.

‘I asked the doctor last night,’ Jia Qiang said hurriedly. ‘He said it wasn’t serious. He said you were to take two doses of medicine and he’d see you again the day after tomorrow. How was I to know you’d bring up blood again today? I’ll go and fetch him now.’

He was about to go when Ling-guan called him back.

‘Stop! The sun’s vicious out there now. If you go storming off to fetch him, I won’t see him when he comes.’

Jia Qiang had to stop where he was. Bao-yu watched, spellbound. Only now did he understand the deeper meaning of her tracing ‘qiang’. Unable to remain, he slipped away. Jia Qiang’s whole attention was on Ling-guan, and he did not see him out. The other girls did that instead.2

Weighing it all over in his mind, Bao-yu returned in a daze to the House of Green Delights, where Dai-yu and Aroma were sitting talking. As soon as he came in he gave Aroma a long sigh.

‘What I said last night was wrong. No wonder Father says I see the sky through a tube and measure the sea with a ladle. Last night I said your tears would all be for my burial. That was wrong. I can’t have them all. From now on, each of us can only have our own share of tears.’

To Aroma, last night’s talk had been mere nonsense, already forgotten. She had not expected him to bring it up again.

‘You really are a little mad,’ she said with a laugh.

Bao-yu made no reply. From then on, he understood profoundly that each person had an allotted share in the bonds of love. Yet he would often grieve in secret: ‘Who will be there one day to shed tears at my burial?’

These were things Bao-yu carried in his heart, and we must not presume to guess too freely at them. Dai-yu, seeing him in this state, knew that he had fallen under some new spell somewhere, but could hardly question him closely.

‘I’ve just heard from Aunt Wang that tomorrow is Aunt Xue’s birthday,’ she said. ‘She asked me to find out, if I came this way, whether you’re going. Send someone to the front to let her know.’

‘I didn’t even go to Uncle She’s birthday last time. If I go to this one, what if I meet someone? I’m not going to any of them. It’s so hot, and there would be all that dressing up. Aunt Xue won’t necessarily be offended if I don’t go.’

‘What a thing to say!’ Aroma put in quickly. ‘It isn’t the same as your elder uncle. She lives so close, and she’s your aunt. If you don’t go, won’t she start wondering? If you’re afraid of the heat, go first thing in the morning, kowtow to her, have a cup of tea and come back. Wouldn’t that be the proper thing?’

Before Bao-yu could answer, Dai-yu smiled. ‘You ought to go, if only for the sake of someone who chases mosquitoes away for you.’

Bao-yu did not understand. ‘What do you mean, chases mosquitoes?’

Aroma told him how, while he was asleep the day before with no one to sit beside him, Miss Bao had stayed for a little while.

‘That was wrong of me,’ Bao-yu said at once. ‘How could I lie there asleep? It was disrespectful to her.’ Then he added, ‘I shall certainly go tomorrow.’

They were still talking when Shi Xiang-yun appeared, neatly dressed, to say goodbye. Her family had sent to fetch her. Bao-yu and Dai-yu rose quickly and offered her a seat, but she would not sit down, so the two could only accompany her to the front. Xiang-yun’s eyes were full of tears. With her own servants standing by, however, she did not dare show too much distress.

Presently Bao-chai hurried over, and parting became harder than ever. Bao-chai, though, understood that if Xiang-yun’s servants reported this to her aunt, she might suffer for it when she got home, so she urged her to go. They all saw her to the inner gate. Bao-yu wanted to go farther, but Xiang-yun stopped him. A moment later she turned and called him close, then whispered, ‘Even if the old lady forgets about me, you must keep reminding her to send for me.’

Bao-yu promised again and again. Only after they had watched her get into the carriage and drive away did they all go back inside. To learn what followed, listen to the next chapter.
