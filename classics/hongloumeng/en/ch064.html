Chapter 64 A secluded maiden writes her grief in Songs of Five Beauties; A libertine pledges love with a nine-dragon pendant

Chapter end

Now that everything at home was ready, Jia Rong hurried back to the temple to report to Jia Zhen. All that night they assigned the various duties to the servants and prepared the funeral banners, carrying-poles and everything else required. The hour of the Hare on the fourth day of the lunar month was chosen for bringing the coffin into the city, and messages went out to their relations and friends. On the appointed day the funeral procession was a splendid spectacle, with guests in their multitudes. From the Temple of the Iron Threshold to the Ning mansion, the crowds lining the road numbered tens of thousands and more. Some sighed; others envied. There were also half-educated scholars quoting, ‘In funeral rites, better frugal grief than lavish ease.’ All along the route the talk ran on, with everyone having a different opinion.

It was not until between the hours of the Goat and Monkey that they arrived. The coffin was set down in the main hall. When the offerings and formal lamentations were over, the friends and relations gradually dispersed, leaving members of the clan to share the duties of receiving and seeing off the guests. Of the close relations, only Lady Xing’s elder brother stayed to keep them company. Bound by the rules of mourning, Jia Zhen and Jia Rong had to lie on straw beside the coffin with lumps of earth for pillows, resenting the rigours of mourning. Once everyone had gone, they still found chances to slip away and carry on with the young You sisters. Bao-yu too wore mourning in the Ning mansion each day, returning to the garden only after the guests had left in the evening. Xi-feng was not yet well and could not often be there, but on days when an altar was opened for the chanting of scriptures, or friends and relations came to make offerings, she would struggle over to help You-shi with the arrangements.

One day, after the morning meal offering, Jia Zhen and the others, worn out by their exertions of the past several days, dozed beside the coffin. The days were still long at this season. Seeing that no visitors were coming, Bao-yu decided to go home and see Dai-yu, first returning to the House of Green Delights. The courtyard was silent and deserted. A few old women and little maids had found convenient places to cool themselves under the covered walk; some were lying asleep, others nodding where they sat. He did not disturb them. Only Si-er saw him and hurried forward to lift the door-curtain. Just as she raised it, Parfumée came running out with a laughing face and nearly collided with him. At the sight of Bao-yu she stopped short, still smiling.

‘How did you get here? Quick, stop Skybright for me. She wants to hit me!’

Before she had finished, there was a clatter and a rattle inside, as though something had spilled all over the floor. Skybright came after her, scolding:

‘Let’s see where you can run to, you little hussy! You’ve lost, and you won’t take your beating.

‘Bao-yu’s out. Who’s going to save you now?’

Bao-yu hastily barred her way, smiling.

‘She’s only your little sister. Whatever she’s done to offend you, forgive her for my sake.’

Skybright had not expected him back at this hour either. Taken by surprise, she could not help laughing.

‘Parfumée must be a fox-spirit in disguise! A spell to summon spirits and command their captains couldn’t have worked so fast.’

Then she laughed again.

‘I’m not afraid of you, even if you really have called down a god!’

She pulled her hand free and tried once more to catch Parfumée, who was already hiding behind Bao-yu. Taking Skybright by one hand and Parfumée by the other, he led them indoors. On the kang to the west, Musk, Ripple, Emerald and Purple Silk were playing jacks for melon seeds. Parfumée had lost to Skybright, refused to let her hit her and run away. Skybright, giving chase, had spilled the jacks she was holding against her bosom all over the floor.

‘Good!’ said Bao-yu happily. ‘These days are so long. When I’m out, I’m always afraid you’ll get lonely and do nothing but eat and sleep till you make yourselves ill. It’s much better to find something to play at and amuse yourselves.’

Not seeing Aroma, he asked:

‘Where’s your sister Aroma?’

‘Aroma?’ said Skybright. ‘She’s getting holier by the day. She’s in her room, sitting alone and facing the wall. I haven’t been in for some time, so I don’t know what she’s doing. There isn’t a sound. Go and look. Perhaps she’s attained enlightenment by now.’

Laughing, Bao-yu went into the inner room. Aroma was sitting on the bed near the window, knotting a length of grey cord. She stood up at once when he came in.

‘What stories has that creature Skybright been telling you?’ she said with a smile. ‘I wanted to finish these knots and hadn’t time to fool about with them, so I put them off. “Go and play,” I said. “While Master Bao’s out, I’m going to sit quietly here and rest my mind.” So she’s made up all this nonsense about me: “facing the wall” and “meditating”. Just wait till I tear that mouth of hers!’

Bao-yu sat down close beside her, smiling, and watched her knotting.

‘On such a long day, you ought to rest too, or have some fun with the others. Or you could go and see Cousin Lin. It’s terribly hot. What’s this for?’

‘I noticed that your fan-case is still the one we made the year of young Mrs Rong’s funeral over at the other house.

‘You only use that dark-blue thing when there’s a death in the clan or among our friends and relations in summer, once or twice a year, so ordinarily it’s hardly worth making one. But now there’s mourning at the other house, you’ll be going over and wearing it every day. That’s why I’m hurrying to make another. Once I’ve finished knotting this cord, I’ll take off the old one and put this on. You may not mind about such things, but if Her Old Ladyship came home and saw it, she’d say we were shirking and couldn’t even be bothered to look after what you wear.’

‘How good of you to think of it,’ said Bao-yu, smiling.

‘Only don’t hurry too much. It would be far worse if you made yourself ill with the heat.’

As he spoke, Parfumée brought him a cup of tea newly cooled in water. Bao-yu had always had a delicate constitution, and even in the hottest months they dared not give him ice. Instead they stood the teapot in a basin of freshly drawn well-water, changing the water from time to time, merely to take the warmth out of the tea. He drank half a cup from Parfumée’s hand.

‘Before I came away,’ he told Aroma, ‘I told Tealeaf to send word at once if any important guest came to Cousin Zhen’s. Unless there’s something that needs me, I shan’t go back.’

He left the room, then turned to Emerald and the others.

‘If I’m wanted, come and find me at Miss Lin’s.’

With that he went straight towards the Naiad’s House to see Dai-yu.

Just beyond Drenched Blossoms Bridge he met Snowgoose leading two old women, all carrying water caltrops, lotus roots, melons and fruit.

‘Your mistress never eats these cold things,’ he said. ‘What does she want all this fruit for? Is she inviting one of the young ladies, or one of the married ladies?’

‘I’ll tell you,’ Snowgoose said with a smile, ‘but you mustn’t tell her.’

Bao-yu nodded his promise. Snowgoose told the two women to take the fruit ahead and give it to Nightingale.

‘If she asks where I am, say I’ve something to do and I’m coming.’

The women said they would and went on. Only then did Snowgoose explain.

‘Miss Lin has just been feeling a little better these last two days. After our meal today, Third Miss came to ask her to go and see Mrs Lian, but she didn’t go. Something must have come into her mind, for she began to grieve all by herself. Then she took up her brush and wrote a great deal. I don’t know whether it was poems or lyrics. When she told me to fetch the fruit, I heard her tell Nightingale to clear the ornaments off the little zither table in her room and move it into the middle of the outer room. Then she wanted the little dragon-patterned tripod vessel put on it, ready for when the fruit came.

‘If she were inviting someone, she wouldn’t need to get an incense burner out first. And if it’s for burning incense, well, she generally has fresh flowers and fruit and quinces in her room, and she’s not much for perfuming her clothes. Even if she did burn incense, she’d do it where she usually sits or lies down. Unless the old women have made the room smell, and she wants incense to sweeten it! I really don’t know what it’s for.’

She hurried away as soon as she had finished.

Bao-yu lowered his head and thought it over.

‘From what Snowgoose says, there must be a reason. She wouldn’t set out all the vessels for food beforehand merely to sit and talk with one of the girls. Perhaps it’s the anniversary of my uncle’s or my aunt’s death. But every year on those days Grandmother has special dishes prepared and sent over for Cousin Lin to make a private offering, and I remember that those dates have already passed. Perhaps it’s because this is the seventh month, the season of melons and fruit, when every household visits its graves for the autumn offerings. That may have stirred her feelings, and she’s making an offering in her own room, in the spirit of the Book of Rites: “In spring and autumn, present the foods of the season.” That could be it. But if I go now and find her grieving, I’m bound to do all I can to comfort her, and then her sorrow may be shut up inside her. If I don’t go, she may grieve too much, with no one there to restrain her. Either way, she could make herself ill. I’d better go and see Cousin Feng first, sit with her a little and then come back. If Cousin Lin is still distressed, I’ll find some way to comfort her. She won’t have grieved too much, but she’ll have had some relief for her sorrow, instead of bottling it up and becoming ill.’

Having made up his mind, he went out of the garden and straight to Xi-feng’s rooms.

A crowd of women with household duties had just finished reporting to her and were coming away. Xi-feng was leaning against the doorway, talking to Patience. She smiled when she saw Bao-yu.

‘So you’re back! I’ve just told Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife to send word to your pages: if there’s nothing to keep you, they’re to ask you to come home and rest when you have the chance. Besides, with all those people over there, how could you stand the smells? And here you are already.’

‘Thank you for thinking of me, cousin,’ said Bao-yu. ‘I had nothing to do today, and I noticed you hadn’t been over for the last two days. I didn’t know whether you were much better, so I came back to see.’

‘It’s much the same. Three good days and two bad ones. With Grandmother and Her Ladyship away, these good women of ours! Is there one of them who’ll behave herself? Every day it’s either fighting or quarrelling, and now we’ve even had two or three cases of gambling and theft. Cousin Tan helps with the management, of course, but she’s still an unmarried girl.

‘Some things she can be told, and some things you simply can’t tell her. I can only force myself to keep going. I never have a moment’s peace. Getting better! I’m thankful if I don’t get worse.’

‘All the same, cousin, you must look after yourself and try to worry less,’ Bao-yu said.

They chatted a little longer. Then he took his leave and went straight back into the garden.

When he entered the courtyard of the Naiad’s House, the last threads of smoke were curling from the incense burner, and fine sweet wine remained from the libation. Nightingale was watching the servants carry the table back indoors and put the ornaments away. Bao-yu knew that the offering was over. He went into the room and found Dai-yu lying with her face turned inwards, languid and ill, as though she could scarcely bear her weakness.

‘Master Bao’s here,’ Nightingale announced at once.

Dai-yu slowly sat up and invited him to sit down, with a smile.

‘Are you much better these last two days, cousin?’ Bao-yu asked. ‘You do look a little more rested. But why have you been grieving again?’

‘What nonsense! I’ve been perfectly all right. When have I been grieving?’

‘There are still tear-marks on your face,’ he said, smiling. ‘How can you expect to deceive me? You’re so often ill as it is. Whatever happens, you ought to find some comfort for yourself, instead of giving way to useless sorrow.

‘If you ruin your health, you’ll make me…’

He broke off, suddenly aware that what followed would be difficult to say. Though he and Dai-yu had grown up together, though their hearts were in accord and they wished to live and die together, this was an understanding held within. They had never spoken of it face to face. Besides, Dai-yu was sensitive, and a careless word of his had often offended her.

Today he had come expressly to comfort her, yet here he was speaking carelessly again, unable to finish. He grew anxious, afraid she would be angry. Then, reflecting that his concern was entirely sincere, his anxiety turned to grief, and tears rolled down his cheeks. Dai-yu had at first resented his failure to weigh his words. Now the sight of him moved her. Always ready to weep, she could not help joining him, and the two faced each other in silent tears.

Nightingale brought in the tea. Supposing that they had been quarrelling again, she said:

‘Miss Lin’s only just beginning to get better, and here you are upsetting her again, Master Bao. What is the matter with you?’

‘Who would dare upset her?’ Bao-yu said, wiping his eyes and smiling.

To cover his embarrassment he got up and wandered about. A corner of paper was showing beneath the inkstone. He could not resist picking it up. Dai-yu tried to rise and snatch it from him, but he had already tucked it inside his gown.

‘Dear cousin, do let me read it!’ he pleaded, smiling.

‘Whatever it is, you have to rummage through it the moment you arrive.’

Before she had finished, Bao-chai came in.

‘What is it you want to read, Cousin Bao?’ she asked with a smile.

Bao-yu had not yet seen what was written on the paper and did not know what Dai-yu thought about it. Not daring to answer rashly, he merely looked at her and smiled. As Dai-yu invited Bao-chai to sit down, she explained:

‘I’ve read about a great many women in the old histories who were gifted and beautiful. Some had lives one could rejoice in or envy; others had lives to grieve and sigh over.

‘I had nothing to do after our meal today, so I thought I’d choose a few of them and put together some verses to express what I felt. Tan came just then to ask me to go and see Cousin Feng, but I was feeling languid and didn’t go with her. I’d only finished five poems when I grew tired and put them down there. I didn’t expect our young master to come in and find them. There’s really no harm in letting him read them. What I dislike is his copying things out for other people at every opportunity.’

‘When have I shown them to anyone?’ Bao-yu protested. ‘As for that fan yesterday, I was fond of those poems on the white crab-apple blossom, so I copied them onto it in small regular script for myself. It was only to have them ready to read in my hand. I know perfectly well that a young lady’s poems and handwriting aren’t to be passed about outside. Since you spoke to me, I’ve never taken it out of the garden.’

‘Cousin Lin is right to be concerned,’ said Bao-chai. ‘Once you’ve written them on a fan, you might forget and carry it into the study. If the gentlemen there saw it, wouldn’t they be sure to ask who wrote them?

‘If they were to get about, it would do us no credit. As the old saying has it, “For a woman, lack of talent is virtue.” A quiet and chaste disposition comes first; even needlework is only second. As for verses, they’re merely a pastime for women in their own rooms. It doesn’t matter whether we can write them or not. Girls from families like ours have no need of a reputation for literary talent.’

Then she turned to Dai-yu with a smile.

‘There’s no harm in letting me see them. Only don’t let Cousin Bao take them away.’

‘In that case,’ said Dai-yu, smiling, ‘perhaps you needn’t read them either.’

She pointed at Bao-yu and laughed.

‘He’s already snatched them away.’

Only then did Bao-yu take the paper from inside his gown and draw close to Bao-chai so that they could read it carefully together. This was what they found:

Xi Shi

The beauty of an age vanished with the foam; In Wu’s palace they remembered her in vain.

Mock not the eastern girl who aped her frown: White-haired, she still washed silk beside the stream.

Lady Yu

Black Steed cries to the night wind; her heart breaks. Her secret grief confronts the double-pupilled lord.

Qing Bu and Peng Yue bowed to later butchery; Better her death by the sword in Chu’s own tent.

Ming-fei (Wang Zhao-jun)

Her stunning beauty left the Han palace; Beauty meets cruel fate in every age.

Even if beauty mattered little to the king, Why let a painter grant or take his favour?

Green Pearl

Rubble and pearl alike were flung away: When did Commandant Shi prize loveliness?

Only a fool’s good fortune, earned in a former life, Won company in death to soothe his loneliness.

Red Whisk

Li Jing’s bow and bold speech proclaimed his worth; Her far-seeing eyes knew greatness in distress.

Lord Yang, a breathing corpse within his tent: How could he tether such a heroine?

Bao-yu could not praise them enough.

‘You’ve written exactly five, cousin. Why not call them Songs of Five Beauties?’

Without allowing any argument, he took up a brush and wrote the title at the end. Bao-chai too spoke in their praise.

‘Whatever the subject of a poem, what matters is finding a fresh turn to the thoughts of earlier writers. Follow in someone else’s footsteps, and however polished your language may be, you’ve already fallen into the second rank. In the end it isn’t good poetry. Take the many poems people have written about Zhao-jun. Some lament her fate, some blame Yan-shou, and others mock the Han emperor for having his painter portray beauties instead of worthy ministers. There are all sorts of approaches. Later Wang Jing-gong wrote:

“Her living grace no painter could have caught; They killed Mao Yan-shou then, and all for nothing.”

‘And Yong-shu wrote:

“What reached his ears and eyes he judged so ill: How could he rule barbarians ten thousand li away?”

‘Each found a view of his own, unlike anyone else’s. Cousin Lin’s five poems today may also be said to have fresh conceptions and to open up new ground.’1

She was about to continue when someone came to report:

‘Master Lian is back. Word came in from outside just now. He’s been at the eastern mansion for some time, so he should be here soon.’

Bao-yu rose at once and went to wait just inside the main gate. As it happened, Jia Lian was dismounting outside and came in at that moment. Bao-yu knelt before him, asked after Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the rest, and then paid his respects to Jia Lian himself. They walked in hand in hand. Li Wan, Xi-feng, Bao-chai, Dai-yu, Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun were already waiting in the central hall. They all exchanged greetings.

Jia Lian told them:

‘Grandmother will be home early tomorrow. She’s been very well all the way. She sent me ahead today to see how things were at home. I have to go back out of the city to meet her in the fifth watch tomorrow.’

They asked some more questions about the journey. Since Jia Lian had come a long way, they then took their leave and let him go to his rooms to rest. There is no need to describe the passing of that night.

The next day, at about the time of the morning meal, Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the others duly arrived. After everyone had welcomed them, the old lady sat a little while and drank a cup of tea, then took Lady Wang and the others over to the Ning mansion. The sound of weeping inside rose to the skies. Jia She and Jia Lian had come straight over as soon as they had brought Grandmother Jia home. Now, as she entered, they led the members of the clan out to meet her, all in tears. Father and son supported her, one on each side, as she went up to the coffin. Jia Zhen and Jia Rong knelt and flung themselves into her arms, sobbing bitterly. At her great age, the sight was more than she could bear. She held them both and wept without restraint. Only after Jia She and Jia Lian had pleaded long with her did she quieten a little. Then, moving to the right of the coffin, she saw You-shi and her daughter-in-law. They clung to one another and wept bitterly all over again. When the weeping was over, everyone came forward in turn to pay their respects and ask after her.

Jia Zhen reflected that Grandmother Jia had only just returned and had had no rest. If she stayed there with everything before her eyes, she was bound to grieve. Again and again he begged her to go home. Lady Wang and the others urged her too, until at last she reluctantly agreed. Sure enough, at her age she could not withstand exposure and sorrow. That night her head felt heavy, her eyes ached, her nose was blocked and her voice thickened. A doctor was sent for in haste to take her pulse and prescribe. The household was kept in turmoil for half a night and a whole day. Fortunately the illness was dispersed quickly, before it passed into the channels. By the third watch she had sweated a little; her pulse was calm and her skin cool. Only then did everyone feel easier. The following day she continued taking medicine to recover her strength.

Several more days passed, and the day came for Jia Jing’s funeral procession. Grandmother Jia was still far from well, so Bao-yu remained at home to attend her. Xi-feng had not recovered either and did not go. Jia She, Jia Lian, Lady Xing, Lady Wang and the others led the servants, men and women, in accompanying the coffin to the Temple of the Iron Threshold. They did not return until evening. Jia Zhen, You-shi and Jia Rong stayed at the temple to keep vigil. Only after the hundred days were over would they escort the coffin back to the family’s native place. The house was again left in the care of Old Mrs You, Er-jie and San-jie.

Jia Lian had long heard of the You sisters and regretted that he had had no chance to see them. With Jia Jing’s coffin at the house, he had lately met Er-jie and San-jie every day and become well acquainted with them. He could not help lusting after them. He knew, too, of the long-standing taunts about Jia Zhen and Jia Rong sharing them like stags covering the same does. He therefore took every opportunity to flirt and exchange inviting glances. San-jie remained cool towards him, but Er-jie was very willing. There were too many eyes about, however, for him to attempt anything. He was afraid of making Jia Zhen jealous as well, and dared not act rashly. For the time being, he and Er-jie had to content themselves with an unspoken understanding.

Now that the coffin had been taken away, Jia Zhen’s house was almost empty. Only Old Mrs You, Er-jie and San-jie, with a few maids and old women for the rough work, remained in the principal rooms. All the other maids and concubines had gone to the temple. The men and women servants outside merely made their rounds at night and guarded the gates by day. Unless there was business, they did not come into the inner rooms during the daytime. Jia Lian was eager to seize his chance. On the pretext of keeping Jia Zhen company, he too lodged at the temple, and from time to time came into the Ning mansion, ostensibly to manage household business for him, but really to pursue Er-jie.

One day a junior steward, Yu Lu, came to report to Jia Zhen.

‘The awnings, carrying-poles and mourning cloth, together with the hire of bearers and blue-clad attendants, cost one thousand one hundred and ten taels altogether. Five hundred taels have been paid, leaving six hundred and ten still owing. Yesterday both firms sent people to press for payment. I’ve come to ask for your instructions, sir.’

‘Draw it from the treasury,’ said Jia Zhen. ‘Why come and tell me about it?’

‘I went to the treasury yesterday, sir. But since His Lordship ascended to Heaven, so much has been drawn for all the different expenses, and what’s left has to cover the hundred-day services and the costs at the temple. They really can’t pay it out just now. That’s why I’ve come to you today. Perhaps you could advance it from your private funds, or borrow it from some other account. If you tell me what to do, I can see to it.’

Jia Zhen laughed.

‘You still think it’s like the old days, with silver lying about unused? Borrow it wherever you can and pay them.’

‘If it were one or two hundred, I could shift things about and borrow it,’ Yu Lu replied with a smile. ‘But where am I to find five or six hundred at a moment’s notice?’

Jia Zhen thought for a while, then turned to Jia Rong.

‘Go and ask your mother. After the procession yesterday, the Zhens from Jiangnan sent five hundred taels towards the funeral offerings. It hasn’t been paid into the treasury yet. Get that and give it to him for the time being.’

Jia Rong hurried over to ask You-shi, then returned to his father.

‘Two hundred taels of that money were spent yesterday. The remaining three hundred were sent home for Grandmother to keep.’

‘In that case, take him with you and get it from your grandmother. Hand it over to him.

‘See whether everything’s all right at home while you’re there, and ask after your two aunts. Yu Lu can borrow the rest to make it up.’

Jia Rong and Yu Lu had agreed and were just withdrawing when Jia Lian came in. Yu Lu hurried forward to pay his respects. Jia Lian asked what was happening, and Jia Zhen told him everything.

‘Here’s just the chance to go to the Ning mansion and find Er-jie,’ Jia Lian thought.

‘What a small matter!’ he said aloud. ‘Why borrow from anyone? Some silver came in for me yesterday, and I haven’t spent it yet. Let me make up the difference. Wouldn’t that save trouble?’

‘Excellent,’ said Jia Zhen. ‘Give Rong his orders and let him fetch it at the same time.’

‘I’ll have to fetch it myself,’ Jia Lian said hastily. ‘Besides, I haven’t been home for several days. I must pay my respects to Grandmother and to Their Lordships and Their Ladyships. I’ll look in at your house too, to see whether the servants have been making trouble, and call on Old Mrs You.’

‘Only I feel bad about troubling you again,’ said Jia Zhen, smiling.

‘Between brothers, what does it matter?’ Jia Lian answered with a smile.

Jia Zhen gave Jia Rong further instructions.

‘Go with your uncle, and pay your respects to Her Old Ladyship and Their Lordships and Their Ladyships over there. Give them my respects and your mother’s. Ask if Her Old Ladyship is quite well again.

‘Is she still taking medicine?’

Jia Rong agreed to everything. He followed Jia Lian out, and with a few pages they mounted and rode into the city together. Uncle and nephew chatted on the road. Jia Lian, with something on his mind, brought up You Er-jie. He praised her beauty, her character, her easy manners and gentle speech. There was nothing about her, he said, that did not invite respect and affection.

‘Everyone says how good your aunt is. To my mind, she hasn’t a fraction of your Second Aunt’s merits.’

Jia Rong guessed what he was after.

‘If you’re so fond of her, uncle,’ he said with a smile, ‘why shouldn’t I make the match and get her for you as a second wife?’

‘Are you joking, or do you mean it?’ Jia Lian asked, smiling.

‘I mean it.’

‘That would be fine,’ said Jia Lian, smiling again. ‘Only I’m afraid your aunt wouldn’t allow it. And your grandmother might not agree either. Besides, I’ve heard your Second Aunt is already promised to someone.’

‘None of that matters. My Second Aunt and Third Aunt aren’t my grandfather’s daughters. Grandmother brought them with her when she married him.

‘I’m told that while she was still in the other family, she promised Second Aunt to the Zhangs, the bailiffs of an imperial grain estate. The match was arranged before the children were born. Later the Zhang family got into a lawsuit and lost everything, and Grandmother remarried and left her former household. They haven’t been in touch for more than ten years now. Grandmother often complains about the match and wants to break it off. Father wants to find Second Aunt another husband too. We’re only waiting for a good family. Then all we need do is send someone to find the Zhangs, give them ten-odd taels and have them write a paper cancelling the betrothal. They’re desperately poor. Why should they refuse once they see the silver? Besides, they know what sort of family we are. There’s no fear of their refusing. And if it’s someone like you, uncle, wanting her for a second wife, I’ll guarantee that both Grandmother and Father will agree.

‘It’s only my aunt who’ll be difficult.’

By this point Jia Lian’s heart was flowering with delight. He could find nothing to say and merely grinned foolishly. Jia Rong thought a little longer, then smiled.

‘If you’ve the courage, uncle, follow my plan and I’ll guarantee there’ll be no trouble. It will only cost a little more.’

‘What is it? Tell me quickly. I’ll do whatever you say.’

‘When you go home, don’t let a word or a look betray you. Wait till I’ve told Father and settled everything with Grandmother. Then buy a house somewhere near the back of our mansion, with everything it needs, and send two households of servants over to attend her. Choose a day and marry her so quietly that neither man nor ghost will know. Tell the servants they’re not to let out a word. My aunt lives shut away in those great courts and high walls. How is she going to find out? You can live at both places. After six months or a year, even if it does come out, the worst you’ll get is a scolding from your father.

‘You need only say that my aunt hasn’t borne you any children and that you made this arrangement outside, without telling anyone, for the sake of the succession. Even she will have to let it pass when she sees the raw rice has been cooked. Then ask Her Old Ladyship to help, and there’s nothing that can’t be settled.’

As the old saying goes, desire clouds the judgment. Jia Lian, thinking only of possessing Er-jie’s beauty, listened to Jia Rong’s arguments and believed the plan could not fail. He put aside every objection: that he was still in mourning, that he would be taking another wife while leaving his present wife in place, that his father was strict and his wife jealous. He did not know that Jia Rong’s intentions were anything but kind. Jia Rong had long had an affair with his aunt, but Jia Zhen’s presence prevented him from having his full enjoyment of it. If Jia Lian married her, she would have to live outside the mansion, and Jia Rong meant to slip in for his own debaucheries whenever Jia Lian was away. Nothing of this occurred to Jia Lian.

‘My good nephew,’ he said gratefully, ‘if you really bring this off, I’ll buy you two girls of the rarest beauty to thank you.’

They had reached the Ning mansion’s gates as he spoke.

‘Go in, uncle, and get the silver from Grandmother,’ said Jia Rong. ‘Then give it to Yu Lu. I’ll go and pay my respects to Her Old Ladyship first.’

Jia Lian nodded with a smile.

‘Don’t tell her I came with you.’

‘I know.’

Then Jia Rong whispered in his ear:

‘If you see Second Aunt today, don’t be too impatient. If you make trouble now, it’ll be harder to arrange things later.’

‘Stop your nonsense and go!’ Jia Lian said, laughing. ‘I’ll wait for you here.’

Jia Rong went off to pay his respects to Grandmother Jia.

As Jia Lian entered the Ning mansion, the head servants came forward with the others to pay their respects and accompanied him in a crowd as far as the hall. He asked a few questions of each, merely to fulfil his duty, then dismissed them and went alone towards the inner rooms.

Jia Lian and Jia Zhen had always been close. They were cousins, and there was no one here from whom Jia Lian felt obliged to keep his distance. He never waited to be announced. As he approached the principal rooms, an old woman waiting under the verandah lifted the curtain and let him in. On the southern kang he found only You Er-jie, sewing with two maids. Old Mrs You and San-jie were nowhere to be seen. He went up at once to greet Er-jie and ask after her. Er-jie smiled and offered him a seat, then sat down beside the eastern partition. Jia Lian, however, yielded the place of honour to her. After a few polite remarks, he asked with a smile:

‘Where are your mother and your younger sister? I don’t see them.’

‘They’ve just gone to the back on some business,’ she replied, smiling. ‘They’ll be here soon.’

The maids had gone to pour tea, leaving no one near them. Jia Lian kept darting looks at Er-jie. With her head lowered, she only smiled and took no notice. He did not dare to begin touching her. Seeing her play with a silk handkerchief to which a purse was tied, he felt about his waist to make an opening.

‘I’ve forgotten to bring my betel purse. If you have any betel nut, cousin, spare me a piece.’

‘I have some,’ she said, ‘but I never let anyone eat mine.’

Smiling, Jia Lian began to move closer to take it. Afraid of the appearance it would make if anyone saw, Er-jie quickly smiled and tossed it over. He caught it, tipped out everything in the purse, picked up a half-chewed piece and put it in his mouth. The rest he tucked away inside his gown.

He was just about to carry the purse back to her himself when the two maids returned with the tea. Taking a cup and drinking, he secretly unfastened the Han jade pendant with nine dragons that he was wearing and tied it to the handkerchief. When the maids turned their heads, he tossed it back. Er-jie did not pick it up. She sat drinking her tea as though she had not seen it. Then a curtain rustled at the back, and Old Mrs You and San-jie came in with two little maids. Jia Lian signalled with his eyes for Er-jie to pick up the handkerchief, but she still took no notice. Not knowing what she meant, he grew very anxious. He could only go forward to greet Old Mrs You and San-jie. Looking back at Er-jie, he saw her smiling as though nothing had happened. He looked again for the handkerchief. It had vanished. Only then was he reassured.

When they had all sat down and exchanged a little conversation, Jia Lian said:

‘My cousin’s wife tells me a packet of silver was left with you for safekeeping the other day, ma’am. We need it to pay someone today, so Cousin Zhen sent me to fetch it. I was to see that everything was all right at home as well.’

Old Mrs You at once told Er-jie to take the key and fetch the silver. Jia Lian went on:

‘I wanted to pay my respects to you too, ma’am, and see the two young ladies. You’re looking well. Only I’m afraid the girls have had a hard time of it in our house.’

‘What a thing to say!’ Old Mrs You answered with a smile. ‘We’re all close relations. We have to live somewhere, whether it’s at home or here. To tell you the truth, Master Lian, since my late husband died, we’ve had a hard enough time making ends meet. It’s only your cousin’s help that has kept us going. Now that there’s been such a great bereavement in my son-in-law’s family, we can do nothing else to help. All we’re doing is looking after the house. What hardship is there in that?’

As she was speaking, Er-jie returned with the silver and handed it to her mother.

Old Mrs You passed it to Jia Lian. He told a little maid to call an old woman and gave her his orders:

‘Take this to Yu Lu. Tell him to carry it over to the other house and wait for me.’

The woman said she would and went out.

Just then they heard Jia Rong speaking in the courtyard. In a moment he came in, paid his respects to his grandmother and aunts, then turned to Jia Lian with a smile.

‘Your father was asking for you just now, uncle. He said there was something he wanted you to do. He was going to send someone to the temple, but I told him you’d be there soon. He said that if I met you on the way, I was to tell you to hurry.’

Jia Lian was about to rise when Jia Rong turned to his grandmother.

‘That uncle I told you about the other day, Grandmother, the one Father wants to find for Second Aunt: his face and figure are very like this uncle’s. What do you think? Would that be good?’

As he spoke he secretly pointed at Jia Lian and made a sign with his lips towards Er-jie. She was too embarrassed to say anything. San-jie, looking neither quite amused nor quite angry, began to scold him.

‘You rotten little monkey’s brat! Can’t you find anything else to wag that damned tongue about? Just wait till I tear your mouth!’

She went after him as she spoke. Jia Rong had already run outside, laughing. Jia Lian too took his leave with a smile. In the hall he warned the servants again against gambling and drinking, then quietly begged Jia Rong to speak to his father as soon as he got back. He took Yu Lu over to the other house, made up the full sum of silver and handed it to him to take away. Then he went to pay his respects to Jia She and afterwards to Grandmother Jia. Of that we need say no more.

Seeing that Yu Lu had gone with Jia Lian to fetch the silver, Jia Rong had nothing to keep him. He went back into the inner rooms and teased and flirted with his two aunts for a while before leaving. He reached the temple that evening and reported to Jia Zhen.

‘The silver’s been handed over to Yu Lu. Her Old Ladyship is quite well again and has stopped taking medicine.’

He then took the opportunity to mention Jia Lian’s wish, expressed on the road, to marry You Er-jie as his second wife. He explained how a house could be bought for her outside, without Xi-feng knowing.

‘It’s only because he has difficulty getting an heir. And he’s met Second Aunt. Strengthening a family tie is better than marrying someone from a family you know nothing about. That’s why Uncle Lian begged me so often to speak to you.’

He said nothing about the scheme being his own. Jia Zhen considered it, then smiled.

‘There’s no great harm in it, really.

‘Only I don’t know whether your Second Aunt would want it. Go and talk to your grandmother tomorrow. Let her find out for certain what your aunt thinks before we decide.’

He gave Jia Rong a long set of instructions, then went over to tell You-shi. She knew how improper the whole business was and tried hard to stop it. But Jia Zhen had made up his mind. She had always been accustomed to giving way to him; besides, she and Er-jie had not been born of the same mother, so she did not feel free to interfere too deeply. In the end she could only leave them to their folly.

Early next morning Jia Rong duly went into the city again to see his grandmother and tell her what his father proposed. He embroidered it with a great many words of his own: how good a man Jia Lian was; how Xi-feng was ill and could never recover; how they would buy a house for the present and live outside, and in six months or a year, once Xi-feng was dead, Second Aunt would be brought into the mansion as the principal wife. Then he described the betrothal gifts his father would provide, the wedding Jia Lian would arrange, how the old lady would be taken in and cared for in her old age, and how they would undertake to find Third Aunt a husband too. He made it all sound so wonderful that Old Mrs You could hardly refuse. Besides, she had long depended entirely on Jia Zhen’s support. Now he was arranging the match himself, she would have no trousseau to buy, and Jia Lian was a young gentleman of wealth and family, ten times better than Zhang Hua. She hurried to consult Er-jie.

Er-jie was a woman of easy virtue. She had already had improper relations with her brother-in-law, and had often regretted the mistaken betrothal to Zhang Hua, which had left her with no prospect of a settled life. Now she saw that Jia Lian wanted her, and her brother-in-law himself was arranging the marriage. What objection could she have? She nodded her assent.

Her answer was taken to Jia Rong, who reported it to his father. The next day Jia Zhen sent for Jia Lian to come to the temple and told him in person that Old Mrs You had agreed. Jia Lian was beside himself with joy and could not thank father and son enough. He and Jia Zhen began to plan. Men were sent to look for a house and have jewellery made, and to buy Er-jie’s trousseau and the bed, curtains and other things needed in the new home. Within a few days everything was ready. A house of more than twenty rooms had been bought in Little Flower Lane, about two li behind Ning-Rong Street, and two little maids had been bought as well. Jia Zhen also provided a household of servants, Bao Er and his wife, to attend Er-jie when she moved in. When the couple heard of such a fine opportunity, how could they fail to come?

Men were also sent to summon Zhang Hua and his father and force them to write Old Mrs You a cancellation of the betrothal. Zhang Hua’s grandfather had been bailiff of an imperial grain estate. After his death, Zhang Hua’s father held the same post. He had been friendly with Old Mrs You’s former husband, and the match between Zhang Hua and You Er-jie had been arranged before they were born.

Later, unexpectedly, the family had become involved in a lawsuit and lost its property. They could barely clothe and feed themselves, let alone afford a wife for the son. Old Mrs You had married out of her former family, and the two households had been out of touch for more than ten years. Now, summoned by the Jia servants and forced to break off the match with Er-jie, Zhang Hua and his father were unwilling at heart. But they feared the power of Jia Zhen and his people and dared not refuse. They could only draw up the agreement cancelling the betrothal. Old Mrs You gave them twenty taels, and the engagement was ended. We need say no more of it.

With everything ready, Jia Lian and the others chose the third day of the lunar month, an auspicious day, to bring Er-jie home as his bride. The next chapter will tell of that. Truly:

Lust seized the kindred branches of one tree,

And armed the marriage boughs with spear and halberd.
