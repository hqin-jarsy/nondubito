Chapter 18 Imperial grace allows Consort Yuan to visit her parents; Amid the joys of family reunion, Bao-yu presents his verse

Chapter end

Bao-yu could think of nothing but getting back to the inner rooms. But Jia Zheng gave him no instructions, and he had to follow him to his study. Only then did his father remember him. ‘Why are you still here?’ he barked. ‘Have you not had enough of wandering about? After all this time, have you never thought how your grandmother must be worrying? Get inside. All her affection is wasted on you!’ Bao-yu was at last able to withdraw. Outside the courtyard, several of Jia Zheng’s pages came up and caught him round the waist. ‘You have us to thank for the master’s being so pleased today. Her Old Ladyship sent out several times to ask, and every time we told her he was pleased. If she had called you in, you would never have had your chance to shine. Everyone says those poems of yours were better than anything other people write. Now you have done so well, you ought to give us something.’ Bao-yu laughed. ‘A string of cash apiece.’ ‘As though we had never seen a string of cash! Give us that purse.’ One began untying his purse, another his fan-case, and before he could protest they had stripped off every ornament he wore.

‘Now see him safely in!’ they said. One picked him up, and the others crowded round and escorted him to the second gate of Grandmother Jia’s quarters. She had already sent out several times to look for him. His nurses and maids came to join him. When they had attended him into her presence and learnt that he had not been badly treated, they were naturally delighted.

Presently Aroma brought tea. Seeing that not one of his ornaments remained, she laughed. ‘Those shameless creatures have taken everything off you again.’ Lin Dai-yu came over to look. Sure enough, there was nothing left. ‘Did you give them the purse I made you too?’ she asked. ‘You need never expect anything else from me!’ She went angrily back to her room, seized the scent-bag she had half finished at his request a few days earlier, and took her scissors to it. Bao-yu knew he was in trouble the moment he saw her anger and hurried after her, but she had already cut it open. He had seen the bag before. Unfinished though it was, it was exquisitely made and had cost a great deal of work; to see it cut to pieces for no reason made him angry too. He hastily undid his collar, untied her purse from the front of the red jacket he wore underneath, and held it out. ‘Look! What do you call this?

‘When have I ever given anyone something you made me?’ Seeing how carefully he treasured it, wearing it inside his clothes evidently to keep anyone from taking it, Dai-yu regretted her rashness in cutting the scent-bag before she knew the truth. Ashamed and angry, she hung her head without a word. ‘There was no need to cut it up,’ said Bao-yu. ‘I know you cannot be bothered to make me anything. Shall I respectfully return this purse as well?’

He tossed it into her lap and turned to go. This made her angrier still. Her throat tightened, she could hardly breathe for sobbing, and tears rolled down her face. She took up the purse and began to cut that too. Bao-yu hurried back and snatched it away. ‘Dear cousin, do spare it!’ he said with a smile. Dai-yu flung down the scissors and wiped her eyes. ‘You need not be nice to me one moment and horrid the next. If you are angry, have done with me. What is the point of all this?’ She climbed angrily onto the bed and lay facing the wall, wiping her tears. Bao-yu came up, calling her his dear cousin over and over again, and would not stop apologising.

In the rooms in front, Grandmother Jia was loudly calling for Bao-yu. His nurses and maids hastened to answer, ‘He is in Miss Lin’s room.’ ‘Good, good, good!’ she said. ‘Let the cousins play together. His father has kept him under his thumb all this time; let him enjoy himself a little. Only do not let them quarrel, and she must not pinch him.’ They promised to see to it. At last Dai-yu could endure Bao-yu’s pestering no longer. She got up. ‘You mean to give me no peace, so I shall go away from you.’ She made for the door. ‘Wherever you go, I shall follow,’ he said, laughing.

As he spoke, he picked up the purse and put it on again. Dai-yu reached to snatch it. ‘You said you did not want it, and now you are wearing it again. I am ashamed for you!’ She gave a sudden little laugh. ‘Dear cousin, make me another scent-bag tomorrow,’ said Bao-yu. ‘That will depend entirely on whether I feel like it.’ Talking as they went, the two left her room for Lady Wang’s main apartments, where Bao-chai also happened to be.

Lady Wang’s quarters were a scene of great bustle. Jia Qiang had returned from Gusu with the twelve girls he had bought, the teachers he had engaged, and the theatrical costumes and other equipment. Aunt Xue had moved to a quiet set of rooms to the north-east. Pear-tree Court had long since been cleared and repaired, and the teachers were now to train the actresses there. Women of the household who had once learnt singing and performed themselves, but were now white-haired old women, had been appointed to supervise and look after them. Jia Qiang was put in charge of their daily expenditure and receipts, and the accounts for everything they required, large or small. Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife also came to report. ‘All ten of the young Buddhist and Taoist nuns we were to find and engage or buy are here, and the twenty sets of religious robes we ordered are ready too. There is one other young woman, practising the religious life without taking the tonsure. She comes from Suzhou, from a family of scholars and officials. She was always ill as a little girl. Her family bought any number of substitutes to enter religion in her place, but none did any good. Only when she entered the Buddhist life herself did she recover. That is why she practises without taking the tonsure. She is just eighteen this year, and her religious name is Adamantina.

‘Both her parents are dead now. She has only two old nurses and one little maid to look after her. She is thoroughly accomplished in literature and calligraphy, and needs no more lessons in the scriptures. She is very beautiful too. She heard that there were relics of Guanyin and ancient palm-leaf scriptures in Chang’an, so she came up with her teacher last year. She is now living at the Muni Convent outside the western gate.

‘Her teacher was exceptionally skilled in the divine calculations of the Prior Heaven, but passed away last winter. Adamantina meant to escort her teacher’s coffin home. Before dying, however, her teacher told her, “For the ordering of your daily life, you ought not to return home. Remain here quietly. In time your future will take its course.”

‘So she has not gone back.’ Before she could finish her report, Lady Wang said, ‘In that case, why not bring her here?’ ‘We did invite her,’ Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife replied. ‘She said, “In the houses of marquises and dukes they are sure to use their rank and power to bully people. I will never go.”’ ‘As the daughter of an official, she is naturally rather proud,’ said Lady Wang. ‘What harm would there be in sending her a proper invitation?’ Lin Zhi-xiao’s wife agreed and went out to have one of the secretaries write an invitation to Adamantina. The carriage and sedan sent to fetch her the following day, and what followed, must be left for later; we cannot relate it now. At this point someone reported that the builders were waiting for gauze and figured silk to cover things, and asked Xi-feng to unlock the upper storeroom and select some. Someone else wanted her to open the treasury to receive the gold and silver vessels. Neither Lady Wang nor any of the maids in the main apartments had a moment to spare. ‘Let us get out from under their feet and find Tan-chun,’ said Bao-chai. She took Bao-yu and Dai-yu off to amuse themselves in Ying-chun’s rooms with the other girls, and we need say no more of them for the present.

Lady Wang and the others remained busy day after day until, towards the end of the tenth month, everything was at last ready. The supervisors had settled all their accounts; the antiques and scholars’ curios had been arranged everywhere. Those responsible for buying birds and animals had obtained everything, from cranes and peacocks to deer, rabbits, chickens and geese, and delivered them to suitable places in the garden to be kept and tended. Jia Qiang’s company had mastered twenty different plays, and the young Buddhist and Taoist nuns had learnt to recite several volumes of scriptures and spells. Only now could Jia Zheng feel somewhat at ease. He invited Grandmother Jia and the others to inspect the garden, consider each detail, and settle the finishing touches. When there was no omission or unsuitable arrangement left, he chose a day to submit his memorial.

On the day it was submitted, the vermilion endorsement granted his petition: the Noble Consort was graciously permitted to visit her family on the fifteenth of the first month of the following year, the Festival of the First Full Moon. Once the Jia household received this gracious decree, they were busier than ever, night and day. They could not even celebrate the New Year properly. In no time the Lantern Festival was almost upon them. From the eighth day of the first month, eunuchs came out to inspect the arrangements: where she would change, where she would sit informally, where receive homage, where banquet, and where retire to rest. Other eunuchs, charged with inspecting the premises and overseeing security, came with numerous junior eunuchs to guard the various approaches, put up screening curtains, and instruct the household where to withdraw, where to kneel, where to serve food and where to make reports. The rules of ceremony were endless. Outside, officers of the Board of Works and the military intendant of the Five Districts had the streets swept and idlers driven away. Jia She and the others supervised the craftsmen making ornamental lanterns, fireworks and the like. By the fourteenth, all was ready. No one, high or low, slept that night.

At the fifth watch on the fifteenth, Grandmother Jia and everyone else entitled to rank put on full ceremonial dress according to their grade. Throughout the garden, dragons writhed on the hangings and coloured phoenixes flew across the curtains. Gold and silver blazed; pearls and jewels vied in brilliance. A hundred-blend incense burned in the censers, and ever-blooming flowers filled the vases. Not a cough broke the stillness. Jia She and the men waited outside the west street gate; Grandmother Jia and the women outside the main gate of the Rong mansion. Curtains screened every street end and alley entrance. They were growing impatient when a eunuch rode up on a large horse. Grandmother Jia hurried to receive him and ask for news. ‘There is plenty of time yet! Her Highness has her evening meal at the first quarter of the early wei hour, then worships the Buddha at Baoling Palace at the second quarter after mid-wei. At the first quarter of early you she attends the feast and views the lanterns at Taiming Palace. Only then can she ask leave to depart. I doubt she will set out before early xu.’ ‘In that case, Grandmother, Aunt, please go back to your rooms,’ said Xi-feng. ‘There will be time enough to come out when she is due.’ Grandmother Jia and the others retired for the moment. Xi-feng was left in charge of all arrangements in the garden, and stewards were told to take the eunuchs away for food and wine.

Soon men were bringing in candles by the shoulder-load, and the lamps were lit everywhere. They had just finished when the sound of galloping came from outside. Ten or so eunuchs ran in, panting and clapping their hands. Those already there understood the signal: ‘She is coming!’ Each took his assigned station. Jia She led the sons and nephews of the whole clan outside the west street gate, while Grandmother Jia led its women outside the main gate to receive her. For a long time all was silent. Then two red-robed eunuchs came slowly into view on horseback. At the west street gate they dismounted, sent their horses outside the screening curtains, and stood facing west with their hands at their sides. After another long interval, a second pair did the same. Presently ten or so pairs had arrived, and only then could the faint sound of delicate music be heard. In pairs came dragon banners and phoenix fans, pheasant plumes and kui-crested standards, followed by portable censers decorated with gold, burning imperial incense. Then came a golden-yellow canopy with a curved handle and seven phoenixes, followed by the ceremonial crown, robe, girdle and shoes. Attendant eunuchs bore fragrant beads, embroidered handkerchiefs, rinsing bowls, fly-whisks and other articles.

When these successive parties had passed, eight eunuchs came slowly along carrying a golden-topped palanquin embroidered with phoenixes on golden yellow. Grandmother Jia and the others hurriedly knelt beside the way. Several eunuchs ran ahead to raise Grandmother Jia, Lady Xing and Lady Wang to their feet. The palanquin passed through the main gate and the ceremonial gate, then turned east. At the entrance to a courtyard a eunuch carrying a fly-whisk knelt and invited the Consort to alight and change.

The palanquin was carried through the entrance, and the eunuchs withdrew. Only the zhaorong and caipin attendants remained to help Yuan-chun down. The courtyard blazed with lanterns of every kind, exquisitely fashioned from gauze and figured silk. Above was an illuminated tablet bearing the words ‘Embodying Benevolence, Bathed in Virtue’. Yuan-chun went inside, changed, and came out to re-enter her palanquin and proceed into the garden. There incense drifted in curling clouds, flowers and coloured decorations shone on every side, lantern answered lantern, and delicate music sounded continually. No words could exhaust that scene of peace and prosperity, of wealth and cultivated pleasure. At that moment I thought back to the desolation and loneliness beneath Green Ridge Peak in the Mountain of Great Desolation. Had the scabby-headed monk and the lame Taoist not brought me here, how could I ever have seen such a world? I wanted to compose a ‘Rhapsody on Lanterns and Moonlight’ or an ‘Ode to the Imperial Home Visit’ to record the occasion, but feared falling into the stale conventions of other books. A rhapsody and a panegyric together could not do full justice to the scene. Without either, you readers can surely imagine its splendour for yourselves.

We may therefore save the time, paper and ink, and get on with the story. From her chair, Consort Jia surveyed the magnificence inside and outside the garden and sighed to herself at the extravagance and expense. A eunuch carrying a fly-whisk now knelt to invite her aboard a boat, and she alighted. A ribbon of clear water wound like a moving dragon. Along the stone balustrades on either bank hung lanterns of crystal and coloured glass, shining like silver light on snowy waves. The willows, apricots and other trees had neither blossom nor leaf, but these had been fashioned from pith, silk, figured silk, paper and gauze to suit each tree and stuck onto its branches. Several lamps hung from every tree. Even the lotus, water-plants, ducks and egrets in the pool had been made from shells, feathers and similar materials. Above and below, the lanterns competed in brilliance: a world of glass, a universe of jewels. Of the exquisite miniature landscapes and lanterns aboard the boat, its beaded curtains and embroidered hangings, its cassia oars and orchid paddles, we need hardly speak. Presently they entered a stone-lined inlet. Above it an illuminated tablet clearly displayed ‘Knotweed Banks and Flowering Inlets’. Now, those words, like ‘The Phoenix Comes in State’ and the other inscriptions, had only been devised in the previous chapter when Jia Zheng happened to test Bao-yu’s lessons and poetic talent. Why should these tablets and couplets now be used in earnest?

Jia Zheng came from generations of scholars; the guests who attended him standing or sat in his company were all men of ability. Could none of them produce a fine inscription? Why make do with a child’s playful efforts? That would be like some newly rich family squandering money on paint and varnish, then writing up ‘Green willows at the front gate droop with golden locks; blue hills behind the house stand like a brocade screen’, and imagining the result a spectacle of the highest taste. Could that be the conduct of the Ning and Rong households portrayed throughout The Story of the Stone? On the face of it, there is a glaring contradiction.

An explanation of the circumstances will make everything clear. Before she entered the palace, Consort Jia had been brought up from childhood by Grandmother Jia. When Bao-yu was born, she was already the elder sister and he the little brother. Knowing that her mother had borne this son only as she was growing older, she loved him with a tenderness quite different from her feelings for her other younger brothers. They were both with Grandmother Jia, and scarcely ever apart. Before Bao-yu went to school, when he was only three or four, she guided his hand and taught him by word of mouth until he had learnt several books and some thousands of characters. In name they were sister and brother; in their affection, almost mother and son. After entering the palace, she repeatedly sent messages to her parents: ‘Take the greatest care over his upbringing. Without firmness he will never amount to anything; with too much, I fear something unforeseen may happen and bring you sorrow.’ She never ceased to think of him with anxious love. A few days earlier, Jia Zheng had heard that the schoolmaster praised Bao-yu behind his back for considerable talent of an unconventional kind. He had not believed it. When the garden happened to be completed, he therefore asked Bao-yu to devise inscriptions, simply to test the quality of his imagination.

The tablets and couplets he produced were no masterpieces, but for a young boy they had some merit. It would have been easy to engage an eminent writer to compose others, yet the flavour of something made within the family seemed more pleasing. Besides, when the Consort saw them and knew they were her beloved brother’s work, they might fulfil something of her long-held hopes for him. That was why Bao-yu’s inscriptions had actually been used. Those he had not finished that day had since been supplied. But enough of this digression. Consort Jia looked at the four Chinese characters and smiled. ‘“Flowering Inlets” is quite right. Why do we need “Knotweed Banks”?’ The eunuch seated beside her hurried into a little boat, landed, and sent the message flying to Jia Zheng, who immediately had the inscription altered. Presently the boat reached the inner landing. She left it and returned to her palanquin. Before her rose graceful palaces like those of jade, and lofty halls like those of cassia. A stone archway clearly bore the words ‘Precious Mirror of the Celestials’. She at once ordered these replaced with ‘Villa for the Family Visit’. Then she entered the temporary palace. Courtyard torches blazed into the sky; fragrant chips were strewn upon the ground. Trees of fire and jewelled flowers shone before golden windows and jade balustrades.

No telling could exhaust the rolled-up shrimp-whisker curtains, the otter-skin carpets, the musk and camphor drifting from the censers, the screens of pheasant-tail fans. Truly:

Golden gates and jade doors: an immortal’s dwelling; Cassia halls and orchid palaces: the home of an imperial consort.

‘Why has this hall no tablet?’ the Consort asked. An attendant eunuch knelt to reply. ‘This is the principal hall. Your subjects outside the palace did not presume to devise one.’ She nodded without speaking. A master of ceremonies knelt to invite her to ascend the throne and receive homage. Music rose on both sides of the steps. Two eunuchs led Jia She, Jia Zheng and the other men into their appointed ranks below the terrace. From the hall a zhaorong announced the command: ‘The ceremony is dispensed with.’

The eunuchs led Jia She and the others away. Another party conducted the Dowager Lady of Rong and the women up the eastern steps to their ranks on the terrace. Again the zhaorong announced, ‘The ceremony is dispensed with.’ They too were led away. When tea had been offered three times, the Consort left her throne and the music ceased. She retired to a side hall to change, and only then was her conveyance prepared to take her out of the garden for the family reunion.

In Grandmother Jia’s main room, she wished to make the customary family obeisances, but Grandmother Jia and the others kept kneeling to prevent her. With tears filling the Consort’s eyes, they came forward at last to greet one another. She held Grandmother Jia with one hand and Lady Wang with the other. All three had so much in their hearts to say, and none could say a word. They could only sob and weep together. Lady Xing, Li Wan, Wang Xi-feng, and Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun stood round them, silently shedding tears. At length the Consort mastered her grief, forced a smile, and tried to comfort her grandmother and mother. ‘You sent me away to that place where I can see no one. Now at last I have come home, and we can be together for a little. Why cry instead of talking and laughing? Soon I must go, and who knows when I shall come again?’ At these words she choked once more. Lady Xing hurried forward to comfort her. Grandmother Jia and the others persuaded her to sit, and came up to greet her one by one, with more unavoidable tears. Then the male stewards and household officers of both mansions paid their respects outside the hall, followed by the married women in charge of household affairs, leading the maids.

‘Why are Aunt Xue, Bao-chai and Dai-yu not here?’ the Consort asked. Lady Wang replied, ‘As relatives outside the household without official rank, they did not presume to enter unbidden.’ The Consort at once sent for them. Aunt Xue and the girls came in and were about to perform the state obeisances, but these too were dispensed with. Each came forward to exchange news and speak of their separation. Bao-qin and the other maids whom the Consort had taken into the palace then came to kowtow. Grandmother Jia and the others hastened to raise them and ordered that they be entertained in another room. The Ning mansion and Jia She’s household had people ready to entertain the attendant eunuchs, the caipin and zhaorong, and the rest of the retinue. Only three or four junior eunuchs remained to answer calls. Mother, daughter, sisters and cousins could now speak freely of their separation and of private family affairs.

Jia Zheng came to the curtain to enquire after her well-being. The Consort lowered it and made her obeisance to him. Through the curtain she said tearfully, ‘A farming family may live on pickled vegetables and salt, wearing plain cloth, but they can still enjoy being together. We have wealth and honour beyond measure, yet our flesh and blood are scattered. What pleasure is there in it?’ With tears in his own eyes Jia Zheng replied, ‘Your subject comes of a humble, obscure family. Among a flock of doves and crows, who could have expected the auspicious appearance of a phoenix? Your Highness receives Heaven’s favour from above and makes manifest our ancestors’ virtue below. The rare essences of mountains and rivers, sun and moon, and the far-reaching virtue of our forebears have gathered in one person, and good fortune has reached my wife and me. Our present sovereign has revealed the great life-giving virtue of Heaven and Earth, bestowing a generosity without precedent in any age. Though we subjects dashed our brains and livers to the ground, how could we repay one ten-thousandth of it? We can only labour faithfully at our duties from morning to night and pray that our sovereign may live for ten thousand years, to the good fortune of all beneath Heaven. Your Highness must not trouble your precious heart over the declining years of my wife and myself. We beg you instead to cherish your own health. By serving His Majesty with constant diligence, care and reverence, you may prove worthy of the immense grace of his tender concern.’ The Consort in turn urged him to put affairs of state first, tend his health whenever he had leisure, and not worry about her.

Jia Zheng further reported, ‘The inscriptions for all the pavilions, terraces, studios and houses in the garden were composed by Bao-yu. If one or two are at all worthy of your notice, we should be honoured if you would bestow new names.’ Hearing that Bao-yu could compose such inscriptions, Consort Yuan smiled. ‘He really has made progress.’ Jia Zheng withdrew. The Consort saw that the Xue and Lin cousins stood out even among the other girls: they were indeed like lovely flowers and yielding jade. ‘Why has Bao-yu not come to see me?’ she asked. Grandmother Jia replied, ‘Without a command, no male from outside the palace would venture in.’ The Consort ordered him brought at once. A junior eunuch went out and led him in. After he had performed the state obeisances, she called him nearer, took his hand and drew him into her arms. Stroking his head and neck, she smiled. ‘How much taller you have grown since…’ Before she could finish, her tears were falling like rain.

You-shi, Xi-feng and the others came forward to announce that the banquet was ready and invite the Consort to proceed. She rose with the others, ordered Bao-yu to lead the way, and walked with them to the garden gate. Amid the lanterns and trees of fire, every kind of extraordinary display lay before them.

Entering the garden, they first visited ‘The Phoenix Comes in State’, ‘Red Fragrance and Green Jade’, ‘The Apricot Tavern Flag in Sight’ and ‘Clear Fragrance of Heng and Zhi’. They climbed towers, walked through galleries, crossed water and followed the hills, lingering to admire each view. Every place had a different arrangement, every ornament some novelty. The Consort praised it all most warmly, but added, ‘There must not be such extravagance in future. All this goes far beyond what is necessary.’

They came at last to the principal hall. The Consort dispensed with ceremony and took her seat, and the great banquet began. Grandmother Jia and the others sat below her; You-shi, Li Wan and Xi-feng served the soup and wine themselves. Consort Yuan called for brush and inkstone, took a spotted-bamboo brush in her own hand, and bestowed names on the places she liked best. Her inscriptions ran as follows. For the tablet: ‘Remember Grace and Consider Duty’.

Heaven and Earth unfold their boundless mercy; common people and household servants alike give thanks;

An unprecedented favour reaches across the ages; the Nine Provinces and all nations share its grace and glory.

This tablet and couplet were for the principal hall. ‘Prospect Garden’ was the name of the garden itself. ‘The Phoenix Comes in State’ was named ‘Naiad’s House’. ‘Red Fragrance and Green Jade’ became ‘Delight in Red and Joy in Green’, and the residence was named ‘House of Green Delights’.

‘Pure Scent of Heng and Zhi’ was named ‘Allspice Court’; ‘The Apricot Tavern Flag in Sight’ became ‘Kudzu-washing Grange’. The main tower was ‘Prospect Tower’, the soaring building to the east ‘Brocade-adorned Pavilion’, and the obliquely set building to the west ‘Fragrance-bearing Pavilion’. There were also such names as ‘Knotweed Breeze Pavilion’, ‘Lotus Pavilion’, ‘Amaryllis Eyot’ and ‘Leafy Islet’. More than ten further tablets had four-character inscriptions, among them ‘Pear Blossom in Spring Rain’, ‘Autumn Wind Cuts the Paulownia’ and ‘Night Snow on Reeds’; it is difficult to record them all here. She ordered that none of the existing tablets and couplets need be taken down. Then she began by writing this quatrain:

Embracing hills and holding water, finely built,

How much labour brought this garden to completion!

The sights of Heaven and the human world are gathered here;

This fragrant garden merits the name of Prospect Garden.

When she had finished, she smiled at her sisters and cousins. ‘You all know very well that I have never been quick-witted, nor much good at verse. Tonight I have merely done what was required, so as not to fail such a setting. Some other day, when I have a little leisure, I must write an “Account of Prospect Garden”, an “Ode to the Imperial Home Visit” and other pieces to record this occasion. Each of you must also compose an inscription and a poem. Do the best you can on the spur of the moment, and do not let my poor efforts constrain you. I am delighted that Bao-yu has learnt to compose inscriptions and verse. It is more than I expected. Of these places, I love Naiad’s House and Allspice Court most, and next to them the House of Green Delights and Kudzu-washing Grange. These four principal sites deserve poems of their own. The couplets you wrote before were good, but now you must compose an eight-line poem in five-character lines for each. Let me test you here in person, and feel that the trouble I took over teaching you as a child was not wasted.’ Bao-yu could only agree. He withdrew to think.

Of Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun, Tan-chun was the most gifted. Yet even she felt she could scarcely compete with Bao-chai and Dai-yu, and could only do her best, like the rest, to fulfil the command. Li Wan too managed to put together an eight-line poem. The Consort first read the girls’ poems in order. They were as follows:

A freer spirit, a happier heart Tablet inscription by Ying-chun

The garden stands complete, its scenes exquisitely devised;

At your command, ashamed, I write of freedom and delight.

Who could believe the world contained a place like this?

Who, wandering here, could fail to feel the spirit ease?

All creation vies in splendour Tablet inscription by Tan-chun

This famous garden rises in magnificence;

Your command makes me ashamed of my poor learning.

Its subtle beauties will not come at once to words:

Indeed, all things are shining with their own splendour.

Literary brilliance and grace Tablet inscription by Li Wan

Fair waters, shining hills enfold and wind again;

Their grace and brilliance surpass the isles of Penglai.

Green cut for singers’ fans blurs into fragrant grass;

Red sets off silken skirts in the Falling Plum Blossom dance.

Such pearls and jade must surely tell of a flourishing age;

What fortune brings an immortal down from the jewelled terrace!

Since this famed garden won the honour of her visit,

No common mortal has been permitted to come here.

Radiance gathered, blessings centred Tablet inscription by Xue Bao-chai

A fragrant garden built west of the imperial city;

Bright sun and auspicious clouds enfold its wonders.

Orioles gladly leave the valley for the lofty willows;

Tall bamboo waits for the phoenix to come in state.

Letters have flourished on this night of imperial visiting;

Filial virtue must grow as these home visits spread.

Inspired verse and immortal talent fill your coloured brush;

Ashamed of myself, how dare I add another word?

An immortal garden beyond the world Tablet inscription by Lin Dai-yu

Where has this famous garden been built?

An immortal realm apart from worldly dust.

It borrows the beauty of mountains and rivers

And adds new wonders to the scene.

Fragrance mingles with Golden Valley wine;

Flowers charm the guest from halls of jade.

What good fortune could win us such favour,

Such frequent visits of the palace carriage?

When the Consort had read them, she praised them all, then smiled. ‘Our Xue and Lin cousins’ poems are different from the rest, after all. We foolish sisters cannot rank with them.’ Dai-yu had intended to display her extraordinary gifts that evening and outshine everyone. But the Consort had ordered only one inscription and one poem apiece. She could hardly disobey by writing more, and had merely tossed off an eight-line poem in five-character lines to suit the occasion. Bao-yu had not yet finished. He had completed only the poems on Naiad’s House and Allspice Court and was working on the House of Green Delights. His draft contained the line ‘Green jade stays furled in spring’. Bao-chai caught sight of it. While the others were paying no attention, she quickly turned, nudged him, and whispered, ‘She disliked “Red Fragrance and Green Jade” and changed it to “Delight in Red and Joy in Green”. If you insist on “green jade” now, will it not look as though you mean to challenge her? There are plenty of other expressions for plantain leaves. Think of one and change it.’ Bao-yu wiped his brow. ‘I cannot remember a single allusion or source just now.’ Bao-chai smiled. ‘Just change the word “jade” in “green jade” to “wax”.’ ‘Is there a source for “green wax”?’ At his question, Bao-chai quietly clicked her tongue and nodded,

then said with a smile, ‘If you are like this tonight, I suppose that when you answer questions in the golden hall you will forget even “Zhao, Qian, Sun, Li”! Have you forgotten the first line of Qian Xu’s Tang poem on the plantain: “A cold candle, smokeless, its green wax dry”?’ Bao-yu felt his mind suddenly clear. ‘What an idiot I am!’ he said, laughing. ‘It was there before my eyes and I could not think of it. You really are a “one-word teacher”. From now on I shall call you Teacher, never Sister again.’

Bao-chai laughed softly too. ‘Get on with the poem, instead of all this talk of sisters. Who is your sister? The one up there in the yellow robe is your sister, yet you come claiming me for another!’ But she was afraid their joking would waste his time, so she moved away. Bao-yu went on and finished the poem, making three in all.

Dai-yu was naturally disappointed at having had no chance to fulfil her ambition. Seeing Bao-yu labouring over four eight-line poems on his own, she thought she might as well write two for him and spare him where his powers failed. She went over to his table and whispered, ‘Have you finished them all?’ ‘Only three,’ he said. ‘There is still “The Apricot Tavern Flag in Sight”.’

‘Then copy out the first three. By the time you have finished, I shall have written this one for you.’ She lowered her head in thought, and in a moment had composed the whole poem. She wrote it on a slip of paper, rolled it into a ball and tossed it in front of him. Bao-yu opened it and felt it was ten times better than his own three. Delighted beyond expectation, he hastily copied everything out in careful formal script and presented it. The Consort read:

The Phoenix Comes in State Respectfully inscribed by your subject Bao-yu

The lovely jade has newly borne its fruit,

Fit welcome for the phoenix now.

Each stem is green enough to drip;

Each spray of leaves brings forth coolness.

Bursting through paving, it checks water on the steps;

Piercing the blind, it bars the censer’s scent.

Do not stir those clear, fragmented shadows:

A pleasant dream lingers as the days grow long.

Clear Fragrance of Heng and Zhi

Hengwu fills the quiet garden;

Luo and bi vines add to the fragrance.

They softly cushion the grass of all three months of spring,

And gently draw a single thread of scent.

Light mist bewilders the winding path;

Cold green drips along the turning gallery.

Who speaks of the song of the pond?

The Xie family’s secluded dream is long.

Delight in Red and Joy in Green

Deep in the courtyard, the long day is still;

Two lovely figures rise together.

Green wax stays furled in spring;

The red-robed beauty has not slept all night.

One leans on the railing, crimson sleeves hanging;

One rests by the rock, sheltering green haze.

They stand facing each other in the east wind;

Their master surely knows how to cherish them.

The Apricot Tavern Flag in Sight

The apricot flag invites the traveller to drink;

A country grange comes into view.

Water-caltrops, floating-heart, and water for the geese;

Mulberry, elm, and rafters for the swallows.

A bed of spring chives is ready for cutting;

For ten li, the scent of flowering rice.

In this flourishing age no one goes hungry;

Why toil so busily at plough and loom?

The Consort was delighted with what she read. ‘He really has improved!’ She singled out ‘The Apricot Tavern Flag’ as better than the preceding three and changed ‘Kudzu-washing Grange’ to ‘Sweet-rice Village’. She then ordered Tan-chun to copy on coloured paper all the dozen or more poems just composed, and had a eunuch carry them to the men outside. Jia Zheng and the others read them with endless praise. Jia Zheng also presented an ‘Ode to the Homecoming’. The Consort ordered delicacies, including fine milk pastries and golden sliced fish, to be given to Bao-yu and Jia Lan. Jia Lan was still very young and understood little of what was happening. He merely followed his mother and kept close to his uncle to perform the ceremonies, so there is no separate account of him. Jia Huan, who had fallen ill before the New Year and had not recovered, was being nursed in a quiet place; there is therefore no account of him either.

Meanwhile, Jia Qiang and the twelve actresses were waiting impatiently below the tower. A eunuch came hurrying up. ‘The poems are finished. Bring the list of plays at once!’ Jia Qiang hastily presented the brocade-covered programme, together with the roster of all twelve girls. Soon the eunuch returned. Only four scenes had been selected: first, ‘The Lavish Feast’; second, ‘Praying for Skill’; third, ‘An Immortal Encounter’; and fourth, ‘The Soul’s Departure’. Jia Qiang hurried to get the performers ready. Their singing outdid voices that could split stone, and they danced with the allure of heavenly demons. Though all was costume and acting, they conveyed every shade of sorrow and joy. They had just finished when a eunuch came in carrying a golden tray of cakes and other dainties. ‘Which is Ling-guan?’ he asked. Jia Qiang knew at once that these were gifts for her. Delighted, he hastened to receive them and told her to kowtow. ‘Her Highness commands,’ the eunuch continued, ‘“Ling-guan is excellent. Let her perform two more scenes, any two she pleases.”’ Jia Qiang eagerly agreed and told Ling-guan to perform ‘A Walk in the Garden’ and ‘A Dream Interrupted’.

Ling-guan considered neither scene proper to her own theatrical role and flatly refused. She insisted on ‘The Rendezvous’ and ‘The Quarrel’. Jia Qiang could not prevail against her and had to let her have her way. The Consort was delighted. ‘Do not make things difficult for this girl,’ she ordered. ‘See that she is well taught.’ She gave her an additional reward of two lengths of palace satin, two purses, small gold and silver ingots, food and other things. The banquet was then cleared away, and they toured the places they had not yet visited. Suddenly, seeing a Buddhist temple encircled by hills, the Consort had her hands washed afresh and went in to burn incense and worship. She wrote another tablet, ‘Mercy’s Vessel across the Sea of Suffering’, and bestowed additional favours on the secluded Buddhist and Taoist nuns.

Presently a eunuch knelt to report, ‘The gifts are all ready. Please examine their distribution according to rank.’ He presented a summary. The Consort read it from beginning to end, found everything appropriate, and ordered it carried out. The eunuchs withdrew to distribute the gifts one by one. Grandmother Jia was to receive one gold ruyi and one jade ruyi, an aloeswood walking-stick, a string of kyanam-wood prayer beads, four lengths of palace satin patterned with ‘Wealth, Honour and Lasting Spring’, four lengths of palace silk patterned with ‘Long Life and Enduring Happiness’, ten ingots of purple gold with the ‘Brush, Ingot and Ruyi’ design, and ten silver ingots with the ‘Auspicious Celebration and Fish’ design. Lady Xing and Lady Wang each received the same, less the four articles comprising the two ruyi, the stick and the beads. Jia Jing, Jia She and Jia Zheng each received two sets of new books by the Emperor, two boxes of fine ink, two gold and two silver jue wine-cups, and ceremonial gifts of silk as above. Bao-chai, Dai-yu and the other girls each received one set of new books, a fine inkstone, and two pairs of gold and silver ingots in the newest designs. Bao-yu received the same. Jia Lan received two gold and silver neck-rings and two pairs of gold and silver ingots. You-shi, Li Wan and Xi-feng each received four gold and silver ingots and four lengths of silk.

A further twenty-four lengths of silk and one hundred strings of good copper cash were for the nurses and maids in the rooms of Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the girls. Jia Zhen, Jia Lian, Jia Huan and Jia Rong each received one allotment of ceremonial silk gifts and a pair of gold ingots. Another hundred lengths of coloured satin, a thousand taels of gold and silver, imperial wine and a splendid feast were granted to everyone in both mansions who had supervised the garden building, arranged its furnishings, attended to service, managed the plays or tended the lamps. A further five hundred strings of good copper cash were for the kitchen hands, actors, variety performers and miscellaneous workers.

When everyone had thanked her for these favours, the attendant eunuch reported, ‘It is already the third quarter after the middle of the chou hour. We beg Your Highness to return to the palace.’ Tears welled up again in the Consort’s eyes. Forcing a smile, she held Grandmother Jia’s and Lady Wang’s hands tightly, unable to let them go. Again and again she urged them, ‘Do not worry about me. Take good care of yourselves. His Majesty’s grace is boundless: you may now come into the palace to see me once a month. We shall have plenty of chances to meet. Why be so miserable? If his favour allows me to come home again next year, you must on no account spend so extravagantly.’ Grandmother Jia and the others were sobbing too hard to speak. Unwilling as the Consort was to part, the rules of the imperial household could not be broken. She had to harden her heart, enter her palanquin and go. Those who remained at last managed to comfort Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang, and supported them out of the garden.
