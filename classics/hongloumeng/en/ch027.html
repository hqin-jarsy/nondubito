Chapter 27 At Dripping Emerald Pavilion, Noble Consort Yang plays with bright-winged butterflies; Beside the fragrant grave, Flying Swallow weeps for fallen petals1

Chapter end

Now Dai-yu was still weeping when she heard the courtyard gate open and saw Bao-chai come out, with Bao-yu, Aroma and a whole party seeing her off. She was about to go up and question Bao-yu, but feared that asking him in front of everyone would embarrass him and make things awkward. She slipped aside and let Bao-chai pass. Only after Bao-yu and the others had gone back in and shut the gate did she come out again. Still looking towards it, she shed a few more tears. At last, feeling the futility of it, she turned and went home, where she listlessly removed what remained of her make-up.

Nightingale and Snowgoose were well acquainted with Dai-yu’s ways. She would sit idle and dejected, frowning or sighing; and when nothing whatever seemed to have happened, for no reason that anyone could discover, her tears would begin again and scarcely ever dry. At first people had tried to comfort her, fearing that she missed her parents or her home, or had suffered some slight. They had done what they could to soothe and reassure her. But as month followed month and year followed year, and she was still the same, they grew accustomed to it and ceased to take much notice. So now no one troubled her. They left her to sit in her misery and went to bed themselves. Dai-yu leaned against the bed-rail, her arms clasped round her knees and her eyes full of tears, as motionless as an image carved in wood or moulded in clay. She sat like this until well into the second watch before at last going to sleep. Nothing further happened that night.

The next day was the twenty-sixth of the fourth month. As it happened, the solar term Grain in Ear began that day in the wei hour. An old custom required offerings of every kind to be set out on this day to honour the Flower God and bid him farewell. Once Grain in Ear had come, it was said, summer had arrived, the flowers would all shed their petals, and the Flower God must relinquish his office. He therefore needed a proper send-off. The custom was particularly favoured among the women, and everyone in Prospect Garden was up early. Some of the girls wove petals and willow twigs into miniature sedan chairs and horses; others folded figured silks, brocades and gauzes into standards, pennons and banners, all fastened with coloured thread. These things hung from every tree and every flowering spray. Embroidered ribbons fluttered throughout the garden, and the flower-laden branches tossed and swayed. As for the girls themselves, so beautifully were they dressed that peach blossom blushed and apricot blossom yielded, swallows envied them and orioles were put to shame. There was no describing it all.

Bao-chai, Ying-chun, Tan-chun, Xi-chun, Li Wan and Xi-feng were amusing themselves in the garden, together with Qiao-jie, Da-jie, Caltrop and all the maids. Only Dai-yu was missing.

‘Where is Cousin Lin?’ Ying-chun asked. ‘What a lazy girl! Surely she can’t still be asleep?’

‘Wait here,’ said Bao-chai. ‘I’ll go and pester her into coming.’

Leaving the others, she went straight towards Naiad’s House.2

On the way she met Wen-guan and the other eleven girls of the troupe coming to join them. They greeted her and chatted for a while. Bao-chai turned and pointed.

‘They’re all over there. Go and find them. I’m just fetching Miss Lin, and then I’ll come.’

She continued along the winding path towards Naiad’s House. Suddenly she looked up and saw Bao-yu go in. She stopped, lowered her head and considered. Bao-yu and Dai-yu had grown up together. As cousins they often took little heed of what might be thought of their intimacy, and passed from teasing and laughter to pleasure or anger without warning. Besides, Dai-yu was always prone to suspicion and sulks. If she herself went in after him now, it would put Bao-yu in an awkward position and make Dai-yu suspicious. No, she had better turn back. Having made up her mind, she did so.

She was just going to find the other girls when she caught sight of a pair of jade-pale butterflies ahead, each as large as a round fan. One rose while the other dipped, dancing into the wind, a delightful sight. Wanting to catch them for fun, Bao-chai took a fan from her sleeve and went down on to the grass after them. Up and down, to and fro they flew, threading the flowers and passing the willows, until they seemed about to cross the water. They drew Bao-chai after them, stepping softly all the way to Dripping Emerald Pavilion in the middle of the pond. Fragrant sweat was streaming from her and she was panting in little breaths. She had lost all desire to catch the butterflies and was about to turn back when she heard voices whispering inside the pavilion. It stood over the water, approached on all four sides by covered walks and winding bridges, and its carved lattice windows were covered with paper.

Hearing the voices from outside, Bao-chai stopped and listened closely.

‘Look at this handkerchief. If it really is the one you lost, take it. If not, it must go back to Master Yun.’

Another voice answered:

‘Of course it’s mine! Give it here.’

‘And what will you give me for finding it? You don’t expect me to do it for nothing, do you?’

‘I’ve promised you a reward. Of course I shan’t cheat you.’

‘You must reward me for finding it and bringing it to you. But what about the person who picked it up? Aren’t you going to give him anything?’

The other replied:

‘Don’t talk nonsense. He’s a gentleman. If he finds something of mine, naturally he ought to give it back. What should I give him a reward for?’

‘But what am I to tell him if you don’t? Besides, he said over and over that I wasn’t to give it to you unless there was something for him.’

After a pause came the answer:

‘Very well. Take this one of mine and give it to him as his reward. But what if you tell anyone? You must swear you won’t.’

‘If I tell a single soul, may I grow a boil and die a wretched death!’

Then the other voice said:

‘Oh! We’ve been so busy talking, someone might have crept up outside and heard us. We’d better push all these windows open. Even if anyone sees us here, they’ll only think we’re chatting for fun. And if anyone comes close, we’ll see them and stop talking.’

Bao-chai was alarmed to hear this.

‘No wonder fornicators and thieves have always had their wits about them!’ she thought. ‘Once they open the windows and find me here, won’t they be ashamed! And that voice sounded very like Crimson from Bao-yu’s rooms. She’s always been conceited and ambitious, a thoroughly crafty, difficult creature. Now I’ve heard her secret, she may turn on me in desperation. A desperate man rebels, a cornered dog will leap a wall. She might make trouble, and I should find myself in a very awkward position. Even if I hurry away, I don’t suppose I can escape in time. I shall have to try the golden cicada’s trick of slipping out of its shell.’

Before she had finished thinking, she heard a creak. Deliberately making her footsteps louder, she called out with a laugh:

‘Little Pin-pin, let’s see where you can hide!’

She made a show of hurrying after someone. Crimson and Zhui-er inside had only just pushed a window open. At the sight of Bao-chai calling out and running past, both stood dumbfounded.

Bao-chai turned to them with a smile.

‘Where have you hidden Miss Lin?’

‘We haven’t seen her,’ said Zhui-er.

‘I saw her from the other side of the stream just now, crouching here and playing with the water. I meant to creep up and give her a fright, but before I could reach her she saw me, slipped round to the east and disappeared. I wonder if she’s hidden herself in here.’

As she spoke, she went in and made a pretence of looking round. Then she came straight out and walked away, saying:

‘She must have crawled into one of the grottoes again. A snake might give her a bite for her pains!’

As she walked off, still talking, she was secretly amused. That had got her out of it; she wondered what the two girls would make of it all. Crimson, however, had taken her story for the truth. Once Bao-chai was well away, she caught hold of Zhui-er.

‘This is dreadful!

‘Miss Lin was crouching here. She must have heard us!’

Zhui-er too was silent for a long while.

‘What are we to do?’ Crimson asked again.

‘Even if she did hear, what’s it to anyone?’ said Zhui-er. ‘Let everyone mind their own business, and that’s the end of it.’

‘It wouldn’t be so bad if Miss Bao had heard,’ said Crimson. ‘But Miss Lin has such a sharp tongue, and nothing escapes her. If she’s heard us and lets something slip, what shall we do then?’

They were still talking when Wen-guan, Caltrop, Chess, Scribe and the others came up to the pavilion. The two girls had to drop the subject and join in their laughter and chatter.

Just then they saw Xi-feng standing on the slope, beckoning. Crimson hastily left the others and ran over to her.

‘What can I do for you, ma’am?’ she asked with a smile.

Xi-feng looked her over. Seeing a neat, pretty girl who knew how to speak, she smiled too.

‘My maids haven’t come into the garden with me today. I’ve just remembered something and want to send someone out, but I don’t know whether you’re capable of it. Can you give a message without leaving anything out?’

‘Just tell me what to say, ma’am. If I leave anything out and spoil your business, you may punish me as you please.’

‘Which young lady do you serve?’ Xi-feng asked, smiling. ‘If I send you off and she comes looking for you, I shall know what to tell her.’

‘I’m in Master Bao’s rooms.’

‘Oh!’ Xi-feng laughed.

‘So you’re one of Bao-yu’s girls! No wonder. Very well. Go to my rooms and tell Sister Patience that there’s a roll of silver on the table in the outer room, underneath the stand holding the Ru-ware plate. It’s a hundred and sixty taels, the embroiderers’ wages. When Zhang Cai’s wife comes to collect it, have her watch while it’s weighed, then give it to her to take away. And there’s a little purse further inside, by the head of the bed. Bring that to me.’

Crimson set off at once. When she returned, Xi-feng was no longer on the slope. She saw Chess coming out of a grotto and standing there tying her skirt, and hurried over.

‘Sister, do you know where Mrs Lian has gone?’

‘I didn’t notice,’ said Chess.

Crimson turned and looked about. Over by the pond, Tan-chun and Bao-chai were watching the fish. She went up and asked with a deferential smile:

‘Can you tell me where Mrs Lian has gone, young ladies?’

‘Try your eldest mistress’s courtyard,’ said Tan-chun.

Crimson went on towards Sweet-rice Village. Coming straight towards her were Skybright, Mackerel, Emerald, Purple Silk, Musk, Scribe, Picture, Oriole and a crowd of others. The moment Skybright saw her, she said:

‘Just you go on running wild! The courtyard flowers aren’t watered, the birds aren’t fed, the tea stove isn’t lit, and you’re out here strolling about.’

‘Master Bao said yesterday that the flowers weren’t to be watered today,’ Crimson answered. ‘Every other day is enough.

‘And when I fed the birds, you were still asleep, sister.’

‘What about the tea stove?’ Emerald asked.

‘It isn’t my turn to tend it today. Don’t ask me whether there’s any tea.’

‘Just listen to her!’ said Mackerel. ‘Don’t waste your breath. Let her go on strolling.’

‘You might first ask whether I’ve been strolling,’ Crimson said. ‘Mrs Lian sent me with a message and told me to fetch something.’

She held up the purse for them to see. At this they fell silent, and everyone went their separate ways. Skybright gave a cold laugh.

‘So that’s it! She’s flown up to a higher branch and thinks we’re beneath her now. She may have managed half a word, but does the mistress even know her name yet? And look how pleased she is with herself! Running an errand once or twice is nothing. She’ll still have to do as she’s told afterwards. If she’s so clever, let her leave this garden today and stay on her high branch for good. Then we’ll see!’

Still talking, she went on her way.

It was no use arguing. Crimson swallowed her anger and went to find Xi-feng. Sure enough, when she reached Li Wan’s rooms, Xi-feng was there talking to her. Crimson went up to report.

‘Sister Patience says she put the silver away just after you left, ma’am. When Zhang Cai’s wife came for it just now, Sister Patience weighed it out before her and handed it over.’

She handed over the purse.

‘Sister Patience also told me to say that Wang-er came in for your instructions before going to that other household. She gave him the message as you would have wished and sent him off.’

‘And what did she tell him, to suit my wishes?’ Xi-feng asked with a smile.

Crimson replied:

‘Sister Patience said, “Our mistress sends her greetings to the mistress here. Our Second Master has been away from home. Although there has been a delay of two days, the mistress need have no worries. When the Fifth Mistress is better, our mistress will come with the Fifth Mistress to visit the mistress here. The Fifth Mistress sent someone the other day to say that the uncle’s wife had sent word, asking after the mistress and also asking the married daughter of this house for two Wondrous All-Purpose Longevity Pills. If any can be had, the mistress need only send someone to bring them to our mistress. When someone goes that way tomorrow, they can take them along to the uncle’s wife over there.”’

Before she had quite finished, Li Wan exclaimed:

‘Oh dear! I can’t make head or tail of all that. Nothing but mistresses and masters!’

‘No wonder you can’t,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘There are four or five households involved.’

She turned back to Crimson with a smile.

‘Good girl! Well done for getting it all straight. You’re not like those others, twisting and squirming and buzzing like mosquitoes. You’ve no idea, sister-in-law: apart from the few people I always have at hand, I dread having to speak to anyone nowadays. They have to stretch every sentence out and chop it into two or three bits, weighing each word, putting on airs, humming and hawing. They make me burn with impatience, and they haven’t the least idea! Our Patience used to do it too. I asked her, “Must you buzz like a mosquito to be a beauty?” It took several tellings before she improved.’

Li Gong-cai laughed.

‘Of course, they should all be brazen, down-at-heel rogues like you!’

‘Now this girl is good,’ Xi-feng went on. ‘She hasn’t said much on either of these two occasions, but you can hear how briskly she gets to the point.’

She smiled at Crimson.

‘You shall come and wait on me tomorrow. I’ll take you for my daughter. With a little training from me, you’ll make something of yourself.’

Crimson burst out laughing.

‘What are you laughing at?’ Xi-feng demanded. ‘You think I’m young, only a few years older than you, and here I am setting up as your mother? Don’t flatter yourself! Just ask about. There are people far older than you begging to call me Mother, and I won’t give them the time of day. I’m doing you an honour!’

‘That isn’t what I’m laughing at, ma’am,’ Crimson said, smiling. ‘You’ve got the generations wrong. My mother is your daughter, and now you want me for a daughter too.’

‘Who is your mother?’

‘You mean you don’t know her?’ Li Gong-cai asked with a laugh.

‘She’s Lin Zhi-xiao’s daughter.’

Xi-feng was astonished.

‘Oh! So she’s his girl!’

Then she laughed.

‘You couldn’t get a sound out of Lin Zhi-xiao or his wife with an awl. I’m always saying what a well-matched pair they are, one deaf as heaven and the other dumb as earth. Who would have expected them to produce such a clever girl? How old are you?’

‘Seventeen,’ Crimson replied.

Asked her name, she said:

‘It used to be Hong-yu, “red jade”, but because the jade was the same as Master Bao’s, they only call me Crimson now.’

Xi-feng frowned and turned her head aside.

‘What a nuisance! You’d think jade had done someone some good. Jade for you, jade for me!’

Then she went on:

‘If she’s so willing to come with me, what was her mother about? I told her, “Lai Da’s wife has so much on her hands these days, I don’t suppose she knows who’s who in this household. Pick me out two good maids, will you?” She agreed readily enough. But instead of choosing anyone, she sent this girl somewhere else. Would it have been so dreadful to put her with me?’

‘Now you’re being suspicious again,’ Li Wan said with a laugh.

‘The girl came in before you asked her mother. How can you blame her?’

‘In that case, I’ll speak to Bao-yu tomorrow,’ said Xi-feng. ‘He can get someone else and this girl shall come with me. Though I don’t know whether she wants to.’

Crimson smiled.

‘It isn’t for us to say what we want or don’t want. But if I waited on you, ma’am, I could learn to judge people’s looks and moods, how to behave indoors and out, with those above me and below me, and gain some knowledge of affairs both great and small.’

Just then one of Lady Wang’s maids came to fetch Xi-feng, who took her leave of Li Gong-cai. Crimson returned to the House of Green Delights. We may leave her there.

Let us now return to Dai-yu. Having slept so poorly, she rose late the next morning. When she heard that all the girls were in the garden holding a farewell gathering for the flowers, she was afraid they would laugh at her for being foolish and lazy. She hurried to wash and dress and came out. She had just reached the courtyard when Bao-yu entered, smiling.

‘My dear cousin, did you tell on me yesterday? You kept me worrying all night.’

Dai-yu turned and called to Nightingale:

‘Tidy the room and lower one of the gauze window panels. When the big swallow comes back, let down the curtain and hold it with the lion-shaped weight. Once you’ve lit the incense, put the cover on the burner.’

Still speaking, she walked on towards the gate. Bao-yu supposed that she was still upset about what had happened at midday yesterday. He knew nothing of the affair that evening, and went on bowing and making apologetic gestures. Dai-yu would not so much as look him in the face. She went out on her own and straight off to find the other girls. Bao-yu was bewildered.

‘From the way she’s behaving,’ he thought, ‘it doesn’t seem to be yesterday’s business. But I came back late yesterday and didn’t see her. I can’t have offended her in any other way.’

Thinking this, he could not help hurrying after her. Bao-chai and Tan-chun were over there watching the cranes. When Dai-yu arrived, the three stood talking together. Presently Tan-chun saw Bao-yu and smiled.

‘Brother Bao, how are you? I haven’t seen you for three whole days.’

‘And how are you, sister?’ he asked with a smile. ‘I was only asking our eldest sister-in-law about you the other day.’

‘Come over here, brother. I want a word with you.’

He followed her to a pomegranate tree.

‘Has Father sent for you at all these last few days?’ she asked.

‘No,’ Bao-yu said, smiling.

‘I thought I heard yesterday that he’d called you out.’

‘Someone must have got it wrong. He hasn’t sent for me.’

Tan-chun smiled again.

‘I’ve saved another ten-odd strings of cash these last few months. Take it, and next time you go out, bring me back some good calligraphy or paintings, or some nice little things, light and delicate.’

‘I wander all over, inside the city and out, through great arcades and little temples,’ Bao-yu said, ‘and I’ve never found anything really novel and finely made. It’s all antiques in gold, jade, bronze or porcelain that there’s nowhere to put, or else silks, food and clothes.’

‘Who wants those?’ said Tan-chun. ‘Something like the little willow basket you bought last time, or the incense box hollowed out of a whole bamboo root, or the little clay stove. Those are just the things. I loved them more than I can say, but everyone else took a fancy to them too and carried them off as if they were treasures.’

‘Oh, so that’s what you want!’ Bao-yu laughed. ‘Those cost next to nothing. Give the boys five hundred cash and they’ll bring you a cartload.’

‘What do the pages know?’ said Tan-chun. ‘You choose them: plain without being vulgar, simple without being clumsy. Bring me lots of things like that. I’ll make you another pair of shoes like the last ones, only I’ll take even more pains with them. How would that be?’

Bao-yu laughed.

‘Talking of shoes reminds me of something. I was wearing them that day and happened to meet Father. He didn’t like the look of them at all and asked who had made them. I didn’t dare breathe a word about my Third Sister. I said they were a present from my uncle’s wife for my birthday the other day. When he heard they were from my uncle’s wife, he couldn’t very well object, though after a while he did say, “What’s the point? A waste of labour and good silk, making things like that.” When I came back I told Aroma. She said that was nothing: Aunt Zhao was furious and full of complaints. “Her own brother goes about with his shoes and stockings hanging off him, and no one sees! Yet she has time to make things like these!”’

Tan-chun’s face darkened at once.

‘How can anyone talk such utter nonsense!

‘Is it my duty to make shoes? Doesn’t Huan have his allowance? Hasn’t he people to look after him? His clothes are as good as anyone’s, his shoes and stockings too, and he has a roomful of maids and old women. What does she mean by complaining like that? Who is meant to hear it?

‘If I happen to have nothing to do and make a pair of shoes now and then, I shall give them to whichever brother I please. Who has any right to stop me? She’s getting angry for nothing.’

Bao-yu nodded, smiling.

‘You don’t understand. Naturally, she’s thinking of something else.’

Tan-chun grew angrier still and turned her head away.

‘Now you’re talking nonsense too! Of course she’s thinking of something else, but it’s all mean, underhand notions. Let her think as she likes. I recognise Father and Mother, and no one else. As for my sisters and brothers, I’m fond of whoever is fond of me. All this talk of being born to a concubine means nothing to me. I know I ought not to speak of her like this, but there’s no excuse for such stupidity! And here’s another absurdity. The last time I gave you that money to bring me back those little things, she saw me a couple of days afterwards and began saying how she had no money and how hard things were. I paid no attention.

‘But later, when the maids had gone out, she began complaining. Why did I give you my savings to spend instead of giving them to Huan? It made me want to laugh and lose my temper all at once. I went out and joined Mother.’

While they were talking, Bao-chai called over with a laugh:

‘When you’ve finished, come back! Now we can see who’s brother and sister. You leave everyone else behind to talk secrets.

‘We mustn’t hear so much as a word!’

Only then did Tan-chun and Bao-yu return, laughing. Finding Dai-yu gone, Bao-yu knew that she had slipped off somewhere to avoid him. After thinking it over, he decided he might as well wait a couple of days for her anger to subside before going to see her. Then he looked down and saw fallen balsam and pomegranate blossoms, with flowers of every other kind, lying layer upon layer like brocade over the ground. He sighed.

‘She’s so angry, she hasn’t even come to gather the flowers. I’ll take them over for her and ask her about it tomorrow.’

Just then Bao-chai called them to go out with her.

‘I’ll come in a moment,’ said Bao-yu.

He waited until the two girls were far away, then gathered the blossoms in the fold of his robe. Over hills and across streams, through trees and among flowers he went, straight to the place where he and Dai-yu had buried the peach blossom that day. He was nearly at the flowers’ grave, though not yet round the slope, when he heard sobbing on the other side. Between her sobs someone was pouring out her grief, weeping most bitterly.

‘I wonder whose maid that is,’ Bao-yu thought. ‘She must have been badly treated and come here to cry.’

He stopped where he stood and listened. This was what he heard her lament:

Flowers fade, flowers fly, flowers fill the sky; Their red gone, their fragrance ended, who will grieve?

Gossamer, softly tethered, drifts about the spring pavilion; Light willow-down clings, fluttering against embroidered curtains.

A girl within her chamber mourns the waning spring; Her breast is full of sorrow, with nowhere to release it.

She takes her flower hoe and leaves the embroidered room, Pacing to and fro, how can she bear to tread on fallen flowers?

Willow strands and elm seeds flourish for themselves, Unheeding of the peach and plum blossoms that drift away.

Peach and plum can flower again next year; Who knows who will be in this chamber then?

By the third month the fragrant nest is built; How heartless are the swallows in the beams!

Next year the flowers will bloom for them to peck, Yet they never think: when people leave, the beams are empty, the nest falls too.

Three hundred and sixty days in every year, The knives of wind, the swords of frost press cruelly close.

How long can brightness, freshness, beauty last? Once scattered on the wind, how hard they are to find!

Flowers in bloom are easy to see, but fallen, hard to find; Before the steps, she who buries flowers is sick at heart.

Alone she leans upon her hoe, shedding secret tears; They fall on empty branches, leaving marks of blood.

The cuckoo has no word: dusk has come. Hoe on her shoulder, she returns and shuts the doors one by one.

A blue-flamed lamp lights the wall as she first falls asleep; Cold rain beats on the window, her quilt not yet warm.

You wonder why my spirit is so deeply hurt? Half is love of spring, and half resentment.

I love its sudden coming, resent its sudden going; It comes without a word, it goes unheard.

Last night beyond the courtyard rose a mournful song: Was it the souls of flowers and of birds?

The souls of flowers and birds are both too hard to keep; The birds are silent still, the flowers still ashamed.

If only wings would grow beneath my arms, To follow the flowers in flight to the end of heaven!

At the end of heaven, Where is a fragrant mound?

Better to gather lovely bones in a brocade pouch, And cover their grace with a handful of pure earth.

Pure they came into the world, pure let them leave it: Better than sinking in foul mud in a ditch.

Now you are dead, I gather you for burial; Who can foretell the day when I shall die?

Today I bury flowers, and people call me foolish; In years to come, who knows who will bury me?

See how, as spring wanes, the flowers slowly fall: That is the time when beauty grows old and dies.

One day spring ends and beauty has grown old; Flowers fall, people die, neither knowing of the other!

  2. The Chinese text lists both Qiao-jie and Da-jie. Both names are retained without deciding whether they refer to separate children. ↩
