Chapter 58 In apricot shade a false phoenix weeps for his make-believe mate; Behind a madder-red gauze window true feeling weighs a lover’s foolish reasoning

Chapter end

Now, when the three girls saw Tan-chun and the others coming in, they hastily dropped the subject. After the newcomers had exchanged greetings with them, they all talked and laughed for a while before dispersing. But the elderly imperial consort mentioned earlier had now died. All the titled ladies were required to attend court and take their appointed places in the mourning observances according to their rank.

An imperial decree proclaimed throughout the realm that titled households were to hold no banquets or musical entertainments for a year, while among the common people no marriages were to take place for three months. Grandmother Jia, Lady Xing, Lady Wang, You-shi and Xu-shi, mothers-in-law, daughters-in-law and granddaughters-in-law, went to court every day to attend the sacrifices, returning only after the middle of the wei hour.

After twenty-one days in a side palace within the imperial precincts, the coffin was to be conveyed to the ancestral mausoleum in a place called Xiaoci County. The journey there and back took ten days or so. Once brought there, the coffin would lie in state for several more days before being placed in the underground chamber, so the whole absence would amount to about a month.

Jia Zhen and his wife at the Ning mansion could hardly avoid going too. That would leave neither household with anyone in charge. After discussing the problem, they reported that You-shi was in childbirth, thus contriving to have her excused so that she could help manage affairs at both mansions. Aunt Xue was asked to look after the girls and maids in the garden, and had little choice but to move in.

Bao-chai already had Xiang-yun and Caltrop with her. Aunt Li and her daughters had left Li Wan’s rooms for the present, but might still come back for three to five days at a time, and Grandmother Jia had also entrusted Bao-qin to Li Wan’s care. Xiu-yan was staying with Ying-chun. Tan-chun was overwhelmed with household business, and Aunt Zhao and Jia Huan kept coming to pester her, which made her rooms most inconvenient. Xi-chun’s rooms were small. Besides, Grandmother Jia had charged Aunt Xue over and over again to take care of Dai-yu, whom Aunt Xue had always loved and pitied most. The opportunity now offering itself, she moved into Naiad’s House and shared Dai-yu’s room, taking the greatest care over her medicines and food. Dai-yu was endlessly grateful. From then on she called her Mother, just as Bao-chai did, and addressed Bao-chai and Bao-qin directly as her elder and younger sisters. They might all have been born of the same mother, and she felt closer to them than to anyone else.

Grandmother Jia was delighted with this arrangement and felt quite at ease. Aunt Xue confined herself to looking after the girls and keeping their maids in order. She would offer no opinion on any household business, great or small. You-shi came over every day, but did little more than put in an appearance and was equally unwilling to throw her weight about. She alone was left to see to everything in her own household, and each day she also had to arrange all the food, bedding and furnishings required at Grandmother Jia’s and Lady Wang’s lodgings. She was therefore very hard at work.

With the masters and mistresses of both mansions so occupied, the stewards too were in a state of bustle. Some accompanied them to court, some attended to the lodgings outside the palace, and others went ahead to inspect accommodation for the journey. Deprived of proper direction, the servants in both households took their ease, or seized the chance to form factions and share in the petty tyranny of those temporarily put in charge. Only Lai Da and a few other stewards remained at the Rong mansion to look after outside affairs. Several of the men Lai Da ordinarily relied on had gone away. Others were appointed in their place, but they were all unfamiliar with the work, and he found nothing went smoothly. They knew no better: one cheated without restraint, another lodged groundless complaints, another recommended people for no good reason. They made trouble everywhere in so many ways that it would be impossible to recount them all.

Seeing that the other official households were releasing and sending away all their players, male and female, You-shi and the others decided to raise the question with Lady Wang when she returned. They too wished to send away their twelve girls. Someone suggested, ‘These girls were bought, after all. Even if they no longer learn to sing, we could keep them as servants and simply let their teachers go.’

‘Girls who have learnt to act are not quite the same as girls brought up to serve,’ said Lady Wang. ‘They too are the daughters of respectable families, sold into this work because their parents could not support them. They have spent several years now painting themselves up and playing the fool. Since this chance has come, we should give them a few taels for travelling expenses and let each go her own way. Our ancestors always used to do that. To do otherwise now would damage our hidden store of virtue, quite apart from being miserly.

‘There are still a few older ones here, it is true. Each had some particular reason for not wanting to go home. That is why they stayed on as servants and, when they grew up, were married to our male servants.’

‘Then let us ask all twelve,’ said You-shi. ‘For any who wish to go home, we can send word to their parents to come and fetch them in person, and give them a few taels for the journey. That would be safest. Unless we insist on a parent or close relative coming, some scoundrel might claim a girl under a false name and sell her again. That would defeat the kindness we meant to show. Any who do not wish to go can stay.’

‘That is a sensible arrangement,’ Lady Wang said with a smile.

You-shi and the others sent someone to tell Xi-feng. They also instructed the general steward’s office to give each teacher eight taels of silver and leave them free to depart. Everything at Pear-tree Court was to be checked, entered in an inventory and put safely away, and someone appointed to keep watch at night. Then the twelve girls were summoned and questioned in person. More than half did not want to go home. Some said that though their parents were living, selling them was all their parents cared about, and if they went back they would simply be sold again. Others had lost their parents or had been sold by uncles or brothers. Some had no one to turn to, and some said they could not bear to leave those who had been so kind to them.

Only four or five wished to leave. On hearing this, Lady Wang could only let the others stay. The four or five who were going were sent to their godmothers’ homes to wait for their own parents to collect them. Those who did not wish to leave were distributed among the rooms in the garden as servants. Grandmother Jia kept Wen-guan for herself. She assigned Parfumée, who played principal female roles, to Bao-yu; Rui-guan, who played young female roles, to Bao-chai; Ou-guan, who played young male roles, to Dai-yu; Kui-guan, who played major painted-face roles, to Xiang-yun; Dou-guan, who played comic painted-face roles, to Bao-qin; and Ai-guan, who played older male roles, to Tan-chun. You-shi asked for Qie-guan, who played older female roles.

Each now had her place. Like weary birds let out of a cage, they spent their days at play in the garden. Everyone knew they could not sew and were unused to domestic service, so they were seldom scolded.

One or two of the more thoughtful girls, however, worried that they would have no useful skill to rely on in future. They too set their former art aside and began learning sewing, spinning, weaving and the other kinds of women’s work.

One day there was a great sacrifice at court. Grandmother Jia and the others left in the fifth watch, went first to their lodgings for some cakes and light refreshments, then entered the palace. When the early breakfast was over, they withdrew to their lodgings, had breakfast there and rested briefly. They then went back to court, remaining until the noon and evening sacrifices were finished. Only then did they return to their lodgings to rest, and after supper go home.

Their lodgings happened to be in the family shrine of a high official, where Buddhist nuns tended the incense and practised their devotions. There were a great many rooms, all very clean, arranged in two courts, east and west. The Rong household rented the eastern court and the Prince of Beijing’s household the western one. The Dowager Princess and the Princess of Beijing rested there each day. With Grandmother Jia and her party in the eastern court, they went in and out together and could look after one another. There is no need to dwell on these outside details.

In Prospect Garden, meanwhile, Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang’s daily absences, together with the month away accompanying the coffin before their return, gave all the maids and older women plenty of leisure. They spent much of it wandering in the garden. All the women who had attended the girls at Pear-tree Court had now been withdrawn from there and scattered about the garden to wait on orders, adding several dozen people to its population.

Among Wen-guan and her companions there were proud tempers, girls who relied on their patrons to bully those beneath them, girls who were particular about their clothes and food, and girls with sharp tongues. Most were little inclined to keep quietly within the rules.

The older women had consequently all nursed grievances against them, though they had not dared argue with them openly. Now that the school had been disbanded, the women had their wish. Some washed their hands of the girls, while others, narrower-minded, still cherished old resentments. But with the girls assigned to individual mistresses’ and masters’ rooms, they did not dare molest them.

As it happened, that day was Qingming. Jia Lian had prepared the customary annual offerings and taken Jia Huan, Jia Cong and Jia Lan to the Temple of the Iron Threshold to make sacrifices before the coffins and burn paper money. Jia Rong of the Ning mansion and several other members of the clan had each prepared their offerings and gone there too.

Bao-yu had not recovered enough to go. After his meal he felt drowsy.

‘It is such a lovely day,’ Aroma said. ‘Go out for a little walk. Don’t put down your bowl of gruel and go straight to sleep, or it will lie heavy inside you.’

Bao-yu could only do as she said. Leaning on a stick, his feet slipped loosely into his shoes, he made his way out of the court. Since the garden had lately been allotted to the older women to manage, each with her own task, everyone was busy. Some were trimming bamboo, some pruning trees, some planting flowers and others sowing beans. On the pond the boatwomen were moving their boats about, scooping up mud to plant lotus roots. Caltrop, Xiang-yun and Bao-qin sat with their maids on the rocks, watching the work for amusement. Bao-yu slowly approached them.

‘Quick, send that boat away!’ Xiang-yun cried with a laugh when she saw him. ‘They have come to take Cousin Lin home!’

Everyone laughed. Bao-yu reddened, but laughed too.

‘No one has a kind thought for someone who has been ill. You have to mimic him and make fun of him as well.’

‘Even the way you are ill is different from everyone else,’ said Xiang-yun, laughing. ‘You invite a laugh, and then you blame us!’

Bao-yu sat down with them and watched the bustle for a while.

‘There is a wind here, and the stone is cold,’ Xiang-yun said. ‘Why not go and sit indoors for a bit?’

Bao-yu had been about to visit Dai-yu anyway. He got up, took his stick, said goodbye and followed the bank by Drenched Blossoms Bridge. The willows trailed golden threads, and the peach trees unfurled rosy clouds. Beyond the rocks stood a great apricot tree. Its flowers had all fallen; thick leaves cast a green shade, and the branches were already set with many little apricots the size of beans.

‘How many days can I have been ill?’ Bao-yu thought. ‘And I have missed the apricot blossom altogether. Already, before I knew it, “Green leaves have made a shade, and branches teem with fruit.”’

He gazed up at the little apricots, unwilling to turn away. Then he remembered that a husband had been chosen for Xing Xiu-yan. Marriage was a great matter for men and women, certainly, and could not be dispensed with; yet it meant one lovely girl fewer. In no more than two years, for her too, ‘Green leaves have made a shade, and branches teem with fruit.’ A few more days and this tree’s fruit would fall, leaving its branches empty. A few more years and Xiu-yan’s raven hair would surely turn to silver, her rosy face wither. The thought grieved him. He stood there weeping and sighing over the apricot tree.1

While he was lamenting, a little bird flew down onto a branch and began to chatter. Bao-yu’s foolish fancy took possession of him again.

‘It must have been here when the apricot was in full bloom,’ he thought. ‘Now there are no flowers, only fruit and leaves, so it cries out like this. That note must be the sound of weeping. What a pity Gongye Chang is not here for me to ask him.

‘But when the flowers open next year, will this little bird remember to fly back here to meet them?’2

As he indulged these wandering thoughts, a flare of fire suddenly rose from the other side of the rocks and frightened the bird away. Bao-yu started. Then he heard someone shouting over there.

‘Ou-guan! Are you looking to get yourself killed? How dare you bring paper money in here and burn it? I’m going to tell the mistresses. Mind your skin!’

More puzzled than ever, Bao-yu hurried round the rocks. Ou-guan was crouching there, her face streaked with tears. She still held a lighted piece of paper in her hand and was grieving over the ashes of the paper money.

‘Who are you burning it for?’ he asked at once. ‘You mustn’t burn it here. If it is for your parents or brothers, tell me their names. I can have the boys outside make up parcels, write the names on them and burn them for you.’

At the sight of him, Ou-guan fell silent. He questioned her several times without getting an answer. Suddenly an older woman came up in a fury and seized her.

‘I have told the mistresses already. They are terribly angry.’

Ou-guan was still a child at heart. Afraid of being disgraced and losing face, she refused to go.

‘I tell you, don’t get above yourselves,’ the woman said. ‘Do you think you can carry on as you please, as you did outside? There are rules here.’ She pointed to Bao-yu. ‘Even our young master keeps them. What sort of creature are you, coming here to make trouble? Being frightened won’t help you now. Come along!’

‘She wasn’t burning paper money,’ Bao-yu said quickly. ‘Cousin Lin sent her to burn some waste writing paper. You did not look properly, and you have accused her wrongly.’

Ou-guan had been quite at a loss. Bao-yu’s arrival had only increased her fear. Now, suddenly hearing him make excuses for her, she changed from misery to joy and answered stoutly herself.

‘Did you really see that it was paper money? I was burning paper Miss Lin had spoilt in writing!’

The woman grew angrier still. Bending over the ashes, she picked out two scraps that had not burnt right through and held them up.

‘Still answering back! Here is the proof. We’ll settle this in the hall!’

She caught Ou-guan by the sleeve and began dragging her off.

Bao-yu caught hold of Ou-guan and knocked the woman’s hand away with his stick.

‘Take those scraps back if you like. I’ll tell you the truth. In the night I had a dream. The spirit of the apricot blossom asked me for a string of white paper money. No one from my own rooms was to burn it: someone unfamiliar had to do it for me, and then my illness would get better more quickly. So I procured the white paper money and specially asked Cousin Lin to let Ou-guan come and burn it and say the prayers for me. No one was to know. That is why I have been able to get up today, and then you had to see her. Now I’m feeling ill again, and it is all because you interfered! And you still want to report her. Go on, Ou-guan. When you see them, tell them exactly what I have said. When Her Old Ladyship comes home, I shall say this woman deliberately offended the spirit, praying for my early death.’

Ou-guan now knew what to do. She began pulling the woman along herself. The woman dropped the scraps of paper and turned to Bao-yu with an ingratiating smile.

‘I didn’t know, Master Bao. If you tell Her Old Ladyship, that will be the end of an old woman like me.

‘I’ll go back and tell the mistresses it was an offering to a spirit on your behalf, and that I was mistaken.’

‘You are not to go and report anything more,’ Bao-yu said. ‘Then I shan’t tell.’

‘But I have told them already, and they sent me to bring her. How can I not go back? Well, I’ll say I found her, but Miss Lin called her away.’

Bao-yu thought for a moment, then nodded his agreement, and the woman had to go away.

‘Who were you really burning it for?’ he asked Ou-guan. ‘If it were for your parents or brothers, I should think you had all had people burn offerings for them outside already. There must be some private reason for burning these few sheets here.’

Deeply grateful for the protection he had just given her, Ou-guan knew him now for someone of her own kind.

‘No one knows about this except Parfumée in your rooms and Rui-guan in Miss Bao’s,’ she said through her tears. ‘You happened upon me today, and then you showed me such kindness, so I must tell you too. But you must not speak of it to anyone else.’ She began to cry again. ‘I can’t very well tell you myself. When you go back, ask Parfumée quietly, where no one can hear, and you will know.’

With that she went away with an unconcerned air.

Puzzled, Bao-yu could only make his way to Naiad’s House. Dai-yu was pitifully thinner than ever, but when he asked after her he was told she was already much better than before. She saw how much weight he too had lost. Remembering what had happened, she could not help shedding tears. After they had talked a little, she urged him to go back, rest and look after himself. He had to return.

He was eager to ask Parfumée what lay behind it all, but Xiang-yun and Caltrop had come and were chatting and laughing with Aroma and Parfumée. He could not summon her without inviting questions, so he had to wait.

Presently Parfumée went off with her godmother to wash her hair. Her godmother chose to let her own daughter wash first and only then told Parfumée it was her turn. Parfumée accused her of favouritism.

‘You give me the water your daughter has used.

‘You take all my monthly allowance. Isn’t it enough that you live off me? Must you give me her leavings of everything too?’

Her godmother’s shame turned to anger.

‘Ungrateful little thing! No wonder everyone says there isn’t an actor alive you can get on with. However decent you are to begin with, that trade ruins you. You little cunt, finding fault with this and that, never an end to your filthy nagging, like a mule that bites the rest of the herd!’

Mother and daughter began to quarrel. Aroma hurriedly sent someone to them.

‘Less shouting! Her Old Ladyship is out, so none of you can say a quiet word.’

‘It is all Parfumée making trouble,’ said Skybright. ‘I don’t know what she has to be so proud of. She can act in a couple of plays, and you would think she had killed a bandit king and captured the rebels.’

‘One hand cannot clap,’ said Aroma. ‘The old one is far too unfair, and the young one is far too tiresome.’

‘You can hardly blame Parfumée,’ Bao-yu said. ‘As the saying goes, “Whatever is wronged cries out.” She has lost her family’s care, and there is no one here to look after her. They take her money and ill-treat her too. How can you blame her?’ He turned to Aroma. ‘How much does she get a month? Wouldn’t it be simpler if you took charge of it and looked after her from now on?’

‘If she needs looking after, when have I ever refused? Must I have her few coins before I will look after her? I should only be asking to be abused.’

Aroma rose, went into the other room and fetched a bottle of scented hair oil, some eggs, fragrant soap, hair cords and such things. She called an older woman and sent them to Parfumée, telling her to get fresh water, wash her hair herself and stop quarrelling.

Her godmother felt more humiliated than ever.

‘No conscience at all! Making up stories that I keep back your money!’

She struck Parfumée several times, and the girl began to cry. Bao-yu started out of the room, but Aroma hastened to stop him.

‘What are you going to do? I’ll go and speak to her.’

Skybright was there first. Pointing at the godmother, she said, ‘You really ought to know better at your age. You don’t give her what she needs to wash her hair. We supply it for nothing, and instead of being ashamed, you have the face to hit her. Would you dare hit her if she were still at the school learning her art?’

‘Call me mother for one day, and I am your mother for life,’ said the woman. ‘If she puts me to shame, I have every right to beat her.’

Aroma called Musk.

‘I am no good at quarrelling, and Skybright is too quick-tempered. Go and say a word or two to frighten her into behaving.’

Musk went over at once.

‘Stop shouting. Now let me ask you something. Never mind our rooms. Where, anywhere in this garden, have you seen a woman disciplining her daughter in a master’s or mistress’s rooms? Even if she were your own daughter, once she had been assigned to her place and given a master or mistress, they would have the right to beat or scold her. So would the senior maids. Who gave her parents leave to step in halfway and interfere? If you all went on controlling them like this, what would be the point of sending them to learn from us?

‘The older you get, the less you know of the rules! You saw Zhui-er’s mother come here and make a scene the other day, and now you want to copy her? Don’t you worry. There has been one person ill after another lately, and Her Old Ladyship has had no time to think, so I have not reported it. Once things are quieter, we shall make a thorough report and take you all down a peg. Bao-yu is only just getting better. Even we dare not speak loudly, and here you are beating someone until she howls like a wolf and shrieks like a ghost. Our superiors have hardly been out of the house a few days, and already you think there are no laws or rules. You take no notice of us. Another day or two and you’ll be beating us as well.

‘If she gives you up as a godmother, do you suppose she’ll be left buried in dung and straw?’

Bao-yu struck the threshold with his stick in anger.

‘It is extraordinary how these old women all have iron hearts and bowels of stone. They cannot look after anyone: they only torment them. How can it go on like this, year after year?’

‘What do you mean, “How can it go on”?’ Skybright said. ‘Turn the lot of them out. We don’t need these fine-looking good-for-nothings!’

The woman was too mortified to say a word. Parfumée wore only a little padded jacket of crab-apple red and lined silk trousers scattered with flowers, their legs hanging loose. Her glossy black hair fell down her back, and she was dissolved in tears.

‘You have taken a Miss Ying-ying and turned her into Hong-niang under the lash!’ Musk said, laughing. ‘Now you aren’t dressing up, you still leave yourself all loose and untidy like this.’

‘She looks very well as she is,’ Bao-yu said. ‘Don’t make her all tight and trim.’

Skybright took her over, washed her hair clean, wrung it dry in a towel and gathered it into a loose, languid knot. Then she told her to put on her clothes and come into Bao-yu’s room.

Just then the woman in charge of the inner kitchen came to ask, ‘Supper is ready. Shall we send it in?’

A little maid went in to ask Aroma.

‘After all that commotion,’ Aroma said with a smile, ‘I never noticed how many times the clock struck.’

‘Something has gone wrong with that wretched thing again,’ said Skybright. ‘It will have to be repaired.’ She took up the watch and looked at it. ‘Wait about half a cup of tea’s time. That will do.’

The little maid went away.

‘Talking of mischief,’ Musk said with a laugh, ‘Parfumée deserves a few smacks herself. She was fiddling with the pendulum for ages yesterday, and then it stopped working.’

As they talked, they laid out the things for supper. Presently the little maid came in carrying the food box and stood waiting. Skybright and Musk opened it and found only the usual four small dishes.

‘He is better now, and they still won’t give him a couple of light dishes,’ Skybright said, laughing. ‘How long must we go on with this gruel and pickles?’

She arranged the dishes, then looked again in the box. There was a bowl of ham and fresh bamboo-shoot soup. She quickly set it before Bao-yu. He leaned towards the table and took a sip.

‘How hot!’

‘My dear bodhisattva!’ Aroma said with a smile. ‘How many days have you gone without meat, to be as greedy as this?’

As she spoke, she took up the bowl and blew gently on it. Seeing Parfumée beside her, she handed it to her with a smile.

‘You must learn to wait on him too, instead of doing nothing but stare and sleep. Blow gently. Don’t spatter it with spit.’

Parfumée did as she was told and blew on it several times, very properly.

Her godmother had meanwhile hurried to bring the rice and stood waiting outside the door. When Parfumée and the other girls had first arrived, their godmothers had been assigned to them outside, and had gone with them straight to Pear-tree Court. These women belonged to the third rank of servants in the Rong household. Their work had been no more than to starch and wash the girls’ clothes; they had never waited in attendance inside and knew nothing of the rules of the inner apartments. Only through the girls had they now gained entry to the garden and followed their goddaughters to their new rooms.

After her dressing-down from Musk, this woman had learnt a little. She was terrified that Parfumée might no longer be allowed to acknowledge her as godmother, costing her a good many benefits, and was intent on winning the maids over. Seeing Parfumée blow on the soup, she hurried in, smiling.

‘She is too inexperienced. She might break the bowl. Let me blow on it.’

She reached for it as she spoke.

‘Get out!’ Skybright shouted. ‘Even if she smashes the bowl, it is not your place to blow on it. When did you find a chance to come in past this partition? Out with you!’

Then she turned on the little maids.

‘Are you all blind? She doesn’t know, but why didn’t you tell her?’

‘We tried to turn her out, and she wouldn’t go,’ they protested. ‘We told her, and she wouldn’t believe us. Now you have got us scolded too. Will you believe us at last? Of the places we are allowed to go, you may enter only half. The other half are out of bounds to you. Yet you go running into a place where even we aren’t allowed, and as if that weren’t enough, you have to put your hands and mouth to work as well!’

As they spoke, they pushed her out. Several older women waiting below the steps to collect the empty boxes and dishes laughed at her.

‘Didn’t you look in a mirror before you went in, sister?’

Burning with shame, resentment and anger, she could only swallow it.

Parfumée blew on the soup a few more times.

‘That will do,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Mind you don’t exhaust yourself. Taste a little. Is it ready?’

Parfumée thought he was joking. She only smiled and looked at Aroma and the others.

‘Why shouldn’t you taste it?’ Aroma said.

‘Watch me,’ Skybright said with a laugh, and took a sip.

Parfumée followed her example.

‘It is ready.’

She handed it to Bao-yu. He drank half the bowl, ate a few slices of bamboo shoot, then half a bowl of gruel, and wanted no more. They cleared everything away. A little maid brought a basin, and when he had washed and rinsed his mouth, Aroma and the others went out to eat.

Bao-yu gave Parfumée a look. She was clever by nature and had studied acting for several years. What was there she did not understand? She pretended to have a headache and said she did not want supper.

‘If you are not eating, stay here and keep him company,’ said Aroma. ‘I’ll save this gruel for you. You can have it later if you are hungry.’

They all went out.

Alone with Parfumée, Bao-yu told her the whole story in detail, from the moment he had seen the flare of fire: how he had found Ou-guan, how he had lied to protect her, and how she had told him to ask Parfumée. Then he asked who it was she had been making offerings to.

Parfumée was all smiles, but she gave a sigh.

‘It is a story to make you laugh and sigh together.’

Bao-yu eagerly asked her to explain.

‘Who do you think she was making offerings to?’ Parfumée said with a smile. ‘Di-guan, who died.’

‘That is friendship,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Quite right too.’

‘Friendship? Nothing of the sort. It is the maddest notion. She says she played the young male roles and Di-guan the young female roles, and they were always husband and wife. It was only make-believe, of course, but day after day the words they sang and everything they acted were full of real tenderness and loving care. So the two of them went mad. Even when they weren’t acting, at meals or sitting about, whatever they did, they were all love and affection for each other.

‘When Di-guan died, Ou-guan cried till she was half dead herself. She has never forgotten her, and burns paper money at every festival. Afterwards Rui-guan took her place. We saw Ou-guan being just as tender and attentive to her, and asked whether she had found a new love and forgotten the old. She said, “There is a great principle behind this too. Suppose a man loses his wife, and circumstances make it necessary for him to marry again. Then marrying again is what he ought to do. As long as he doesn’t put the dead wife aside and never speak of her, that is deep and faithful love. If he refuses to remarry simply because she is dead, and lives alone for the rest of his life, failing in a great duty, that is not right either. It would only make the dead woman uneasy.” Isn’t she mad and foolish? Isn’t it laughable?’

This foolish speech chimed precisely with Bao-yu’s own foolish nature. He was at once delighted and full of grief, sighing, marvelling and declaring it beyond compare.

‘When Heaven has made people like that,’ he said, ‘what need is there for a foul male creature like me to defile the world?’

He caught Parfumée by the hand.

‘In that case I have something to tell her too. It would be awkward to say it to her face. You must tell her for me.’

Parfumée asked what it was.

‘She must never burn paper money again. Paper money is a later heresy, not a teaching handed down by Confucius. Whenever a season or festival comes round, she need only have an incense burner ready and burn incense on the day, in whatever way is convenient. If her whole heart is sincere and reverent, that will reach the spirit. Foolish people do not understand. Whether they worship a god, a Buddha or the dead, they insist on making different grades, each with its own rules. They do not know that everything depends on just one thing: a sincere heart.

‘Even in days of panic and wandering, when you have no incense, any earth or grass will serve as an offering, provided it is clean. Not only the dead, but gods and spirits too will come to receive it. Look at my table: I keep just one incense burner there. I burn incense often, without regard to dates. None of them knows why, but each time I have a reason of my own. If there is plain tea, I offer a cup of tea; if there is fresh water, a cup of water. Or there may be fresh flowers, or fresh fruit, even meat soup or fish dishes. As long as the heart is sincere and the intention pure, even a Buddha may come to receive them. That is why I say it is reverence that matters, not empty forms. Tell her at once that she must not burn paper money again.’

Parfumée agreed. Presently, when supper was over, someone came to report, ‘Her Old Ladyship and Her Ladyship are home.’
