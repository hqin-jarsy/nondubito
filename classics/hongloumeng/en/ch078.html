Chapter 78 An old scholar at leisure calls for a lament for the Beautiful General; A lovestruck young gentleman invents an elegy for the Hibiscus Maiden

Chapter end

After the two nuns had taken Parfumée and the others away, Lady Wang went to pay her morning respects to Grandmother Jia. Finding the old lady in good spirits, she took the opportunity to report:

‘There was a girl in Bao-yu’s rooms called Skybright. She was getting quite grown-up, and scarcely a day in the year passed without her being ill. I often noticed that she was more mischievous than the others, and lazy too. The other day she took to her bed again for over ten days. I had a doctor see her, and he said it was girls’ consumption, so I had her sent out without delay. Even if she recovers, there is no need to bring her back. We might as well grant her family leave to marry her off. I also took it upon myself to let those girls who had been trained for the stage go. For one thing, knowing all those plays, they have no sense of what they ought or ought not to say. They talk any nonsense that comes into their heads, and how could we have the young ladies listening to it? For another, they did perform for us for a time, and it is only right that we should let them go without asking payment. Besides, there are too many maids. If we find we are short-handed, we can choose a few more to come in.’

Grandmother Jia nodded.

‘That is quite reasonable. I was thinking of doing the same myself.

‘But I thought Skybright an excellent girl. How has she come to this? In looks, quickness, conversation and needlework, I thought most of the others fell short of her. She was the one I had in mind to attend Bao-yu in years to come. Who could have guessed she would change like this?’

‘There was nothing wrong with Your Old Ladyship’s choice,’ Lady Wang said, smiling. ‘I am afraid her fate did not allow her such good fortune, and that is why she contracted this illness. As the saying goes, “A girl changes eighteen times before she is grown.” And people with abilities are apt to be a little wayward. There can be very little Your Old Ladyship has not seen. I began giving this matter my attention three years ago. At first she was the one I chose too, and I kept an eye on her. Watching quietly, I found that although she was better than the others in all sorts of ways, she was not very steady. For steadiness and a proper sense of behaviour, no one comes before Aroma. They say a wife should be virtuous and a concubine beautiful, but surely an even temper and sober conduct are better still. Aroma may be a little less pretty than Skybright, but among the girls in his rooms she would still rank first or second.

‘Besides, her manners are generous and her heart is honest. All these years she has never encouraged Bao-yu’s mischief to please him. Whenever he does anything outrageous, she does nothing but plead with him till she is ready to drop. So after watching and weighing her up for two years, until I was quite sure of her, I quietly stopped her maid’s allowance and set aside two taels out of my own monthly silver for her instead. I only meant to let her understand that she should take still more care and learn to do well. I have made no announcement, first because Bao-yu is still young, and if his father heard he might say it would interfere with his studies; and second because Bao-yu might then regard her as his own woman, who would not dare to advise or reprove him, and grow more self-indulgent than ever. That is why I have only told Your Old Ladyship today.’

Grandmother Jia smiled.

‘So that is how matters stand! That is better still.

‘Aroma was always such a quiet child. I used to think she was a gourd with no opening. But if you know her so well, you can scarcely be far wrong. And your idea of not telling Bao-yu is better still. Let none of us mention it. We need only know it in our hearts. I know perfectly well that Bao-yu will never listen to advice from a wife or concubine. I cannot make him out. I have never seen a child like him. All his other mischief is natural enough; it is this fondness for the maids that I cannot understand. It worries me, and I have often watched him without his knowing. When I saw him always playing with the girls, I supposed that as he grew older his desires were growing too: he must have learnt about men and women, and that was why he wanted to be close to them. But after watching and testing him carefully, I found it was not that at all.

‘Isn’t it strange? Perhaps he was a girl who took the wrong turn in being reborn.’

Everyone laughed. Lady Wang went on to tell her how Jia Zheng had praised the boys that morning and taken them out with him, which pleased Grandmother Jia still more.

Presently Ying-chun came in, dressed to go out, and took her leave. Xi-feng also came to pay her morning respects. After attending Grandmother Jia at breakfast, they stayed talking and laughing for a while. When the old lady lay down for her midday rest, Lady Wang called Xi-feng over and asked whether she had had her pills prepared.

‘Not yet,’ said Xi-feng. ‘I am still taking the decoction. Please don’t worry about me, Aunt. I am much better now.’

Seeing that her spirits had recovered, Lady Wang believed her. She told her about dismissing Skybright and the others, then added:

‘And how is it that Bao-chai has gone home to sleep without a word, and none of you knew? I looked round everywhere on my way through the other day. I found that new nurse of Lan’s a very flighty creature too. I don’t like her either. I have spoken to your sister-in-law. We may as well let the woman go. Lan is growing up now and has no use for a nurse. I asked your elder sister-in-law, “Surely you knew Bao-chai had gone out?” She said Bao-chai had told her, but was only staying two or three days and would come back when your Aunt Xue was better. There is really nothing very wrong with your aunt, only the same cough and pain in her back that she has every year. There must be some reason for the girl’s going. Can someone have offended her?

‘She takes things to heart. We have had our relatives staying with us all this time; it would be a poor ending if we had offended them.’

‘Who would want to offend her for no reason?’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘Besides, she is in the garden all day, with no one but her cousins about her.’

‘Perhaps it was Bao-yu,’ said Lady Wang. ‘He speaks without thinking, like a simpleton, and never knows what to avoid. When he gets excited, there is no telling what he may blurt out.’

‘Now you really are worrying too much, Aunt,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Send him out on serious business to say something sensible, and he does look like a simpleton. But bring him in among his cousins, or even among the maids, young and old, and no one could be more accommodating. He is so afraid of offending anyone that there is no chance of anyone being angry with him. I think Cousin Xue must have gone because of the recent search through the maids’ belongings. Naturally, a search meant we distrusted the people in the garden. She is a relation, with maids and women of her own living there, and we could hardly search her rooms. She was afraid we might suspect her, and that was why she took it to heart and moved out of the way.

‘It was only proper of her to avoid suspicion.’

Lady Wang thought this sounded right. She lowered her head and considered it for a while, then sent for Bao-chai, explained what had happened the other day to put her mind at rest, and told her to come back and live as before.

‘I had meant to move out earlier,’ Bao-chai said with a polite smile, ‘but you had so many important things to attend to, Aunt, that I could not conveniently mention it. As it happened, Mother was unwell again the other day, and the two women we can rely on at home were ill too, so I took the opportunity to go. Now that you know, I can explain my reasons properly and take my leave today, so that I can move my things.’

‘You are too stubborn,’ Lady Wang and Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘The sensible thing is to move back in. Don’t let some trifle estrange you from your own relations.’

‘But that is quite a misunderstanding,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘Nothing of that sort made me leave. Mother is much less alert and energetic than she used to be, and at night she has no one she can rely on. There is only me.

‘Then my brother will soon be bringing home a wife. There is so much needlework to be done, and we still have not got together all the things we shall need for the house. I must help Mother see to it. You and Cousin Feng know our circumstances, Aunt; I am not making excuses. Thirdly, ever since I came to live in the garden, that little gate in the south-east corner has often been left open for me to pass through. Other people going in and out are bound to use it as a short cut too, and there is no one to question them. If something should happen through that gate, wouldn’t it be awkward for both of us? My coming into the garden to live was never a matter of much importance. A few years ago we were all young, and there was nothing for me to do at home. Rather than stay outside, I thought I might as well join my cousins here. Whether we did needlework or talked and laughed, it was better than sitting at home feeling dull. Now we have all grown older, and we all have things to attend to. Besides, year after year something has gone wrong on your side, Aunt. The garden is too large. Anything you cannot keep an eye on, even for a moment, may cause trouble. Fewer people would mean less to worry about.

‘So I am not only determined to take my leave today. I would urge you to cut back wherever you can, Aunt. It need not detract from the dignity of a great household. To my mind, the expense of the garden could be done away with altogether. We cannot go on talking of the old days. You know our family well. Surely we were not always as reduced as we are now?’

After hearing her out, Xi-feng turned to Lady Wang with a smile.

‘She is quite right. There is no need to press her.’

Lady Wang nodded.

‘I have nothing to say to that. You must do as you think best.’

While they were speaking, Bao-yu and the other boys came back. Bao-yu said his father’s party had not yet broken up, but he had sent them home first for fear it would grow dark.

‘You haven’t disgraced yourselves today, have you?’ Lady Wang asked at once.

‘Disgraced ourselves?’ said Bao-yu, laughing. ‘We have come away with a great haul of presents!’

The older women followed with the things they had taken from the pages at the inner gate. Lady Wang saw three fans, three fan pendants, six boxes of brushes and ink between them, three strings of scented beads and three jade cord-rings.

‘These are from Academician Mei,’ Bao-yu said, ‘those from the Vice-Minister, and these from Mr Li. A share for each of us.’

He took a little protective Buddha of sandalwood from inside his clothes.

‘The Duke of Qing gave me this specially.’

Lady Wang asked who had been at the party and what verses they had written. When she had finished, she had someone take Bao-yu’s share of the presents and went with him, Lan and Huan to see Grandmother Jia. The old lady was delighted with the gifts, and of course had questions of her own.

But Bao-yu could think of nothing but Skybright. As soon as he had answered all the questions, he said the jolting of his horse had made his bones ache.

‘Go back to your room at once and change,’ Grandmother Jia told him. ‘Move about a little and you will be all right. You are not to lie down.’

Bao-yu hurried off into the garden.

Musk and Ripple were already waiting with two little maids. As Bao-yu came away from Grandmother Jia’s, Ripple took up the brushes and ink, and they all went into the garden with him. Bao-yu kept saying how hot he was. As he walked, he took off his cap, loosened his belt and stripped off his outer garments for Musk to carry. He was left in a lined damask jacket the pale yellow of pine pollen, with trousers of a blood-red scarlet showing beneath it. Ripple recognised the trousers as Skybright’s work and sighed.

‘Put those trousers away after this. The thing is here, but the person is gone.’

‘Yes, Skybright made them,’ Musk put in quickly, smiling. Then she too sighed. ‘The thing is here, but the person is dead and gone!’

Ripple gave her a tug.

‘Those trousers with the pine-yellow jacket and dark-blue boots really show off that indigo-dark head and snow-white face,’ she said with a laugh.

Bao-yu walked ahead, pretending not to hear. After a few more steps, he stopped.

‘I want to walk about a little. What shall we do?’

‘What is there to be afraid of in broad daylight?’ said Musk.

‘Surely we shan’t lose you!’

She told the two little maids to stay with him.

‘We will take these things back and come again.’

‘Dear sister, wait a little and I will go too,’ said Bao-yu.

‘We will be back as soon as we have put them away. With both our hands full we look like a ceremonial procession: one carrying the Four Treasures of the Study, the other carrying cap, robe, belt and shoes. What a sight we make!’

This was exactly what Bao-yu wanted, and he let them both go.

He took the two little maids behind a rock, but did nothing in particular. He only asked:

‘Did your sister Aroma send anyone to see your sister Skybright after I went out?’

‘She sent Mamma Song,’ one of them answered.

‘What did she say when she came back?’

‘She said Sister Skybright cried out all night with her neck stretched stiff. Early this morning she shut her eyes and stopped calling. She didn’t know anything that was happening, and couldn’t make a sound. She was only gasping for breath.’

‘Who was she calling for all night?’ Bao-yu asked quickly.

‘Her mother,’ said the little maid.

Bao-yu wiped his eyes.

‘Who else?’

‘She didn’t hear her call for anyone else.’

‘You silly girl. You can’t have listened properly.’

The other little maid was very quick-witted. Hearing what he said, she stepped forward.

‘She is silly, isn’t she?’

Then she turned to Bao-yu.

‘Not only did I hear it properly, I slipped out to see her myself.’

‘How did you come to go and see her yourself?’ Bao-yu asked eagerly.

‘I thought how different Sister Skybright had always been from other people, and how very good she was to us. Now that she had been wronged and sent away, there was nothing else we could do to help her. The least we could do was go and see her ourselves, to show we hadn’t forgotten all her kindness. Even if someone found out and told Her Ladyship and we got a beating, we would willingly take it. So I made up my mind to take the beating and slipped out to see her. And what should I find? She had been clever all her life, and she was just the same at the end. She knew there was no talking to those common creatures, so she had only shut her eyes to rest her mind. As soon as I came she opened them, took my hand and asked, “Where has Bao-yu gone?” I told her what had happened.

‘She sighed and said, “Then I shall not see him.” I said, “Why not wait till he comes back, sister, and see him once? Wouldn’t that satisfy you both?” She smiled. “You don’t know. I am not dying. There is a flower deity missing in Heaven, and the Jade Emperor has commanded me to take charge. I have to take up my post over the flowers at two quarters past the middle of the wei hour. Bao-yu will not be home till three quarters past. Only one quarter between us, but we cannot meet. When someone in this world has to die, the King of the Underworld crosses off the name and sends little devils to seize the soul. If you want even a brief delay, you only have to burn paper money and pour out offerings of broth and rice. The devils forget everything in their scramble for money, and the dying person can stay a little longer.

‘“But heavenly immortals are coming to summon me. How could I keep them waiting?” I didn’t quite believe it when she said that. But when I came back to the house I took care to look at the timepiece. Sure enough, she breathed her last at two quarters past the middle of the wei hour, and at three quarters past someone came to fetch us, saying you were back. The times matched exactly.’

‘You cannot read,’ Bao-yu said eagerly, ‘so you don’t know. There really are such things. It isn’t just that there is one deity for all the flowers. Each kind of flower has a deity of its own, and there is a chief over them all. But I wonder whether she has become the chief, or only the deity of one kind of flower?’

For a moment the maid could invent nothing to answer. It was the eighth month, however, and the hibiscus was flowering beside the garden pond. What she saw gave her an idea.

‘I asked her what kind of flower she would be in charge of,’ she said quickly, ‘so that we should know how to make offerings to her afterwards. She said, “The secrets of Heaven must not be revealed. But since you are so devoted, I shall tell you. You may tell Bao-yu, and no one else.

‘“If you reveal Heaven’s secret to anyone but him, the five thunderbolts will strike your head.” Then she told me she was to be in charge of these very hibiscus flowers.’

Bao-yu found nothing strange in this. His grief gave way to joy. Pointing to the hibiscus, he smiled.

‘It would take someone like her to look after this flower. I knew she must have some great work to do. Of course, though she has risen out of the sea of suffering, I cannot help feeling sad and missing her, now that we shall never meet again.’

Then he thought, ‘I did not see her before she died, but if I go and bow before her spirit now, at least I shall have done something for the affection of these five or six years.’

He hurried back to his room and changed his clothes again. Saying only that he was going to see Dai-yu, he went out of the garden alone and made his way to the place he had visited before, expecting the coffin to be there. But as soon as Skybright had breathed her last, her cousin and his wife had gone in to report it, hoping to get the customary funeral money as quickly as possible. Lady Wang had ordered ten taels to be given them for cremation and burial expenses.

‘Take her outside and have her burnt at once,’ she had said. ‘A girl who has died of girls’ consumption must not be kept here!’

They took the money, hired men to lay her in her coffin, and had it carried to the burning-ground outside the city. Her remaining clothes, shoes, pins and rings were worth some three or four hundred in silver. Her cousin and his wife kept these to provide for their own future, locked the door and went together with the funeral. They had not returned when Bao-yu arrived, and he found no one. He stood there alone for a long time. There was nothing else to be done, and at last he turned back into the garden. On getting back to his room, he found everything dreary and went to look for Dai-yu nearby. But she was not at home. When he asked where she had gone, the maids said she was visiting Miss Bao-chai. He went on to Allspice Court, only to find it silent and deserted, the rooms emptied of their furniture. He was startled.

An old woman came towards him, and he quickly asked her what had happened.

‘Miss Bao-chai has moved out. We have been left in charge here. Not everything has gone yet. We have been helping carry some things over, and it is nearly finished now. Please go outside, sir, so that we can sweep the dust away. You will be spared the walk to this place from now on.’

Bao-yu stood dazed for a long time. The scented creepers and unfamiliar vines in the courtyard were as fresh and green as ever, yet the whole place seemed to have become desolate since yesterday. His grief deepened. He went out without a word. The shady green path outside the gate, too, remained empty for a long while, no longer full of maids from the different rooms coming and going in an endless stream, without anyone having arranged it. He bent to look at the water below the bank. It still flowed past, gently, tenderly.

‘Can there really be such heartlessness in heaven and earth?’ he thought.

After grieving for a while, he remembered the five who had gone: Chess, Picture, Parfumée and the others. Skybright was dead. Now Bao-chai’s whole establishment had gone too. Ying-chun had not yet left for good, but she had not been back for days, and matchmakers kept coming to propose marriages for her. Before long, everyone in the garden would surely have scattered. There was no use making himself miserable over it. Better to find Dai-yu and spend the day with her, then come back and pass his time with Aroma. Perhaps the two or three of them would, after all, die together and go to the same home.

With that thought he went back to Naiad’s House, but Dai-yu still had not returned. He supposed he ought to go out and wait to see Bao-chai off. Yet he could not bear the sadness of it; better not to go. He returned dejectedly to his rooms.

While he was sitting there at a loss, one of Lady Wang’s maids came to find him.

‘The master is back and wants you. He has another good subject for a poem. Come along, quickly!’

Bao-yu had no choice but to follow her out.

By the time he reached Lady Wang’s rooms, his father had gone. Lady Wang had him escorted to the study. Jia Zheng was talking to his secretaries about the pleasures of their autumn excursion.

‘Just as the party was breaking up,’ he said, ‘someone mentioned a story that deserves to be remembered for ever. “Romantic charm and grace, loyal courage and generosity”: it embodies all that those eight characters convey. An excellent subject. Everyone wants to write a lament.’

The secretaries eagerly asked what this wonderful story was.

‘There was once a prince whose title was Prince Heng,’ Jia Zheng began. ‘He was sent to hold Qingzhou. He was very fond of women and liked martial exercises in his leisure hours, so he chose a number of beauties and had them practise the arts of war every day. Whenever he was free from official duties, he would hold banquets for days on end and make the women rehearse battles and assaults on fortified positions. Among his women was one of the Lin family, the fourth daughter. Not only was she the most beautiful, she was more skilled in arms than the rest. Everyone called her Lin Si-niang.

‘Prince Heng was so delighted with her that he promoted her over all the other women and called her “the Beautiful General”.’

‘Wonderful! Extraordinary!’ the hangers-on exclaimed. ‘To put “General” after “Beautiful” makes it more charming and romantic still. An absolutely matchless turn of phrase! This Prince Heng must have been the most romantic figure of all time.’

‘Naturally,’ Jia Zheng said, smiling. ‘But there is something more remarkable and moving to come.’

They all looked astonished and eagerly asked what could have happened next.

‘The following year,’ Jia Zheng continued, ‘a rabble of surviving rebels, Yellow Turbans and Red Eyebrows, gathered again and began plundering the eastern country of Shandong.

‘Prince Heng thought these dogs and sheep scarcely worth a major expedition and went out with light cavalry to suppress them. But the rebels proved cunning and resourceful. He fought them twice without success, and in the end they killed him. All the civil and military officials in Qingzhou said, “If the prince could not defeat them, what can we do?”

‘They were about to surrender the city. When Lin Si-niang heard the dreadful news, she assembled her women commanders and gave them their orders. “You and I have all enjoyed the prince’s favour. Though we live beneath Heaven and tread the earth, we have not repaid one ten-thousandth of it. Now that he has died for his country, I believe I too should die for him. Any who wish to follow me must come at once. Those who do not should disperse now.” All the women answered with one voice that they would go. So Lin Si-niang led them out of the city that very night and fought her way straight into the rebel camp. The rebels were taken unawares, and several of their leaders were killed. But when they saw it was only a handful of women, who could not hope to achieve anything, they turned their weapons on them and fought in earnest. Not one of Lin Si-niang’s company was left alive. In that way they fulfilled her loyal and righteous resolve. When the news reached the central capital, everyone from the Son of Heaven down to the officials marvelled in astonishment. Naturally, the court later sent someone else to put down the rising. The imperial army arrived, and the rebels vanished into nothing. We need not go into that.

‘But this affair of Lin Si-niang: now you have heard it, do you not find it admirable?’

‘Admirable and extraordinary indeed,’ the secretaries sighed. ‘A wonderful subject. We certainly ought all to write laments for her.’

Someone had already brought brush and inkstone. With a few slight changes to Jia Zheng’s account, he made a short preface and handed it to him.

‘That will do,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘They already have an original preface over there. Only yesterday we received a gracious imperial command to investigate people from former dynasties onwards who deserved commendation but had been overlooked and never reported for recognition. Monks, nuns, beggars and women are all to be included. If there is even one praiseworthy deed, their histories are to be collected and sent to the Board of Rites, so that imperial honours may be requested. The original preface has accordingly gone to the Board. When everyone heard this news, they wanted to write a Lament for the Beautiful General in honour of her loyalty.’

‘And so they should,’ the others said, smiling. ‘But what is still more admirable is that the present dynasty continually bestows unprecedented honours and gracious favours, such as none of its predecessors can match. “In this sage reign nothing is left undone.” The Tang poet said it beforehand, and it has come true in our own dynasty.

‘Only in our age has that line ceased to be empty praise.’

‘Quite so,’ Jia Zheng agreed, nodding.

While they were speaking, Jia Huan and his nephew arrived, and Jia Zheng had them look at the subject. Both could write verse. In the extent of their attainments they were not far behind Bao-yu, but in the first place their learning ran in a different channel. They probably surpassed him in examination studies, while in miscellaneous reading they were far inferior. Secondly, their inventiveness was slow and heavy. They lacked Bao-yu’s airy grace, and even wrote poems as though they were eight-legged essays, with results that were inevitably stiff, commonplace and awkward. Bao-yu could not be called a scholar, but he had the advantage of natural intelligence and a longstanding love of miscellaneous books. He considered that even the ancients sometimes made things up or got them wrong, and that there was no need to be over-scrupulous. If one was forever afraid of this or that, one might heap together a composition, but it would be very dull. With that conviction in mind, he found no difficulty in a subject, easy or hard. He was like one of those glib talkers who can conjure something out of nothing: trusting to a nimble tongue, they ramble on, drag in anything that occurs to them and spin out a lengthy tale.

There may be nothing in it that will bear examination, yet they leave everyone at the table basking in a spring breeze. Even a stern moralist with a weighty argument cannot quite crush that charm. Of late Jia Zheng, growing older, had lost much of his appetite for fame and advancement. His original nature had, after all, been that of a free spirit who enjoyed poetry and wine. It was only among his sons and nephews that he had felt obliged to enforce the proper path.

Now he saw that, although Bao-yu neglected his books, he understood these matters quite well. Judged fairly, he did not bring such terrible discredit on his ancestors. Indeed, when Jia Zheng thought of those ancestors, they had all been much the same. Some had mastered examination studies, but not one had risen to greatness through them. Perhaps this was the Jia family’s appointed lot. Besides, his mother doted on the boy. He no longer insisted on forcing examination work upon him, and that accounted for his recent treatment of Bao-yu. He also wished that Huan and Lan, alongside their examination studies, might acquire something of Bao-yu’s gift. Whenever there was verse to be written, he therefore called all three together to compete.

But enough of this digression. Jia Zheng now told the three boys to compose a lament each. The first to finish would be rewarded, and the best would receive an extra reward. Jia Huan and Jia Lan had lately written several poems in front of company and were growing bolder. Having read the subject, they began thinking it over. Jia Lan was the first to finish. Jia Huan, afraid of being left behind, soon had his ready too. Both had written out their poems, while Bao-yu was still lost in thought. Jia Zheng and the others began by reading theirs.

Jia Lan’s was a seven-character quatrain:

Lin Si-niang, the Beautiful General, With jade for flesh and bone, and iron within, Gave up her life to follow and repay Prince Heng: Today the very earth of Qingzhou breathes perfume.

The secretaries praised it extravagantly.

‘For a young gentleman of thirteen to write like this! It shows what a deep tradition of learning there is in the family. No idle claim!’

‘A child’s language,’ Jia Zheng said, smiling, ‘but he has done his best.’

They went on to Jia Huan’s, an eight-line poem in five-character regulated verse:

Painted beauties know no sorrow; The general’s purpose will not rest.

Hiding her tears, she leaves the broidered curtains; With grief at heart, she marches out of Qingzhou.

She means to pay her debt to princely kindness, But how can she avenge him on the foe?

Who writes ‘Loyal and True’ upon her tomb? Her peerless grace lives on a thousand ages.

‘Better still!’ they all exclaimed. ‘Those few more years make a difference. Quite another conception.’

‘No very great faults,’ said Jia Zheng, ‘but it does not really touch the heart.’

‘Surely it is good enough. Master Huan is scarcely two years older. If he can do this before he has even come of age, with a few years’ work the uncle and nephew will rival the two Ruans.’

‘You praise them too highly,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile. ‘Their fault is that they will not study.’

He asked Bao-yu how he was getting on.

‘Master Bao is polishing every detail,’ the others said. ‘No doubt it will be full of charm and pathos, quite unlike these.’

‘I don’t think this subject suits the modern regulated forms,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘It needs the old manner: a long song or ballad, if it is really to touch the heart.’

They all rose, nodding and clapping.

‘Didn’t I say his conception would be different? With every subject, he first considers which form will suit it. That is the true method of an experienced hand.

‘Just as in cutting clothes, you must take the measurements before putting scissors to cloth. This subject is called Lament for the Beautiful General, and it already has a preface. A long song is certainly the right form. Something modelled on Bai Le-tian’s Song of Everlasting Sorrow, or a ballad on an ancient theme, half narrative, half lyric, fluent and free: only that could do it justice.’

Jia Zheng agreed. Taking up a brush to write, he smiled at Bao-yu.

‘Very well. You recite and I will write it down. If it is no good, I shall give you a beating. Who gave you leave to boast before you had done anything?’

Bao-yu had to begin. He recited a line:

Prince Heng loved arms and loved fair women too,

Jia Zheng wrote it down and shook his head.

‘Coarse.’

‘It must be like that to sound antique,’ said one of the secretaries. ‘It is not really coarse. Let us hear what comes next.’

‘We will leave it for the present,’ said Jia Zheng.

Bao-yu continued:

And so had beauties taught to ride and shoot.

Rich song and brilliant dance could give no joy; Ranks drawn and halberds levelled were his delight.

When Jia Zheng had written this down, they all exclaimed:

‘That third line alone has the plain, vigorous strength of ancient verse. Excellent! And these four lines of straightforward narrative are exactly right.’

‘Do not lavish undeserved praise,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘Let us see how he makes the turn.’

Bao-yu recited:

Before their eyes no dust or sand rose up; The general’s lovely shadow moved in red lamplight.

‘Wonderful!’ they all cried. ‘What a phrase, “no dust or sand rose up”! And then “lovely shadow moved in red lamplight”. His choice of words and lines is inspired.’

Bao-yu went on:

At times a battle cry came fragrant from her lips; Frost-spears and snowy swords were hard for tender hands to raise.

They clapped their hands and laughed.

‘More vivid than ever! Was our Master Bao there on the day, to see her tenderness and smell her fragrance? How else could he enter into it so closely?’

‘However fierce and brave women may be when they practise arms in their own quarters,’ Bao-yu said with a smile, ‘how could they be like men? One need not ask to know how delicate and timid they would look.’

‘Get on with it,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘Must you find another excuse to show off?’

Bao-yu had to think again for a moment, then recited:

Clove-bud knots upon a hibiscus cord,

‘A turn to tao, “cord”, in the xiao rhyme group: better still!’ they all exclaimed. ‘Now it really flows and soars. And what exquisite, delicate beauty in that line!’

Jia Zheng wrote it down and looked at it.

‘This is a bad line. You have already had “fragrant from her lips” and “hard for tender hands to raise”. Why more of the same? Your strength is failing, so you are trying to get by with a heap of ornaments.’

‘Even a long song needs a little decorative language,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Otherwise it feels rather bare.’

‘You go on using it,’ Jia Zheng said, ‘but how will you turn from this line to martial matters?

‘A couple more lines of this, and you will be putting feet on a snake.’

‘Then perhaps the next line could make the turn and bring this part to a close,’ said Bao-yu.

Jia Zheng gave a cold laugh.

‘How great do you suppose your powers are? You have opened things out with a rambling line, and now you propose to turn and conclude in a single one. Aren’t your ambitions rather beyond your ability?’

Bao-yu lowered his head, thought for a little, then gave them a line:

Holding no lustrous pearls, but a precious sword.

‘Will that do?’ he asked quickly.

They struck the table and cried that it was superb. Jia Zheng wrote it down, looked at it and smiled.

‘Leave it for the present. Go on.’

‘If it will do, I should like to go straight on to the end,’ said Bao-yu. ‘If it will not, please cross it out altogether, and I will think of a different idea and find new words for it.’

‘Too much talk!’ Jia Zheng snapped. ‘If it is bad, write it again. What if you have to write ten versions or a hundred? Are you afraid of the work?’

Bao-yu could only think for a while and continue:

The battle over, late at night, heart and strength fail; Rouge-marks and powder stain the merfolk silk.

‘Another passage finished,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘What comes next?’

Bao-yu recited:

Next year the roving rebels swept through Shandong, Fierce enough to swallow tigers and leopards, swarming like bees.

‘Excellent, that “swept”!’ they said. ‘A single word shows the difference in ability. And the whole turn is free from stiffness.’

Bao-yu continued:

The prince led Heaven’s troops to wipe them out; He fought once, fought again, and could not win.

A bloody wind snapped wheat upon the ridges; Sun shone on banners; the tiger tent stood empty.

Green hills were silent, water whispered on: That was the hour Prince Heng fell in battle.

Rain drenched white bones, and blood had stained the grass; The moon chilled yellow sand; ghosts watched the corpse.

‘Excellent, excellent!’ they all cried. ‘The arrangement, the narrative, the language: everything is perfect. Let us see how he brings in Si-niang. There is bound to be another wonderful turn, another extraordinary line.’

Bao-yu recited:

Soldiers and generals sought to save themselves; Qingzhou was all but ashes before their eyes.

Then, unexpected, loyalty shone from the women’s rooms: Prince Heng’s own favourite rose in wrath.

‘What a graceful unfolding of the story,’ they all said.

‘Too much of it,’ said Jia Zheng. ‘I am afraid the rest will be cumbersome.’

Bao-yu continued:

Who stood first in the favour of Prince Heng? Lin Si-niang, the Beautiful General.

She ordered Qin’s girls forward, drove Zhao’s women on: Bright plum and sumptuous peach approached the field.

Tears on embroidered saddles, heavy grief of spring; Armour of iron silent, night air cold.

Victory or defeat could not be foretold; They swore in life or death to repay their former lord.

The raging rebels could not be withstood: Alas for broken willows, shattered flowers!

Souls clung about the walls, close to their home; Horses trampled rouge; sweet fragrance filled the marrow.

The news sped like a star into the capital: Whose sons and daughters did not grieve?

The Son of Heaven, alarmed, mourned the lost defence; Civil and military officers hung their heads.

Why should these officers uphold the court, Yet fall below a woman, Lin Si-niang?

For Si-niang I breathe a long, long sigh; My song is done; what I would say still wanders.

When he had finished, the praise went on and on, and everyone read the whole poem through again.

‘You have managed a few lines,’ Jia Zheng said with a smile, ‘but after all it does not quite touch the heart.’

Then he told them to go. Like prisoners who had received a pardon, all three boys came out together and returned to their own rooms.

For the others there was nothing more to relate. When evening came, they went to bed. Bao-yu alone was full of grief. Back in the garden, he suddenly caught sight of the hibiscus by the pond and remembered the little maid’s story that Skybright had become its goddess. Joy stirred in him again, and he stood for a while looking at the flowers and sighing. Then he remembered that he had made no offering before her spirit since her death. Why not make one before the hibiscus now? It would fulfil his duty, and be more distinctive than the common way of mourning before a spirit tablet. He was about to begin the ceremony.

Suddenly he checked himself.

‘Even so, I must not be careless. My dress and cap must be in order, and the offerings properly prepared, if I am to show sincere respect.’

He thought a little.

‘Following an ordinary funeral ceremony would never do. I must find a fresh way, devise a form of my own, romantic and strange, with nothing of the world about it. Only that would be worthy of the two of us. Besides, the ancients said, “Water from stagnant pools and roadside puddles, the humble duckweed, southernwood and tangled water-plants, may be offered to princes and dukes and presented to spirits.” It is not the value of the thing that matters, only the sincerity and reverence of the heart. That is the first point. Secondly, the elegy and lament must be my own conception. I must trust my own eye and hand, not borrow an old pattern and fill in a few words to fool the ear and eye. I must shed tears and weep blood, choke on every word, sob at every sentence. Better too little art and too much sorrow: I must never prize fine language at the cost of grief. Besides, the ancients often used veiled criticism. I should not be the first to begin it. But people today are so bewitched by fame and success that all taste for antiquity has been washed away. They are afraid it will be unfashionable and hinder their careers.

‘I care nothing for fame or success, nor am I writing for the world to read and praise. Why should I not go far back to the men of Chu for my models: Great Words, Summoning the Soul, Encountering Sorrow, Nine Changes, Withered Trees, Questions and Challenges, Autumn Floods and Life of the Great Man? I can mingle single lines, chance upon a brief couplet, use a historical allusion or devise an allegory. I shall let my thoughts go where they will and my brush follow. In happiness I can make a game of writing; in sorrow, use words to record my pain. When the words convey the meaning and the meaning is exhausted, I shall stop. Why should I be confined, like the rest of the world, to a tiny square?’

Bao-yu was no reader of books to begin with. Now that he had got this crooked notion into his head, how could he possibly produce good poetry or prose? But he wrote as he pleased, with no thought of winning recognition or admiration, and let his absurdities run riot. In the end he invented a long piece, wrote it out in regular script on a length of the ice-fine merfolk gauze Skybright had always liked, and called it Elegy for the Hibiscus Maiden. It began with a prose preface and ended in song. He also prepared four things she had liked and, by the moonlight that night, had the little maid carry them to the hibiscus flowers.

After making his bows, he hung the elegy from a hibiscus branch and read it through his tears:

‘In an era of unchanging peace, in the month when hibiscus and cassia vie in fragrance, on a day when nothing can be done, Impure Jade of the House of Green Delights reverently offers the stamens of many flowers, ice-fine merfolk gauze, water from Drenched Blossoms and Maple-dew tea. Small though these four things are, let them convey his sincerity and attest his faith. He presents this sacrifice to the Hibiscus Maiden, who in the White Emperor’s palace watches over the beauties of autumn, and says:

‘Sixteen years have passed since you came into this turbid world. The native place and surname of your forebears have long been lost beyond recovery. Yet I, Jade, was granted your companionship among quilts and pillows, at washing and combing, on evenings of rest, feasting and play, in intimate familiarity, for only five years and eight months and a little over. When I remember you alive, gold and jade cannot express the worth of your substance; ice and snow cannot express the purity of your nature; stars and sun cannot express the brightness of your spirit; flowers and moon cannot express the beauty of your face. All your sisters admired your grace and refinement; all the old women revered your kindness and virtue. Who could have foreseen that doves and poison-birds, hating the height of its flight, would bring the eagle into their nets; that weeds and burrs, envying its fragrance, would have the sweet orchid cut down!

‘A flower is frail: how could it withstand a raging whirlwind? A willow is full of sorrow: how could it endure a sudden storm? You chanced to meet the slander of venomous insects, and sickness entered beyond all cure. So the red faded from your cherry lips, whose music became groans; the fragrance withered on your apricot face, whose colour gave way to the look of hunger. Lies, rumours, insults and shame came from behind screens and curtains; thorns and brambles spread about your windows and doors. It was not through fault of your own that you were cast down: you died under a burden of abuse. Sorrow sank you into endless darkness; false accusation left you wronged beyond redress. Your lofty nature was envied: in a woman’s chamber you knew the bitterness of Changsha. Your fiery honesty brought you danger: a woman’s fate was crueller than the wilds of Yu. You stored your bitterness alone. Who pitied a life cut short? The immortal cloud has scattered; your fragrant footsteps cannot be found.

‘Juku Island is lost to sight: whence shall the incense that defeats death come? The spirit raft is lost upon the sea: I cannot obtain the drug that restores life. Only yesterday I drew your brows in smoky blue; today the jade of your ring is cold, and whom shall I ask to warm it? Medicine still remains in the vessel on the stove; the traces of tears still stain my lapel. The mirror is divided, the phoenix parted: in sorrow I open Musk’s scented, moon-like dressing-case. The comb has turned to a dragon and flown: in grief I break the teeth of Sandal’s fragrant cloud-comb. Gold ornaments lie abandoned in the weeds; a kingfisher-green box is gathered from the dust. Magpie Tower stands empty, the needle for the Seventh Night hanging there in vain. The mandarin-duck girdle is broken: who will join its five-coloured threads? And now it is autumn’s golden season, the White Emperor’s time. Beneath a lonely quilt there are dreams; in an empty room there is no one. Moonlight dims on the paulownia steps: fragrant soul and lovely shadow have both dissolved. Scent fades within the hibiscus curtains: soft breathing and low words are heard no more. Withered grass reaches the sky, and not reeds alone; sorrowful sounds fill the earth, and all are crickets. Dew wets the moss on the evening steps; no cold beating-block sounds pass through the curtain. Rain wets the creeping figs on the autumn wall; across the courtyard a plaintive flute is scarcely heard. Your fragrant name has not died: the parrot beneath the eaves still calls it. Before your lovely form was lost, the begonia beyond the railing had already aged. Behind the screen where we played hide-and-seek, your lotus-petal feet are silent; before the court where we matched grasses, the orchid shoots wait in vain.

‘Embroidery threads lie cast aside, the work unfinished: who will cut the silver paper and coloured strands? Ice-fine silk is broken; the gilded iron has not pressed the imperial scent into it. Yesterday, under a stern command, I hurried to the carriage and journeyed far from the fragrant garden. Today, braving a loving mother’s authority, I took my staff, only to leave your lonely coffin behind too soon. When I heard that the coffin had been burnt, I was ashamed to have broken our pledge of a shared grave; when fire overtook your stone chamber, I blushed to earn the reproach that we had not become ashes together.

‘Now, in the west wind by an ancient temple, green corpse-lights linger; on a barren mound at sunset, white bones lie scattered. Catalpas and elms sigh; fleabane and wormwood murmur. Beyond the misty grave the apes cry; about the smoky ridges the ghosts weep. I thought that within the red gauze curtains a young gentleman’s love was deep; only now do I believe that beneath the mound of yellow earth a maiden’s lot was cruel. The tears of blood at Runan fall, drop on drop, into the west wind; the love left over at Zize speaks silently to the cold moon. Alas! Surely this was the work of evil human sprites; can the gods and spirits be jealous too? Clamp shut the mouths of slandering slaves: how could their punishment be lenient? Cut open the hearts of violent women: still my anger would not be appeased!

‘Your ties with the dusty world were brief, but how could my poor devotion end? My yearning thoughts drove me to ask and ask again. Only then did I learn that the Lord on High had marked your worth and that a summons awaited you in the Palace of Flowers. In life you ranked with orchids; in death you govern hibiscus. The little maid’s account may sound unfounded; to Impure Jade’s mind it has firm grounds. Why? Long ago Ye Fa-shan drew out a soul to compose an inscription, and Li Chang-ji was summoned to write a record. The events differ, but the principle is one. If the task is matched to the talent, how could the choice fall carelessly upon an unfitting person? Now I believe that Heaven’s appointments are most justly weighed, most aptly made, and do not wrong the gifts bestowed upon their holders. Hoping that your undimmed spirit may ascend and descend here, I venture, despite the vulgarity of my words, to trouble your discerning ear. I sing to summon you:

‘Why is Heaven so blue, ah, so blue: do you ride a jade dragon through its vault? Why is earth so vast, ah, so vast: do you ride a chariot of jade and ivory down to the earth of the Yellow Springs? I see a canopy’s many-coloured brilliance: or is it the light of Winnowing Basket and Tail?

‘Are feathered standards arrayed before you, with Rooftop and Void guarding your sides? Do you drive Feng-long, lord of thunder, as your close attendant, while Wang-shu of the moon moves away? I hear the creaking of chariot wheels: are you journeying behind phoenix and sacred waterbird? I ask of the rich, hidden fragrance: have you strung sweet heng and du herbs into a girdle?

‘Your skirts dazzle and sparkle: have you fashioned luminous moon-pearls into earrings? Have you spread luxuriant herbs to make an altar, and raised lotus flames to burn orchid oil? Have you decorated gourds for ritual cups and strained fine green wine to fill them with cassia liquor? I gaze into the clouds, my eyes fixed: is there something I seem to glimpse?

‘I bend towards the hidden depths and strain my ears: is there something I dimly hear? You seek boundless roaming, with nothing to bar your way: could you bear to abandon me in the dust? Shall I ask Feng-lian, lord of wind, to drive my carriage, hoping to join your reins and go home with you? My heart is full of sorrow: what use is all this empty crying? You lie at ease in your long sleep: has Heaven’s course brought its change here? Now that the deep grave is secure, and you have returned to the true, into what else could you change? I am still fettered, still suspended here: will your spirit come to me and bid me follow? Come, ah, come and stay: will you not come!

‘Yet if your dwelling is in primal vastness, your abode in stillness, even were you here I could not see you. I draw up misty creepers for screens along your way; I array spear-like sweet flag in serried ranks. I rouse the willow eyes that love to sleep; I free the lotus heart from bitterness. The Pure Maiden is invited to the cassia crag; the Lady of the Luo is welcomed at the orchid islet. Nong-yu plays the reed organ; Han-huang strikes the wooden tiger. The consort of Mount Song is summoned; the Lady of Mount Li is notified. The turtle manifests its power by the Luo; the beasts perform the Xianchi dance. Deep in the Red Water, dragons sing; gathered in the Pearl Grove, phoenixes rise. Come to my sincerity, not to vessels and baskets of offerings. Set your wheels moving from the City of Rosy Clouds; turn your banners back towards the Dark Garden. The manifest and hidden seem to meet, then gathering vapours suddenly bar the way. Smoke and cloud part and join; mist and rain blur all to emptiness. The dusty haze draws in, the stars are high; stream and hills shine fair beneath the moon at its height. Why is my heart so troubled, as though in waking or in dream the vision lived? I sigh and gaze in sorrow; I weep and wander. Human voices are still; Heaven’s music sounds in the bamboo. Startled birds scatter in flight; fish make a sound as their mouths open and close upon the water. To record my grief is my prayer; to complete the rites, my hope of blessing. Alas, alas! May you accept this offering!’

When he had finished reading, he burnt the silk and poured out the tea. Still he could not bear to leave. The little maid had to urge him again and again before he turned away. Suddenly someone behind the rocks said with a laugh:

‘Please stay a moment.’

Both were startled. The little maid looked round and saw a figure coming out among the hibiscus flowers.

‘Oh no! A ghost!’ she shrieked. ‘Skybright has really come back to show herself!’

Bao-yu, frightened too, hastily turned to look. But for that, you must listen to the next chapter.
