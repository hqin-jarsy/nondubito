Chapter 55 A foolish concubine shames her daughter in a petty quarrel; Crafty servants plot to deceive a young mistress

Chapter end

Now the Lantern Festival was over. The reigning emperor governed the realm by filial piety, and a Dowager Consort in the palace was unwell. All the imperial consorts had therefore reduced their meals and laid aside their adornments. Not only could they no longer visit their families, but feasting and entertainments had been suspended too. There was consequently no lantern-riddle gathering at the Rong mansion that year.

Scarcely was the New Year bustle over when Xi-feng had a miscarriage. For a month she stayed in her rooms, unable to manage affairs, with two or three imperial physicians prescribing for her every day. Trusting in her strength, she went on planning and calculating even though she did not go out. Whenever something occurred to her, she sent Patience to report it to Lady Wang. Whoever tried to dissuade her, she would not listen.

Lady Wang felt as though she had lost her right arm. How much energy could one person have? She decided important matters herself, but temporarily entrusted all the lesser household business to Li Wan. Li Wan valued goodness more than ability, and inevitably gave the servants too much licence. Lady Wang therefore appointed Tan-chun to deal with matters jointly with her. It was only to be for a month: once Xi-feng had rested and recovered, everything would be handed back to her. But Xi-feng’s constitution was deficient in qi and blood. Young as she was, she had never learnt to look after herself, and her constant determination to outdo others in strength and cleverness had further exhausted her mind and energies. Though it was a miscarriage, it left her seriously depleted. After a month she began to suffer from bleeding as well. She would not speak of it, but the others, seeing how yellow and thin her face had grown, knew that she had not had the rest and care she needed.

Lady Wang told her simply to take her medicine and attend to her recovery, and forbade her to concern herself with affairs. Xi-feng herself was afraid that the illness might grow serious and make her a laughing-stock. She therefore hoped to snatch a respite in which to recover, impatient to be her old self again at once. Yet it was not until the eighth or ninth month, after taking medicine and resting all that time, that her strength gradually returned and the bleeding slowly ceased. But that belongs to a later part of the story.

For the present, seeing her in this condition, Lady Wang could hardly release Tan-chun and Li Wan from their duties. There were so many people in the Garden that she feared they might not all be properly looked after. She therefore sent specially for Bao-chai and asked her to keep a careful watch everywhere.

‘Those old women are no use. Give them a spare moment and they’re drinking or playing cards. They sleep all day and play cards all night. I know all about it. While Feng was about, they had someone to be afraid of. Now they’ll be taking advantage again. My dear, you’re a sensible girl. Your younger cousins are still children, and I have no time. Will you take a little trouble for me for a day or two and keep an eye on things? If there’s anything I haven’t thought of, come and tell me. Don’t wait until the old lady asks about it and I have nothing to say. If those people misbehave, speak to them freely. If they won’t listen, report it to me. We mustn’t let anything serious happen.’

Bao-chai could only agree.

It was the first month of spring. Dai-yu’s cough had returned, and Xiang-yun too, affected by the season’s unhealthy airs, lay ill at Allspice Court, with doctors and medicines coming and going all day. Li Wan and Tan-chun lived some distance apart. Now that they were working together, things were not as they had been in former years, and it was inconvenient for the people who had to come and report to them. They agreed to meet every morning in the small three-bay reception hall south of the Garden gate, transact their business there, and return to their rooms after breakfast, around midday. The hall had originally been set aside for the eunuchs on duty during the Imperial Visit. Since then it had had no use, apart from the old women keeping watch there at night. The weather was now warm; it needed no elaborate fitting-out. A few furnishings would make it ready for the two of them.

This hall too had a plaque. Its four characters read ‘Fostering Benevolence, Instructing in Virtue’, though the servants simply called it the Council Hall. The two now came there daily at the middle of the mao hour and did not disperse until noon. The married women responsible for the various household duties came and went in an unending stream to make their reports.

When they first heard that Li Wan was to manage things alone, the servants were secretly delighted. She had always been kindly, ready with favours but never with punishments; she would plainly be easier to put off than Xi-feng. Even when Tan-chun was added, they thought her no more than a young, unmarried girl, normally quiet and even-tempered. They paid little heed to her and grew far more slack than they had dared to be under Xi-feng. But after three or four days, when several matters had passed through her hands, they began to discover that Tan-chun was every bit as exacting in detail as Xi-feng. She merely spoke more quietly and had a gentler manner.

It happened that during these days more than a dozen houses of princes, dukes, marquises, earls and hereditary officials, all related to the Rong and Ning households or connected with them by friendship, often of long standing, had promotions or demotions, weddings or funerals to mark. Lady Wang had no leisure from the calls, receptions and farewells of congratulation or condolence. That left no one to see to things in the main house, so the two spent the whole day in the hall.

Bao-chai, meanwhile, supervised matters in Lady Wang’s apartments all day and left only when she returned. Every evening, in a pause from her needlework before bed, she took a small sedan-chair and made a round of the Garden with the night-watch attendants. With the three of them managing things in this way, the servants found that they had to be even more careful than when Xi-feng was in charge. Inside and outside alike, they complained among themselves:

‘We’ve only just got rid of one sea-patrolling yaksha, and now we’ve three mountain-guarding Tai Sui gods instead. We can’t even steal a little time at night for a drink and some fun!’

One day Lady Wang was going to a banquet at the Marquis of Jinxiang’s. Li Wan and Tan-chun washed and dressed early, attended her departure, then returned to sit in the hall. They had just begun their tea when Wu Xin-deng’s wife came in to report.

‘Aunt Zhao’s brother, Zhao Guo-ji, died yesterday. It was reported to Her Ladyship yesterday. She said she knew, and told us to bring the matter to you, miss, and to Mrs Zhu.’

Having said this, she stood to one side with her hands lowered and said no more. Quite a number of people were there to report on business, all intent on discovering how the two would manage. If they proved competent, everyone would settle down to a proper fear of them. If there were the slightest flaw or impropriety, the servants would not only cease to fear them: once through the inner gate, they would invent any number of stories to make them a laughing-stock. Wu Xin-deng’s wife had already decided on her course. Before Xi-feng she would eagerly have offered suggestions and looked up plenty of precedents for Xi-feng to choose from. Now, despising Li Wan for her honesty and Tan-chun for being a young girl, she said no more than this, to see what ideas they might have of their own.

Tan-chun consulted Li Wan. Li Wan thought for a moment.

‘When Aroma’s mother died the other day, I heard she was given forty taels. Allow forty for this case too.’

Wu Xin-deng’s wife promptly said yes, took the authorisation tally and was leaving when Tan-chun called her back.

‘Wait. Come back here.’

The woman had no choice but to return.

‘Don’t draw the silver yet. Let me ask you something. In former years, the old concubines in Grandmother’s apartments belonged to two different classes: some were household-born and some had been brought in from outside. When a relation of a household-born woman died, how much was granted? And how much when it was one of those brought in from outside? Tell us the two rates.’

At this question Wu Xin-deng’s wife suddenly forgot everything. She hurried to put on a smile.

‘It’s hardly an important matter. Whatever you give, who would dare dispute it?’

‘That’s nonsense,’ Tan-chun said with a smile. ‘If you ask me, a hundred would be very nice. But if we don’t follow precedent, it’s not only that you’ll laugh at us. We shan’t be able to face your Second Mistress afterwards.’

‘In that case, I’ll go and look up the old accounts,’ the woman said, smiling. ‘I can’t remember just now.’

‘You’ve been doing this work for years,’ Tan-chun said, still smiling, ‘yet you can’t remember, and come here to make difficulties for us.

‘Do you have to go and look it up on the spot when you report to your Second Mistress? If that’s how it is, Cousin Feng can hardly be called strict. She must be positively lenient! Go and fetch it at once for me to see. Let it wait another day and no one will say you’ve been careless. They’ll think we don’t know what to do.’

Scarlet-faced, Wu Xin-deng’s wife hurried out. The other women put out their tongues. Inside, other business was reported.

Presently Mrs Wu returned with the old accounts. Tan-chun found that two household-born women had each received twenty taels, and two who had been brought in from outside had each received forty. There were also two further cases among the latter, one of a hundred taels and one of sixty. Beneath each of these entries the reason was given: in one case, sixty extra taels had been allowed for bringing the parents’ coffins from another province; in the other, twenty extra had been allowed to buy a burial plot. Tan-chun handed the accounts to Li Wan to read.

‘Allow twenty taels,’ she said. ‘Leave the accounts here. We want to look through them carefully.’

Wu Xin-deng’s wife went away.

Suddenly Aunt Zhao came in. Li Wan and Tan-chun promptly offered her a seat. Aunt Zhao began at once:

‘It’s bad enough having everyone in these rooms trample on my head. You ought to think a little, miss, and stand up for me.’

She began to cry as she spoke, tears running down her face and her nose streaming.

‘Who do you mean, Aunt?’ Tan-chun asked at once. ‘I don’t understand.

‘Who’s trampling on you? Tell me, and I’ll stand up for you.’

‘You are, miss. Who am I to tell?’

Tan-chun rose hastily.

‘I would not presume to.’

Li Wan rose too and tried to soothe her.

‘Please sit down, both of you, and listen,’ said Aunt Zhao. ‘I’ve spent all these years wearing myself away in these rooms like oil burning in a lamp, and borne you and your brother, and now I’m not even as good as Aroma. What face have I left? Never mind me: you’ve none left either!’

‘So that’s what it is,’ Tan-chun said with a smile. ‘As I said, I wouldn’t presume to break the rules or act against what is right.’

She sat down, opened the accounts for Aunt Zhao to see and read them aloud to her.

‘These are the old rules handed down by our ancestors. Everyone follows them. Am I alone to change them? And it isn’t only Aroma. If Huan takes someone from outside in time to come, she will naturally be treated in the same way as Aroma. This isn’t a question of who stands higher or lower. There’s no question of gaining or losing face.

‘They are Her Ladyship’s servants. I’m acting according to the old rules. If they think the arrangement good, they have our ancestors and Her Ladyship to thank for their kindness. If they call it unfair, they’re foolish and don’t know their good fortune. We can only let them complain. If Her Ladyship gave someone a house as well, what credit would that bring me? If she gave them not a single cash, what discredit would that bring me? With Her Ladyship out, I should think you’d be better resting quietly, Aunt. Why must you keep worrying over things? Her Ladyship is very fond of me, but your constant troublemaking has distressed her time and again. If only I were a man and could go out into the world, I should have left long ago and made something of myself. Then I would have a way of my own. But I’m a girl. I haven’t the liberty to speak even one word out of turn. Her Ladyship understands it all perfectly. She has asked me to look after the household now because she thinks well of me, and before I’ve done a single good thing, you come to humiliate me. If she hears of this and thinks it puts me in a difficult position, she may stop me managing things. That really would be a disgrace, for you as well as for me!’

As she spoke, tears began to roll down her cheeks in spite of herself. Aunt Zhao could find nothing else to answer.

‘If Her Ladyship is fond of you, all the more reason to give us a helping hand. You’re so busy winning her affection that you forget us.’

‘How have I forgotten you? What helping hand am I to give? You should each ask yourselves that. What master doesn’t value someone who works hard and is useful? What decent person needs to be pulled along by someone else?’

Li Wan kept trying to pacify Aunt Zhao.

‘Don’t be angry, Aunt. You can’t blame her. Of course she wants to help you. How can she say so openly?’

‘Now you’re being foolish too, Sister-in-law,’ Tan-chun said at once. ‘Who am I to help? Since when do young ladies advance the servants? You should know for yourselves whether they’re good or bad. What has it to do with me?’

‘Who asked you to help other people?’ Aunt Zhao demanded angrily. ‘When you weren’t in charge, I didn’t come asking you. But now what you say goes. One is one and two is two.

‘Your uncle’s dead. If you gave another twenty or thirty taels, would Her Ladyship really refuse you? She’s a good mistress, plainly enough. It’s you people who are so sharp and mean. A pity she has kindness to spare and nowhere to use it! Don’t worry, miss, it isn’t your money that’s wanted. I was hoping that once you were married you’d give the Zhao family a little extra help. But you haven’t even grown your feathers yet, and already you’ve forgotten your roots and fly only for the highest branches!’

Before she had finished, Tan-chun’s face had turned white with anger. She could scarcely breathe. Sobbing and catching her breath, she demanded:

‘Who is my uncle? My uncle was only just promoted at the New Year to Inspector-General of Nine Provinces. Where has this other uncle sprung from? I’ve always treated you with the respect that is due to you, and the more respect I show, the more relations it produces! If that’s what you say, why did Zhao Guo-ji stand up when Huan went out, and follow him to school? Why didn’t he put on an uncle’s airs? What is the point? Who doesn’t know you bore me? Every two or three months you must find some excuse to turn it all over again, from top to bottom, as if you were afraid someone might not know and had to announce it specially.

‘I don’t know who’s disgracing whom! Luckily I still have some sense. Anyone foolish enough not to understand what is right would have flown into a rage long ago.’1

Li Wan was beside herself trying to soothe them, while Aunt Zhao went on grumbling. Suddenly someone announced:

‘The Second Mistress has sent Miss Patience with a message.’

Only then did Aunt Zhao stop. Patience came in, and Aunt Zhao hastened to offer her a seat with a smile, asking at once:

‘Is your mistress any better?

‘I’ve been meaning to come and see her. I just haven’t had a moment.’

Li Wan asked Patience what had brought her.

‘My mistress says that Aunt Zhao’s brother has died,’ Patience replied with a smile. ‘She was afraid the two of you might not know there was a precedent. The ordinary rule allows only twenty taels. But she asks the young lady to decide. Something more could be added.’

Tan-chun had already wiped away her tears.

‘Why add anything for no reason?’ she said at once. ‘Was anyone here carried for twenty-four months before being born?

‘Or did he go to war and carry his master to safety on his back? Your mistress is very clever. She has me break the precedent while she plays the kind one. She can happily do favours with money that Her Ladyship won’t miss. Tell her I daren’t add or subtract anything or offer ill-considered suggestions. If she wants to add to it, let her be the benefactor. When she’s well enough to come out, she can add as much as she pleases.’

Patience had understood half the situation as soon as she arrived. These words made it clearer still. Seeing Tan-chun’s anger, she dared not treat her with their usual cheerful familiarity, but stood silently to one side, her hands lowered.

Just then Bao-chai came from Lady Wang’s apartments. Tan-chun and the others rose to offer her a seat. Before they could speak, another woman came in on business. As Tan-chun had been crying, three or four little maids brought a washbasin, towels, a hand-mirror and the other things she needed. She was sitting cross-legged on a low wooden couch. The girl with the basin came up to her, knelt on both knees and held it high. Two other little maids bent their knees beside her, holding the towels, mirror, rouge and powder. Seeing that Scribe was not there, Patience hurried forward to turn back Tan-chun’s sleeves and take off her bracelets. She accepted a large towel and spread it over the front of Tan-chun’s clothes. Only then did Tan-chun put her hands into the basin and begin to wash. The woman started her report:

‘Madam, miss, the family school needs to draw Master Huan’s and Master Lan’s school allowances for the year.’

Patience spoke first.

‘What’s your hurry?

‘You can see with your own eyes that the young lady is washing her face. Instead of waiting outside, you start talking! Are you as blind to what’s proper in front of the Second Mistress? The young lady may be generous, but I shall tell my mistress that none of you has any regard for her. And when you all suffer for it, don’t blame me.’

Frightened, the woman hastily put on a smile.

‘It was careless of me.’

She hurried out as she spoke.

Smoothing powder over her face, Tan-chun gave Patience a cold smile.

‘You came a little too late. There was something else to laugh at. Even Sister Wu, who’s been doing this work so long, came to put us off without first looking things up properly. Luckily we questioned her. She had the face to say she’d forgotten. I asked whether she forgot things and went to look for them afterwards when reporting to your mistress. I shouldn’t think your mistress would have the patience to wait.’

‘If she’d tried that once, she’d have had a couple of tendons in her legs broken long ago,’ Patience replied quickly, smiling. ‘Don’t believe them, miss. They see that Mrs Zhu is a bodhisattva and you’re a modest young lady, so of course they try to shirk and put you off.’

Then she called towards the door:

‘Go on, behave as you please. We’ll have something to say about it when my mistress is well again.’

The women outside all answered with smiles:

‘Miss, you understand things better than anyone. As the saying goes, “Let whoever does the wrong bear the blame.” We wouldn’t dare deceive the young lady.

‘A daughter of the house is an honoured guest. If we really made her angry, we should die without so much as a place to be buried.’

‘It’s as well you understand that,’ said Patience with a cold smile. Then, smiling deferentially at Tan-chun, she went on:

‘You know how much the Second Mistress has to do. How could she keep an eye on all these things? She’s bound to overlook something. As they say, “The onlooker sees clearly.” You’ve been watching quietly these past few years, miss. If there are things that ought to be added or cut which she hasn’t attended to, please make the changes. First, it will benefit Her Ladyship’s affairs; and second, it will be a fitting expression of the kindness you’ve always shown my mistress.’

Before she had finished, Bao-chai and Li Wan both laughed.

‘Clever girl! No wonder Feng is so fond of her. There was nothing to add or cut in the first place, but after hearing that, we shall have to find a thing or two to consider, just to do justice to your speech.’

‘I was bursting with anger, and no one would take the edge off it,’ said Tan-chun, smiling. ‘I was just going to vent it on her mistress when she happened to arrive. Now she’s said all this, I don’t know what to do.’

She called the woman back in and asked:

‘What is this year’s silver for Master Huan and Master Lan at the family school meant to pay for?’

‘Refreshments at school, or paper and brushes,’ the woman replied. ‘Each of them has eight taels to spend in the year.’

‘All the young masters’ expenses are provided for by the monthly allowances drawn in their own rooms,’ said Tan-chun.

‘Aunt Zhao draws Huan’s two taels; Aroma, in Grandmother’s establishment, draws Bao-yu’s two; and Lan’s is drawn in his mother’s rooms. Why should each have these extra eight taels at school? So that’s what they go to school for: the eight taels! From today, that item is abolished. Patience, tell your mistress when you go back that I said this allowance must certainly be stopped.’

‘It ought to have been stopped long ago,’ Patience said with a smile. ‘My mistress did mean to stop it last year, but in the New Year rush she forgot.’

The woman could only agree and withdraw. Just then women from Prospect Garden arrived carrying the meal-boxes.

Scribe and Su-yun had already brought over a small dining-table, and Patience hurried to help serve the dishes.

‘You’ve delivered your message. Go and see to your own affairs,’ Tan-chun said with a smile. ‘Why busy yourself here?’

‘I’ve nothing else to do,’ Patience replied, smiling. ‘My mistress sent me partly with the message and partly because she thought you might be short of help. I was to help the other girls wait on you and Mrs Zhu.’

‘Why not bring Cousin Bao’s meal here, so that she can eat with us?’ Tan-chun asked.

The maids hurried out under the eaves and told one of the women to take a message.

‘Miss Bao is eating here in the hall. Tell them to send her meal over.’

Hearing this, Tan-chun called out:

‘Don’t order people about indiscriminately! Those are the housekeepers who attend to important business. Sending them to fetch food and tea! Have you no idea of rank at all?

‘Patience, you’re standing here. You go and ask for it.’

Patience answered at once and went out. The women quickly caught hold of her and whispered with smiles:

‘There’s no need for you to go, miss. We’ve already sent someone.’

As they spoke, they dusted a stone ledge with their handkerchiefs.

‘You must be tired after standing so long, miss. Rest a little here in the sun.’

Patience sat down. Two old women from the tea-room brought a cushion and spread it out.

‘The stone’s cold. This is quite clean. Please make do with it for a little while, miss.’

‘Thank you very much,’ Patience said with a polite smile.

One of them also brought a bowl of fine, freshly brewed tea, saying softly with a smile:

‘This isn’t the tea we ordinarily have. It’s what we serve the young ladies. Have a little to moisten your throat, miss.’

Patience half rose to receive it, then pointed at the women and spoke in a low voice.

‘You’ve gone much too far. She’s a young lady and won’t storm and rage. That’s her sense of dignity, and you take it as a reason to slight her and impose on her.

‘If you really drive her into a fury, the worst they’ll say of her is that she’s rough, and there it ends. You’re the ones who’ll suffer more than you can bear. If she chooses to be wilful, even Her Ladyship has to give way a little, and the Second Mistress daren’t do much about it either. Yet you’re bold enough to think nothing of her. It’s an egg dashing itself against a stone.’

‘We never dared take such liberties,’ they all said hastily. ‘It was Aunt Zhao who made the trouble.’

‘Enough, my good ladies,’ Patience whispered. ‘“When a wall is falling, everyone gives it a push.” Aunt Zhao is certainly muddle-headed, but whenever anything happens, you put all the blame on her. Do you suppose I haven’t learnt in these past few years how little regard you have for anyone, or how cunning you are? If the Second Mistress were the least bit less capable, you ladies would have brought her down long ago. Even as it is, the moment you get an opening you try to make things difficult for her. More than once you’ve had things to say at her expense. Everyone says she’s formidable and you’re all afraid of her. Only I know that in her heart she’s not exactly unafraid of you.

‘We were talking of that only the other day. Things could hardly go smoothly from beginning to end: there were bound to be a couple of angry encounters. The Third Miss may be a girl, but you’ve misjudged her. Of all my mistress’s elder and younger sisters-in-law, she’s the only one my mistress is half afraid of. And now you people think she’s beneath your notice!’

As she spoke, Ripple came over. The women hurried to greet her.

‘Rest a little too, miss. They’re laying the meal inside. Wait until they’ve cleared the table before you go in to report.’

‘I’m not like you,’ Ripple said with a laugh. ‘How can I wait here?’

She was going straight up to the hall when Patience called:

‘Come back, quickly!’

Ripple turned, saw Patience and laughed.

‘What are you doing here, mounting an outer guard?’

She came back and sat on Patience’s cushion.

‘What have you come about?’ Patience asked quietly.

‘To ask when we can draw Bao-yu’s monthly allowance and our own.’

‘That’s no great matter. Go straight back and tell Aroma from me not to bring any business here today, whatever it is. Bring one request, and it will be refused. Bring a hundred, and all hundred will be refused.’

‘Why? What’s happened?’ Ripple asked hastily.

Patience and the women all hurried to tell her the reason.

‘They’re looking for some difficult business and someone with standing to make an example of, to overawe everyone else and show them how things are to be done. Why should you be the first to run up against it? If you go in now and they make examples of you, they’ll have the old lady and Her Ladyship to consider. But if they don’t, people will say they favour one over another: that they’re frightened of anyone with the old lady and Her Ladyship behind them and daren’t touch them, but only take the pliable ones by the nose.

‘Listen: she means to overrule a couple of the Second Mistress’s arrangements too. Only then will everyone stop talking.’

Ripple put out her tongue and laughed.

‘Luckily Sister Patience was here, or I should have come away shamed, with my nose covered in ashes. I’ll hurry back and let them know.’

She rose and went away.

Bao-chai’s meal arrived next, and Patience hurried in to wait on them. Aunt Zhao had gone by then. The three were eating on the wooden couch, Bao-chai facing south, Tan-chun west and Li Wan east. The women waited quietly under the verandah. Inside, only the maids who were their close personal attendants served them; no one else dared go in unbidden.

‘Let’s all save ourselves trouble,’ the women whispered to one another. ‘No more underhand schemes. Even Mrs Wu has just had a rebuff. What standing have the rest of us?’

They talked quietly as they waited for the meal to end so that they could report. Inside, not a sound could be heard, not even a bowl or chopstick. Presently one maid lifted the curtain high and two others carried out the table. Three girls had been waiting in the tea-room with three basins of water. As soon as the table was out, they went in, and shortly came back with the basins and rinsing-bowls. Only then did Scribe, Su-yun and Oriole enter, each carrying a tea-tray with three covered bowls of tea.

When the three came out again, Scribe told the little maids:

‘Wait on them properly. We’ll come and take over when we’ve eaten. Don’t go sneaking off to sit down again.’

Only now did the women make their reports, slowly, one at a time, behaving themselves instead of treating the business with their former carelessness and contempt.

Tan-chun’s anger had gradually subsided. She said to Patience:

‘There’s something important I’ve long wanted to discuss with your mistress, and I’ve just remembered it. Come back as soon as you’ve eaten. Cousin Bao is here too. The four of us can talk it over, and then ask your mistress in detail whether it can be done.’

Patience agreed and went back.

Xi-feng asked why she had been away all day. Smiling, Patience told her everything that had happened.

‘Good, good, good!’ Xi-feng laughed. ‘Well done, Third Miss! Didn’t I say she was good? It’s only a pity her luck was poor and she wasn’t born of Her Ladyship.’

‘Now you’re talking foolishly too, ma’am,’ Patience said with a smile. ‘Even if Her Ladyship didn’t bear her, would anyone dare look down on her or treat her differently from the others?’

‘You don’t understand,’ Xi-feng sighed. ‘A son and a daughter may both be born of a concubine, but a girl isn’t in the same position as a man. When it comes to arranging a marriage, there are frivolous people nowadays whose very first question is whether the girl was born of the principal wife or of a concubine. Many won’t have one born of a concubine. Little do they realise that, never mind a concubine’s daughter, even our maids are better than other people’s young ladies. I wonder what unlucky fool will lose his chance by choosing between the two, and what fortunate man who makes no such distinction will win her.’

Then she turned to Patience with a smile.

‘You know how many ways I’ve devised to economise in these past few years. I doubt there’s anyone in the whole household who doesn’t hate me behind my back.

‘I’m riding a tiger now. I may see through a few things, but I can’t simply let up all at once. Besides, more goes out of this household than comes in. Everything, great and small, is still done according to the rules followed when the old lady was in charge, but the income from the estates is no longer what it was. Economise too much, and outsiders laugh at us, the old lady and Her Ladyship have to put up with privations, and the servants complain that we’re mean. But unless we arrange to cut expenses soon, in a few years we shall have used up everything.’

‘That’s just it,’ said Patience. ‘There are still three or four young ladies, two or three young masters, and the old lady. Those great expenses are all ahead of us.’

‘I’ve thought of that too,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘There should be enough. When Bao-yu takes a wife and Cousin Lin marries, neither need draw on the household funds. The old lady has private savings to provide for them. The Second Miss belongs to the Elder Master’s side, so she doesn’t count either. That leaves three or four. Allow ten thousand taels apiece, even at the most extravagant reckoning.

‘Huan’s wedding won’t cost much. Three thousand taels will do; a little saving somewhere or other would cover it. When the old lady’s time comes, all the main things are ready. There will only be odds and ends to pay for, three to five thousand taels at the outside. With a little more economy now, we can make it up by degrees. What worries me is some unexpected business cropping up, a thing or two out of nowhere. That would be disastrous. But leave the future for now. Eat your meal and hurry back to hear what she wants to discuss. This is just the opportunity I need. I’ve been fretting for want of a right arm. There’s Bao-yu, but he isn’t made for this sort of thing. Even if I won him over, he’d be no use. Mrs Zhu is a living Buddha, and no use either. The Second Miss is still less use, and doesn’t belong to this branch of the family in any case. The Fourth Miss is young, and little Lan younger still. As for Huan, he’s a half-frozen kitten with singed fur, only waiting for a hot stove or heated kang to crawl into. To think that two such utterly different creatures came out of the same mother’s belly! I can’t reconcile myself to it when I think of that.

‘Then there are Cousin Lin and Cousin Bao. They’re both capable, but unfortunately they’re relations staying with us, and one can hardly ask them to manage our household. Besides, one is a paper beauty-lantern that a breath of wind would spoil; the other has made up her mind: “If it’s no affair of mine, I won’t open my mouth. Ask me, and I’ll shake my head and know nothing.” One can hardly press her too far. That leaves only the Third Miss. She has both the brains and the tongue for it, she’s a proper member of our own family, and Her Ladyship is fond of her. The coolness on the surface is all because of that old creature Aunt Zhao and the trouble she makes. In her heart, Her Ladyship feels for her just as she does for Bao-yu. She’s not like Huan, whom it’s truly hard to be fond of. Left to me, he’d have been thrown out long ago. Now that she has ideas of this sort, we ought to work with her and give each other a hand. I shan’t be on my own any more. In all fairness, before Heaven and our own consciences, having someone like her to help will save us some worry and benefit Her Ladyship’s affairs too. And if you look at it from the side of self-interest and scheming, I’ve been too ruthless. It’s time I drew back a little.

‘We should look back a little. If we keep pursuing and squeezing them until they hate us bitterly, they’ll hide knives behind their smiles. Between the two of us, we have only four eyes and two minds. One unguarded moment, and we may come to grief. Let her come forward and take charge while the pressure is on, and people’s old hatred of us will ease for a time. There’s something else. I know how sensible you are, but I’m afraid you may not quite bring yourself round to it, so I’m telling you now. She may be a girl, but she understands every last thing. She’s simply careful what she says. And she knows more of books and writing than I do, which makes her more formidable still. As the saying goes, “To catch the bandits, first catch their chief.” If she means to make a start and set an example, she’s bound to start with me. If she overrules any arrangement of mine, don’t argue. The more respectful you are, and the more you say she’s right to overrule it, the better. Whatever you do, don’t be afraid I’ll lose face and start opposing her. That would spoil everything.’

Before she had finished, Patience laughed.

‘You really do take people for fools. I’ve already done all that, and now you tell me!’

‘I was afraid you had eyes and thoughts for no one but me,’ Xi-feng said with a laugh. ‘That’s why I had to warn you.

‘If you’ve done it already, you’re cleverer than I am. There you go, losing your temper again, and it’s all “you” and “I”.’

‘And I shall say “you”! If you don’t like it, here’s my face. Give it another beating. It’s not as though these cheeks haven’t tasted one before!’

‘You little baggage!’ Xi-feng laughed. ‘How many times are you going to rake that up? You see how ill I am, and still come to provoke me. Come and sit down. No one’s coming, anyway. Let’s get on with eating together.’

As she spoke, Felicity came in with two or three other little maids to set up the small kang table. Xi-feng ate only bird’s-nest congee and two small dishes of delicacies; her regular daily dishes had been stopped for the time being. Felicity put Patience’s four regular dishes on the table and served her rice. With one knee bent on the edge of the kang, Patience remained half standing below it as she ate with Xi-feng, then helped her rinse her mouth and wash. When that was done, she gave Felicity a few instructions and went back to Tan-chun. The courtyard was quiet; everyone had dispersed.
