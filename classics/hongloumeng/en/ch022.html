Chapter 22 A song leads Bao-yu to an insight into Chan; Lantern riddles sadden Jia Zheng with words of ill omen

Chapter end

When Jia Lian heard Xi-feng say that she had something to discuss with him, he stopped and asked what it was.

‘Cousin Xue’s birthday is on the twenty-first. What do you mean to do about it?’

‘How should I know? You’ve managed any number of grand birthday celebrations. Have you suddenly run out of ideas?’

‘For grand birthdays there are rules to go by. But hers is neither a great occasion nor quite an ordinary one. That’s why I’m asking you.’

Jia Lian lowered his head and thought for a while.

‘You’re being foolish today. There’s a precedent ready to hand: Cousin Lin. Whatever we did for Cousin Lin’s birthdays in past years, we can do for Cousin Xue now.’

Xi-feng gave a cold laugh.

‘Do you suppose I don’t know even that? I had settled on the very same thing. But yesterday I heard Her Old Ladyship asking everyone’s age and birthday. She heard that Cousin Xue would be fifteen this year. Though it isn’t a round-number birthday, she is approaching the hairpin age, and Her Old Ladyship said she wanted to give her a birthday party. If she really does, it will naturally have to be different from Cousin Lin’s in past years.’

‘In that case, make it a little more than Cousin Lin’s.’

‘That’s what I thought. I only wanted to know how you felt. If I added things on my own, you’d blame me for not telling you properly.’

Jia Lian laughed. ‘Enough, enough. I’m not thanking you for that empty show of consideration.

‘It’s all I ask that you don’t pry into my affairs. As if I would blame you!’

With that he went straight off, and we need say no more of him for the present. Shi Xiang-yun, meanwhile, had stayed two days and was about to go home.

‘Wait until after your cousin Bao-chai’s birthday,’ Grandmother Jia told her. ‘See the plays, and then go.’

Xiang-yun could only stay. She sent someone home to fetch two kinds of needlework she had done earlier, as her birthday gifts for Bao-chai.

Ever since Bao-chai’s arrival, Grandmother Jia had been pleased with her steadiness and even temper. Now that her first birthday since coming to the house had arrived, the old lady contributed twenty taels of her own money, summoned Xi-feng and entrusted her with arranging the feast and plays.

Xi-feng entered into the fun with a laugh. ‘When our revered ancestress gives one of the children a birthday party, who would dare find fault, whatever she chose to do? There’s no need for feasts and plays at all. Still, if you want some merrymaking, I’m afraid you’ll have to spend a few taels of your own. You go to all the trouble of digging out these twenty mouldy, rotten taels to play the hostess, and expect me to make up the rest! If you really couldn’t afford it, I’d say nothing. But gold and silver, round pieces and flat, are breaking through the bottoms of your chests, while you squeeze us dry. Look about you. Aren’t we all your children? Will Cousin Bao be the only one to carry you up Mount Wutai one day? You’re saving all your private treasure for him. Even if we aren’t fit to use it, don’t make life hard for us. Is this enough for the feast? For the players?’

The whole room burst out laughing.

Grandmother Jia laughed too. ‘Just listen to that tongue! I thought I could talk, but I can’t get the better of this monkey. Your mother-in-law wouldn’t dare answer me back, yet here you are, chattering away at me.’

‘My mother-in-law dotes on Bao-yu just as much as you do,’ said Xi-feng, laughing. ‘There’s nowhere I can go for justice, and now you accuse me of answering back!’

This set Grandmother Jia laughing again and left her in excellent spirits. That evening, after everyone had paid their respects, the mothers and daughters and cousins were chatting and laughing around her when she asked Bao-chai what plays she liked to hear and what she liked to eat. Bao-chai knew very well that the old lady enjoyed lively plays and sweet, soft food, and named all the things Grandmother Jia herself had always liked. Grandmother Jia was more delighted than ever. Next day she sent over her gifts of clothes and curios in advance. Lady Wang, Xi-feng, Dai-yu and the others all gave presents according to their means; there is no need to list them.

By the twenty-first a neat little stage for a family performance had been put up in Grandmother Jia’s inner courtyard. A newly formed company of child players had been engaged, offering both the Kun and Yiyang styles. Several tables for the family feast were laid in Grandmother Jia’s principal room. There was not a single visitor from outside: only Aunt Xue, Shi Xiang-yun and Bao-chai counted as guests; everyone else belonged to the household.

Early that morning, finding Lin Dai-yu nowhere about, Bao-yu went to look for her in her room. She was reclining on the kang.

‘Get up and come to breakfast,’ he said with a smile. ‘The plays will be starting soon. Which would you like to see? I’ll ask for it.’

Dai-yu gave a cold laugh. ‘If that’s how you feel, engage a company specially and have them perform the plays I like. There’s no need to ask me now, taking advantage of somebody else’s occasion.’

‘Nothing could be easier,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘We’ll do just that tomorrow, and let the others take advantage of ours.’ He pulled her up as he spoke, and they went out hand in hand.

After breakfast, when it was time to choose the plays, Grandmother Jia insisted that Bao-chai should choose first. Bao-chai tried to decline the honour, but in the end had to ask for an episode from Journey to the West. Grandmother Jia was naturally delighted. She next called on Xi-feng, who also knew the old lady’s liking for lively plays, and her still greater liking for comic business and jokes. She chose Liu Er Pawns His Clothes. Sure enough, Grandmother Jia was even more pleased. She then asked Dai-yu to choose. Dai-yu tried to defer to Aunt Xue, Lady Wang and the others.

‘I arranged today specially to have some fun with you young ones,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Let’s please ourselves and leave them out of it. Do you think I went to all the trouble of a feast and plays for their sake? They’re getting food and entertainment for nothing. That’s quite enough of a bargain without letting them choose the plays as well!’

Everyone laughed. Dai-yu then made a choice. Bao-yu, Shi Xiang-yun, Ying-chun, Tan-chun, Xi-chun and Li Wan each chose in turn, and the plays were performed one after another.

When the wine was served, Grandmother Jia again asked Bao-chai to choose. She chose Lu Zhi-shen Runs Riot Drunk on Mount Wutai.

‘Must you always choose things like that?’ said Bao-yu.

‘You’ve listened to plays all these years for nothing. What do you know of the merits of this one? The staging is splendid, and the words are finer still.’

‘I’ve always dreaded all that noise and bustle.’

Bao-chai laughed. ‘If you think that’s all there is to this play, you really don’t understand the theatre.

‘Come here. Let me tell you whether it’s just noise and bustle. It’s a northern Rouged Lips suite, ringing and measured by turns. The beauty of its rhythms goes without saying. But there’s one song in it, Clinging Grass, whose words are wonderfully written. What would you know of that?’

At this glowing account Bao-yu moved nearer and begged, ‘Dear cousin, do recite it for me.’

Bao-chai recited: ‘Vainly I wipe a hero’s tears, And take my leave of the recluse’s home. I thank your mercy for my tonsure beneath the lotus throne. No bond was destined: in an instant we must part. Naked I come and go, with nothing to bind me. Where can I find a straw cape for mist and a hat for rain, to roll my bedding and go alone? Let me beg as chance may lead, in straw sandals, with a broken bowl!’

Bao-yu slapped his knee with delight and traced circles of approval in the air. He praised it over and over, then praised Bao-chai for knowing every book there was.

‘Be quiet and watch the play,’ said Lin Dai-yu. ‘They haven’t even started At the Monastery Gate, and you’re already performing Feigning Madness.’

This made Xiang-yun laugh too. They all settled down to watch.

When the performance ended that evening, Grandmother Jia, who had taken a great fancy to the child playing the young heroine and to one playing a clown, sent for both to be brought in. Seen close to, they seemed more touching still. She asked their ages: the child playing the young heroine was only eleven, the clown-player only nine. Everyone sighed over them. Grandmother Jia ordered extra meat and fruit for the two children, with a further reward of two strings of cash.

‘This child in costume is the very image of someone,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘Surely you can see who it is?’

Bao-chai knew, but merely smiled, unwilling to say. Bao-yu had guessed too, but dared not speak.

‘Why, the very image of Cousin Lin,’ Shi Xiang-yun said, laughing.

Bao-yu glanced at Xiang-yun at once and gave her a warning look. But everyone had heard. They looked closely and began to laugh, agreeing that the likeness was unmistakable.

Presently they dispersed. That evening, as Xiang-yun was changing, she told Kingfisher to open her clothes-bundle, put everything in order and pack it all up.

‘What’s the hurry?’ said Kingfisher. ‘There’ll be time enough to pack on the day you leave.’

‘I’m going first thing tomorrow. What am I staying here for? To watch other people’s faces and take my cue from their looks? Where’s the pleasure in that?’

Hearing this, Bao-yu hurried over and took her hand.

‘Dear cousin, you’ve misunderstood me. Cousin Lin takes things to heart. The others knew perfectly well, but wouldn’t say anything for fear of upsetting her. Then you spoke before you thought. How could she help being angry with you? I was afraid you would offend her; that was why I gave you that look. Being angry with me now is worse than wasting my good intentions. It’s treating me unfairly. If it had been someone else, what would I care if they offended ten people?’

Xiang-yun flung his hand off. ‘Don’t try your pretty speeches on me. Of course I’m not as good as your Cousin Lin. Anyone else can talk about her and make fun of her, but the moment I say anything, I’m in the wrong.

‘Of course I’m not fit to speak of her. She’s a young lady, a mistress, and I’m a servant, a slave-girl. I mustn’t offend her!’

‘I was trying to help you,’ Bao-yu cried in distress, ‘and now I’ve only made things worse. If I had any other thought, may I turn to ashes this instant and be trampled by ten thousand people!’

‘It’s the New Year. Don’t let your tongue run on with such nonsense. Keep those pointless curses, that foolish, crooked talk, for the touchy people who are always taking offence and know how to keep you in order. Don’t make me spit in your face.’

She went straight into Grandmother Jia’s inner room and lay down, still seething.

Crestfallen, Bao-yu could only go in search of Dai-yu. He had barely reached the threshold when she pushed him out and shut the door. Unable to understand this either, he stayed outside her window, calling softly, ‘Dear cousin.’ Dai-yu paid him no attention.

Bao-yu hung his head unhappily and searched for his fault. Aroma already knew what had happened, and that this was no moment to try to reason with him. Bao-yu simply stood there in a daze. Dai-yu, supposing that he had gone back to his room, rose and opened the door, only to find him still standing outside. Now she felt awkward. She could hardly shut it again, so she withdrew and lay down on the bed. Bao-yu followed her in.

‘There must be a reason for everything,’ he said. ‘If you tell me, at least I needn’t feel unjustly treated. What can have made you suddenly so angry when everything was perfectly all right?’

Lin Dai-yu gave a cold laugh. ‘A fine thing to ask me! How should I know the reason? I’m only here for you all to laugh at, to be compared to a player for your amusement.’

‘I didn’t compare you to anyone. I didn’t laugh. Why be angry with me?’

‘So you want to compare me too? You want to laugh? You’re worse without comparing or laughing than the people who did both!’

Bao-yu had no answer to this and said nothing.

‘That much I could forgive,’ Dai-yu went on. ‘But why did you make signs to Yun? What did you mean by that? Was joking with me beneath her dignity? Of course she’s a nobleman’s daughter, and I’m a poor nobody’s girl. If she joked with me and I answered back, wouldn’t she have invited contempt for herself? Wasn’t that what you meant? Very kind of you, too. Only she wouldn’t accept your kindness either, and got angry just the same. So then you used me to win her over, saying I was touchy and always taking offence. And you were afraid she would offend me and I would be angry with her. If I was angry with her, what business was that of yours? If she offended me, what business was that of yours?’

Bao-yu realised that she had overheard his private talk with Xiang-yun. Thinking it over, he had only tried to mediate for their sake, afraid that the two would quarrel and grow estranged. Yet he had not reconciled them at all; instead he had earned reproaches from both. It was just as he had read the other day in The Classic of Nanhua: ‘The skilful labour and the clever worry; those without ability seek nothing. They eat their fill and wander at ease, drifting like an unmoored boat.’ And again: ‘The mountain tree invites its own plundering; the spring invites its own draining.’

The more he thought about it, the less pleasure he found in anything. For the moment there were only these two people, and he could not even keep on good terms with them. What did he hope to manage in the future? At this point he felt there was no need to defend himself or make an answer. He turned and went back to his own room. Seeing him leave without a word, Dai-yu knew that his reflections had disheartened him and that he had gone off in a huff. This only made her angrier.

‘Now that you’ve gone, don’t ever come back,’ she said. ‘And don’t ever speak to me again.’

Bao-yu took no notice. Back in his room he lay on the bed, staring blankly. Aroma knew the whole story, but dared not speak of it yet. She tried to distract him with something else.

‘Today’s plays will lead to several more days of them. Miss Bao is sure to give a party in return.’

Bao-yu gave a cold laugh. ‘And what business is it of anyone’s whether she does or not?’

This was not his usual tone. Aroma tried again, smiling. ‘What a thing to say! Here we are at the New Year, with all the ladies and girls enjoying themselves. What has made you like this?’

‘Whether the ladies and girls enjoy themselves has nothing to do with me either,’ he said with another cold laugh.

Aroma smiled. ‘If they’re friendly and you’re friendly, won’t everyone have a pleasant time together?’

‘What do you mean by “everyone together”? They can have their “everyone together”. As for me, “Naked I come and go, with nothing to bind me.”’

At these words the tears began to fall.

Seeing this, Aroma would say no more. Bao-yu pondered the meaning of that line and could not help bursting into sobs. He swung himself out of bed, went to the writing-table and took up his brush. On the spot he composed this verse:

You realise; I realise;

Heart realises; mind realises.

Only when there is no realisation

May it be called realisation.

Nothing can be called realisation:

That is a ground to stand on.

Having written it, he understood his meaning himself, but feared others might not. He therefore composed a song to the tune Clinging Grass and wrote it after the verse. He read the whole thing through once more, felt free of every encumbrance and, thoroughly pleased with himself, went to bed.

Dai-yu, meanwhile, had seen how resolutely Bao-yu had left her this time. On the pretext of looking for Aroma, she came to see what was happening.

‘He’s asleep already,’ Aroma told her with a smile.

Dai-yu was about to go back when Aroma said, ‘Do stay a moment, miss. Here’s something he’s written. Will you see what it means?’ She quietly brought over the song and verse he had just composed and gave them to Dai-yu.

Dai-yu read them and knew that Bao-yu had written them in a fit of vexation. She hardly knew whether to laugh or sigh.

‘It’s only something he’s written for amusement,’ she told Aroma. ‘It’s nothing to worry about.’

She took the sheet back to her own room and read it with Xiang-yun. Next day she showed it to Bao-chai as well. Bao-chai read the song:

Without an I, there can be no you;

Let one fail to understand another.

Go freely, come freely, with nothing to hinder you.

In all this vastness, why sorrow, why grief, why joy?

Amid this bustle, why speak of closeness, distance, intimacy?

What was all that busy striving for?

Now I turn and think it over: there was no pleasure in it at all!

After reading the song, she read the verse too and laughed.

‘He’s found enlightenment! It’s all my fault. It all started with the song I recited yesterday. Taoist books and Chan conundrums can change a person’s whole nature. If he really takes to talking this nonsense and fixes on these ideas, it will all have come from that one song. I shall be the chief culprit.’

As she spoke, she tore the sheet into little pieces and handed them to the maids. ‘Burn these at once.’

‘You shouldn’t have torn it up,’ said Dai-yu, smiling. ‘Let me question him. Come with me. I guarantee I’ll make him give up these foolish fancies and wild notions.’

All three went to Bao-yu’s room. The moment they entered, Dai-yu said with a smile, ‘Bao-yu, answer me this. What is most precious is called “bao”, a treasure; what is hardest is “yu”, jade. What is precious in you? What is hard in you?’

Bao-yu could not answer at all. The three clapped their hands and laughed. ‘So dull and stupid, yet you want to investigate Chan!’

‘Your verse ends,’ Dai-yu continued, ‘“Nothing can be called realisation: That is a ground to stand on.” That’s all very well, but to my mind it isn’t quite perfect. I’ll add two lines.’ She recited: ‘With no ground to stand on, Only then is all swept clean.’

‘Now that really is complete enlightenment,’ said Bao-chai. ‘Long ago Hui-neng, the Sixth Patriarch of the Southern School, came to Shaozhou when he first went in search of a teacher. Hearing that the Fifth Patriarch, Hong-ren, was at Huangmei, he went to serve as a kitchen monk. The Fifth Patriarch wanted to choose a successor to his teaching, so he ordered each of his disciples to compose a verse. The senior monk, Shen-xiu, said: “The body is the bodhi tree; The heart is like a stand for a bright mirror. Diligently wipe it, time after time; Let no dust remain.”

‘At the time, Hui-neng was pounding rice in the kitchen. When he heard the verse, he said, “Fine it may be, but understanding is still to come.” Then he recited a verse of his own: “Bodhi was never a tree; Nor is the bright mirror a stand. From the first there was not a single thing: Where could dust settle?” The Fifth Patriarch handed down his robe and bowl to him.

‘Today’s verse has the same meaning. But that question you put to him just now has still not been settled. Are you going to let it drop?’

‘You couldn’t answer then,’ said Dai-yu with a smile, ‘so you’ve lost. It would be nothing remarkable if you found an answer now. Only you mustn’t talk about Chan again. You don’t even know what we two know, or do what we two can do, yet you want to investigate Chan!’

Bao-yu had thought himself enlightened. Then a single unexpected question from Dai-yu had left him speechless, and Bao-chai had produced sayings of the masters to illustrate the point. He had never seen either of them display these abilities before. He considered it for a moment. ‘So their understanding was ahead of mine, and even they haven’t reached enlightenment. Why should I go looking for trouble?’

Then he laughed. ‘Who was investigating Chan? It was only a passing joke.’ And the four were as they had been before.

Suddenly someone announced that Her Highness had sent out a lantern riddle for them all to guess. Those who guessed it were each to make one of their own and send it in. The four hurried to Grandmother Jia’s principal room. There they found a young eunuch carrying a flat-topped, four-sided lantern of white gauze, made specially for riddles, with one already written on it. Everyone crowded round to read it and call out guesses.

The young eunuch conveyed a further command. ‘When the young ladies have guessed it, they mustn’t say the answer aloud. Each should write it secretly on a piece of paper. The papers will all be sealed together and sent into the palace for Her Highness to judge.’

Bao-chai and the others went closer and saw a quatrain of seven-character lines, with nothing particularly novel about it. They were obliged to praise it, of course, and said how hard it was, deliberately appearing to ponder, though in fact they had guessed it at first sight. Bao-yu, Dai-yu, Xiang-yun and Tan-chun had all solved it too. Each spent some time secretly writing down an answer.

Jia Huan, Jia Lan and the others were summoned as well. All applied their wits, made their guesses and wrote them down. Each then chose an object, composed a riddle about it, copied it out in careful regular script and hung it on the lantern. The eunuch left. That evening he returned with a message.

‘All the answers to Her Highness’s riddle were correct except those of the Second Young Lady and the Third Young Master. Her Highness has also guessed the young ladies’ riddles, but does not know whether she is right.’

He produced her written answers. Some were right and some wrong, but everyone indiscriminately declared them correct. The eunuch then presented the gifts to those who had guessed rightly: a palace-made poetry tube and a tea whisk for each. Only Ying-chun and Jia Huan received nothing.

Ying-chun regarded it as a small matter of amusement and did not mind. Jia Huan, however, felt mortified, especially when the eunuch added, ‘The Third Young Master’s riddle makes no sense. Her Highness hasn’t guessed it either. She told me to bring it back and ask him what it is.’

Everyone gathered round to see what he had written:

Elder brother has just eight horns;

Second brother has only two.

Elder brother only sits upon the bed;

Second brother likes to squat upon the roof.

They all burst out laughing at it. Jia Huan had to tell the eunuch, ‘One is a pillow, the other an animal-head roof ornament.’

The eunuch committed this to memory, accepted some tea and left.

Seeing Yuan-chun enter into the spirit of the game, Grandmother Jia became merrier still. She ordered a small, exquisitely made lantern screen to be prepared at once and set up in the middle of the room. The girls were each to compose their riddles in secret, write them out and paste them on the screen. Fragrant tea, choice fruits and all sorts of curios were laid ready as prizes for correct answers.

Returning from court, Jia Zheng saw how pleased his mother was. As it was the festive season, he too came that evening to entertain her and join in the fun. Wine and fruit were set out, prizes prepared, and coloured lanterns hung in the principal room. Grandmother Jia was invited to enjoy the lanterns. At the table of honour sat Grandmother Jia, Jia Zheng and Bao-yu. Below them, Lady Wang, Bao-chai, Dai-yu and Xiang-yun shared another table, while Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun had one to themselves. The floor was crowded with women servants and maids. Li Gong-cai and Wang Xi-feng sat at a separate table in the inner room.

Noticing Jia Lan’s absence, Jia Zheng asked, ‘Where is Lan?’

A woman servant hurried into the inner room to ask his mother. Mrs Li rose and answered with a smile, ‘He said that the Master hadn’t sent for him, so he wouldn’t come.’ The woman took this answer back to Jia Zheng.

Everyone laughed. ‘A born oddity, and as stubborn as an ox!’

Jia Zheng at once sent Jia Huan and two women to fetch Jia Lan. Grandmother Jia had him sit beside her and gave him handfuls of fruit to eat. They all talked and laughed together.

Ordinarily Bao-yu would hold forth at length. Today, with Jia Zheng there, he could manage nothing but meek words of assent. Xiang-yun, though a young lady of the sheltered inner rooms, had always been fond of conversation, but with Jia Zheng at the table she too kept her mouth shut.

Dai-yu was by nature reluctant to mix with others and had little wish to talk. Bao-chai, never given to careless speech or hasty action, remained as composed as ever. Thus, although it was a family party intended for pleasure, everyone seemed constrained and ill at ease. Grandmother Jia knew that Jia Zheng’s presence alone was the cause. After three rounds of wine she began sending him off to rest. Jia Zheng understood her intention too: with him gone, the young people could enjoy themselves. He hastened to put on a smile.

‘I heard that you were holding a grand gathering for elegant spring lantern riddles, Mother, so I brought prizes and a feast specially to join you. Must all your affection go to your grandsons and granddaughters? Can’t your son have a little of it?’

‘While you’re here they don’t dare talk or laugh,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘It only makes it dull for me. If you want to guess riddles, I’ll give you one. But you’ll have to pay a forfeit if you can’t guess it.’

‘Of course I shall,’ Jia Zheng said quickly, laughing. ‘And if I do guess it, I shall expect a prize.’

‘Naturally.’ Grandmother Jia recited: ‘A light-bodied monkey stands on a treetop. Name a fruit.’

Jia Zheng already knew it was a lychee, but deliberately made a succession of wrong guesses, paying out a good many forfeits before at last giving the right answer and receiving a present from Grandmother Jia. Then he recited a riddle for her:1

Upright and square in form,

Firm and hard in body;

Though it cannot speak,

When there are words, it always answers.

Name an everyday object.

Having recited it, he whispered the answer to Bao-yu. Bao-yu understood and whispered it in turn to Grandmother Jia. She thought it over, found that it fitted perfectly and said, ‘An inkstone.’

‘Just as one would expect of you, Mother,’ said Jia Zheng, laughing. ‘Right at the first guess!’ He turned round. ‘Bring up the prizes at once.’2

The women standing below answered and carried up large and small trays together. Grandmother Jia examined the contents one by one. They were all new and ingenious things to use or play with during the Lantern Festival, and she was very pleased.

‘Pour your father some wine,’ she ordered. Bao-yu held the pot, and Ying-chun carried over the cup.

‘Look at the screen,’ Grandmother Jia told Jia Zheng. ‘The girls made all those. Try some more, and let me hear your answers.’

Jia Zheng assented, rose and went to the screen. The first riddle read:

I can strike terror through a demon’s heart;

My body is like bundled silk, my breath like thunder.

One crash has only just struck fear in you;

You turn to look, and I am ash already.

‘That’s a firecracker,’ said Jia Zheng.

‘Yes,’ Bao-yu answered.

Jia Zheng read the next:

Heaven’s turning, human effort: their principles have no end;

With effort but no turn of fortune, success is hard to find.

Why all this jostling confusion the whole day long?

Only because the numbers of yin and yang differ.

‘An abacus,’ said Jia Zheng.

‘Yes,’ said Ying-chun, smiling.

Further down he read:

Children below the steps turn up their faces;

No adornment better suits the Clear Brightness season.

Once the wandering thread snaps, all strength is gone;

Do not blame the east wind for the parting.

‘This is a kite,’ said Jia Zheng.

‘Yes,’ said Tan-chun, smiling.

He read on:

The form I wore before came to nothing;

I hear no water-chestnut gatherers’ songs, but Buddhist scriptures.

Do not say this life is sunk in a black sea:

Within my nature shines a great bright light.

‘That must be a great votive lamp before the Buddha,’ said Jia Zheng.

‘Yes, a great votive lamp,’ Xi-chun replied, smiling.

Jia Zheng pondered inwardly. ‘Her Highness’s firecracker goes off with a bang and scatters. Ying-chun’s abacus falls into a tangle of confusion at a touch. Tan-chun’s kite is a thing that drifts and floats about. Xi-chun’s votive lamp is lonelier and more secluded still. This is the joyous Lantern Festival. Why should they all make games out of such ill-omened things?’

The more he thought about it, the gloomier he became. In his mother’s presence he dared not let it show, and forced himself to go on looking. Next came a regulated poem of eight seven-character lines, written by Bao-chai. He read it aloud:

Who comes from court with smoke in both his sleeves?

Beside the qin, beneath the quilt, I have no place.

Dawn’s tally needs no cock-calling timekeeper to announce it;

Through the night’s five watches, no maid need replenish me.

My head burns, morning after morning, evening after evening;

My heart is seared, day after day and year after year.

Time slips away: be sure to treasure it,

And let wind and rain, shade and sunshine change as they will.

When he had finished, Jia Zheng thought, ‘The object itself is not so very bad. But for children so young to write such lines is still more ominous. None of them is destined for lasting happiness and long life.’ This thought made him more distressed than ever, and he looked deeply grieved. Eight or nine tenths of his earlier animation had drained away. He merely bowed his head and brooded.

Seeing him like this, Grandmother Jia wondered whether he might be physically tired. She was also afraid that his presence would prevent the girls from enjoying themselves freely.

‘You needn’t guess any more,’ she told him. ‘Go and rest.

‘We’ll sit a little longer and then break up ourselves.’

At these words Jia Zheng hurriedly assented several times. He made another effort to persuade Grandmother Jia to drink, then withdrew. Back in his own room he could not stop thinking. He tossed and turned, unable to sleep, overcome with sorrow and foreboding. Of him we need say no more for now.

Once Jia Zheng had gone, Grandmother Jia said, ‘Now you can enjoy yourselves freely.’

She had barely finished when Bao-yu was already at the lantern screen, gesticulating and criticising everything. This line in one riddle was poor; the answer to another did not quite fit. He was like a monkey let out of its cage.

‘Wouldn’t it be more civilised to sit as we were before,’ said Bao-chai, ‘and all talk and laugh together?’

Xi-feng hurried out of the inner room and joined in. ‘Your father ought to make you stay within a step of him every day. I forgot just now. Why didn’t I suggest, while he was here, that you should compose a verse riddle too? If I had, you’d be sweating over it this very moment.’

This made Bao-yu frantic. He caught hold of Xi-feng and clung to her like twisted toffee, pestering her and refusing to let go. Grandmother Jia chatted and laughed a little longer with Li Gong-cai and the girls, until she too began to feel tired. Listening, she heard that the water-clock had already marked the fourth watch. She ordered the food cleared away and distributed among the servants, then rose.

‘Let’s go to bed.

‘Tomorrow is still a festival day, and we ought to be up early. We can play again tomorrow evening.’
