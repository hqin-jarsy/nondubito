Chapter 35 Silver Bai tastes the lotus-leaf soup herself; Golden Oriole Huang deftly knots a plum-blossom cord

Chapter end

Now Bao-chai had heard Dai-yu’s cutting remark quite clearly, but her thoughts were with her mother and brother. Without turning round, she walked straight on. Dai-yu remained in the shade of the flowers, looking from afar towards the House of Green Delights. Li Gong-cai, Ying-chun, Tan-chun, Xi-chun and all sorts of other people had been to see Bao-yu and gone away again, group after group, until all had dispersed. Only Xi-feng had not appeared. Dai-yu wondered:

‘Why hasn’t she come to see him? Even if she were tied up with something, surely she would come and make a little show of herself to please Grandmother and Aunt. There must be a reason why she hasn’t come by this time.’

Still puzzling, she looked up again and saw another bright cluster of people approaching the House of Green Delights. Looking closely, she made out Grandmother Jia leaning on Xi-feng’s hand, with Lady Xing and Lady Wang behind, followed by Aunt Zhou and the maids and married servants. They all went into the courtyard.

Dai-yu nodded involuntarily. Thinking how fortunate people were who had parents, she soon had tears running down her face again. A little later she saw Bao-chai, Aunt Xue and their party enter too. Suddenly Nightingale came up behind her.

‘Come and take your medicine, miss. The boiled water’s gone cold again.’

‘What do you want of me?’ said Dai-yu. ‘Always hurrying me! What business is it of yours whether I take it or not?’

Nightingale smiled. ‘Your cough has only just got a little better, and already you won’t take your medicine. It may be the fifth month, with the weather so hot, but you still ought to take care. You’ve been standing in this damp place all morning. It’s time you went back and rested.’

These words made Dai-yu realise that her legs were a little sore. She stood blankly for a while, then slowly made her way back to Naiad’s House, leaning on Nightingale.

Inside the courtyard gate, the ground was patterned with uneven bamboo shadows and patches of moss, some dark, some light. They brought to mind two lines from The Western Chamber:

‘Does anyone pass through this secluded place? Cold, pale dew lies scattered on the green moss.’

She sighed inwardly. ‘Shuang-wen, Shuang-wen, you were indeed ill-fated. Yet even in your misfortune you had your widowed mother and your young brother. Today I, Lin Dai-yu, have neither widowed mother nor young brother. The ancients said that beauties are ill-fated. But I am no beauty. Why should my fate be harder even than Shuang-wen’s?’

She walked on, absorbed in these thoughts. Suddenly the parrot on the verandah saw her and swooped down with a squawk, giving her a start.

‘You wretched thing! You’ve flapped dust all over my head again.’

The parrot flew back to its perch and called, ‘Snowgoose! Quick, lift the curtain! Miss is here!’

Dai-yu stopped and tapped the perch with her hand. ‘Has it had fresh food and water?’

The parrot gave a long sigh, remarkably like Dai-yu’s own customary sighs, and began to recite:

‘Today I bury flowers, and people call me foolish; In years to come, who knows who will bury me? See how, as spring ends, the flowers slowly fall: That is the time when beauty grows old and dies. One day spring ends and beauty has grown old; Flowers fall, people die, neither knowing of the other!’

Dai-yu and Nightingale both laughed.

‘Those are all lines you’re always reciting, miss,’ said Nightingale. ‘How clever of it to remember them!’

Dai-yu had the perch taken down and hung on a hook outside the round window. Then she went indoors and sat just inside it. After she had taken her medicine, she watched the bamboo shadows outside fall across the gauze. The room was filled with a moist green shade, and the tables and sleeping mats seemed to breathe coolness. With nothing to relieve her low spirits, she amused herself by teasing the parrot through the window gauze, and taught it to recite some of her favourite poems and lyrics. Let us leave her there for the moment.

When Bao-chai had reached home earlier, she had found her mother dressing her hair.

‘What have you come running here for so early?’ Aunt Xue asked as soon as she saw her.

‘I wanted to see how you were, Mother. After I left yesterday, did he come back and make more trouble?’

She sat down beside her mother as she spoke, and could not help beginning to cry. At the sight of her tears, Aunt Xue could no longer hold back her own. She wept for a while, then tried to comfort her.

‘My child, don’t grieve. Wait till I deal with him. If anything happened to you, who should I have left to depend on?’

Xue Pan heard from outside and hurried in. Facing Bao-chai, he made one bow after another, now to one side, now to the other.

‘Dear sister, forgive me this once! I’d been drinking yesterday, and I came home late. I must have run into spirits on the road. I hadn’t come to my senses when I got home. Heaven knows what nonsense I talked; I don’t even know myself. No wonder you’re angry.’

Bao-chai had been crying with her face covered. Now she could not help finding this funny. She raised her head and spat on the ground.

‘There’s no need for all this play-acting.

‘I know how tired you are of Mother and me. You’re trying every way you can to drive us away, so you can have peace of mind.’

‘Where do you get that from?’ Xue Pan asked hastily, smiling. ‘You leave me no ground to stand on! You’ve never been one to take things amiss and twist words like this.’

Aunt Xue broke in. ‘You can hear your sister twisting words well enough. What about those things you said last night? Were they right and proper? You really must have lost your senses!’

‘Don’t be angry, Mother. And don’t be upset, sister. From now on I won’t go drinking and roaming about with them again. How’s that?’

‘Now you’re coming to your senses!’ Bao-chai said with a smile.

‘If you could stick to that,’ said Aunt Xue, ‘dragons would lay eggs.’

‘If I ever go out with them again, sister, and you hear of it, spit on me! Call me a beast, say I’m not human. How’s that?

‘Why should you both have to worry every day over me alone? It might be forgivable to make Mother angry, but if I keep making my sister worry about me too, I’m less than human. Now Father’s gone, instead of being more dutiful to Mother and more loving to my sister, I make one angry and the other unhappy. I really am worse than a beast.’

As he spoke, tears began rolling from his eyes in spite of himself. Aunt Xue had stopped crying, but his words stirred her grief again.

Bao-chai forced a smile. ‘Haven’t you made enough trouble? Now you’ve set Mother crying again.’

Xue Pan hastily checked his tears and smiled. ‘How have I made Mother cry? Enough, enough, enough! Let’s drop it and say no more.

‘Call Caltrop to pour some tea for my sister.’

‘I don’t want tea,’ said Bao-chai. ‘Once Mother’s washed her hands, we’ll go over.’

‘Let me see your neck circlet,’ said Xue Pan. ‘Perhaps it’s time to have it brightened up.’

‘It’s shining gold already. What would be the use?’

‘You ought to have some more clothes now too. Tell me what colours and patterns you’d like.’

‘I haven’t even worn all the clothes I have. What do I want more for?’

Presently Aunt Xue changed her clothes and took Bao-chai by the hand and went in. Only then did Xue Pan go out.

Aunt Xue and Bao-chai came into the garden to visit Bao-yu. At the House of Green Delights, the many maids and older women standing in the porches and covered walkways showed them that Grandmother Jia and the others were there. Mother and daughter went inside and exchanged greetings with everyone. Bao-yu was lying on the couch. When Aunt Xue asked if he was better, he tried hastily to raise himself.

‘Better,’ he answered. ‘But I can’t bear putting you and my cousin to so much trouble, Aunt.’

Aunt Xue helped him lie down again. ‘Whatever you’d like, just tell me.’

‘When I think of anything, I’ll be sure to ask you for it,’ he said with a smile.

Lady Wang asked too, ‘What would you like to eat? We can send it over later.’

‘There’s nothing in particular,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘But that soup they made once, with the little lotus leaves and little seed-pods, was rather good.’

Xi-feng laughed beside him. ‘Listen to him! Nothing so very grand about his tastes, only such a lot of bother!

‘Of all things, he has to want that.’

Grandmother Jia at once called repeatedly for someone to have it made.

‘Don’t be in such a hurry, Grannie dear,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Let me think who put the moulds away.’

She turned and told an old woman to ask the kitchen steward for them. After some time the woman returned.

‘The kitchen steward says all four sets of soup moulds were handed back.’

Xi-feng considered. ‘I remember who they went to. Most likely they’re in the tea room.’

She sent to ask the tea-room steward, but they had not received them either. In the end they were brought by the person in charge of the gold and silver utensils.

Aunt Xue took them first to have a look. It was a little box holding four sets of silver moulds, each over a chi long and a cun square, with hollows chiselled in them the size of beans. There were chrysanthemums, plum blossoms, lotus seed-pods, water-chestnuts, thirty or forty shapes altogether, all most delicately made. She smiled at Grandmother Jia and Lady Wang.

‘Your household really has thought of everything. Even a bowl of soup has all these patterns! If you hadn’t told me, I could have looked at this and never known what it was for.’

Xi-feng answered with a laugh before anyone else could speak. ‘You wouldn’t know about these, Aunt. They thought them up last year when they were preparing the food. They press some sort of dough into the moulds and borrow a little fragrance from fresh lotus leaves. It all depends on a good broth. There’s really nothing in it. Who would eat it every day? They made it once to show what it was like. Whatever made him think of it today?’

She took the box and handed it to a woman, telling her to have the kitchen take several chickens at once, add the other ingredients and make ten or so bowls.

‘What do we want so much for?’ asked Lady Wang.

‘There’s a reason,’ Xi-feng replied, smiling. ‘We don’t often make this at home. Now Cousin Bao has thought of it, it wouldn’t seem quite right to make it just for him, while Grandmother, Aunt and you had none. Better make enough for everyone while we’re about it, and let me look good on the strength of it too.’

‘You monkey!’ Grandmother Jia laughed. ‘How clever you are! Winning people over with the household’s money.’

Everyone laughed, and Xi-feng hastened to join in.

‘Never mind that. I can still afford this little treat for you.’

She turned to the woman. ‘Tell the kitchen to put in whatever it needs and make it properly. They can collect the money from my account.’

The woman assented and went away.

Bao-chai smiled from her place beside them. ‘In the years I’ve been here, I’ve watched carefully. However clever Feng is, she’ll never be cleverer than Grandmother.’

‘I’m old now,’ Grandmother Jia answered. ‘What cleverness have I left? When I was little Feng’s age, I could do even better than she can. She may not be up to what we were, but she’s not bad, and she’s far ahead of your aunt. Poor thing, your aunt hardly speaks, like a block of wood. That never showed her to advantage before her parents-in-law. Feng has such a pleasing tongue. How can you blame people for loving her?’

‘Does that mean you don’t love people who don’t talk much?’ Bao-yu asked with a smile.

‘Quiet people have their own ways of being lovable. And those with pleasing tongues have faults of their own that can make them less likeable than the quiet ones.’

‘That’s what I thought,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘My elder sister-in-law doesn’t talk much, but you treat her just the same as Cousin Feng. If only the good talkers were lovable, Cousin Feng and Cousin Lin would be the only ones among all these sisters and cousins worth loving.’

‘Speaking of the girls,’ said Grandmother Jia, ‘I’m not saying this to flatter Mrs Xue to her face, but it’s the plain truth: beginning with the four girls of our own family, not one of them can compare with Bao-chai.’

‘Now you’re being partial, Your Old Ladyship,’ Aunt Xue said quickly, smiling.

Lady Wang hastened to add, with a smile, ‘She often tells me in private how good Bao-chai is. That’s no empty compliment.’

Bao-yu had been leading Grandmother Jia on in the hope of hearing Dai-yu praised. To hear her praise Bao-chai instead was quite unexpected. He looked at Bao-chai and smiled, but she had already turned away to talk to Aroma.

Someone came to announce the meal. Grandmother Jia rose, told Bao-yu to take good care of himself, and gave the maids another round of instructions. Then, supported by Xi-feng and inviting Aunt Xue to go before her, she went out with the others. She asked whether the soup was ready yet, then said to Aunt Xue and the rest:

‘Whatever you’d like to eat, just tell me. I know how to make Feng get it for us.’

Aunt Xue smiled. ‘You do enjoy teasing her, Your Old Ladyship. She’s always finding things to offer you, and in the end you can eat so little of them.’

‘Don’t say that, Aunt,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Our dear old Grannie only objects to human flesh because it’s sour. If she didn’t, she’d have eaten me up long ago.’

Before she had finished, Grandmother Jia and everyone else burst out laughing. Even Bao-yu, in his room, could not hold back his laughter.

‘That tongue of Mrs Lian’s really is terrifying!’ Aroma said with a smile.

Bao-yu smiled and reached for her hand. ‘You’ve been standing all this time. Aren’t you tired?’

As he spoke he drew her down to sit beside him.

‘You’ve forgotten again,’ Aroma said, smiling. ‘While Miss Bao’s still in the courtyard, ask her if Oriole can come and knot a few cords for you.’

‘Good thing you reminded me!’ Bao-yu said with a smile. He lifted his head and called through the window:

‘Cousin Bao! Could Oriole come after dinner and make me a few knotted cords? Would she have time?’

Bao-chai turned back. ‘Of course she would. I’ll send her over in a little while.’

Grandmother Jia and the others had not quite heard. They stopped to ask Bao-chai, and only understood when she explained.

‘Good child,’ said Grandmother Jia, ‘do send her to make a few for your cousin. If that leaves you with no one to wait on you, I’ve plenty of idle maids. Take whichever you like.’

Aunt Xue, Bao-chai and the others laughed. ‘We’ll just send her to do it. What is there for her to attend to? She does nothing all day but idle and play.’

Talking as they went, they started forward again and came upon Shi Xiang-yun, Patience, Caltrop and the others picking balsam flowers beside the rocks. Seeing them approach, the girls came to meet them. Presently they were outside the garden. Afraid Grandmother Jia might be tired, Lady Wang asked her to sit in the main apartments. Grandmother Jia’s legs were indeed sore, and she nodded her agreement. Lady Wang sent a maid ahead at once to have the seats made ready.

Aunt Zhao was pleading illness, so only Aunt Zhou was there with the married servants and maids, busily lifting curtains, setting backrests upright and spreading cushions. Grandmother Jia came in on Xi-feng’s arm and sat down with Aunt Xue, each in her proper place as hostess and guest.

Bao-chai and Shi Xiang-yun sat below them. Lady Wang herself brought tea to Grandmother Jia, and Li Gong-cai served Aunt Xue.

‘Let the younger daughters-in-law wait on us,’ Grandmother Jia told Lady Wang. ‘Sit there yourself, so we can talk.’

Only then did Lady Wang sit down on a small stool. She told Xi-feng, ‘Have Her Old Ladyship’s dinner served here, and bring some extra dishes.’

Xi-feng assented and went out to send word to Grandmother Jia’s apartments. The women there passed the message outside, and the maids all hurried over. Lady Wang then sent for the girls. After a good deal of fetching, only Tan-chun and Xi-chun came. Ying-chun was feeling unwell and would not eat. As for Dai-yu, it went without saying: ordinarily she could manage only five meals out of ten, and no one paid much heed any more.

Presently the food arrived and the tables were set in place. Xi-feng stood holding a bundle of ivory chopsticks wrapped in a towel.

‘Grandmother and Aunt needn’t stand on ceremony,’ she said, smiling. ‘Just leave it all to me.’

‘This is how we do things,’ Grandmother Jia told Aunt Xue with a smile.

Aunt Xue smiled her assent. Xi-feng laid out four pairs: two at the head for Grandmother Jia and Aunt Xue, and one at either side for Bao-chai and Shi Xiang-yun. Lady Wang, Li Gong-cai and the others stood watching the dishes being set out. Xi-feng first hurried to ask for clean dishes so that she could select food for Bao-yu.

Presently the lotus-leaf soup arrived. Grandmother Jia inspected it. Lady Wang turned, saw Silver nearby and told her to take some to Bao-yu.

‘She can’t carry it all by herself,’ said Xi-feng.

Just then Oriole and Xi-er came in. Knowing they had finished their meal, Bao-chai told Oriole, ‘Cousin Bao has just asked you to make some knotted cords. You two can go together.’

Oriole assented and went out with Silver.

‘It’s such a long way, and so hot,’ said Oriole. ‘How are we going to carry it?’

‘Don’t worry. I have a way,’ Silver said with a smile.

She called an older serving-woman, had the soup, food and other things put into a food-box, and told her to carry it behind them. The two girls went empty-handed. Only when they were inside the gate of the House of Green Delights did Silver take it from her and go with Oriole into Bao-yu’s room. Aroma, Musk and Ripple were laughing and joking with him. At the sight of the two newcomers, they all hurried to get up.

‘How did you two happen to come at just the same time?’ they asked with smiles, taking the things from them as they spoke.

Silver sat on a stool, but Oriole did not venture to sit. Aroma quickly brought her a footstool, and still she would not sit down.

Bao-yu had been delighted to see Oriole. But suddenly seeing Silver too, he thought of her elder sister Golden and felt both grief and shame. He forgot Oriole for the moment and began speaking to Silver. Aroma, afraid Oriole would be embarrassed at being neglected, and seeing that she would not sit down, took her out to the other room for tea and a talk.

Musk and the others brought bowls and chopsticks and prepared to serve the meal. But Bao-yu would not eat.

‘Is your mother well?’ he asked Silver.

Her face was full of anger. She would not look him in the eye, and only after a long time did she say one word: ‘Yes.’

Bao-yu felt rebuffed. After another long pause, he could only put on a smile and ask, ‘Who sent you to bring me this?’

‘The mistresses, of course!’

Seeing her still so mournful and sullen, Bao-yu knew it was because of Golden. He wanted to humble himself and coax her round, but with so many people there he could not bring himself to do it. By every pretext he could devise, he sent them all out, then again smiled and asked her all sorts of questions. At first Silver remained displeased, but however bitterly she spoke, he showed no temper at all and remained gentle and kind. She began to feel embarrassed herself, and a little warmth came into her face.

‘Dear sister,’ Bao-yu begged with a smile, ‘bring me the soup and let me taste it.’

‘I’ve never fed anyone. Wait till they come back.’

‘I’m not asking you to feed me.

‘I can’t walk, that’s all. If you hand it to me and I drink it, you can go back sooner, report that you’ve done your errand, and have your dinner. If I keep wasting time, won’t you be starved? If you can’t be bothered to move, I’ll have to bear the pain and get it myself.’

He began struggling to get off the bed, unable to keep from crying out. At this Silver could no longer keep her seat.

‘Lie down! What sins did you commit in some former life, to have them catch up with you here and now? And you expect me to think anything of you!’

As she spoke, she suddenly gave a little snort of laughter and brought the soup.

‘Dear sister,’ Bao-yu said, smiling, ‘if you’re angry, be as angry as you like here. But do look more pleasant when you see Grandmother and Mother. If you still look like this, you’ll be scolded again.’

‘Drink it, drink it!’ said Silver. ‘Don’t try your sweet talk on me. I don’t believe a word of it.’

She urged him to take two mouthfuls. Then Bao-yu deliberately said, ‘It isn’t nice. I don’t want any more.’

‘Amitabha! If this isn’t nice, what is?’

‘It has no taste at all. If you don’t believe me, try it yourself.’

In sheer defiance, Silver did taste it.

‘Now it’s delicious,’ Bao-yu said with a smile.

Only then did Silver understand that he had tricked her into taking a mouthful.

‘You said it wasn’t nice. Even if you say it is now, I shan’t let you have any.’

Bao-yu went on smiling and pleading for it, but she would not give it to him. She called the others to serve his meal.

The maids had only just come in when someone announced, ‘Two women from Second Master Fu’s household have come to pay their respects. They would like to see Master Bao.’

Bao-yu knew they were servants of the assistant prefect Fu Shi. Fu Shi was a protégé of Jia Zheng’s, and for years had owed his success to the Jia family’s name and influence. Jia Zheng thought highly of him, and treated him differently from his other protégés. His household often sent people over.

Now Bao-yu ordinarily detested stupid men and foolish women. Why should he ask to see these two old women today? There was a reason. He had heard that Fu Shi had a younger sister named Fu Qiu-fang, a jewel of a young lady, whom people often praised for both talent and beauty. He had never seen her himself, but his distant thoughts and admiration were full of sincere respect. Afraid that refusing to admit the servants would slight Fu Qiu-fang, he hastened to have them shown in.

Fu Shi had come up in the world only recently. Because Qiu-fang had some beauty and exceptional intelligence, he had set his heart on using his sister to make a marriage alliance with a great and noble family. He would not readily promise her to anyone, and so had delayed until now.

Fu Qiu-fang was now twenty-three and still unbetrothed. But the great and noble families thought Fu Shi a shabby scholar of little family standing, and would not seek the match. He had his own purposes in cultivating the Jia family too.

The two women he had sent today happened to be particularly ignorant. Told that Bao-yu would see them, they came in, paid their respects and had barely exchanged a few words. Silver stopped teasing Bao-yu as soon as she saw strangers arrive. Holding the soup in her hands, she listened intently. Bao-yu was equally intent on talking to the women. As he ate, he reached for the soup. Both were looking at the visitors, and he put out his hand too sharply and upset the bowl, spilling the hot soup over his hand.

Silver was not scalded, but she jumped and then hastily laughed. ‘Whatever are you doing!’

The maids hurried forward in alarm to take the bowl. Bao-yu did not even notice that his own hand was scalded. He kept asking Silver, ‘Where did it burn you? Does it hurt?’

Silver and all the others laughed.

‘You’re the one who’s scalded,’ Silver said, ‘and you keep asking me!’

Only then did Bao-yu notice his own burn. Everyone hurried to clear up. He ate no more, washed his hands, took some tea and spoke a little longer with the two women. Then they took their leave. Skybright and the others saw them as far as the bridge before returning.

Once no one was near, the women talked as they walked.

‘No wonder people say their Bao-yu is all fine looks and muddle inside, good to look at but no use for anything,’ one said with a laugh. ‘He really is a bit of a fool.

‘Scalding his own hand and asking someone else whether it hurts! What is that if not foolishness?’

The other laughed too. ‘The last time I came, I heard plenty of people in his own household complaining. He certainly is foolish. There he was in a downpour, soaked like a bedraggled chicken, and telling other people, “It’s raining. Quick, get under cover!” Isn’t that funny?

‘Often, when no one’s about, he laughs and cries all by himself. If he sees swallows, he talks to them. If he sees fish in the river, he talks to the fish. When he looks at the stars and the moon, he’s either heaving great sighs or muttering away.

‘And he hasn’t an ounce of spirit. He even puts up with those little maids’ tempers. When he’s taking care of things, even a loose end of thread is precious. When he’s wasting them, he doesn’t care if they’re worth a thousand or ten thousand.’

Talking all the way, the women left the garden, said goodbye to the others and went home. Of them we need say no more.

Once they had gone, Aroma brought Oriole back and asked Bao-yu what kind of cords he wanted knotted.

‘I was so busy talking, I forgot you,’ he told Oriole with a smile. ‘There’s nothing else I wanted you for, just to make me a few knotted cords.’

‘For holding what?’ asked Oriole.

‘Never mind what they hold. Make me a few of every kind.’

Oriole clapped her hands and laughed. ‘Goodness! If that’s what you want, I shan’t finish in ten years.’

‘Dear sister, you’ve nothing to do just now,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘Make them all for me.’

‘How could she finish them all at once?’ Aroma said with a smile. ‘Choose a couple you need most for now.’

‘What would those be?’ asked Oriole. ‘Fans, scented pendants and sashes, I suppose.’

‘Sashes will do,’ said Bao-yu.

‘What colour is the sash?’

‘Scarlet.’

‘Scarlet needs a black cord to look well. Or dark blue: that will keep the colour from being too strong.’

‘What goes with pine-pollen green?’

‘Peach-pink.’

Bao-yu smiled. ‘That would be lovely and bright. I’d like something soft and elegant with a touch of brightness too.’

‘Spring green and willow yellow are my favourites.’

‘Very well. Make a peach-pink one, and a spring-green one too.’

‘What pattern?’

‘How many patterns are there?’

‘Incense Stick, Upturned Stool, Elephant’s Eye, Paired Diamonds, Linked Rings, Plum Blossom and Willow Leaf.’

‘What was the pattern you made for the Third Miss the other day?’

‘That was Clustered-Heart Plum Blossom.’

‘That’s the one I like,’ said Bao-yu, and asked Aroma to bring the thread. She had just fetched it when an old woman called outside the window, ‘The young ladies’ dinner is ready.’

‘Go and eat,’ Bao-yu said. ‘Be quick and come back.’

‘How can we go with a guest here?’ Aroma asked with a smile.

Oriole smiled as she sorted the thread. ‘What makes you talk like that? Go and eat, then come back.’

Only then did Aroma and the others go, leaving two little maids to answer calls. Bao-yu watched Oriole knotting the cord and chatted with her.

‘How old are you now?’

‘Sixteen,’ she answered, her hands still working.

‘What is your surname?’

‘Huang.’

Bao-yu smiled. ‘Then your surname and your name go perfectly together. Huang means yellow: you really are a yellow oriole.’

Oriole laughed. ‘My name used to have two Chinese characters: Golden Oriole. Miss thought it was awkward to say, so she just called me Oriole, and now everyone does.’

‘Cousin Bao must be fond of you. When she marries, you’ll be going with her, of course.’

Oriole smiled with closed lips.

‘I’m always saying to Aroma,’ Bao-yu went on, smiling, ‘I wonder what lucky man will have the joy of both of you one day, mistress and maid.’

‘You don’t even know the qualities our miss has that no one else in the world possesses,’ Oriole said with a smile. ‘Her looks are the least of it.’

Her sweet simplicity, her gentle ways and the entrancing sound of her talk and laughter had already quite overcome Bao-yu. How much more so when she brought up Bao-chai!

‘What qualities?’ he asked. ‘Dear sister, tell me all about them.’

‘I’ll tell you, but you mustn’t go and tell her.’

‘Of course not,’ Bao-yu said, smiling.

Just then a voice outside asked, ‘Why is it so quiet here?’

They both turned. It was none other than Bao-chai.

Bao-yu hastily asked her to sit. She did so, and asked Oriole, ‘What are you making?’ As she spoke she looked at the work in her hands. It was only half done.

‘What’s so interesting about that?’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘You’d do better to knot a cover for the jade.’

The suggestion reminded Bao-yu at once. He clapped his hands and smiled.

‘You’re right, cousin! I’d forgotten. But what colour would go well with it?’

‘Mixed colours certainly won’t do. Scarlet would clash, yellow wouldn’t show to advantage, and black would be too dark. Let me think of a way. Bring the gold thread, put it with black pearl-thread, twist them together strand by strand, and knot the cover with that. Then it will look well.’

Bao-yu was delighted and called repeatedly for Aroma to fetch the gold thread. Just then she came in carrying two bowls of food.

‘How strange!’ she told him. ‘Her Ladyship has just sent someone with two dishes for me.’

‘They must have plenty today and sent some for all of you,’ Bao-yu said with a smile.

‘No, they were sent expressly for me. And I’m not to go over and kowtow to thank her. It really is strange.’

‘If they’re for you, eat them,’ Bao-chai said, smiling. ‘What is there to puzzle over?’

Aroma smiled. ‘It’s never happened before. It makes me feel quite embarrassed.’

Bao-chai smiled with closed lips. ‘Embarrassed by this?

‘There’ll be things to embarrass you much more than this before long.’

Aroma heard the implication. She knew Bao-chai was not one to make light, cutting jokes at other people’s expense, and now remembered what Lady Wang had meant the other day. She said no more about it, but showed Bao-yu the dishes.

‘I’ll wash my hands and come back for the thread.’

With that she went straight out. After eating and washing her hands, she came back and took out the gold thread for Oriole to use. By this time Bao-chai had already gone: Xue Pan had sent someone to ask her over.

Bao-yu was watching the knotting when two maids arrived from Lady Xing with two kinds of fruit for him.

‘Can you walk yet?’ they asked. ‘If you can, you’re to come over tomorrow for a little change. Her Ladyship is very concerned about you.’

‘If I can walk, I shall certainly go and pay my respects to her,’ Bao-yu answered at once. ‘The pain is better than it was. Please tell her not to worry.’

He asked the two maids to sit and called Ripple, telling her to take half the fruit they had just brought to Miss Lin. Ripple assented and was about to go when Dai-yu’s voice was heard in the courtyard.

‘Ask her in at once!’ Bao-yu called.
