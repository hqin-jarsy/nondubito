Chapter 1 Zhen Shi-yin encounters the Spiritual Jade in a dream; Jia Yu-cun, in straitened circumstances, cherishes a woman of the inner rooms

Chapter end

This is the opening chapter of the book. The author himself says: ‘Having once lived through a dream, I have concealed the true events, a phrase that in Chinese echoes the name Zhen Shi-yin, and written this Story of the Stone. Hence the words “Zhen Shi-yin encounters the Spiritual Jade in a dream”.’ But what does the book record, and why was it written? In his own words: ‘Now, worn down by the dusty business of the world, with nothing accomplished, I suddenly think of all the women I knew in those days. When I consider them one by one, I find that in conduct and understanding every one of them was my superior. How could I, a full-grown man, truly be less worthy than those women?

‘These are days when shame is plentiful, regret useless, and nothing can be done. At such a time I wish to set down a record of my offences for all the world to see: how, favoured by Heaven above and sheltered by my ancestors’ virtue below, I lived in fine silks and feasted on delicacies; how I betrayed my parents’ loving instruction and my teachers’ and elder brothers’ guidance, until I came to this, half my life spent in failure and want, with nothing achieved. My guilt cannot be excused. But the women of those inner rooms were distinct and living people. My own unworthiness must never be allowed to efface them all.

‘Even now, with rough rafters, a brushwood window, an earthen stove and a cord-strung bed, the breezy mornings and moonlit evenings, the willow by the steps and the flowers in the courtyard have done no harm to my spirits or my pen. Why should I not use fictitious words and homely speech, a phrase that in Chinese echoes the name Jia Yu-cun, to unfold a story that will give people pleasure? Hence “in straitened circumstances, cherishes a woman of the inner rooms”.’ This is the true meaning of the heading to the first chapter. Since the book opens with those words about cherishing the women of the inner rooms, we know that the author’s intention was to record the friends and affections of those days, not to rail at the world or abuse his times. Where he touches on the ways of the world, these things have had to be told; they are not his main purpose. Reader, bear this well in mind.

A poem says:

Why labour so, and hurry through this fleeting life?

The finest feast must end; the guests must part.

A thousand joys and griefs are equally unreal;

Past and present, one absurd and empty dream.

Do not speak only of the tears on crimson sleeves:

There are love’s fools, whose bitterness endures.

Look closely: every word is written in blood.

Ten years of toil were no small thing.

Ladies and gentlemen, where do you suppose this book came from? Its origins may sound absurd, but examine them closely and there is much to interest you. Let me explain them, so that you may read without bewilderment. When Nüwa smelted stones to mend the sky, she made thirty-six thousand five hundred and one rough blocks at Fable Cliff on the Mountain of Great Desolation, each twelve zhang high and twenty-four zhang square. The goddess used only thirty-six thousand five hundred. The one block left unused she abandoned at the foot of Green Ridge Peak on that mountain.

Now, after passing through the furnace, this stone had acquired consciousness. Seeing all the other stones chosen to repair the sky, while it alone lacked the qualities required, it reproached itself and sighed, crying out in shame and sorrow day and night. One day, in the midst of its lamentations, it saw a Buddhist monk and a Taoist approaching in the distance. Their bearing was extraordinary, their appearance quite unlike that of ordinary men. Talking and laughing, they came to the foot of the peak, sat beside the stone, and launched into a spirited conversation. At first they spoke of cloud-wrapped mountains and misty seas, of immortals and mysteries. Then they turned to the splendour, wealth and honours of the dusty world. As the stone listened, worldly desire stirred within it. It too longed to enjoy that wealth and splendour among men. Distressed by its own coarse, clumsy nature, it could do nothing but speak in a human voice to the monk and the Taoist. ‘Masters, your disciple is a clumsy thing, unable even to salute you. I have just heard you speaking of the glory and prosperity of the human world, and I long for them with all my heart. Though my substance is coarse and clumsy, I have a little understanding. From your immortal appearance and bearing, I know you can be no ordinary beings. Surely you possess the ability to mend the heavens and save the world, and the goodness to aid all creatures and help mankind.

‘If you would show me a little mercy and take your disciple into the dusty world, to enjoy a few years among the rich and noble, in the tender company of women, I should treasure your great kindness for ever and never forget it through ten thousand ages.’ Both immortals gave a hearty laugh. ‘Excellent, excellent! There are pleasures in that dusty world, certainly, but you cannot count on them lasting. And the two four-character sayings “Perfection has its flaw” and “Good things meet many obstacles” are never far behind. In an instant, joy carried to its height gives birth to sorrow; people and things are changed. In the end it is all a dream, and every scene returns to emptiness. You would do better not to go.’ But the stone was burning with worldly desire. It would not listen, and begged them over and over again. Knowing it could not be restrained, the two immortals sighed. ‘This too is ordained: stillness carried to its utmost seeks movement, and something arises out of nothing. Very well, we shall take you to enjoy yourself. Only do not regret it when things cease to please you.’ ‘Of course not, of course not,’ said the stone. The monk went on: ‘You may have understanding, but your substance is still so coarse, and there is nothing rare or precious about you. As you are, you would be good for nothing but a stepping-stone.

‘Very well. I shall use the power of the Buddha to help you. When your appointed trials are over, you will return to your original substance, and the matter will be closed. What do you say?’ The stone could not thank him enough. The monk recited a spell, drew a charm, and exercised his magic. In an instant the great stone became a beautiful piece of jade, bright and translucent, reduced to the size of a fan-pendant, small enough to wear or hold. Resting it on his palm, the monk smiled. ‘In appearance you are a treasure now. But you still have no real virtue. I must engrave a few words on you, so that anyone who sees you will know at once that you are something extraordinary.

‘Then I shall take you to a flourishing and enlightened country, to a family of learning, ceremony and high office, to a place bright with flowers and willows, where tenderness and wealth abound, and there you may settle and enjoy your life.’ The stone was beside itself with delight. ‘What special qualities have you bestowed on your disciple, and where are you taking me? Please explain, so that I may understand.’ The monk smiled. ‘Do not ask yet. In time you will understand for yourself.’ He slipped the stone into his sleeve and floated away with the Taoist. No one knew where they went or whose house they sought.

Who knows how many lifetimes and ages passed? Then a Taoist called the Taoist of Emptiness, travelling in search of the Way and immortality, happened to pass beneath Green Ridge Peak, by Fable Cliff on the Mountain of Great Desolation. On a great stone he saw writing, clear and distinct, setting out a story in detail. He read it from the beginning. It was the story of a stone unfit to mend the sky, transformed and brought into the world by the Boundless Master and the Ethereal Adept, there to experience parting and reunion, sorrow and joy, and the warmth and coldness of human dealings. At the end was a verse:

Unfit to mend the blue expanse of heaven,

I entered the dusty world and spent those years in vain.

Here is what came before and after this life of mine:

Who will copy it out and make a strange tale of it?

After the poem came the place where the stone had fallen, the household into which it had been born, and an account of all that it had experienced. The small affairs of family and inner rooms, with poems written in idle moments, were recorded quite fully and might offer amusement or relieve boredom. But dynasty and dates, countries and geography had all been omitted and could not be traced. The Taoist of Emptiness said to the stone: ‘Brother Stone, you say your story has some interest, and that is why you have written it here, hoping it will be made known as a tale. But as I see it, first, there is no dynasty or date to be verified; and second, there are no wise and loyal ministers whose good government puts the court in order and reforms the people’s manners. All you have are a few unusual women, some loving, some foolishly devoted, some with a little talent or goodness, but none with the virtue and accomplishments of Lady Ban or Lady Cai. Even if I copy it out, I fear people will not care to read it.’

The stone laughed. ‘My master, why be so foolish? If it is a matter of supplying a dynasty, how hard would it be for you to borrow some dates from the Han or Tang and insert them? But the unofficial histories have always followed the same old track. By avoiding that convention, I can offer something fresh and different. All that matters is that the events and feelings ring true. Why be so particular about dynasties and dates? Besides, very few ordinary people enjoy books about government. Far more like something entertaining to read at leisure. The unofficial histories are full of slanders against rulers and ministers, or insults to other men’s wives and daughters, and accounts of villainy and debauchery without end. Then there are those writings on love and lust, so filthy that they poison the very pen and paper and corrupt the young. They too are beyond counting. As for stories of beautiful women and gifted scholars, a thousand books are cast in the same mould. Inevitably they stray into licence, filling their pages with Pan Ans and Zi-jians, Xi Shis and Wen-juns. The author only wants a chance to display his own couple of amorous poems and rhapsodies, so he invents a man and woman, gives them names, and introduces some villain to make trouble between them, like the clown in a play.

‘And the maids cannot open their mouths without spouting classical particles, literary learning or moral principles. Examine the stories one by one and they are full of contradictions and wildly improbable talk. Better these women I have seen and heard myself during half a lifetime. I dare not claim they surpass everyone in the books of former ages, but the events of their lives may at least dispel some sorrow and boredom. There are also a few poor verses and familiar sayings, enough to make you choke with laughter over your rice or enliven a cup of wine. As for their partings and reunions, joys and griefs, their rise and fall and changes of fortune, I have followed each step and trace. I have not dared invent the slightest detail merely to entertain readers and so falsify the account. Nowadays the poor spend their days struggling for food and clothing; the rich never feel they have enough. Even when they have a little leisure, there are lusts to pursue, beauties to dote on, wealth to covet and troubles to seek. Where would they find time for books about government? I do not ask people to hail my story as a marvel, nor insist that they take pleasure in reading it. I only hope that, when they might otherwise lie about after gorging themselves or indulge in drunken lust, or when they wish to withdraw from the world and escape their cares, they will turn to it for amusement. Would that not save them some of their life and strength?

‘Compared with chasing illusions and pursuing what is false, it would spare them the harm of quarrelsome tongues and the weary running about of their legs. It might also give them something new to look at, unlike those old manuscripts stuffed with absurd coincidences, sudden partings and meetings, gifted men and virtuous women, Zi-jians and Wen-juns, Hong-niangs and Xiao-yus, all the same familiar routine. What do you think, master?’ The Taoist of Emptiness considered this for a while, then went through The Story of the Stone again. He saw that, though it contained words exposing treachery, reproving flatterers and condemning wickedness, its purpose was not to attack the times or rail at the world. Indeed, wherever the proper human relationships were concerned, it praised the achievements and virtues of benevolent rulers and good ministers, loving fathers and dutiful sons, with unending devotion. In this it was quite unlike other books. Though its main subject was love, it only recorded what had happened, and could not be compared with inventions full of lascivious invitations, secret assignations and clandestine pledges. Since it had nothing to do with the affairs of the day, he copied it from beginning to end and brought it back to circulate as a tale. Thereafter, through emptiness he perceived form; from form he came to love; by transmitting love he entered form; and from form he awakened to emptiness. So the Taoist of Emptiness changed his name to the Monk of Love, and renamed The Story of the Stone The Monk of Love’s Record.

Wu Yu-feng called it Dream of the Red Chamber. Kong Mei-xi of Eastern Lu called it The Mirror of Love and Desire. Later, Cao Xueqin spent ten years reading it in the Studio of Mourning the Red, adding and deleting five times, making a table of contents and dividing it into chapters. He entitled it The Twelve Beauties of Jinling.

He also prefaced it with this quatrain:

A whole book of absurdities; A handful of bitter tears!

They all call the author a fool. Who understands the taste of it?

When Red Inkstone copied, reread and commented on it again in the jiaxu year, the title remained The Story of the Stone. Its origin is now clear. Let us see what story was written on the stone.

According to the writing on the stone, after the earth sank in the south-east there was, in that corner of the world, a place called Gusu, with a city known as Changmen, one of the foremost places in the dusty world for wealth, elegance and pleasure. Outside Changmen lay Ten-li Street, and in that street, Renqing Lane. In the lane stood an old temple, so cramped that everyone called it Gourd Temple. Beside it lived a retired local official, surnamed Zhen, whose personal name was Fei and courtesy name Shi-yin. His lawful wife, née Feng, was gentle and virtuous, with a thorough understanding of propriety. Though not particularly rich or exalted, the family was counted among the distinguished households of the neighbourhood. Zhen Shi-yin had a tranquil disposition and cared nothing for rank or fame. He spent his days enjoying flowers, tending bamboo, drinking wine and reciting poems, leading a life worthy of an immortal. There was only one thing lacking: now fifty he had no son, only a daughter whose infant name was Ying-lian. She was just three years old.

One long, hot summer day, Shi-yin was sitting idly in his study. At last his hands grew tired; he laid aside his book and leaned on the desk to rest. Without noticing, he drifted into sleep. In his dream he came to a place he could not identify. Suddenly he saw a monk and a Taoist approaching, talking as they walked.

He heard the Taoist ask, ‘Where are you going with that stupid thing?’ The monk laughed. ‘Never fear. There is a case of love that is due to be settled now. Those bound up in this affair of love have not yet been born into the world.

‘I shall take this opportunity to slip the stupid thing in among them and let it have some experience.’ ‘So another lot of love’s debtors are about to bring about another round of trials and pass through the world?’ said the Taoist. ‘But in what region, and where?’

The monk laughed. ‘It is an amusing business, quite unheard of in all the ages. On the bank of the Spirit River in the west, beside the Stone of Three Lives, there grew a Crimson Pearl plant. The Divine Luminescent Attendant from the Palace of Vermilion Flaw used to water it daily with sweet dew, and so the plant lived on for many years. Nourished by the essence of heaven and earth, as well as rain and dew, it finally shed its plant’s substance and took human shape, attaining the form of a girl. All day she wandered beyond the Heaven of Parting Sorrow, eating Honey-green Fruit when hungry and drinking water from the Sea of Grief when thirsty. But she had not repaid the kindness of that watering, and a lingering feeling knotted itself within her and would not end. Now, recently, the Divine Luminescent Attendant happened to be seized by worldly longing. Wishing to take advantage of this enlightened and peaceful reign to descend to earth and experience the bonds of illusion, he has registered his name before the Fairy Disenchantment. Disenchantment also raised the matter of the unpaid debt for the watering: this would be a chance to settle it. The fairy Crimson Pearl said, “His gift was sweet dew. I have no such water to give him in return.

‘“Since he is going down to become a human being, I shall do the same. If I repay him with all the tears I shed in my lifetime, that should be enough to discharge the debt.” This one affair has drawn any number of love’s debtors into it, to go along with them and settle the case.’

‘That certainly is unusual,’ said the Taoist. ‘I have never heard of a debt repaid in tears. This story should be still more intricate and delicate than the love affairs of the past.’ ‘Of the lovers of the past,’ said the monk, ‘only the outlines of their lives, and their poems and other writings, have been handed down. Not so much as a meal or a drink in their homes and inner rooms has ever been recorded. Besides, most love stories are only about seductions, secret trysts and elopements. They have never revealed even a little of the true feelings of young men and women. Among this company entering the world, the fools for love and slaves to lust, the wise, the foolish and the worthless will all be different from those described before.’

‘Why should we not go down as well and deliver a few of them?’ said the Taoist. ‘Would that not be a meritorious undertaking?’ ‘Exactly what I had in mind,’ the monk replied. ‘Come with me to the Fairy Disenchantment’s palace first, and we shall hand over this stupid thing properly. Once this whole company of love’s doomed spirits has descended, we shall go too. Half of them have already entered the dusty world, but they are not all there yet.’ ‘In that case,’ said the Taoist, ‘I shall come with you.’

Zhen Shi-yin had heard all this clearly, but had no idea what the ‘stupid thing’ might be. Unable to restrain himself, he went forward, bowed and greeted them with a smile. ‘My respects, immortal masters.’ The monk and the Taoist hastily returned his salutation. Shi-yin went on: ‘What I have just heard you say about causes and consequences is something rarely heard in the human world. But your disciple is dull and ignorant, and cannot fully comprehend it. If you would enlighten my foolishness and let me hear the particulars, I would listen with the utmost attention. Even a little understanding might spare me the suffering of being lost in the world.’

The two immortals smiled. ‘This is a mystery that cannot be revealed beforehand. When the time comes, do not forget the two of us, and you will be able to leap out of the fiery pit.’ Shi-yin could not press them further. Instead he smiled and said, ‘The mystery cannot be revealed beforehand, but you spoke just now of a “stupid thing”. What is it? Might I perhaps see it?’ ‘As for that,’ said the monk, ‘you are indeed destined to meet it once.’ He took it out and handed it to Shi-yin. Shi-yin saw a beautiful piece of shining jade, clearly engraved with four characters: ‘Spiritual Jade’. On the back were several lines of smaller writing. He was about to examine them when the monk announced that they had reached the realm of illusion, snatched the jade from his hand, and went with the Taoist through a great stone archway. Four large characters above it read ‘Land of Illusion’. On either side was a line of a couplet:

When the false is taken as true, the true is also false;

Where nothing passes for something, something returns to nothing.

Shi-yin meant to follow them. He had just raised his foot when a thunderclap sounded, as if the mountains were collapsing and the earth giving way.

Shi-yin cried out. Opening his eyes, he saw the fierce sun blazing and the plantain leaves swaying gently. Half the dream had already slipped his mind. Then he saw the nurse coming towards him with Ying-lian in her arms. His daughter was growing prettier every day, as pretty as a painted jade carving, clever and endearing. He held out his hands for her, took her in his arms and played with her for a while, then carried her to the street to watch the bustle of a passing festival procession. He was about to go back in when a monk and a Taoist came along. The monk had a scabby head and bare feet; the Taoist was lame and shaggy-haired. They gestured, talked and laughed like a pair of madmen. When they reached the gate and saw Shi-yin holding Ying-lian, the monk burst into loud weeping. ‘Good sir,’ he said, ‘why are you holding that thing in your arms, born to live but not to prosper, and to bring trouble on her parents?’

Shi-yin took this for mad talk and paid him no attention. But the monk went on: ‘Give her to me! Give her to me!’ Losing patience, Shi-yin turned to carry his daughter inside. The monk pointed at him, laughed aloud and recited four lines:

You pamper her and spoil her: I laugh at your folly;

The water-chestnut flower faces the falling snow in vain.

Beware, when the Lantern Festival is over:

Then comes the hour of smoke dispersed and fire gone out.

Shi-yin heard the words distinctly. Uneasy, he wanted to ask who they were. But the Taoist was already saying, ‘We need not travel together. Let us part here, each to his own business. After three ages I shall wait for you at Mount Beimang. Once we meet, we can go together to the Land of Illusion and strike our names from the register.’ ‘Excellent, excellent!’ said the monk.

With that they went off, leaving not a trace. Shi-yin thought to himself: ‘Those two must have some history. I ought to have asked them. It is too late to regret it now.’ While he stood lost in thought, a poor scholar who lodged next door in Gourd Temple came out. His surname was Jia, his personal name Hua, his courtesy name Shi-fei, and his literary name Yu-cun. Jia Yu-cun came from Huzhou, from a family of scholars and officials. But he had been born into an age of decline: the resources left by his parents and forebears were exhausted, and the family had dwindled until he alone remained. There was nothing for him in his native place, so he had set out for the capital to win a degree and office and restore the family fortunes. He had reached this place the year before last and become stranded here. Lodging temporarily in the temple, he earned his daily living by selling his calligraphy and writing essays. Shi-yin therefore often spent time with him. Seeing Shi-yin now, Yu-cun hastened to bow and asked with a smile, ‘Are you standing at your gate, sir, because there is some news in the streets?’

‘No,’ Shi-yin said, smiling. ‘My little daughter was crying, so I brought her out to play. I am dreadfully bored; you have come at just the right moment. Come into my little study and talk. It will help us both pass this long day.’ He had someone take his daughter inside, and led Yu-cun by the hand to the study. A boy brought tea. They had exchanged only a few remarks when a servant hurried in to announce, ‘Mr Yan has come to call.’

Shi-yin rose hastily, apologising. ‘Forgive me for bringing you in only to leave you. Please sit a little while; I shall be back directly.’ Yu-cun also rose and courteously urged him to go. ‘Please attend to your guest, sir. I am always coming here; what does it matter if I wait a little?’ As he spoke, Shi-yin had already gone to the front hall.

Yu-cun passed the time leafing through books. Suddenly he heard a woman cough outside the window. He rose and looked out. A maid was gathering flowers. She had a pleasing bearing and clear, well-shaped features. Though no great beauty, she was decidedly attractive. Without knowing it, Yu-cun stood staring. The Zhen family’s maid had finished picking flowers and was about to leave when she looked up and saw someone at the window. His cap was shabby and his clothes worn; he was plainly poor. But he had a solid waist and broad back, a wide face and a square mouth, with sword-like eyebrows, bright eyes, a straight nose and high cheekbones. The maid quickly turned away, thinking: ‘What a fine, sturdy figure, and yet so ragged! He must be that Jia Yu-cun my master is always mentioning. He has often wanted to help him, but has never found a good opportunity. We have no friends or relations as poor as this. It must be him. No wonder my master says he will not be down on his luck for long.’ Thinking this, she could not help looking back twice.

Yu-cun saw her turn her head and assumed that she had taken a fancy to him. He was overjoyed. Here, he thought, was a remarkable woman with a discerning eye, someone who understood him in his obscurity. Presently the boy came in. Learning that the visitor in the front hall was staying for a meal, Yu-cun felt he ought not to linger, and let himself out by the side passage. When Shi-yin’s guest had gone, he found that Yu-cun had left, and did not send for him again.

Before long it was the Mid-Autumn Festival. After the family feast, Shi-yin had another table laid in the study and walked over to the temple in the moonlight to invite Yu-cun. Ever since the Zhen family’s maid had looked back at him twice, Yu-cun had believed she understood him, and had kept her constantly in his thoughts. Now that it was Mid-Autumn, the moon inevitably stirred his feelings. He improvised a regulated poem in five-character lines:

I cannot tell if the wish of three lives will be fulfilled; Again and again, fresh sorrow comes.

Brows draw together in sadness; How often a backward glance on leaving.

I look at my own shadow in the wind; Who could be my companion beneath the moon?

If the moonlight has any feeling, Let it climb first to that lovely woman’s chamber.

When he had finished, Yu-cun thought of the ambitions of his life and the bitter delay in finding his opportunity. He scratched his head, looked up at the sky and gave a long sigh. Then he declaimed a couplet:

Jade in its case seeks a worthy price;

The hairpin in its casket waits for the time to fly.

Shi-yin arrived just in time to hear it. ‘You certainly have high ambitions, Yu-cun!’ he said with a smile. Yu-cun hastily smiled in return. ‘I was merely reciting a couple of lines by an earlier poet. How could I presume to make such extravagant claims?’ Then he asked, ‘What brings you here, sir?’

Shi-yin smiled. ‘It is Mid-Autumn tonight, what people call the festival of reunion. I thought that, lodging far from home in a monk’s cell, you must feel a little lonely. So I have prepared some wine and come to invite you to drink with me in my poor study. Would you accept this modest offering?’ Yu-cun made no attempt to decline. ‘Since you are so kind,’ he replied with a smile, ‘how could I refuse your generous invitation?’ He went back with Shi-yin to the study.

Tea was soon finished, and the cups and dishes were already laid out. The fine wine and excellent food need no description. They took their seats, at first pouring with care and drinking slowly. Then, as their talk grew livelier, the cups began to pass rapidly. Throughout the neighbourhood pipes were playing in every house, strings and singing sounded at every door. Overhead the full moon shed its brilliant, gathered light. The two men’s spirits rose still higher, and every cup was drained as soon as it was filled. Yu-cun was now decidedly drunk. Unable to contain his exuberance, he addressed the moon, putting his feelings into an improvised quatrain:

On the fifteenth night you come full circle;

Clear light, in brimming handfuls, shelters the jade balustrade.

No sooner is that round disc lifted into heaven

Than every family on earth looks up to see.

‘Wonderful!’ Shi-yin cried. ‘I have always said you would not remain beneath others for long. In these lines you have just recited I can already see the sign of your ascent. Before long you will be stepping among the clouds. Congratulations, congratulations!’ He filled a cup with wine himself to celebrate. Yu-cun drained it and sighed. ‘This is not drunken boasting, sir, but in the learning required these days I might perhaps make up the numbers and earn a name. Only I cannot raise anything for luggage or travelling expenses. The capital is a long way off. Selling calligraphy and writing essays will never get me there.’ Before he had finished, Shi-yin said, ‘Why did you not tell me sooner?

‘I have often had it in mind, but whenever we met you never mentioned it, so I did not like to presume. Now that the matter has come up, little ability as I have, I do know the difference between right conduct and self-interest. Fortunately next year is the year of the great examinations. You should hurry to the capital and try your strength in the spring examinations, so that your learning will not have been wasted. As for the travelling expenses, leave those to me. Then your good opinion of me will not have been entirely misplaced.’ He immediately told the boy to go inside and have fifty taels of silver wrapped up, together with two suits of winter clothes. ‘The nineteenth is an auspicious day,’ he added. ‘You can hire a boat and set off westwards then. When you have soared to the heights, we can meet again next winter. Would that not be a splendid thing?’ Yu-cun accepted the silver and clothes with only a brief word of thanks, making little of the matter, and went on drinking, talking and laughing. It was not until the third watch that they parted.

After seeing Yu-cun off, Shi-yin returned to his room and slept until the sun was high. Thinking over the previous night’s conversation, he decided to write two letters of introduction for Yu-cun to take to the capital, so that he could call on an official household and find somewhere to stay. He sent a servant to invite him over. The man returned and said, ‘The monk says Mr Jia left for the capital at the fifth watch this morning. He left a message for you with the monk: “A scholar should not concern himself with lucky or unlucky days. What matters is the sense of the thing. I could not wait to take my leave in person.”’ Shi-yin could only let it rest. Time passes quickly in leisure. Almost before one knew it, the Lantern Festival had come round. Shi-yin told the servant Huo Qi to take Ying-lian out to see the festive performances and lanterns. In the middle of the night Huo Qi needed to relieve himself, and left Ying-lian sitting on a doorstep. When he returned to pick her up, there was no sign of her. Frantic, he searched for the rest of the night. At dawn she was still missing. Afraid to face his master, Huo Qi fled to another district.

When their daughter failed to return all night, Shi-yin and his wife knew something was wrong. They sent several people out to look, but all came back without a word of news. She was the only child they had had in half a lifetime. How could they not pine for her, lost in a moment like this? They wept day and night, almost driven to take their own lives. As a month went by, Shi-yin fell ill; his wife too became sick with longing for their daughter. Doctors were called every day to treat them. Then, on the fifteenth day of the third month, the monks at Gourd Temple were frying offerings. Through carelessness, fire leapt from the oil pan and caught the paper windows. Most houses in that district had bamboo fences and wooden walls. Perhaps it was their appointed calamity: the flames spread from one to another until the whole street was like the Mountain of Flames. Soldiers and townspeople came to help, but the blaze had already taken hold. How could they put it out? It burned all night before gradually subsiding. No one knew how many houses had gone. Poor Zhen’s house, next door to the temple, had long since become a field of broken tiles and rubble.

Only their lives were spared: Shi-yin, his wife and a few servants were unhurt. In his distress Shi-yin could do nothing but stamp his feet and sigh. After consulting his wife, he decided they must go and live on their farm. But in recent years floods and droughts had ruined the harvests, and bands of thieves were springing up everywhere, seizing land and fields, robbing and stealing, leaving the people no peace. Government troops were sent to hunt them down, and it was impossible to settle there. Shi-yin had to sell the farm and take his wife and two maids to his father-in-law’s home.

His father-in-law, Feng Su, was a native of Daruzhou. Though a farmer, he was fairly well-to-do, and he was not pleased to see his son-in-law arrive in such a plight. Fortunately Shi-yin still had some of the money from the sale of his land. He handed it over to Feng Su, asking him to buy a modest house and some land at a suitable price, within his means, to provide for their future. By a mixture of deception and cheating, Feng Su gave him only a little poor land and a dilapidated house. Shi-yin was a scholar, unused to business or farming. He struggled on for a year or two, growing poorer all the time. Whenever they met, Feng Su had some easy advice to offer. In front of them and behind their backs he complained that they did not know how to manage, that they were lazy and thought only of eating, and so on. Shi-yin realised he had sought help from the wrong person and could not help regretting it. The shocks, anguish, anger and grief of the preceding year had already done him lasting harm. At his age, assailed by poverty and illness, he gradually began to look like a man whose life was drawing to its end.

One day, leaning on a stick, he struggled out to the street to ease his mind. He saw a lame Taoist coming towards him, wild and dishevelled, in hemp sandals and tattered, patchwork clothes, chanting these words:

All know how good it is to be immortal, But with rank and fame they are never done!

Where are the ministers and generals, past and present? In heaps of lonely graves the grass has overrun.

All know how good it is to be immortal, But with gold and silver they are never done!

All day they fret that they have not amassed enough; When at last they have, their eyes close: done.

All know how good it is to be immortal, But with a lovely wife they are never done!

While you live, she speaks of love each day; You die, and off she goes with another one.

All say how good it is to be immortal, But with sons and grandsons they are never done!

There have always been plenty of doting parents; But dutiful children: who has ever seen one?

Shi-yin went up to him. ‘What is all this you are saying? All I can hear is “good” and “done”, “good” and “done”.’ The Taoist laughed. ‘If you really hear those two words, “good” and “done”, you understand something after all. You must know that, with everything in this world, good is done, and done is good. If you are not done, it is not good; if you want it good, you must be done. That is why I call my song The Good-and-Done Song.’ Shi-yin had wisdom carried over from a former life. As soon as he heard this, full understanding dawned within him. He smiled. ‘Wait!

‘What if I explain your Good-and-Done Song?’ ‘Do explain it,’ the Taoist said, smiling. ‘Do.’ Shi-yin began:

Mean rooms, deserted halls: once court tablets filled a bed;

Dead grass and withered poplars: once a place of song and dance.

Cobwebs cover the carved beams,

While green gauze is pasted once more over brushwood windows.

You say the rouge is thick, the powder fragrant: How have both temples turned to frost again?

Yesterday, white bones were taken to the yellow earth;

Tonight, beneath red lamplight, a pair of mandarin ducks share the curtained bed.

Chests full of gold, chests full of silver:

In a blink, a beggar whom everyone reviles.

You sigh that another’s life was short, Not knowing you will come home only to die!

Train a child well: you cannot be sure he will not become a robber.

Choose the wealthy and well-born: who would expect an end in a brothel lane?

Dissatisfied with too small an official’s hat, You come to bear a cangue and chains.

Yesterday, a ragged coat seemed pitifully cold; Today, you grumble that your purple python-patterned robe is too long.

What a clamour! Your song is barely done before I take the stage,

Mistaking a foreign place for home.

How absurd! In the end, all that sewing Was a wedding dress for someone else!

The mad, lame Taoist clapped his hands and laughed. ‘Exactly! Exactly!’ Shi-yin laughed too. ‘Let us go!’ He snatched the pouch from the Taoist’s shoulder and slung it over his own. Without going home, he went lightly away with the mad Taoist.

The whole neighbourhood was stirred, and everyone passed the story on as a piece of news. When his wife heard, she wept until she was almost senseless. She could only consult her father and send people out to search in every direction. But where were they to find any word of him? There was nothing for it: she had to depend on her parents. Fortunately she still had two of her old maids with her. Mistress and servants, all three, did needlework day and night to sell, helping with her father’s household expenses. Feng Su complained every day, but could do nothing about it.

One day the Zhen family’s senior maid was buying thread at the gate when she heard the cries of men clearing the street. Everyone said the new prefect had arrived to take up office. She stood concealed inside the doorway to watch. Guards and runners passed in pairs. Presently a great sedan chair came by, bearing an official in a black hat and scarlet robe.

The maid stared, thinking that his face was very familiar, as if she had seen him somewhere before. Then she went back into the room and put it out of her mind. That evening, just as they were about to go to bed, there was a loud hammering at the gate. Many voices shouted, ‘His Honour has sent for someone from this house to answer questions!’ Feng Su was struck dumb with fright, not knowing what disaster had come upon them.
