Chapter 14 Lin Ru-hai dies in the city of Yangzhou; Jia Bao-yu pays his respects to the Prince of Beijing on the road

Chapter end

Now Lai Sheng, the chief steward of the Ning mansion, heard that Xi-feng had been invited to take charge of affairs within. He assembled his fellow servants and told them, ‘They have asked Mrs Lian from the western mansion to manage things inside. If she comes to draw supplies or gives us any instructions, we must be more careful than usual. Everyone must come early and leave late. Better work hard for this one month and rest afterwards than lose face. She’s a famous spitfire, sour-faced and hard-hearted. Once her temper’s up, she’ll make no allowances for anyone.’ They all agreed that he was right.

‘To be fair,’ another said with a laugh, ‘we could do with her putting the inside of this house in order. Things have really gone too far.’ As they were talking, Lai Wang-er’s wife arrived with an authorisation tally to collect paper for formal announcements, large public notices and paper offerings. The quantities were marked on the requisition. They hurried to offer her a seat and tea, while someone was sent to fetch the specified amount of paper. He carried it alongside her as far as the ceremonial gate, where he handed it over for her to take inside herself.

Xi-feng at once ordered Sunshine to stitch together the account-books. She then summoned Lai Sheng’s wife and asked for the register of the household’s servants and their families. She also ordered that all the married women servants be assembled early next morning to receive their duties. After glancing over the lists and figures and asking Lai Sheng’s wife a few questions, she went home in her carriage. Nothing happened that night to call for an account.

Next morning she arrived at half past six. The married women servants of the Ning mansion had all gathered on hearing of her arrival. Seeing that she was still assigning duties with Lai Sheng’s wife, none dared enter unbidden. They listened and peered through the windows. They heard Xi-feng say, ‘Since you have entrusted this to me, I’m afraid you’ll have to put up with being annoyed. I’m not as good-tempered as your mistress, letting you do as you please. I want no more of “That’s how we’ve always done it in this house.” From now on you’ll do as I say. Cross me by so much as a hair and I shan’t care who has influence and who has none. Everyone will be dealt with openly and alike.’

She told Sunshine to read the register and called the women in one by one to look them over. When she had seen them all, she gave her orders. ‘These twenty are to be divided into two shifts of ten. Their only work each day will be serving tea to visitors in the inner rooms. They need attend to nothing else. These next twenty will also work in two shifts. They are to look after the tea and meals for members of the family and our relations, and nothing else.

‘These forty, again in two shifts, are to tend the incense and replenish the oil before the coffin, hang the mourning curtains, keep vigil, set out the offerings of tea and food, and join in the lamentations whenever they begin. Nothing else concerns them. These four will look after the cups, saucers and tea things in the inner tea-room. If one piece is missing, the four of them must make good the loss. These four will be responsible for the wine vessels and dishes. If a piece goes missing, they too must make good the loss. These eight will supervise the receipt of funeral offerings. These eight will look after the lamp oil, candles and paper offerings for every part of the house. I shall draw the whole supply and hand it over to you eight, who will distribute it according to the quantities I prescribe. These thirty will take turns on night duty, guarding the doors, watching for fire, and keeping the place clean. The rest will be assigned to the different rooms, each person responsible for a particular place. Everything there, from tables, chairs and antiques down to spittoons and dusters, every blade of grass and every plant, is their responsibility. If anything is lost or broken, I shall call the person in charge to account and make them pay for it. Lai Sheng’s wife will make a general inspection every day. If anyone is idling, gambling, drinking, fighting or quarrelling, report to me at once.

‘If you show favour and I find out, all the standing your family has built up in three or four generations won’t save you. Now that everyone has a fixed duty, if anything goes wrong in one branch of the work, it is the people in that branch I shall speak to. My own people always have a watch with them. For every task, large or small, I have a set time. You have a clock in the principal rooms here too. I shall come to call the roll at half past six in the morning. Breakfast will be in the middle of the si hour. Anyone needing a tally or wishing to report on business must come only at the first quarter of the wu hour. At the beginning of the xu hour, after the evening paper-offerings have been burnt, I shall inspect every part of the house myself. When I come back, the handover of keys for the night watch must be properly accounted for. Next morning I shall be here again at half past six. We shall all have to work hard for these few days. Once the business is over, your master will of course reward you.’

She then ordered tea, oil, candles, feather dusters, brooms and other supplies to be issued in the appointed quantities. Furnishings were also brought out: table-fronts, chair covers, cushions, felt mats, spittoons, footstools and the like. As each item was handed over she recorded it, setting down quite plainly who was responsible for each place and who had received each object.

The women took their supplies away, each now with a definite task. It was no longer a matter of choosing the easiest jobs and leaving the hard ones for nobody to undertake. Nor could things disappear from the rooms under cover of confusion. Even the coming and going of visitors became orderly. No longer did someone setting out tea have to rush off to serve a meal, or someone joining the lamentations turn aside to receive a guest. All that disorder, confusion, shifting of responsibility, idling and pilfering had been swept away by the next day.

Xi-feng was thoroughly pleased to find that her authority carried weight and her orders were obeyed. Seeing that You-shi was ill and that Jia Zhen, overcome with grief, was eating very little, she had delicate rice gruels and carefully prepared side dishes made at her own mansion every day and sent over to tempt them to eat. Jia Zhen, for his part, ordered the finest dishes to be sent daily to the annex for Xi-feng alone. She never shrank from hard work.

Every day she arrived at half past six in the morning to call the roll and attend to business. She kept to the annex on her own instead of joining the other married women of the family, and did not go out to receive the visiting ladies either. This was the principal day of the fifth seventh-day observance. The Buddhist ritual monks were opening the ways and breaking open the prisons of hell, passing the lamps to light the dead on their way, paying homage to the Lord of the Underworld, summoning the ghost commanders, inviting Kshitigarbha, opening the golden bridge and leading the sacred banners. The Taoists were submitting their memorials and petitions, worshipping the Three Pure Ones and bowing before the Jade Emperor. The Chan monks were offering incense, performing the rite for feeding hungry ghosts and making their devotions in the Water Repentance rite. There were also thirteen Buddhist nuns, wearing embroidered robes and red shoes, silently reciting before the coffin the spells that would guide the departed. It was a scene of great activity.

Xi-feng knew there would be many visitors that day. Having spent the night at home, she was called by Patience in the middle of the yin hour to rise and make her toilette. By the time she was ready, had changed her clothes and washed her hands, eaten a few mouthfuls of rice gruel with milk and sugar, and rinsed her mouth, it was already half past six. Lai Wang-er’s wife and the other attendants had long been waiting. Xi-feng came out before the hall and got into her carriage. A pair of translucent horn lanterns went ahead, bearing three large Chinese characters meaning ‘Rong Mansion’, and the carriage moved at a measured pace to the Ning mansion.

The lanterns over the main gate shone brightly. On either side stood matching rows of lanterns on poles, making the entrance as light as day. Servants in mourning white waited in lines to right and left. When the carriage had been ushered up to the main entrance, the pages withdrew and the married women servants stepped forward to raise the curtain. Xi-feng alighted, resting one hand on Felicity. Two women carried hand-lanterns as the attendants escorted her inside. The women of the Ning mansion came forward to greet her and pay their respects. She walked slowly into the Garden of Gathered Fragrance and up to the coffin in the Ascension Pavilion. At the sight of it, tears rolled down her face like pearls from a broken string. Many pages stood in the courtyard, hands lowered, waiting to burn the paper offerings. ‘Offer tea and burn the paper,’ Xi-feng ordered. A gong sounded and all the instruments struck up. Someone had already brought a great round-backed chair and set it before the coffin. Xi-feng sat down and began to weep aloud.

As soon as they heard her, men and women, high and low, inside and outside, hurried to join in the wailing. After a while Jia Zhen and You-shi sent someone to comfort her, and only then did she stop. Lai Wang-er’s wife brought tea for her to rinse her mouth. Xi-feng rose, took leave of the members of the clan and went to the annex. She checked the attendance against the register. Every group was complete except for one woman whose duty was receiving and seeing off visiting relations. She had the woman summoned. The woman was already distraught with shame and fear.

Xi-feng gave a cold laugh. ‘I wondered who had kept us waiting. So it’s you! Of course, you have more standing than the rest. That must be why you needn’t obey me.’

‘I come early every day, ma’am. It was only today. I woke and thought it was still early, then dropped off again and came a little late. Please forgive me this once.’

As she spoke, Wang Xing’s wife from the Rong mansion appeared, peering in at the front.

Leaving the first woman’s case unsettled for the moment, Xi-feng asked, ‘What does Wang Xing’s wife want?’ That woman had been longing to be asked first so that she could finish her errand. She hurried inside. ‘A tally to draw thread, ma’am, for the netted trimmings on the carriages and sedan-chairs.’ She handed up a slip. Xi-feng told Sunshine to read it. ‘Two large sedan-chairs, four small sedan-chairs and four carriages, requiring so many lengths of large and small netting, and so many catties of pearl-thread.’ The quantities tallied. Xi-feng told Sunshine to enter them in the register, then took a Rong mansion authorisation tally and tossed it down. Wang Xing’s wife left.

Xi-feng was about to speak when four managers from the Rong mansion came in. All needed tallies to draw supplies. She had Sunshine collect their slips and read them aloud. Of the four requests, she pointed to two. ‘The charges on these two are wrong. Calculate them properly before you come back.’ She tossed down the slips, and the two applicants withdrew, crestfallen.

Seeing Zhang Cai’s wife standing nearby, Xi-feng asked what she wanted. The woman hurried to produce her slip. ‘It’s for the carriage and sedan-chair covers just finished, ma’am. So many taels for the tailors’ work.’ Xi-feng took the slip and told Sunshine to record it. Only after Wang Xing’s wife had returned the tally and the buyer’s countersigned receipt had been checked against it did she give it to Zhang Cai’s wife to collect the money. She then had the remaining slip read. Bao-yu’s outer study had been completed and paper was needed for lining and papering it. She ordered the slip to be kept and the entry made. When Zhang Cai’s wife had cleared her account and returned the tally, she issued it to this applicant in turn.

Then Xi-feng resumed. ‘Tomorrow she’ll oversleep, and the day after I’ll oversleep, and in the end there’ll be nobody here at all. I meant to let you off. But if I’m lenient the first time, I shan’t be able to manage anyone afterwards. Better settle it now.’ Her face hardened. ‘Take her outside and give her twenty strokes!’ she cried. She tossed down the Ning mansion’s authorisation tally. ‘Tell Lai Sheng she is to lose a month’s silver and rice as well!’ Hearing this and seeing her eyebrows bristle, the servants knew she was angry and dared not delay. Those charged with taking the woman out dragged her away, while the bearer of the tally hurried off with the order.

The woman could do nothing. Dragged outside, she received twenty heavy strokes of the paddle and then had to come back in and kowtow her thanks. ‘If anyone is late tomorrow, it will be forty,’ said Xi-feng. ‘The day after, sixty. If you want a beating, by all means be late!’ Then she told them to disperse. Those listening outside the windows went off to their duties. All this time managers from both the Ning and Rong mansions kept coming and going, collecting and returning tallies. The beaten woman withdrew in shame, knowing now what Xi-feng was capable of. Nobody dared shirk. From that day they all worked diligently and cautiously to keep themselves clear of trouble. Of this we need say no more.

Let us return to Bao-yu. Seeing how crowded it was that day, he feared Qin Zhong might be put upon, and quietly suggested that they should go and sit with Xi-feng. ‘She has so much to do,’ said Qin Zhong, ‘and she doesn’t like people going there. Won’t we annoy her?’

‘How could she be annoyed with us? It doesn’t matter. Come along.’ Taking Qin Zhong by the hand, Bao-yu went straight to the annex. Xi-feng was just having her meal. At the sight of them she laughed. ‘What long legs you have! Come on up.’

‘We’ve already eaten, I’m afraid,’ said Bao-yu.

‘Out here on this side, or over at home?’

‘Why should we eat with those louts here? We ate at home, both of us, with Grandmother.’ As he spoke he went over and sat down.

When Xi-feng had finished, a woman from the Ning mansion came for a tally to draw incense and lamp supplies. ‘I reckoned you were due for another issue today,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘When you didn’t come I thought you’d forgotten. So you’ve come for it after all! If you’d forgotten, of course, you would have had to pay for it yourselves. All the better for me.’

‘We did forget,’ the woman said, laughing. ‘We only just remembered. A little later and we wouldn’t have been able to get the tally!’ She took it and went away.

Before long the entry was made and the tally returned. Qin Zhong laughed. ‘You use these tallies in both houses. What if someone secretly made one for himself, drew the silver and ran away?’

‘By your account there’d be no law left in the land,’ said Xi-feng, smiling.

‘Why does nobody from our house come for tallies to get things made?’ Bao-yu asked.

‘When they come for them, you’re still dreaming. Tell me, when are you going to start these evening lessons of yours?’

‘I wish we could start this very minute. But they’re so slow getting the study ready. There’s nothing I can do.’

‘Ask me nicely and I’ll guarantee it will be ready soon,’ Xi-feng said with a laugh.

‘It’s no use your wanting it done quickly. They’ll finish it when they get to it.’

‘Even if they do the work, they need supplies. And what are they to do if I won’t give them the tally?’

At that Bao-yu clambered all over her, demanding it at once. ‘Dear sister, give them the tally! Let them go and get what they need.’

‘I’m so tired my whole body aches. Do you think I can stand being pulled about as well? Don’t worry. They only collected the paper for the walls today. Do you suppose they wait to be told when they need something? What a goose you are!’ Bao-yu did not believe her, so she told Sunshine to find the entry in the register and show him.

They were still playing about when someone announced, ‘Zhao-er, who went to Suzhou, is back.’ Xi-feng at once had him called in. He made a formal kneeling salute and paid his respects. ‘What have you come back for?’ she asked.

‘The Second Master sent me. Mr Lin died on the third day of the ninth month, in the si hour. The Second Master has taken Miss Lin to escort her father’s coffin to Suzhou. They should be back by the end of the year. He sent me to bring the news and pay his respects, to ask whether the Old Lady has any instructions, and to see that all is well with you at home, ma’am. He also wants a few of his heavy fur garments sent out.’

‘Have you seen the others yet?’

‘Yes, ma’am, all of them.’ With that he quickly withdrew.

Xi-feng smiled at Bao-yu. ‘Your Cousin Lin will be staying with us for good now.’

‘Oh, how dreadful! Think how she must have been crying these last few days!’ Bao-yu frowned and gave a long sigh.

Ever since Zhao-er’s arrival, Xi-feng had been anxious for news of Jia Lian. With other people present she had been unable to question him closely. She wanted to go home, but there were so many things to do that she feared leaving even briefly might cause delays and mistakes and expose her to ridicule. She had to endure it until evening. Once home, she summoned Zhao-er again and asked in detail whether all had gone safely on the journey. That very night she got the heavy fur clothes ready. She and Patience checked the bundles themselves, thinking carefully over everything else he might need and packing it all for Zhao-er to take. She also gave him minute instructions. ‘Look after your Second Master properly while you’re away. Don’t provoke him. Keep urging him to drink less, and don’t lead him into getting acquainted with disreputable women. If you do, I’ll break your legs when you come back.’ By the time all this bustle was over, the fourth watch was almost ended. When at last she lay down, she had gone past sleepiness. Before she knew it, the cocks were crowing at dawn. She hurried to wash and dress and go over to the Ning mansion.

With the day for the funeral procession approaching, Jia Zhen took a clerk from the Yin-yang Office in his carriage to the Temple of the Iron Threshold, to inspect the place where the coffin was to rest temporarily. He gave the abbot, Se-kong, detailed instructions to prepare fresh furnishings and invite more eminent monks for the reception of the coffin. Se-kong hurried to provide a vegetarian supper. Jia Zhen had no appetite for food or tea. It was too late to get back into the city, so he passed an uncomfortable night in a quiet room at the temple. Early next morning he returned to the city to arrange the funeral procession. He also sent people ahead to the temple to work through the night, putting the place for the coffin in order and arranging the kitchens, tea-making facilities and accommodation for those who would receive it.

Inside the mansion Xi-feng, seeing how little time remained, was arranging every detail in advance. She also had to assign the Rong household’s carriages, sedan-chairs and servants to accompany Lady Wang in the procession, and secure a place to stay for herself when she went. Just then the titled wife of the Duke of Shan had died, and Lady Wang and Lady Xing had to go to make offerings and attend her funeral. There was the Princess of Xi’an’s birthday, requiring a gift; the titled wife of the Duke of Zhen had given birth to her eldest son, requiring another. Xi-feng’s own elder brother, Wang Ren, was returning south with his family, so she had to write a family letter conveying her respectful greetings to her parents and prepare things for him to take. Ying-chun was ill, too. Physicians were called and medicines taken every day, and their written reports, accounts of her symptoms and prescriptions all needed attention. There was more than can be told. With the funeral procession so near, Xi-feng scarcely had time for food or tea, and could neither sit nor lie down in peace. No sooner had she reached the Ning mansion than people from Rong followed her there. Once back at Rong, she was pursued by those from Ning. Yet all this delighted her. She neither sought ease nor put things off on others. Anxious to give nobody grounds for criticism, she worked night and day, keeping everything in the strictest order.

Everyone in the clan, high and low, praised her. On the evening of the final vigil, two troupes of young actors and performers of assorted entertainments kept the friends, relations and visiting ladies company within. You-shi was still confined to her room. All the arrangements and the reception of the guests fell to Xi-feng alone. There were plenty of other married women in the clan, but some were shy of speaking, some hesitant to move about, some unused to meeting strangers, and others awed by rank or frightened of officials. None could match her unhurried bearing, assured speech and dignified, expansive manner. Consequently she thought little of the rest, giving her orders freely and doing as she pleased, as though no one else were there. All night the lamps blazed as one guest was seen off and another official welcomed. There is no need to describe the bustle. At dawn, when the auspicious hour arrived, a company of sixty-four bearers in blue raised the coffin. On the funeral banner in front were written in large characters the words:

‘The coffin of Qin-shi of the Jia family, honoured with the title gongren by imperial patent under the dynasty grandly founded in obedience to Heaven and unchanging through countless ages; wife of the eldest grandson in the legitimate line of the Duke of Ning of the First Rank, and wife of a Dragon Guard, an Imperial Bodyguard defending the Inner Court and the ways of the Forbidden Palace; having enjoyed a full span of life.’

All the ceremonial equipment and furnishings had been hurriedly made afresh, and everything shone with dazzling splendour. Jewel performed the rites of an unmarried daughter, broke the funeral earthenware basin and went before the coffin as chief mourner, in the deepest distress. Among the officials attending the procession were Niu Ji-zong, now holding the inherited title of Earl of the First Rank, grandson of Niu Qing, Duke of Zhen; Liu Fang, now holding the inherited title of Viscount of the First Rank, grandson of Liu Biao, Duke of Li; Chen Rui-wen, hereditary General of Subduing Might of the Third Rank, grandson of Chen Yi, Duke of Qi; Ma Shang, hereditary General of Far-reaching Might of the Third Rank, grandson of Ma Kui, Duke of Zhi; and Hou Xiao-kang, hereditary Viscount of the First Rank, grandson of Hou Ming, Duke of Xiu. The titled wife of the Duke of Shan had died, and her grandson Shi Guang-zhu was in mourning and could not attend. These six families, together with the houses of Rong and Ning, were those known in former days as the Eight Dukes. There were also grandsons of the Prince of Nan’an and the Prince of Xining; Shi Ding, Marquis of Zhongjing; Jiang Zi-ning, grandson of the Marquis of Pingyuan and hereditary Baron of the Second Rank; Xie Jing, grandson of the Marquis of Dingcheng, hereditary Baron of the Second Rank and a youji officer of the Metropolitan Garrison; Qi Jian-hui, grandson of the Marquis of Xiangyang and hereditary Baron of the Second Rank; and Qiu Liang of the Office of Military and Police Affairs for the Five Districts, grandson of the Marquis of Jingtian.

Besides these there were Han Qi, son of the Marquis of Jinxiang; Feng Zi-ying, son of the General of Divine Might; Wei Ruo-lan, and more young gentlemen of princely and noble families than could be counted. The visiting ladies alone occupied some ten large sedan-chairs and thirty or forty small ones. With all the household’s own chairs and carriages, there were well over a hundred conveyances. Add the ceremonial attendants, equipment and entertainers in front, and the vast procession stretched for three or four li.

They had not gone far before they came upon tall, brightly decorated pavilions beside the road, with feasting tables laid and harmonious music playing. These were the different families’ roadside funeral offerings. The first was the mourning pavilion of the Prince of Dongping’s household, the second that of the Prince of Nan’an, the third that of the Prince of Xining and the fourth that of the Prince of Beijing. Of these four princes, the Prince of Beijing alone had rendered such great services in former days that his descendants still inherited the princely title. Its present holder, Shui Rong, was not yet twenty, handsome in appearance and gentle in disposition. Hearing lately of the death of the wife of the Duke of Ning’s eldest grandson, he had thought of the friendship between their grandfathers, who had shared hardship and glory and never treated one another as men of different surnames. He therefore set aside his princely dignity. He had already visited the house of mourning and made offerings, and now had roadside offerings prepared as well, ordering the officials under him to wait here. He himself had attended court during the fifth watch. As soon as his official business was over he changed into plain clothes and came in his great sedan-chair, with gongs sounding and a ceremonial parasol borne before him. He alighted in front of the pavilion. His officers crowded about him on either side, and neither soldiers nor civilians were allowed to pass.

Presently the great funeral procession from the Ning mansion appeared from the north, like a vast mountain of silver pressing over the earth. The Ning servants clearing the road and carrying messages saw the Prince and hurried back to inform Jia Zhen. Jia Zhen at once ordered the head of the procession to halt. With Jia She and Jia Zheng he hastened forward, and the three greeted the Prince with the formal courtesies required by his rank. Shui Rong inclined his body within the sedan-chair and returned their salute with a smile. He addressed and received them as old family friends, without assuming airs of superiority.

‘Your Highness has repeatedly condescended to honour the mourning for my unworthy daughter-in-law,’ said Jia Zhen. ‘How can we, who owe our standing to favours granted our ancestors, presume to deserve it?’

‘Between old family friends, why speak of such things?’ Shui Rong said with a smile. He turned and ordered his chief household officer to conduct the rite and make the libation on his behalf. Jia She and the others returned the ritual salutations from one side, then came forward again to thank him for his favour.

Shui Rong was most courteous. He asked Jia Zheng, ‘Which is the boy who was born with jade in his mouth? I have wanted to see him several times, but other business has always intervened. I suppose he has come today. Why not ask him to join us?’ Jia Zheng hurried back and ordered Bao-yu to take off his mourning clothes, then brought him forward. Bao-yu had often heard his father, brothers, relations and friends praise Shui Rong in conversation as a worthy prince, gifted and handsome, elegant and free in his manner, who would not always be bound by official convention and the formalities of state. He had often wished to meet him, but his father kept him under such strict control that there had been no opportunity. Now that his father himself had sent for him, he was naturally delighted. As he walked along he had already caught a glimpse of Shui Rong sitting in his sedan-chair. What a striking figure he was! What would he look like at close quarters?
