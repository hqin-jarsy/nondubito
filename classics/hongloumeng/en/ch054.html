Chapter 54 Lady Dowager Shi exposes the stale old formulas; Wang Xi-feng plays the dutiful child in motley

Chapter end

Now Jia Zhen and Jia Lian had quietly made ready their large winnowing baskets of cash. At Grandmother Jia’s cry of ‘Reward!’ they too hastened to order their pages to scatter the money. The whole stage rang with coins, to Grandmother Jia’s great delight. The two men rose. The pages promptly placed a silver wine-pot, freshly warmed, in Jia Lian’s hands, and he followed Jia Zhen briskly inside. Jia Zhen went first to Aunt Li’s table, bowed and took up her cup, then turned for Jia Lian to fill it. They next went to Aunt Xue’s table and filled her cup too. Both ladies rose hastily, smiling.

‘Do sit down, gentlemen. There’s no need for such ceremony.’

Except for Lady Xing and Lady Wang, everyone had now left their seats and was standing respectfully by with hands lowered. Jia Zhen and Jia Lian came before Grandmother Jia’s couch. As it was so low, they bent their knees and knelt, Jia Zhen in front with the cup, Jia Lian behind with the pot.

Though only these two were offering wine, Jia Huan and the other younger men had followed them in, all in a line according to their order of precedence. When the two knelt, the others knelt in a row behind them. Bao-yu hastily knelt too. Xiang-yun gave him a quiet nudge and smiled.

‘What are you kneeling down with them for? If you’re so eager, wouldn’t it be better to go and pour everyone a round yourself?’

‘In a little while,’ he whispered back, smiling.

He waited until the two men had finished pouring and risen before he got up himself.

They went on to pour wine for Lady Xing and Lady Wang.

‘What about our younger sisters?’ Jia Zhen asked with a smile.

‘Off you go,’ Grandmother Jia and the others told them. ‘The girls will be more at their ease.’

Only then did Jia Zhen and the rest withdraw.

The second watch had not yet sounded. They were performing the eight ‘Viewing the Lanterns’ pieces from The Eight Righteous Men. At the height of the excitement, Bao-yu left the table and went towards the door.

‘Where are you going?’ Grandmother Jia called. ‘Those firecrackers outside are fierce. Mind a burning scrap of paper doesn’t fall out of the sky and set you alight.’

‘I’m not going far. I’ll only be out a moment.’

Grandmother Jia told the older women to keep close beside him. Bao-yu went out, but only Musk, Ripple and a few little maids followed him.

‘Why haven’t I seen Aroma?’ Grandmother Jia asked. ‘She’s beginning to give herself airs, sending only the little girls out to wait on him.’

Lady Wang hurriedly rose and answered with a smile.

‘Her mother died the other day. She’s in fresh mourning and couldn’t properly come out here.’

Grandmother Jia nodded, then smiled again.

‘When you’re waiting on your master, you can’t make so much of mourning or not mourning. If she were still with me, would she be absent now? It’s all because we’re too easy-going. There are other people to do the work, so we don’t look into these things, and it ends by becoming the rule.’

Xi-feng quickly came forward, smiling.

‘Even if she weren’t in mourning, she would still have to look after the Garden tonight. Lamps, candles and fireworks are the most dangerous things. Once the plays begin here, who in the Garden won’t slip away for a look? She’s careful, and can keep an eye on everything. Then, when we break up and Bao-yu goes back to bed, everything will be ready for him. If she came too, and the others took no trouble, he’d go home to cold bedding, with no tea or hot water ready. Nothing would be as it should. So I told her not to come, but to mind the rooms. Everything will be ready when we leave, we shan’t have to worry here, and she can observe the proper mourning customs as well. Isn’t that three good things at once?

‘If you want her, Grandmother, I’ll send for her at once.’

‘You’re quite right,’ Grandmother Jia said hastily. ‘You’ve thought it out better than I did. Don’t send for her. But when did her mother die? Why didn’t I know?’

‘Aroma came and told you herself the other day,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘Have you forgotten?’

Grandmother Jia thought for a moment and laughed.

‘Now I remember. My memory really is getting poor.’

‘How could you keep all these things in mind?’ they all said, laughing.

Grandmother Jia sighed.

‘I was thinking how she served me when she was a little girl, then served Yun, and was finally given to that demon Bao-yu. She’s had him plaguing her for years. And she isn’t a servant born and bred in our household. She’s had no great favours from us. When her mother died, I meant to give her a few taels to help with the funeral, and then I forgot.’

‘Her Ladyship gave her forty taels the other day,’ said Xi-feng. ‘That will have been enough.’

Grandmother Jia nodded.

‘That’s something, at least.

‘As it happens, Faithful’s mother died the other day too. Her parents are both down south, and I haven’t sent her home for a visit and to observe her mourning. Let the two of them keep each other company now.’

She also told the women to take them some fruit, dishes, pastries and other things to eat.

‘As if she’d wait till now!’ Amber said with a smile. ‘She went long ago.’

They returned to their wine and the play.

Meanwhile, Bao-yu had gone straight to the Garden. Seeing that he was going back to his rooms, the older women did not follow. They stayed in the tea-room inside the Garden gate to warm themselves at the fire, stealing a little leisure to drink and play cards with the woman in charge of the tea. Bao-yu reached his courtyard. It was brilliantly lit, but not a voice could be heard.

‘Can they all be asleep?’ Musk asked. ‘Let’s creep in and give them a fright.’

They stole on tiptoe past the mirrored partition and peeped in. Aroma and someone else were reclining opposite each other on the heated floor, while two or three old nurses dozed at the far end. Bao-yu thought the pair were asleep too, and was just about to go in when he heard Faithful sigh.

‘You never can tell how things will turn out. With you here by yourself and your parents outside, moving east and west every year with no settled place, you’d have thought you could never be there at the end. Yet this year your mother happened to die here, and you were able to go out and be with her.’

‘Yes,’ Aroma said. ‘I never thought I’d be there at the end for either of my parents.

‘And Her Ladyship gave me forty taels. After bringing me up, Mother at least had that much from me. I daren’t hope for more.’

Bao-yu turned quickly and whispered to Musk and the others.

‘Who would have thought she’d come here too? If I go in, she’ll only go off in a temper. Better for us to go back and let them talk quietly for a while. Aroma was feeling lonely. How fortunate that she’s come.’

They slipped out as quietly as they had come.

Bao-yu went behind the rockery, stood still and lifted his clothes. Musk and Ripple stopped and turned their faces away.

‘Squat down before you undo your underclothes,’ they said, laughing. ‘Mind the wind doesn’t chill your belly.’

The two little maids behind them, understanding that he was passing water, hurried ahead to the tea-room to make ready. Bao-yu had just turned back when two women servants came towards them, asking who was there.

‘Bao-yu’s here,’ Ripple said. ‘Don’t shout and bawl like that. You’ll frighten him.’

‘We didn’t know,’ the women said hurriedly, smiling. ‘We’ve got ourselves into trouble on a great holiday! You young ladies must have had a tiring few days.’

They had reached them as they spoke.

‘What are you carrying?’ Musk and Ripple asked.

‘Her Old Ladyship has sent some food for Miss Jin and Miss Hua.’

Ripple laughed.

‘They’re playing The Eight Righteous Men out there, not The Primal Unity Box. Where has “Lady Golden Flower” sprung from?’1

‘Lift the lids and let me see,’ Bao-yu said with a smile.

Ripple and Musk hurried forward to open the two boxes. The women holding them quickly squatted down. Bao-yu saw that both boxes were filled with the finest fruit and dishes from the feast. He nodded and walked on. Musk and Ripple dropped the lids back any which way and hastened after him.

‘Those two women are pleasant, and know how to speak to people,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘They’re worn out themselves every day, yet they say it’s you who’ve had a tiring few days. They aren’t the sort to keep boasting of all they do.’

‘The good ones are very good,’ Musk replied. ‘But the ones with no manners really have none.’

‘You understand things,’ he said with a smile. ‘Just make allowances for them as poor, clumsy creatures, and leave it at that.’

Talking thus, they reached the Garden gate.

Though the older women had been drinking and playing cards, they had kept coming out to see where he was. When Bao-yu appeared, they all followed him. At the rear verandah of the reception hall they found the two little maids, who had been waiting a long time. One held a small wash-basin; the other had a towel draped over her arm and carried a little pot of hand-cleansing paste.

Ripple quickly put a hand into the basin to test the water.

‘You grow more careless as you grow older. Where did you get this cold water?’

‘Look at the weather, miss!’ the little maid said with a smile. ‘I was afraid it would get cold, so I made a point of pouring it boiling hot. And now it’s cold all the same.’

Just then an old woman came along carrying a kettle of boiling water.

‘Be a dear, Mother,’ the little maid said. ‘Come and pour me some.’

‘My dear child, this is for Her Old Ladyship’s tea. You’d better go and fetch your own. It won’t wear your feet out.’

‘I don’t care whose it is,’ Ripple said. ‘Are you refusing us? I’d empty Her Old Ladyship’s teapot for him to wash his hands.’

The old woman turned, saw that it was Ripple, and hastily lifted the kettle to pour.

‘That’s enough. At your age, you ought to have some sense. Who didn’t know it was Her Old Ladyship’s water? Would anyone ask if they weren’t entitled to it?’

‘My eyes are dim,’ the old woman said with a smile. ‘I didn’t recognise you, miss.’

Bao-yu washed his hands. The little maid poured some cleansing paste from the small pot into his hands, and he rubbed it in.

Ripple and Musk took advantage of the hot water to wash their hands too, and used the cleansing paste before following Bao-yu inside. He asked for a pot of warm wine and began, as the others had done, with Aunt Li and Aunt Xue. Both urged him to sit down.

‘He’s young. Let him pour,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘But everyone must drain this cup.’

She emptied her own as she spoke. Lady Xing and Lady Wang hastily did the same and urged the two guests to follow suit. Aunt Xue and Aunt Li could only drink theirs too.

‘Pour for all your sisters and cousins as well,’ Grandmother Jia told Bao-yu. ‘Go round them properly. Make every one of them drain her cup.’

He assented and went round them in order. When he reached Dai-yu, however, she would not drink. She picked up her cup and held it to his lips. He emptied it at a draught.

‘Thank you,’ she said, smiling.

He poured her another cup.

‘Bao-yu, don’t drink cold wine,’ Xi-feng said with a laugh. ‘It’ll make your hand shake, and tomorrow you won’t be able to write or draw a bow.’

‘I haven’t had any cold wine,’ he said hastily.

‘I know you haven’t. I was only reminding you.’

Bao-yu finished pouring for everyone inside except Jia Rong’s wife, whose cup was filled by the maids.

He went out to the verandah to pour for Jia Zhen and the others, sat with them for a while, then came back in and resumed his own place. Presently soup was served, followed by Lantern Festival dumplings. Grandmother Jia ordered a pause in the plays.

‘The poor children! Give them some piping-hot soup and food before they sing again.’

She also told the servants to take them some of the different fruits, dumplings and other things. When the performance stopped, an older woman brought in two female storytellers who were regular visitors to the household. Two stools were set to one side for them to sit on, and their three-stringed lute and pipa were handed to them. Grandmother Jia asked Aunt Li and Aunt Xue what tale they would like.

‘Anything at all,’ both replied.

‘Have you added any new stories lately?’ she asked the storytellers.

‘We have a new one, set in the last days of the Tang and the Five Dynasties.’

‘What is it called?’

‘The Phoenix Seeks Its Mate.’

‘That’s a good name,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘I wonder how it came by it. Give us an outline first. If it’s good, you can tell it.’

‘Our story tells of a gentleman in the last days of the Tang,’ one began. ‘He came from Jinling, and his name was Wang Zhong. He had served as chief minister under two reigns, and had now retired in his old age to his native place. He had an only son, whose name was Wang Xi-feng.’

Everyone began to laugh.

‘He has our Feng’s name,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing.

A woman servant hurried over and nudged the storyteller.

‘That’s the Second Mistress’s name. Don’t talk nonsense.’

‘Go on, go on,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile.

The storyteller hastily got to her feet, smiling apologetically.

‘We deserve to die! We didn’t know it was your honoured name, ma’am.’

‘What does that matter?’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Go on. Plenty of people have the same name and surname.’

The storyteller continued.

‘That year, Mr Wang sent his son to the capital to sit the examinations. One day the young gentleman was caught in heavy rain and took shelter on an estate. As it happened, the gentleman who owned it, a Mr Li, was an old friend of the Wang family. He invited the young man to stay in his study. This Mr Li had no son, but an only daughter. The young lady’s beautiful name was Chu-luan, and there was nothing she did not understand: qin-playing, chess, calligraphy, painting.’

‘No wonder it’s called The Phoenix Seeks Its Mate,’ Grandmother Jia broke in.

‘You needn’t tell me. I’ve guessed. Naturally this Wang Xi-feng wants to marry Miss Chu-luan.’

‘So Your Old Ladyship has heard this story before!’ the storyteller said with a smile.

‘What hasn’t Her Old Ladyship heard?’ everyone said.

‘Even if she hadn’t heard it, she’d have guessed.’

‘These stories all follow the same old pattern,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘Nothing but beauties and brilliant young scholars. The dullest things imaginable! They make out that other people’s daughters behave so badly, and still call them beauties. They’ve invented something with not a shadow of truth in it. The very first words are always about a family of scholars. The father is sure to be a minister or a chief minister, and the daughter is sure to be his precious darling. Naturally she knows her books and her manners, understands everything, and is a beauty without equal.

‘Then she catches sight of a good-looking young man, and whether he’s a relation or a friend, she thinks at once of marrying him. Her parents are forgotten, her books and her manners are forgotten. Neither a proper ghost nor a proper thief! What is there of a beauty about her? Even if she were full of learning, she couldn’t do such things and still be called a beauty. Suppose a man full of learning took to thieving. Would the law say he was a brilliant scholar, and therefore his case mustn’t be entered among the thefts? You see, the writer has stopped his own mouth. Then again, if she’s the daughter of a great scholarly family, with generations of officials behind it, she must know her books and manners. Even her mother is learned and well brought up. Her father may have retired, but a great household like that must still have plenty of people in it, with nurses and maids enough to wait on the young lady. So why is it that whenever anything of this sort happens in these stories, there’s only the young lady and the one maid who follows her everywhere? Just think for yourselves. What are all the other people doing? Doesn’t the end of the tale contradict the beginning?’

They all laughed.

‘Your Old Ladyship has picked out every lie!’

‘There’s a reason for it,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘One sort of person who writes these books envies other people their wealth and rank, or has asked some favour and been disappointed. So he invents all this to dirty their names.

‘Then there’s another sort who has read so many of these books that he’s bewitched by them. He wants a beauty himself, so he invents one for his own amusement. What does he know of the ways of scholarly families who have held office for generations? Never mind the great families of birth and learning in his books. Take a real family before your eyes, a middling household like ours. Nothing of the sort happens even here, let alone in those great houses.

‘They’ve lied their very jaws off. That’s why we’ve never allowed such stories to be told, and the girls don’t understand such talk. These last few years I’ve grown old, and the young people live some way off. Now and then when I’m bored, I have a little of it told to me. The moment they come, we stop.’

Aunt Li and Aunt Xue both smiled.

‘Those are the proper rules for a great family. Even in our houses, we don’t let the children hear such idle tales.’

Xi-feng came forward to pour wine.

‘Enough, enough!’ she said, laughing. ‘The wine’s getting cold. Take a sip to moisten your throat, Grandmother, before you go on picking the lies apart. We’ll call this instalment The Exposure of Tall Tales, set in this very dynasty, this very place, this very year, month, day and hour. But Grandmother has only one mouth and cannot tell two stories at once. Two flowers bloom: let us speak of one branch at a time. Whether the tale be true or false, we shall leave it for the moment, and return to those who were viewing the lanterns and watching the plays. Grandmother, why not let our two honoured guests drink a cup and watch a couple of scenes before taking up yesterday’s tale again?’

She poured as she spoke, laughing, and before she had finished everyone was helpless with laughter. The two storytellers could not stop laughing either.

‘What a tongue you have, ma’am! If you took to storytelling, there’d be no living left for us.’

‘Don’t get too carried away,’ Aunt Xue said, smiling. ‘There are men outside. It isn’t like an ordinary day.’

‘There’s only Cousin Zhen out there,’ Xi-feng said with a laugh. ‘We still count as brother and sister. We’ve played and made mischief together since we were children, all the way up to now.

‘These last few years, since we became related by marriage, haven’t I learnt any number of rules of behaviour? Even if we hadn’t grown up as brother and sister, even if we only reckoned him a brother-in-law, there’s still the “motley-clad child at play” in the Twenty-four Examples of Filial Piety. If they can’t come and play the child to make Grandmother laugh, and I’ve finally managed to coax a laugh out of her and get her to eat a little more, surely everyone’s pleased. They ought to thank me. Would they turn round and laugh at me instead?’2

‘Indeed, I haven’t had a really good laugh these last two days,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘Thanks to her, I’ve been laughing all this time, and I feel much better for it. I’ll have another cup.’

As she drank, she told Bao-yu to offer his cousin a cup too.

‘No need for him to toast me,’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘I’ll beg a little of Grandmother’s long life.’

She picked up Grandmother Jia’s cup and drank the half-cup of wine left in it, then handed it to a maid and had a fresh cup, which had been standing in warm water, brought in its place. The cups at all the other tables were taken away too, and those waiting in warm water were filled with fresh wine and brought up. Then Xi-feng returned to her seat.

‘If Your Old Ladyship doesn’t care for the story,’ the storyteller said, ‘perhaps you’d like to hear us play a suite?’

‘Play The General’s Command together,’ said Grandmother Jia.

The two promptly tuned their strings, found the key and began to play. Grandmother Jia asked what watch of the night it was.

‘The third watch,’ the older women hastened to reply.

‘No wonder the cold’s beginning to creep in.’

The maids were already bringing extra clothes and changes of clothing. Lady Wang rose and smiled.

‘Wouldn’t you be better off on the heated floor in the warm inner room? Neither of our two guests is a stranger. We can keep them company.’

‘If that’s so, why don’t we all move in?’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘Wouldn’t we be warmer?’

‘I’m afraid there won’t be room for everyone.’

‘I know what to do. We don’t need all these tables now. Put two or three together, and we can all sit close round them. It’ll be cosier, and warmer too.’

‘That will be much more fun,’ they all said, and rose from their places.

The women servants hurried to clear away the remains of the feast. Inside, they set three large tables together in a straight line and laid them with fresh fruit and dishes.

‘No standing on ceremony here,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘Just sit where I tell you.’

She gave Aunt Xue and Aunt Li the places of honour at the head, then sat facing west herself, with Bao-qin, Dai-yu and Xiang-yun close beside her on either side.

‘Sit next to your mother,’ she told Bao-yu.

Bao-yu sat between Lady Xing and Lady Wang. Bao-chai and the other girls were on the west side. Below them came Lou-shi with Jia Jun, then You-shi and Li Wan with Jia Lan between them. Jia Rong’s wife sat across the lower end.

‘Zhen, take your younger brothers and go,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘I shall be going to bed myself.’

Jia Zhen hastily assented, and they all started to come in again.

‘Off you go!’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘No need to come in. We’ve only just sat down, and now we shall all be getting up again. Go and rest. There’s important business tomorrow.’

Jia Zhen agreed at once, then smiled.

‘We ought to leave Rong here to pour the wine.’

‘So we ought. I’d forgotten him.’

‘Yes, Grandmother,’ Jia Zhen replied, and turned to lead Jia Lian and the rest away. The two were naturally delighted. They ordered people to take Jia Cong and Jia Huang to their respective homes; then Jia Zhen invited Jia Lian to go pleasure-seeking and buy a few smiles. Of that we need say no more.

‘I was just thinking,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile, ‘that for all the people here enjoying themselves, we hadn’t a single complete couple. I’d forgotten Rong. Now we’re complete. Rong, sit beside your wife, and you can have your reunion too.’

A woman servant came to announce that the play was beginning again.

‘We women are just getting into our talk, and now they’re going to make a noise,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘Besides, the children must be terribly cold, staying up so late. Never mind. Let them rest for a while. Fetch our own girls and have them sing a couple of scenes on this stage for the others to watch.’

The woman assented and went out. She hurriedly sent someone to Prospect Garden for the girls, while going to the inner gate to summon the pages to attend. The pages went straight to the dressing-room and took out all the adults in the visiting troupe, leaving only the children.

Presently the instructor from Pear-tree Court came through the side gate off the covered walk with Wen-guan and the other girls, twelve in all. The older women carried several soft bundles. There had been no time to bring the trunks, so they had guessed at three or five scenes Grandmother Jia might like and bundled up the costumes for them. They led Wen-guan and the rest in to pay their respects; the girls stood with their hands lowered.

‘In the middle of the New Year, and your teacher won’t let you out for a little amusement,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘What are you going to sing? Those eight pieces from The Eight Righteous Men have given me a headache. Let’s have something quieter. Now look: Aunt Xue and our kinswoman Mrs Li both have troupes in their own households. Who knows how many fine plays they’ve heard? Their young ladies have seen better plays and heard better singing than our girls have. And these little actors here belong to a troupe kept by a famous connoisseur of the theatre. Children though they are, they’re better than the grown-up troupes. We mustn’t give anyone cause to find fault. We shall have to offer something a little different. Have Parfumée sing Seeking the Dream. Just a light accompaniment of tiqin and pipes. No sheng or dizi at all.’

‘Quite so,’ Wen-guan said with a smile. ‘Of course our plays could never find favour in the eyes of our honoured guests and their young ladies. You can only listen to the way we bring out the words, and hear what our voices are like.’

‘Exactly,’ said Grandmother Jia.

Aunt Li and Aunt Xue laughed in delight.

‘What a quick-witted child! She’s joining you in making fun of us.’

‘Our girls are only for a little amusement at home,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘They don’t go out to earn money, so they’re really rather behind the times.’

Then she added, ‘Have Kui-guan sing Huiming Delivers the Letter. No painted face, either.

‘Just those two scenes, to let them hear something out of the ordinary. And I won’t have you sparing yourselves the least bit of trouble.’

Wen-guan and the others went out, hurriedly dressed and took the stage, first for Seeking the Dream, then for Delivering the Letter. Not a sound came from the audience.

‘It really is remarkable,’ Aunt Xue said with a smile. ‘I’ve watched hundreds of troupes, and I’ve never seen one use pipes for the accompaniment.’

‘Oh, there are some,’ Grandmother Jia replied. ‘Take that passage just now, Clear Skies over the Chu River from The Western Tower. The young male lead often accompanies it on a xiao. But for a whole long sequence like this, it is rare. It all depends on how much trouble the patron cares to take. What’s so extraordinary about it? When I was her age,’ she went on, pointing to Xiang-yun, ‘her grandfather had a troupe of young actors. As luck would have it, a qin-player joined them. When they did Listening to the Qin from The Western Chamber, or Wooing with the Qin from The Jade Hairpin, or Eighteen Songs for the Hu Reed-pipe from The Sequel to the Lute, it was all real. How much better was that than this?’

‘That must have been rarer still,’ they all said.

Grandmother Jia called a woman servant and told her to have Wen-guan and the others play the suite Round Lanterns, Full Moon on their wind instruments. The woman went to carry out the order. Jia Rong and his wife now took the wine round together. Seeing Grandmother Jia in such high spirits, Xi-feng smiled.

‘While the storytellers are here, why not have them beat the drum, and we can pass a plum branch? We’ll play “Spring Joy Climbs to the Brows”. How would that be?’

‘A good game,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘Just right for the season and the occasion.’

She promptly ordered a drinking-game drum to be brought, black-lacquered and studded with copper, for the storytellers to play their flourishes on. A spray of red plum blossom was taken from the table.

‘Whoever has it when the drum stops must drink a cup,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘And ought to say something as well.’

‘Who can be like you, Grandmother, with something ready for every occasion?’ Xi-feng said with a smile. ‘What about those of us who can’t? It would be no fun for us. I say we ought to have something for learned and simple alike. Let whoever loses tell a joke.’

Everyone knew how good she was at telling jokes, and what an endless store of fresh and funny tales she had. Her proposal delighted not only the people at the table, but every attendant in the room, old and young. The little maids hurried out to fetch their friends.

‘Come and listen! The Second Mistress is going to tell jokes again.’

Soon the room was packed with maids. The plays had finished and the music ceased. Grandmother Jia had soup, pastries, fruit and dishes sent to Wen-guan and the others, then gave the word for the drum to begin. The storytellers were old hands at this. Now fast, now slow, they drummed: like the last drops of a water-clock, like bursting beans, like a startled horse careering wildly, like a flash of lightning suddenly gone dark. When the drumming slowed, the plum branch passed slowly; when it quickened, the branch sped from hand to hand. Just as it reached Grandmother Jia, the drum stopped dead. Everyone laughed, and Jia Rong hurried up to pour a cup.

‘Of course the joy must come to you first,’ they said, laughing. ‘Then we can have a little of it through your blessing.’

‘I don’t mind the wine,’ Grandmother Jia said with a laugh. ‘It’s the joke that’s a little difficult.’

‘You have more jokes than Feng, and better ones,’ they all said. ‘Favour us with one, and let us have a laugh.’

‘I haven’t anything fresh or funny,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘I shall just have to brazen it out in my old age and tell you one.’

She began:

‘There was a family with ten sons, and they married ten wives. Only the tenth daughter-in-law was clever and quick. She had a quick mind and a ready tongue, and her parents-in-law loved her best. All day long they said the other nine were unfilial. The nine felt very badly treated, and put their heads together. “We nine are dutiful at heart,” they said. “We just haven’t that little hussy’s clever tongue. Our father-in-law and mother-in-law are old, and they only ever say how good she is. Who can we complain to about such unfairness?” The eldest had an idea. “Tomorrow let’s go to King Yama’s temple, burn incense and put our case to him. We’ll ask why, when he sent us to be born as human beings, he gave that little hussy alone a clever tongue and made the rest of us so dull.” They were all delighted and agreed that it was a very good plan. Next day they went to King Yama’s temple and burnt incense. All nine fell asleep beneath the altar table, and their nine souls waited for King Yama to arrive. They waited and waited, but he never came. Just as they were growing desperate, along came Monkey on his somersault cloud. At the sight of the nine souls he raised his gold-banded staff to strike them. Terrified, they fell on their knees and begged for mercy.

‘Monkey asked what was the matter, and the nine hastened to tell him the whole story. He stamped his foot and sighed. “Lucky you met me! Even if you waited till King Yama came, he wouldn’t know the reason.” When they heard that, they begged him, “Great Sage, have mercy on us, and all will be well!” Monkey laughed. “That’s easy enough. The day the ten of you were sent to be born, I happened to be visiting King Yama. I had a piss on the ground, and your youngest sister-in-law drank it. If you want to be clever and glib-tongued now, there’s plenty more piss. I’ll do you another lot, and you can drink that.”’

When she finished, everyone burst out laughing.

‘Well!’ Xi-feng said with a laugh. ‘Lucky we’re all so slow with our tongues, or we’d have drunk monkey’s piss too.’

You-shi and Lou-shi turned to Li Wan, laughing.

‘Who is it here who’s been drinking monkey’s piss? Don’t pretend it’s nothing to do with you.’

‘It isn’t whether a joke’s good or bad,’ Aunt Xue said with a smile. ‘If it fits the occasion, it makes you laugh.’

The drum began again. The little maids were determined to hear Xi-feng tell a joke, and quietly arranged with the storytellers that a cough should be the signal. Soon the branch had gone round twice and reached Xi-feng. The maids coughed deliberately, and the storytellers stopped.

‘Now we’ve caught her!’ everyone cried, laughing. ‘Quick, drink your wine and tell us a good one. Only don’t make us laugh till our sides ache.’

Xi-feng thought for a moment, then smiled.

‘There was another family keeping the fifteenth of the first month. The whole household was admiring the lanterns and drinking wine, and what a lively party it was! Great-grandmothers-in-law, grandmothers-in-law, mothers-in-law, daughters-in-law, grandsons’ wives, great-grandsons’ wives, grandsons, grand-nephews, great-grandsons, great-great-grandsons, grandsons dribbling on and on, granddaughters, daughters’ daughters, granddaughters related through mothers’ sisters, granddaughters related through fathers’ sisters... Oh my, oh my, what a lively party!’

They were laughing already.

‘Listen to that chatter! Who’s she making fun of now?’

‘If you start on me, I’ll tear your mouth,’ You-shi said with a laugh.

Xi-feng rose and clapped her hands, laughing.

‘Here I am taking all this trouble to tell you a story, and you keep interrupting. I shan’t tell you any more.’

‘Go on, go on,’ Grandmother Jia said, smiling. ‘What happened next?’

Xi-feng thought for a moment.

‘Next, they all sat round together in a room, drank wine all night, and then broke up.’

She said it with a perfectly stern, straight face, and added nothing. They stared at her, still waiting for the rest. It fell utterly flat.

Xiang-yun gazed at her for some time. Xi-feng smiled.

‘I’ll tell you another one about the fifteenth. Some people were carrying a firecracker as big as a house outside the city to let it off. It drew a crowd of over ten thousand who followed to watch. One impatient fellow couldn’t wait, so he secretly touched it off with a stick of incense.

‘There was just a “pfft!” Everyone roared with laughter, and the crowd broke up. The man carrying the firecracker complained that the seller hadn’t bound it tightly enough. It had broken up before they could let it off.’

‘Didn’t the man himself hear it go off?’ Xiang-yun asked.

‘The man was deaf,’ said Xi-feng.

They thought back over the story and all burst out laughing. Then, remembering the first one, which she had never finished, they asked:

‘What happened in the first story? You ought to finish that too.’

Xi-feng slapped the table.

‘What a fuss you make! Next day was the sixteenth. New Year was over, the festival was over. I was busy watching everyone clear things away, and couldn’t even keep track of that. How was I to know what happened next?’

At this they all began laughing again.

‘They’ve already sounded the fourth watch outside,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘If you ask me, Grandmother’s tired, and it’s time we followed the deaf man’s firecracker and broke up too.’

You-shi and the others held their handkerchiefs over their mouths and rocked backwards and forwards with laughter, pointing at her.

‘This creature really does know how to chatter!’

‘That Feng gets more of a chatterbox every day,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. Then she gave an order. ‘Now she’s mentioned firecrackers, let’s set off our fireworks and clear our heads of the wine.’

Jia Rong hurried out with the pages to set up screens and frames in the courtyard and hang the fireworks in readiness. These had all been sent in as tribute from various places. Though not very large, they were exquisitely made, with every sort of story represented, interspersed with coloured fireworks. Dai-yu’s constitution was delicate, and she could not bear the crackling and banging. Grandmother Jia took her in her arms. Aunt Xue held Xiang-yun.

‘I’m not afraid,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing.

‘She loves nothing better than letting off great big firecrackers herself,’ Bao-chai and the others said with a laugh. ‘As if she’d be frightened of these!’

Lady Wang drew Bao-yu into her arms.

‘Some of us have nobody to love us,’ Xi-feng said, laughing.

‘You have me,’ said You-shi with a smile. ‘I’ll hold you. Aren’t you ashamed, you great baby, wheedling again? One mention of firecrackers, and you’re showing off again today, you bee-dung eater!’

‘When we break up, let’s go and set some off in our Garden,’ Xi-feng said, laughing.

‘I can do it better than the pages.’

As they spoke, one display after another went off outside. There were plenty of smaller fireworks too: ‘Stars All over the Sky’, ‘Nine Dragons Enter the Clouds’, ‘A Clap of Thunder’, ‘Ten Reports Flying Heavenwards’ and others of the kind. When the display was over, the young actors were told to perform a round of Lotus-fall songs. Money was scattered all over the stage, and the children were made to scramble for it to amuse the company. Soup was brought again.

‘It’s a long night,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘I’m beginning to feel hungry.’

‘We have duck-meat porridge ready,’ Xi-feng replied at once.

‘I’d like something plain.’

‘There’s non-glutinous rice porridge cooked with jujubes too,’ Xi-feng said hastily. ‘It was made for the ladies who are keeping a vegetarian fast.’

Grandmother Jia smiled.

‘If it isn’t greasy, it’s sweet.’

‘There’s almond tea as well, only I’m afraid that’s sweet too.’

‘That will do rather better.’

By now the remains of the feast had been cleared away, and all kinds of delicate little dishes had been set out outside.

Everyone ate a little, just as they pleased. When they had had tea to rinse their mouths, they finally dispersed. Early on the seventeenth, they went again to Ning mansion to perform the rites, waited while the ancestral shrine was closed and the portraits put away, then returned. That day Aunt Xue’s household was giving its New Year feast. The eighteenth was Lai Da’s turn, the nineteenth Lai Sheng’s at Ning mansion, the twentieth Lin Zhi-xiao’s, the twenty-first Shan Da-liang’s and the twenty-second Wu Xin-deng’s. Grandmother Jia went to some of these houses and not to others. Sometimes she enjoyed herself so much that she stayed until everyone else had gone; sometimes she had had enough and came home after half a day or a short while. As for relatives and friends who invited her or came to dine, she met none of them, fearing the constraint. Lady Xing, Lady Wang and Xi-feng saw to all that. Even Bao-yu went nowhere except to Wang Zi-teng’s, the excuse being that Grandmother Jia had kept him at home to amuse her. It was the servants’ own households, where she could do as she pleased, whose invitations she enjoyed accepting for a little outing.

But enough of these digressions. The Lantern Festival was now over—
