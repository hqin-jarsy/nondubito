Chapter 76 At Green Crest Hall, a flute awakens feelings of desolation; At Crystal Hollow Lodge, linked verses lament loneliness

Chapter end

Jia She and Jia Zheng had left with Jia Zhen and the others; we need say no more of them. Grandmother Jia now ordered the surrounding screens removed and the two tables brought together. The women servants wiped the tables, arranged the fruit, changed the cups and washed the chopsticks, setting everything in order once more. Grandmother Jia and the rest put on extra clothes, washed, rinsed their mouths and drank some tea before returning to sit in a circle round the table. Looking about her, Grandmother Jia saw that Bao-chai and her cousin were missing. She knew they had gone home for their family celebration of the full moon. Li Wan and Xi-feng were both ill as well. With four people absent, the company seemed much quieter.

‘In other years, when your husbands were away,’ she said with a smile, ‘we simply invited Aunt Xue over, and all enjoyed the moon together. What a lively time we had! But then I would suddenly think of your husbands, and remember that mothers and sons, husbands and wives, parents and children could not all be together, and that spoiled our pleasure. Now that your husbands are here this year, we ought to be having a happy reunion, yet it isn’t convenient to ask Aunt Xue and her children over for a good talk and a laugh. Besides, there are two more in their family this year. She can hardly leave them behind and come running over here.

‘And Feng has chosen this very time to fall ill. Her talk and jokes alone would make up for ten people missing. It just shows that nothing in this world can ever be quite perfect.’

She gave an involuntary sigh, then called for a large cup and had it filled with hot wine.

‘Today you and your sons are together,’ Lady Wang said, smiling. ‘Surely that makes it happier than other years. There may have been more of us women and children before, but that was never as good as having all your own flesh and blood here, as you have this year.’

‘That’s just it,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘That’s why I’m in the mood to drink out of a large cup. You should all change to large ones too.’

Lady Xing and the others had no choice but to do so.

It was late, they were tired, and they could not hold much wine. All were beginning to feel sleepy, but Grandmother Jia’s spirits showed no sign of flagging, so they had to keep her company. She ordered woollen rugs spread on the steps and the mooncakes, watermelons and other fruit carried down, so that the maids and women servants could also sit in circles to enjoy the moon. Seeing it now in the middle of the sky, more splendid and lovely than before, she said, ‘With a moon like this, we must have a flute.’ She sent for the girls who played in the instrumental ensemble.

‘Too much music would spoil the elegance of it,’ she said. ‘Let the flute-player play at a good distance. That will be enough.’

The girl had just gone off to play when one of Lady Xing’s women came up and spoke a few words to her mistress.

‘What is it?’ Grandmother Jia asked.

‘The Elder Master tripped over a stone on his way out and twisted his leg,’ the woman replied.

Grandmother Jia at once sent two old women to see how he was and told Lady Xing to hurry along too.

Lady Xing rose to take her leave.

‘Zhen’s wife,’ Grandmother Jia added, ‘you might as well go home too. I shall be going to bed shortly.’

‘I’m not going home tonight,’ You-shi said with a smile. ‘I mean to drink with you all night, Grannie.’

‘No, no, that won’t do,’ Grandmother Jia said, laughing. ‘A young married couple like you ought to have your own reunion tonight. How can I keep you apart?’

You-shi blushed. ‘You make us sound quite disgraceful, Grannie! Young we may be, but we’ve been married over ten years and we’re nearly forty. Besides, we’re still in mourning. Staying up to amuse you is one thing. How could we go off for a reunion of our own?’

‘Quite right,’ Grandmother Jia said with a smile. ‘I’d forgotten your mourning wasn’t over. Poor man, your father-in-law has been gone more than two years, and here I am forgetting him. I deserve a whole large cup as a forfeit.

‘In that case, don’t bother to see her out. Stay here with me. Ask Rong’s wife to go with her, and she can go straight home herself.’

You-shi passed on the instruction. Rong’s wife assented and accompanied Lady Xing out to the main gate, where each got into her carriage and went home. We need say no more of them.

Grandmother Jia meanwhile took the company to admire the osmanthus for a while before they returned to the table and had fresh warm wine brought. They were chatting idly when, quite unexpectedly, a flute sounded beneath the osmanthus trees on the other side, its sobbing notes rising and falling, lingering on the air. With that bright moon, that cool breeze, that clear sky and unsullied earth, it seemed to free the heart at once of its troubles and sweep every care away. They all sat upright and still, listening in silence. It continued for about the time it takes to drink two cups of tea. When it stopped, they were full of praise. More warm wine was poured.

‘Well, is it worth listening to?’ Grandmother Jia asked with a smile.

‘It certainly is,’ they answered, smiling. ‘We should never have thought of this. We need you to lead the way, so that we too can open our hearts a little.’

‘It isn’t quite right yet. The slower the tune, the better it sounds.’

As she spoke, she took one of the palace-made mooncakes with melon-seed and rich pine-kernel filling that she herself was eating, ordered a large cup of hot wine poured, and sent both to the flute-player. The girl was to take her time over them, then play another set of tunes with care.

The women assented and had just taken them away when the two old women sent to see Jia She returned.

‘The top of his right foot is a little swollen and pale,’ they reported. ‘He’s taken some medicine now and the pain is easier. It isn’t anything very serious.’

Grandmother Jia nodded and sighed.

‘I worry about him far too much. He must insist that I’m partial, and here I am fussing over him like this.’

She told Lady Wang, You-shi and the others the joke Jia She had made earlier. They smiled and tried to reassure her.

‘Everyone was drinking and telling jokes. A thoughtless word can slip out. How would he dare to criticise you? You mustn’t take it to heart.’

Faithful brought a soft head-wrap and a large cloak.

‘It’s late. The dew will be falling, and the wind may catch your head. You ought to put these on. After a little longer, it will be time for bed.’

‘The one night I’m enjoying myself, and here you come hurrying me off!’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Do you think I’m drunk? I shall stay till daylight, just to spite you!’

She called for more wine. With the wrap round her head and the cloak about her, she drank again while the others kept her company and told amusing stories. From the shadow of the osmanthus came another slender thread of flute music, sobbing and wavering, drifting on and on. This time it was indeed more mournful than before. They all sat silent.

The night was still, the moon bright, and the flute’s voice full of sorrow. Grandmother Jia was old and had been drinking. The sound stirred her heart, and she could not keep back her tears. The others too were overcome by a sense of desolation and loneliness. Only after a while did they notice her distress. They hastily turned to her with smiles and comforting words, called for warm wine and had the flute stopped.

‘Let me try a joke as well,’ You-shi said with a smile. ‘It may cheer you up.’

‘Better still,’ Grandmother Jia answered, forcing a smile. ‘Go on, let me hear it.’

‘There was a family with four sons,’ You-shi began. ‘The eldest had only one eye, the second only one ear, and the third only one nostril. The fourth had all his parts, but he couldn’t speak.’

She had got no further when Grandmother Jia’s eyes began to close, as though she were falling asleep. You-shi stopped and, with Lady Wang, softly called her awake.

Grandmother Jia opened her eyes and smiled. ‘I’m not sleepy. I was only closing my eyes to rest them.

‘Go on talking. I’m listening.’

‘It’s already the fourth watch,’ Lady Wang and the others said with a smile. ‘The wind and dew are heavy. Do go to bed. We can admire the moon again tomorrow, on the sixteenth, and do its beauty justice then.’

‘How can it be the fourth watch already?’

‘It really is,’ Lady Wang replied, smiling. ‘The girls couldn’t stay awake any longer and have all gone to bed.’

Grandmother Jia looked carefully about her. They had indeed all left, with only Tan-chun remaining.

‘Very well, then,’ she said with a smile.

‘You aren’t used to staying up either. Besides, some are frail and some are ill. I have less to worry about with them gone. Only poor Third Girl is still waiting here. Off you go as well. We’re breaking up now.’

She rose and took a sip of plain tea. A little bamboo carrying-chair had been made ready. She sat down in it, wrapped in her cloak, and two old women lifted it, while the rest accompanied her out of the Garden. We shall leave them there.

As the women servants cleared away the dishes, cups and bowls, they found that one of the fine teacups was missing. After searching everywhere without success, the woman responsible asked the others, ‘Someone must have dropped it and broken it. Tell me where you put it, so I can hand in the pieces as proof. Otherwise they’ll say I’ve stolen it.’

‘None of us broke it,’ they all said. ‘Perhaps one of the girls’ attendants did. Think back carefully, or go and ask them.’

That reminded the woman in charge of the crockery.

‘Of course!’ she said, smiling. ‘I remember Kingfisher holding it a while ago. I’ll ask her.’

She had scarcely stepped down on to the paved path when she met Nightingale and Kingfisher coming towards her.

‘Has Her Old Ladyship finished?’ Kingfisher asked. ‘Do you know where our young ladies have gone?’

‘I’ve come to ask you where a teacup has got to, and now you want me to find your young ladies!’

‘I poured out tea for my young lady,’ Kingfisher said, laughing. ‘I only turned my head for a moment, and she’d disappeared too.’

‘Her Ladyship just said they’d all gone to bed.

‘You must have been off amusing yourself somewhere, not to know that.’

‘They couldn’t possibly have gone to bed without telling us,’ Kingfisher said to Nightingale. ‘They’ve probably been strolling somewhere. Perhaps, seeing Her Old Ladyship leave, they’ve hurried round to the front to see her home. Let’s go and look there. Once we’ve found our young ladies, your teacup will turn up too. You can ask for it first thing tomorrow. What’s the hurry?’

‘No hurry, now I know where to look,’ the woman said with a smile. ‘I’ll come to you for it tomorrow.’

She went back to count and put away the things, while Nightingale and Kingfisher made for Grandmother Jia’s rooms. We shall leave them for the moment.

Dai-yu and Xiang-yun had not, in fact, gone to bed. Seeing so many members of the Jia family gathered to enjoy the moon, yet hearing Grandmother Jia lament how few they were and how much livelier it had been in former years, then mention Bao-chai and her cousin going home to admire the moon with their mother and brothers, Dai-yu had been moved to sorrow by the scene. She went away to lean on the railing and weep. Skybright’s serious illness had lately left Bao-yu with no heart for anything, and when Lady Wang repeatedly urged him to go to bed, he had gone. Tan-chun was too troubled by the recent household affairs to have any leisure for amusement. Ying-chun and Xi-chun were there, but she had never been particularly close to either. Only Xiang-yun remained to comfort her.

‘You’re a sensible person,’ she said. ‘Why make yourself miserable like this? I’m in the same position as you, but I don’t take things so much to heart. And with all your illnesses, you ought to take better care of yourself. What annoys me is Cousin Bao-chai. All that affectionate talk, day after day! We agreed long ago that this Mid-Autumn we would all admire the moon together, revive the club and compose linked verses. Now the day has come, and she’s deserted us to enjoy the moon on her own.

‘The club has fallen apart and there’s no poetry. Instead, their fathers and sons, uncles and nephews have taken the field. Emperor Taizu of Song put it well: “How can I permit another to sleep soundly beside my bed?” If they won’t write, let’s compose linked verses ourselves and put them to shame tomorrow.’

Dai-yu could not bear to disappoint her after such spirited consolation.

‘But listen to all this noise,’ she said with a smile. ‘How could anyone find inspiration here?’

‘The moon is lovely from this hill,’ Xiang-yun replied, smiling, ‘but lovelier still beside the water. You know the pond is just at the foot of the slope, and Crystal Hollow Lodge is in the hollow, close to the water. The people who built this Garden knew what they were doing. The high part of the hill is called Green Crest, and the low hollow by the water, Crystal Hollow. The characters tu and ao, “convex” and “concave”, have very rarely been used. To use them outright as the names of buildings is all the more original, with nothing hackneyed about it. These two places, one above and one below, one bright and one dark, one high and one low, one on the hill and one by the water, must have been arranged especially for enjoying the moon.

‘Those who like the hill high and the moon small can come here. Those who like the bright moon reflected in clear water can go there. It’s only because people commonly pronounce the two characters wa and gong, “hollow” and “hump”, that they’ve been thought vulgar and seldom used. Only Lu Fang-weng used ao in his line, “An ancient inkstone, slightly hollowed, holds more ink.” And someone even criticised him for being vulgar. Isn’t that absurd?’

‘Fang-weng wasn’t the only one,’ said Dai-yu. ‘Plenty of the ancients used them. There’s Jiang Yan’s Rhapsody on Green Moss, Dongfang Shuo’s Classic of Spirits and Marvels, even the story in the Record of Paintings about Zhang Seng-you’s painting at the Temple of the One Vehicle. There are more examples than I could list. People nowadays simply don’t know them, and mistake the characters for vulgar ones. To tell you the truth, I chose those two names myself. That year, when they tested Bao-yu, some of the names he suggested were kept, some altered, and some places were left unnamed. Afterwards we all supplied names for the places that still had none, added the sources of our quotations and the positions of the buildings, and sent the whole lot in for Eldest Cousin to see. She sent them back out to be shown to my uncle. To our surprise he was delighted. “If I’d known,” he said, “I should have had all the girls suggest names that day as well. What fun that would have been!” So every name I suggested was used, without a single character changed. Let’s go and look at Crystal Hollow Lodge now.’

They went down the slope together. A single turn brought them to the edge of the pond, where a continuous bamboo railing led straight to the path towards the Lotus Pavilion. The few rooms here lay in the embrace of the hill, a retreat belonging to Green Crest Lodge. Because they stood in a hollow close to the water, the plaque over them read ‘Crystal Hollow Streamside Lodge’. The buildings were few and low, and only two old women were assigned to watch there at night. Having heard that the staff at Green Crest Lodge were on duty that evening and that they themselves were not required, the two had collected their mooncakes and fruit, together with the wine and food given them as a treat. Well fed and well drunk, they had long since put out the lights and gone to sleep.

Seeing the place dark, Xiang-yun said with a smile, ‘All the better that they’re asleep. Shall we sit here under the curved canopy and enjoy the moon beside the water?’

They sat on two spotted-bamboo stools. One bright moon shone in the sky and another in the pond, above and below vying in splendour, as though they were inside a crystal palace, in the chambers of the merfolk. A light breeze passed, wrinkling the green surface into glinting ripples. It seemed to clear the mind and cleanse the spirit.

‘How lovely it would be to sit in a boat now and drink wine,’ Xiang-yun said with a smile. ‘If this were my home, I’d get into a boat this instant.’

‘As the ancients so rightly said,’ Dai-yu answered, smiling, ‘“If everything must be perfect, where can pleasure be found?” I’d have thought this was good enough. But no, you must have a boat as well!’

‘Win Long and then covet Shu: it’s human nature,’ Xiang-yun replied, laughing. ‘The old people are quite right when they say

‘that poor families imagine everything must go as rich families wish. Tell them that the rich can’t have their own way either, and they won’t believe you. They have to experience it for themselves before they understand. Look at us. Our parents are gone, but we’re fortunate enough to live among wealth and rank. Yet how many things there are that neither of us can have as we wish.’

‘It isn’t only us,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘Grandmother, Aunt, even Bao-yu and Tan-chun, whatever the matter, great or small, whether they are right or wrong: none can have everything their own way. The same holds for all of them. How much more for you and me, guests living under another’s roof!’

Fearing that Dai-yu was about to become distressed again, Xiang-yun hastily said, ‘Enough of this idle talk. Let’s compose our verses.’

Just then they heard the lingering notes of the flute begin.

‘Grandmother and Aunt are enjoying themselves tonight,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘That flute is lovely. It will help our inspiration. We both like five-character lines, so let’s make it extended five-character regulated verse.’

‘Which rhyme group?’

‘Let’s count the upright bars of this railing, from this end to that. We’ll take whichever group the number gives us. If there are sixteen, we start again with the first group, xian.

‘Isn’t that a new way of doing it?’

‘Very original,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing.

They got up and counted from one end to the other. There were only thirteen bars.

‘Of all things, the thirteenth group, yuan!’ said Xiang-yun. ‘There are so few rhymes in it. An extended poem may be rather strained if we can’t find enough. You’ll have to give us the first line.’

‘We’ll see which of us is the stronger,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘Only we have no paper and brush to write it down.’

‘Never mind. We can write it tomorrow. I hope we still have that much wit between us.’

‘I’ll begin with a ready-made common phrase,’ said Dai-yu, and recited:

Three fives: the fifteenth, Mid-Autumn night;

Xiang-yun thought a little, then said:

Our quiet outing rivals Lantern Feast.

Across the sky the Sieve and Dipper shine;

Dai-yu smiled and continued:

Across the earth, a wealth of pipes and strings.

In many places wine-cups madly fly;

‘There’s something in that line, “In many places wine-cups madly fly”,’ Xiang-yun said with a smile. ‘It deserves a good match.’

After a moment’s thought she smiled and said:

What house has not thrown wide its windows?

A little cold: the wind cuts sharp and quick;

‘Your answering line is better than mine,’ said Dai-yu. ‘But the next is back to commonplace phrases. You ought to press on harder.’

‘We have a long poem to write and difficult rhymes,’ Xiang-yun said. ‘We need some description to fill it out. If we have any good lines, we should save them for later.’

‘And if you haven’t any good ones later, we’ll see how ashamed you feel,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. She continued:

A lovely night: the scene glows warm and bright.

They tease the yellow-haired elders vying for cakes;

‘That won’t do,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing. ‘You made it up. You’re trying to stump me with some vulgar custom.’

‘As I’ve always said, you haven’t read enough,’ Dai-yu replied with a smile. ‘Eating cakes is an old allusion. Read the Tang history and its treatises before you argue.’

‘You won’t stump me with that,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing. ‘I have a line too.’

She went on:

And laugh at dark-haired girls dividing melons.

New fragrance flourishes in jade cassia;

‘Now you really did invent “Dividing melons”,’ Dai-yu said with a smile.

‘Tomorrow we can both look it up and show everyone,’ Xiang-yun replied, laughing. ‘Don’t waste time now.’

‘Even so, the next line isn’t good either. You needn’t fall back on “jade cassia”, “golden orchids” and such phrases just to fill your turn.’

Dai-yu continued:

Strong colour thrives in golden day-lilies.

Candles light up the precious banquet;

‘“Golden day-lilies” came cheaply to you,’ Xiang-yun said, laughing. ‘What a lot of effort they saved! You’ve landed such a ready-made rhyme. Still, you needn’t sing their august praises for them. And your next line is just filling your turn too.’

‘If you hadn’t said “jade cassia”,’ Dai-yu said with a smile, ‘would I have forced in “golden day-lilies” to match it? Besides, we do need some splendid description. That is what the actual scene calls for.’

Xiang-yun could only continue:

Cups and counters mingle in the splendid garden.

Divided into sides, all honour one commander;

‘That second line is good,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘Only rather hard to match.’

She thought for a moment, then continued:

Guessing the hidden thing, they heed three calls.

The dice show winning colours in red dots;

‘“Three calls” is amusing,’ Xiang-yun said with a smile. ‘You’ve made something elegant out of the commonplace. But then you’re back to dice in your next line.’

She had to continue:

The flower goes round to drums that din too loud.

Clear light sways over courts and buildings;

‘Your answer is good,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘But you’ve slipped away again in the next line. Always filling your turn with wind and moonlight!’

‘We haven’t actually come to the moon yet,’ Xiang-yun replied. ‘We need a little of it to keep to our subject.’

‘We’ll let it stand for now and think it over tomorrow,’ said Dai-yu. She continued:

Pale radiance joins the heavens to the earth.

Rewards and penalties spare neither guest nor host;

‘Why go back to them?’ said Xiang-yun. ‘Better to speak of ourselves.’

Still, she had to continue:

Our verses show which takes the higher place.

Sometimes we lean on rails to shape a thought;

‘Now you and I can come into it,’ Dai-yu said, and continued:

Or rest against a door to frame the scene.

The wine is gone, but feeling lingers still;

‘It’s time for that,’ said Xiang-yun. She continued:

The watch runs out, its pleasures now forgotten.

Gradually talk and laughter fall to silence;

‘Now each step is harder than the last,’ Dai-yu said. She continued:

Only the traces, white as snow and frost, remain.

On dew-wet steps the morning mushrooms cluster;

‘How am I to find a rhyme for that?’ Xiang-yun said with a smile. ‘Let me think.’

She stood up with her hands behind her back. After a little thought she laughed.

‘Got it! Luckily I’ve thought of a word. You nearly beat me.’

She continued:

Courtyard mist enfolds the dusk-closed silk tree.

The autumn torrent pours out marrow of stone;

Dai-yu sprang up too, unable to keep from crying out in admiration.

‘You little rogue! You really were saving your good lines. And to bring out hun, “silk tree”, now! How did you think of it?’

‘I happened to come across the character yesterday in an anthology of writing from successive dynasties,’ Xiang-yun said. ‘I didn’t know what tree it was, so I was going to look it up. Cousin Bao-chai said there was no need: it was what people now call the tree that opens by day and closes by night. I wasn’t quite convinced and looked it up all the same. She was right. She really does know a great deal.’

‘“Silk tree” is particularly apt at this time of night,’ Dai-yu said with a smile, ‘but that’s the least of it. How did you ever think of “The autumn torrent”? That one line puts all the others in the shade. I shall have to rouse myself to find a match, but I can’t possibly do anything like that.’

She thought a little, then said:

Wind-blown leaves heap on rocks, the roots of cloud.

The precious Maiden star keeps pure and lone;

‘That’s quite a good match,’ said Xiang-yun. ‘Only you’ve slipped away in your next line too. Luckily it puts feeling into the scene. You aren’t merely filling your turn with the “precious Maiden”.’

She continued:

The silver toad breathes vapour in and out.

The spirit hare pounds medicine in the moon;

Dai-yu nodded without speaking. After a long pause she recited:

One hastens to the Palace of Broad Cold.

Past the Dipper, invite the Herd-boy and the Weaver;

Xiang-yun too looked up at the moon and nodded, then continued:

Aboard a raft, await Heaven’s granddaughter.

Waxing or waning, the round orb never settles;

‘Back to metaphor and suggestion,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. She continued:

At the month’s dark end and birth, its spirit alone remains.

The water-clock’s last drops fall almost silent;

Xiang-yun was about to answer when Dai-yu pointed out a dark shape in the pond.

‘Look! Doesn’t it seem as though someone has gone into the shadows there in the water? Could it be a ghost?’

‘Seeing ghosts again!’ Xiang-yun said, laughing. ‘I’m not afraid of ghosts. Wait till I give it a knock.’

She bent down, picked up a small flat stone and threw it into the pond. It struck the water with a splash. A great ring spread, breaking up the moon’s reflection and letting it gather again, over and over. From the dark came a harsh cry, and a large white crane rose into the air and flew straight towards the Lotus Pavilion.

‘So that was it!’ Dai-yu said, laughing. ‘It never occurred to me for a moment. It gave me quite a fright.’

‘What a useful crane,’ Xiang-yun said with a smile. ‘It’s come to help me.’

She continued:

The flame behind the window has grown dim.

Across the cold pond passes a crane’s shadow;

Dai-yu cried out in admiration and stamped her foot.

‘Extraordinary! That crane really did come to help her!

‘This is better even than “The autumn torrent”. Whatever can I put against it? There’s only “soul” to answer “shadow”. And “Across the cold pond passes a crane”: how natural it is, how ready to hand, how full of the scene and yet how new! I shall have to lay down my brush.’

‘Think carefully and it will come,’ Xiang-yun said with a smile. ‘Or we can leave it and go on tomorrow.’

Dai-yu merely stared at the sky and took no notice. At last she suddenly smiled.

‘You needn’t boast. I’ve got one too. Listen.’

She answered:

The chill moon buries a flower’s soul.

Xiang-yun clapped her hands in praise.

‘It really is superb! Nothing else could answer it. What a phrase, “buries a flower’s soul”!’

Then she sighed. ‘The poetry is certainly original, but it’s too dejected. You’re ill already. You shouldn’t write anything so eerily exquisite and strange.’

‘How else could I outdo you?’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘I haven’t found the next line yet. All my effort went into this one.’

She had scarcely finished when someone stepped from behind the rocks beyond the railing, saying with a smile, ‘Fine poetry, fine poetry! But indeed too desolate. You needn’t go on. If you continue in the same way, it will only take the distinction from those two lines and seem strained and overdone.’

The two girls were startled by the unexpected voice. When they looked closely, it was none other than Adamantina. Both were astonished.

‘How did you come to be here?’ they asked.

‘I heard you all enjoying the moon, and such lovely flute music,’ Adamantina said with a smile. ‘I came out myself to admire the clear pond and the bright moon. My feet brought me this way, and suddenly I heard you two composing linked verses. It was so wonderfully refined that I stopped to listen. Some of the lines I heard just now are good, but too full of despondency and grief. Such things have a connection with a person’s allotted destiny, which is why I came out to stop you. Grandmother’s party broke up long ago. Everyone in the Garden must be sound asleep by now, and who knows where your maids are looking for you?

‘Aren’t you afraid of catching cold? Come along with me and have a cup of tea at my place. It will probably be daylight soon.’

‘Who would have thought it was so late?’ Dai-yu said with a smile.

The three went together to Green Bower Hermitage. The shrine lamp still burned blue, and the incense in the burner was not yet spent. The old women were all asleep. Only a little maid remained, nodding on a meditation cushion. Adamantina woke her and sent her to brew fresh tea.

Suddenly there was a knock at the door. The little maid hurried to open it and found Nightingale and Kingfisher, with several old women, looking for the two girls. Coming in and seeing them drinking tea, they all laughed.

‘What a search you’ve given us! We’ve been all over the Garden, even to Aunt Xue’s. When we finally reached the little pavilion at the foot of the hill, the night-watch women there had just woken up. We asked them, and they said two people had been talking under the canopy outside, then a third had joined them, and they’d heard them say they were all going to the hermitage. Then we knew it must be here.’

Adamantina at once told the little maid to take them to sit and rest in the other room and give them tea. She herself fetched brush, inkstone, paper and ink, and had the two girls recite their poem while she wrote it down from the beginning. Seeing her in such high spirits, Dai-yu smiled.

‘I’ve never seen you enjoying yourself so much. I’ve never dared presume to ask your advice. Would this be fit for you to look over? If it’s worthless, burn it. If there’s anything that can be put right, please correct it for us.’

‘I wouldn’t venture a rash opinion,’ Adamantina said with a smile.

‘Only there are just twenty-two rhymes so far. It strikes me that you’ve both produced your most arresting lines, and if you go on, you may not have the strength to sustain it. I’d like to tack on a dog’s tail to your sable, but I’m afraid of spoiling it.’

Dai-yu had never seen any of Adamantina’s poetry. Finding her so eager now, she hastened to say, ‘If you really would, then poor as ours is, yours might carry it along and make it good.’

‘To end it now,’ Adamantina said, ‘we really ought to return to our true selves. If we keep abandoning genuine feeling and actual experience to hunt for oddities and marvels, we shall lose the character of young women in the inner apartments, for one thing, and stray from our subject, for another.’

Both agreed that she was quite right. Adamantina took up the brush and wrote at one sweep, then handed the sheet to them.

‘Don’t laugh at me. I think it needs this to turn it round. Then the sorrowful lines earlier on won’t matter so much.’

They took it and read her continuation:

Incense-script fades in the golden tripod;

Ice-like ointment coats the jade basin.

The flute swells a widow’s weeping;

A maid is asked to warm the quilt.

Patterned phoenixes hang on empty bed-curtains;

An idle screen conceals its coloured mandarin ducks.

Thick dew makes the moss more slippery;

Heavy frost makes bamboo hard to grasp.

Still we walk beside the winding pool;

Again we climb the lonely open ground.

Strange rocks: spirits and demons wrestling;

Grotesque trees: tigers and wolves crouching.

Morning light comes through massive carved supports;

Dawn’s dew gathers on openwork screens.

Birds in a thousand trees set the woods astir;

One gibbon’s cry sounds through the valley.

Knowing the forks, how could we forget the path?

Knowing the spring, we need not ask its source.

A bell rings out at Green Bower Temple;

Cocks crow in Sweet-rice Village.

With pleasure here, why let grief follow?

Without sorrow, why should thoughts be troubled?

Tender feelings serve for our own solace;

To whom can we confide these finer joys?

Though up till dawn, speak not of weariness;

Brew tea, and let us talk more closely still.

Beneath it she had written: ‘Linked verses on the scene in Prospect Garden on Mid-Autumn night: thirty-five rhymes.’

Dai-yu and Xiang-yun were full of admiration.

‘We’ve been looking far afield every day and neglecting what was close at hand. Here we have an immortal poet, and all we’ve done is talk of battles on paper!’

‘We can polish it tomorrow,’ Adamantina said with a smile. ‘It must be nearly daylight. We really ought to get some rest.’

The two girls rose to take their leave and went out with their maids. Adamantina accompanied them to the gate and watched them into the distance before closing it and going inside. We shall leave her there.

‘There are still people waiting up for us at Mrs Zhu’s,’ Kingfisher said to Xiang-yun. ‘Where would you like to sleep now?’

‘Tell them to go to bed as you pass,’ Xiang-yun answered with a smile. ‘If I go back, I’m sure to disturb her when she’s ill. Better to bother Miss Lin for the rest of the night.’

They went on to Naiad’s House, where half the servants had already gone to sleep. The two girls went in, removed their ornaments and outer clothes, washed and rinsed their mouths, and at last lay down to rest.

Nightingale lowered the gauze bed-curtains, moved the lamp and went out, closing the door. But Xiang-yun could never sleep easily in an unfamiliar bed. Though her head was on the pillow, sleep would not come. Dai-yu, whose heart-blood was deficient and who often suffered from sleeplessness, had stayed awake past the moment when she was drowsy, and naturally could not sleep either. The two tossed and turned on their pillows.

‘Why aren’t you asleep yet?’ Dai-yu asked.

‘I can’t sleep in a strange bed,’ Xiang-yun answered with a faint smile. ‘And I’ve gone past being sleepy. I shall just have to lie here. Why can’t you sleep either?’

‘This is nothing new for me,’ Dai-yu said with a sigh. ‘In a whole year, I doubt I get as many as ten proper nights’ sleep altogether.’

‘It’s all because of your illness,’ Xiang-yun said. ‘That’s why…’
