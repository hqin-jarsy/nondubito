Chapter 38 Lin the Naiad wins the chrysanthemum contest; Xue of Allspice answers the crab poems with satire

Chapter end

Bao-chai and Xiang-yun had settled their plans; nothing more need be said of that night. Next day Xiang-yun invited Grandmother Jia and the others to admire the osmanthus.

‘She is so keen,’ they all said, ‘we must let her entertain us.’

At noon Grandmother Jia duly came into the garden with Lady Wang and Xi-feng, having invited Aunt Xue and the others to join them.

‘Where shall we go?’ she asked.

‘Wherever you would like, Mother,’ said Lady Wang.

‘Everything is laid out at Lotus Pavilion,’ said Xi-feng. ‘The two osmanthus trees below the slope are flowering beautifully, and the water is so clear and green. Sitting in the pavilion out over the water, we shall have an open view all round. Even looking at the water will freshen our eyes.’

‘Quite right,’ said Grandmother Jia, and led them towards Lotus Pavilion.

The pavilion stood in the pool, with windows on all four sides. Winding covered walks to left and right crossed the water to the banks, and a zigzag bamboo bridge made a secluded approach from behind. As they stepped on to this bridge, Xi-feng hurried forward to support Grandmother Jia.

‘Step out boldly, Grannie. There’s nothing to worry about. Bamboo bridges always creak and crack like this.’

Once inside, they saw two bamboo tables set beyond the balustrade. One held cups, chopsticks and the wine service; on the other were a tea whisk, tea bowls and all the other tea things. Two or three maids were fanning a little stove to boil the tea, while several others tended another stove for warming the wine.

‘How thoughtful, to have arranged for tea,’ Grandmother Jia said delightedly. ‘And both the place and all the things are so clean.’

‘Cousin Bao-chai helped me get them ready,’ Xiang-yun said with a smile.

‘I always say that child is thorough. She thinks of everything.’

As she spoke, Grandmother Jia noticed the paired black-lacquer panels, inlaid with mother-of-pearl, hanging on the pillars, and asked for them to be read aloud. Xiang-yun read:

Returning orchid-oars break the lotus shadows;

Deep in the scent of caltrop and lotus, a bamboo bridge is drawn.

Grandmother Jia looked up at the nameboard, then turned to Aunt Xue.

‘When I was little, we had a pavilion just like this at home. It was called Rosy-cloud Pavilion, or something of the sort. I was about their age then, and my sisters and I used to go there to play every day. Once I lost my footing and fell in. I very nearly drowned. They got me out at last, but I struck my head on a wooden peg and cut it open. That hollow at my temple, about the size of a fingertip, is where it was injured. Everyone was afraid of my having been in the water, and afraid I might have caught a chill as well. They all said I wouldn’t live. But here I am.’

Before anyone else could speak, Xi-feng put in, laughing, ‘If you hadn’t lived, who would be enjoying all these blessings now?

‘That only shows what a store of long life and good fortune you had from the very beginning, Grannie. The spirits arranged for that hollow to be knocked into your head to hold it all. The God of Longevity had a hollow in his forehead to begin with too. It was only after he had filled it with ten thousand blessings and ten thousand years of life that it bulged out into a bump.’

Before she had finished, Grandmother Jia and the others were helpless with laughter.

‘You monkey! You’re spoilt beyond bearing,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘Now you’ve taken to making fun of me. I could tear that glib mouth of yours!’

‘I was afraid the crabs might leave you with a chill inside,’ said Xi-feng. ‘I thought I’d make you laugh and cheer you up. When you’re in good spirits, another crab or two won’t hurt you.’

‘Then you shall stay with me day and night from now on,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘I shall have a good laugh often and keep my spirits up. You shan’t go home.’

‘It’s because you’re so fond of her that she’s grown so spoilt,’ Lady Wang said, smiling. ‘Say that, and tomorrow she’ll be more disrespectful than ever.’

‘I like her as she is,’ said Grandmother Jia. ‘Besides, she’s not a child who doesn’t know where to draw the line.

‘When we’re at home with no outsiders, this is how the women of a family ought to be together. So long as she observes the proper courtesies, that is enough. Why make her stand about like an attendant to a god?’

They went on into the pavilion. Tea was served, and Xi-feng busied herself arranging tables and calling for cups and chopsticks. At the table in the place of honour sat Grandmother Jia, Aunt Xue, Bao-chai, Dai-yu and Bao-yu. Xiang-yun, Lady Wang, Ying-chun, Tan-chun and Xi-chun sat at the table on the east. The table by the door to the west was for Li Wan and Xi-feng, but their seats were left empty. Neither ventured to sit; they attended to Grandmother Jia’s and Lady Wang’s tables instead.

‘Don’t bring too many crabs at once,’ Xi-feng instructed. ‘Keep them in the steamer. Bring ten, and when we’ve eaten those, bring some more.’

She called for water, washed her hands, and stood beside Grandmother Jia to pick out crab meat. The first portion she offered to Aunt Xue.

‘I enjoy it more when I break them open myself,’ Aunt Xue said. ‘You needn’t serve me.’

Xi-feng gave it to Grandmother Jia instead. Her second portion went to Bao-yu.

‘Bring the wine good and hot,’ she said, and sent the little maids for mung-bean meal scented with chrysanthemum leaves and osmanthus flowers, to have ready for washing their hands.

After eating one crab with the company, Xiang-yun left her seat to see to her guests. She went outside and told the servants to take a plateful each to Aunt Zhao and Aunt Zhou. Xi-feng came over to her.

‘You’re not used to looking after a party. Go and eat. I’ll see to things for you now, and have mine when everyone has gone.’

Xiang-yun would not agree. She ordered two more tables to be set in the covered walk and invited Faithful, Amber, Cai-xia, Cai-yun and Patience to sit down there.

‘If you’re staying here to wait on them, Mrs Lian,’ Faithful said to Xi-feng with a smile, ‘we shall go and eat.’

‘Go along, all of you. Leave everything to me.’

Xiang-yun returned to her seat. Xi-feng and Li Wan also ate a little for form’s sake, but Xi-feng was soon up and busy again. Presently she came out into the covered walk. Faithful and the others, who were thoroughly enjoying their meal, rose when they saw her.

‘What have you come out again for, ma’am?

‘Do let us enjoy ourselves for a moment.’

‘You little baggage, Faithful! You get worse and worse,’ Xi-feng said, laughing. ‘Here I am doing your work for you, and instead of being grateful, you complain. Pour me a cup of wine at once.’

Faithful laughed and filled a cup, holding it to Xi-feng’s lips. Xi-feng tipped back her head and drank it. Patience had already picked a shellful of roe for her.

‘Plenty of ginger and vinegar,’ said Xi-feng.

She ate it, then smiled at them. ‘Sit down and eat, all of you. I’m going now.’

‘What a cheek, coming to eat our food!’ said Faithful.

‘Don’t you play your tricks on me. Do you know your Master Lian has taken a fancy to you? He means to ask Grandmother for you as his little second wife.’

‘Pah! What a thing for a mistress to say! If I don’t smear these fishy hands all over your face, my name’s not Faithful.’

She ran at her with her hands out.

‘Dear Faithful, let me off just this once,’ Xi-feng pleaded.

‘If Faithful did go to him, would Patience let her off?’ Amber said, laughing.

‘Look at her. She hasn’t eaten two crabs, but she’s swallowed a whole dish of vinegar. And we’re to think she doesn’t know how to be jealous!’

Patience was just breaking open a crab full of roe. At this mockery she thrust it towards Amber’s face.

‘You little gossiping baggage!’ she scolded, laughing.

Amber laughed too and dodged aside. Patience missed her, stumbled forward, and smeared it neatly across Xi-feng’s cheek. Xi-feng, who was still teasing Faithful, gave a startled ‘Oh!’ Everyone burst out laughing. Xi-feng herself could not help laughing as she swore at Patience.

‘You damned whore! Have you eaten your eyes out? Smearing your mother like that!’

Patience hurried to wipe her face, then went to fetch water herself.

‘Bless the Buddha,’ said Faithful. ‘Retribution!’

Hearing the noise, Grandmother Jia kept calling from her table, ‘What have you seen that’s so funny? Tell us, so that we can laugh too.’

Faithful and the others called back merrily, ‘Mrs Lian came to steal our crabs. Patience lost her temper and smeared her mistress’s face with crab roe. Mistress and maid are fighting!’

Grandmother Jia, Lady Wang and the rest laughed too.

‘Have pity on the poor thing,’ Grandmother Jia said. ‘Give her a few little legs and belly-flaps to eat, and have done with it.’

Laughing, Faithful and the others agreed.

‘There’s a whole tableful of legs here, Mrs Lian,’ they called. ‘Help yourself!’

Xi-feng washed her face and came back to attend to Grandmother Jia and the others for a while longer. Dai-yu alone did not dare eat much. She had a little meat from a claw, then left the table.

Only when Grandmother Jia had finished did everyone else leave their seats. They all washed their hands and spent a little time enjoying themselves, some admiring the flowers, others dabbling in the water and watching the fish. Lady Wang said to Grandmother Jia:

‘It’s windy here, and you’ve just been eating crabs. Hadn’t you better go back to your room for a rest? If you feel like it, we can come again tomorrow.’

‘Yes, indeed,’ said Grandmother Jia, smiling. ‘I thought you were all enjoying yourselves, and I was afraid I should spoil your pleasure if I went. Since you say so, let us go.’

She turned to Xiang-yun. ‘Don’t let your Cousin Bao-yu or your Cousin Dai-yu eat too much.’

Xiang-yun promised.

‘And neither of you two must eat too much either,’ Grandmother Jia added to her and Bao-chai. ‘They taste good, but they’re not very good for you. Too many will give you a stomach-ache.’

They both readily agreed and saw her out of the garden. When they returned, they ordered the remains of the meal to be cleared and the tables laid afresh.

‘We needn’t have them laid,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Let’s write our poems. Put the big round table in the middle, with all the food and wine on it. There’s no need for fixed places. Anyone who wants something can go and eat. Wouldn’t it be more comfortable to sit wherever we please?’

‘Much better,’ said Bao-chai.

‘That’s all very well,’ said Xiang-yun, ‘but there are other people to think of.’

She had another table laid, with hot crabs, and invited Aroma, Nightingale, Chess, Scribe, Picture, Oriole, Ebony and the others to sit down together. Two patterned felt rugs were spread under the osmanthus trees on the slope for the waiting women and little maids. They were told to sit down and eat and drink as much as they liked, and come when they were called.

Xiang-yun took out the list of subjects and pinned it to the wall with a needle. They all examined it.

‘Novel enough, certainly. But I’m afraid we shan’t be able to write anything.’

Xiang-yun explained why they had not prescribed a rhyme. Bao-yu approved.

‘That’s how it ought to be. I dislike being given a rhyme too.’

Dai-yu, who drank very little and was eating no more crab, had an embroidered stool brought to the balustrade and sat down with a fishing rod. Bao-chai played for a while with a sprig of osmanthus, then leaned over the window ledge, picking off the little flowers and dropping them on to the water. Fish rose, their mouths working at the surface. Xiang-yun would lose herself in thought for a moment, then urge Aroma and her companions to help themselves, or call to the people on the slope to eat their fill. Tan-chun, Li Wan and Xi-chun stood in the shade of a weeping willow, watching the gulls and egrets. Ying-chun sat alone in the shadow of the flowers, threading jasmine blossoms with a needle. Bao-yu watched Dai-yu fishing for a while, leaned down beside Bao-chai for a word or two of banter, then went to see how Aroma and the others were getting on with their crabs, and drank a little wine with them.

Aroma picked out a shellful of meat for him too.

Dai-yu put down her fishing rod and came over to the table. She picked up the blackened-silver wine pot with its plum-blossom design and chose a tiny banana-leaf cup of begonia-pink translucent stone. Seeing that she wanted wine, a maid hurried over to pour it.

‘Go on with your meal,’ Dai-yu said. ‘Let me pour my own. It’s more fun.’

She filled the cup halfway, but saw that it was rice wine.

‘I’ve had a little crab and I feel a slight pain at the pit of my stomach. I need a sip of spirits, really hot.’

‘There are spirits,’ Bao-yu said at once, and ordered a pot of the wine steeped with silk-tree flowers to be heated. Dai-yu took only one sip and put it down. Bao-chai came over, took another cup and had a sip too. Then she dipped a brush in ink, went to the wall and marked the first subject, ‘Remembering Chrysanthemums’, adding the character Heng beneath it.

‘Dear Cousin, I’ve already got four lines for the second one,’ Bao-yu said hastily. ‘Let me have that.’

‘I’ve only just managed one poem, and you’re in such a hurry already!’ said Bao-chai, smiling.

Without a word, Dai-yu took the brush, marked the eighth subject, ‘Questioning Chrysanthemums’, and then the eleventh, ‘Chrysanthemum Dreams’, adding the character Xiao.

Bao-yu took the brush too, marked the second subject, ‘Seeking Chrysanthemums’, and added Jiang. Tan-chun came to look.

‘No one’s taken “Wearing Chrysanthemums”. I’ll do that.’

She pointed at Bao-yu, smiling. ‘It has just been proclaimed that no words about women’s chambers are allowed. You’d better be careful.’

Just then Xiang-yun came over and marked the fourth and fifth subjects, ‘Facing Chrysanthemums’ and ‘Chrysanthemums in a Vase’, adding the character Xiang.

‘You ought to have a pen-name too,’ said Tan-chun.

‘There are several halls and pavilions at home,’ Xiang-yun said, ‘but I don’t live in any of them. There would be no fun in borrowing their names.’

‘Grandmother was just saying your family had a waterside pavilion called Rosy-cloud Pavilion,’ said Bao-chai. ‘Surely that belongs to you. It may be gone now, but you’re still one of its old owners.’

They all agreed. Before Xiang-yun could do anything, Bao-yu struck out Xiang for her and wrote Xia instead.1

In the time it would have taken to eat a meal, all twelve subjects were finished. Each writer made a fair copy and handed it to Ying-chun. A fresh sheet of Snow-wave paper was brought, and all the poems were copied on to it together, with each writer’s pen-name beneath the appropriate title. Li Wan and the others began reading from the top:

Remembering Chrysanthemums

Lady of Allspice

I gaze into the west wind, weighed down with thoughts, When knotweed reddens, reeds turn white, and hearts are broken.

By empty fence and old flower-bed, autumn has left no trace; In dreams I know them, under the slender moon and clear frost.

My thoughts follow the wild geese on their distant homeward flight; I sit alone, lost in the sound of evening washing-blocks.

Who will pity my sickness for the yellow flowers, And comfort me: the Double Ninth will bring our meeting?

Seeking Chrysanthemums

Young Master of Green Delights

At leisure I venture out in the clear, frosty weather; Neither wine cup nor medicine bowl shall keep me here.

Who grows them, under the moon, before the frost? Where is their autumn, beyond the rail, beside the fence?

I come far in waxed clogs, intent upon my quest; My cool chant never ends, my pleasure stretches on.

If yellow flowers can pity one who writes them poems, Let today’s wine, slung on my staff, not go to waste.

Planting Chrysanthemums

Young Master of Green Delights

Hoe in hand, I transplant them from an autumn garden, Setting them out with care before the court, beside the fence.

Last night, to my surprise, the rain revived them; This morning I rejoice to see them flowering in frost.

A thousand poems chant coolly of the autumn colours; One cup of wine, poured out in drunken tribute to cold fragrance.

With spring water and packed earth I tend them faithfully; Let them know these paths beside the well are free of dust.

Facing Chrysanthemums

Old Friend of Rosy Clouds

Brought from another garden, dearer than gold, One clump is pale in colour, another deep.

Bareheaded, by the sparse fence I sit, Clasping my knees and chanting in the cool, clear scent.

Count all the others: none disdains the world as you do; Look round: no one but I can understand your heart.

Do not let autumn’s light slip by to no purpose; Face to face, we should treasure each brief moment.

Chrysanthemums in a Vase

Old Friend of Rosy Clouds

What happy company for qin and wine! Slender on my table, they grace its quietness.

Across the seat drifts fragrance from the dew-wet three paths; I put my book aside and face one spray of autumn.2

In clear frost, new dreams visit the paper-curtained bed; In the cold garden at sunset, I recall old wanderings.

We share one temper, and so disdain the world; Peach and plum in the spring wind could not detain us.

Singing of Chrysanthemums

Naiad

The relentless demon of poetry besets me night and morning; Round the fence, against a rock, I murmur to myself.

Beauty gathers at my brush-tip as I write facing the frost; With fragrance held between my teeth, I chant to the moon.

Pitying myself, I fill the page with autumn grief; Who can read these few words and know the autumn heart?

Since Magistrate Tao first weighed your worth, Your noble spirit has been praised through all the ages to this day.

Painting Chrysanthemums

Lady of Allspice

After poems, I play with the brush, heedless in my ardour; Is painting a thing to be toiled at and reckoned out?

Gathered leaves, splashed into a thousand dots of ink; Clustered flowers, tinted into a few traces of frost.

In pale and dark I catch their spirit, their shadows in the wind; Beneath my bracelet-wearing wrist, autumn fragrance rises.3

Do not mistake them for flowers idly picked by the eastern fence; Pasted on a screen, they shall bring comfort at the Double Ninth.

Questioning Chrysanthemums

Naiad

To ask of autumn’s feelings, which no one else can know, Hands behind my back, I murmur at the eastern fence.

Proud in your lonely distinction, with whom do you withdraw? A flower like any other, why do you bloom so late?

How lonely, the garden dew, the frost upon the court! With geese returning and crickets ailing, do you long for someone?

Do not say there is no one in the world to talk to; If you understand speech, why not speak a little now?

Wearing Chrysanthemums

Guest beneath the Plantain

Each day, busy planting by the fence, arranging in a vase; Do not think I pluck them to adorn myself before a mirror.

The young lord of Chang’an had a passion for flowers; The gentleman of Pengze was mad for wine.

Dew from the three paths coolly wets my short temple-locks; Deep autumn’s frost scents my kudzu headcloth.

Such high feeling finds no favour with the world today; Let them clap their hands and laugh beside the road.

Chrysanthemum Shadows

Old Friend of Rosy Clouds

Layer upon layer, fold upon fold, in autumn’s light, They cross unseen, steal softly through the three paths.

Dim lamplight through a window draws them near and far; The fence sifts broken moonlight, holding their delicate tracery.

Cold flowers leave their image: surely the soul remains; Frost-prints convey their spirit, and even dreams are empty.

Treasure the hidden fragrance, do not trample it to pieces; Whose drunken eyes can make out all that dimness?

Chrysanthemum Dreams

Naiad

By the fence, in autumn’s languor, I sleep in peace; With cloud for company and moon beside me, all grows indistinct.

I rise among immortals, not in envy of Zhuang’s butterfly; Remembering the past, I seek my pact with Magistrate Tao.4

In sleep my lingering thoughts break off with the departing geese; Woken with a start, I fret at the crickets’ wilful cries.

Awake, to whom can I tell my hidden sorrow? Withered grass and cold mist hold unending feeling.

Fading Chrysanthemums

Guest beneath the Plantain

Dew gathers, frost weighs down; slowly they droop and lean; The feast of admiration is past: it is Lesser Snow.

The stems still hold some fragrance, the gold is faint; No whole leaf on the branches, their green hangs tattered.

A sinking moon lights half the bed, the crickets sound sick; Beneath cold clouds for ten thousand li the geese fly slowly.

Next year’s autumn wind will bring us together again; For this brief parting, let us not pine for one another.

They praised each poem as they read it, never tiring of complimenting one another.

‘Let me judge impartially,’ Li Wan said, smiling. ‘Taken as a whole, each of you has some striking lines. My impartial verdict today is: “Singing of Chrysanthemums” first, “Questioning Chrysanthemums” second, and “Chrysanthemum Dreams” third. The subjects are fresh, the poems fresh, and the conception fresher still. You mustn’t be angry if I name Naiad the winner. After those come “Wearing Chrysanthemums”, “Facing Chrysanthemums”, “Chrysanthemums in a Vase”, “Painting Chrysanthemums” and “Remembering Chrysanthemums”.’

Bao-yu clapped his hands delightedly. ‘Exactly! Perfectly fair!’

‘Mine isn’t good either,’ said Dai-yu. ‘It is rather too finely wrought.’

‘But the artfulness works,’ said Li Wan. ‘There’s no sign of anything laboured or piled up.’

‘To my mind,’ said Dai-yu, ‘the finest line of all is “In the cold garden at sunset, I recall old wanderings”. It applies the colour from the reverse side.

‘“I put my book aside and face one spray of autumn” is already marvellous. It has said everything about having chrysanthemums in a vase; there is nowhere else to go. So the poem turns back and thinks of them before they were picked and brought indoors. That takes the thought so much deeper.’

‘True,’ said Li Wan, smiling. ‘But your line about “fragrance held between my teeth” is a match for it.’

‘For steadiness of touch, it is still Lady of Allspice,’ said Tan-chun. ‘“Autumn has left no trace” and “in dreams I know them” bring the remembering out beautifully.’

‘And your “coolly wets my short temple-locks” and “scents my kudzu headcloth” leave nothing unsaid about wearing chrysanthemums,’ Bao-chai replied with a smile.

‘“With whom do you withdraw?” and “why do you bloom so late?”’ said Xiang-yun. ‘You really have questioned the chrysanthemums until they have no answer left.’

‘As for your “bareheaded … I sit” and “clasping my knees and chanting”,’ Li Wan said, laughing, ‘you won’t leave them alone for a moment. If chrysanthemums had feelings, they would certainly be sick of you.’

They all laughed.

‘I’ve failed the examination again,’ said Bao-yu. ‘Aren’t “who grows them”, “where is their autumn”, “I come far in waxed clogs” and “my cool chant never ends” all about seeking them? And aren’t “last night’s rain” and “this morning’s frost” about planting them?

‘My only grievance is that they can’t compete with “With fragrance held between my teeth, I chant to the moon”, “Clasping my knees and chanting in the cool, clear scent”, “short temple-locks”, “kudzu headcloth”, “the gold is faint”, “their green hangs tattered”, “autumn has left no trace” and “in dreams I know them”.’

‘When I have a little leisure,’ he added, ‘I’ll write all twelve by myself.’

‘Yours are good too,’ said Li Wan. ‘Only not quite so fresh and ingenious as those particular lines.’

After discussing the poems a little longer, they called for more hot crabs and ate together at the big round table.

‘Crab claws in hand, admiring the osmanthus: this deserves a poem too,’ Bao-yu said, smiling. ‘I’ve already made one. Who else dares try?’

He hurried to wash his hands, took up a brush and wrote it out. They read:

Claw in hand, I relish the cool osmanthus shade; Splashing vinegar, pounding ginger, I am wild with pleasure.

The gluttonous young lord must have his wine; The young lord who scuttles sideways has no guts.

Greed forgets the chill stored in the belly; The fingers’ fishy smell stays fragrant even after washing.

You exist to please the world’s tongues and stomachs; The immortal of Dongpo laughed at a whole life spent so busily.

‘I could give you a hundred poems like that,’ Dai-yu said, laughing.

‘You’ve exhausted your powers for the moment,’ Bao-yu retorted. ‘Instead of admitting you can’t write one, you disparage mine.’

Dai-yu said nothing. Without pausing to think, she took the brush and wrote a poem at a stroke. They read:

Iron armour, long spears: not forgotten even in death; Heaped on a plate, your colour and form tempt me to taste you first.

Each pair of claws is sealed full of tender jade; The bulging shells hold fragrant lumps of red fat.

For all their meat, I love your eight legs the more; Who will urge a thousand cups on me to heighten my delight?

With this fine food I honour this fine festival, As osmanthus brushes the clear wind and chrysanthemums bear frost.

Bao-yu was still applauding when Dai-yu tore up the paper and told someone to burn it.

‘Mine isn’t as good as yours,’ she said, smiling. ‘I’ll burn it. Yours is very good, better than your chrysanthemum poems. Keep it to show people.’

‘I’ve struggled to make one too,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘It may not be any good, but I’ll write it out for you to laugh at.’

She did so, and they read:

In osmanthus mist and paulownia shade I sit and raise my cup; The mouths of Chang’an water for the Double Ninth.

The road before you has no north–south, east–west order; Under your shell, the judgements of the Spring and Autumn are mere black and yellow.5

At this point they could not restrain their admiration.

‘That hits home!’ Bao-yu cried. ‘My poem ought to be burnt too.’

They read on:

Wine has not mastered the fishiness: chrysanthemums are needed still; Against the chill your nature stores, ginger is essential.

Now you have fallen into the pot, what use was any of it? By the moonlit waters only the scent of grain and millet remains.

When they had finished, they all declared it the finest possible poem on eating crabs. It took real talent to put a large meaning into such a little subject, though the satire on the world was rather too savage.

As they spoke, Patience came back into the garden. What had brought her? To find out, listen to the next chapter.

Translator’s notes

  1. Heng (蘅), Xiao (潇), Jiang (绛), Xiang (湘) and Xia (霞) are the characters used to claim subjects. Bao-yu replaces Xiang, from Xiang-yun’s name, with Xia, ‘rosy clouds’, to match her new pen-name. ↩
  2. The three paths, Magistrate Tao and the gentleman of Pengze evoke the poet Tao Yuanming and his life in retirement. ↩
  3. Tiaotuo is an old word for a bracelet. Here autumn fragrance rises imaginatively from beneath the painter’s wrist. ↩
  4. Zhuang’s butterfly recalls Zhuangzi’s dream of being a butterfly, and the question of which was dreaming the other. ↩
  5. ‘Spring and Autumn’ evokes the chronicle and its tradition of moral judgement. Hidden judgements become the black and yellow contents of a crab’s shell. ↩
