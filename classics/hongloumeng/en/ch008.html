Chapter 8 Comparing the Spiritual Jade, Oriole lets slip a hint; Visiting Bao-chai, Dai-yu half betrays her jealousy

Chapter end

Now Xi-feng and Bao-yu returned home and paid their respects to everyone. Bao-yu at once told Grandmother Jia that Qin Zhong wanted to attend the family school. He would have a friend to study with now, just the thing to spur him on. He warmly praised Qin Zhong’s character and behaviour, which made him such an endearing boy. Xi-feng helped him along, remarking that the boy would come another day to pay his respects to the venerable old lady. Between them they quite delighted the old lady. Xi-feng took the opportunity to invite her to the plays the day after next. Old as Grandmother Jia was, she still had a great zest for pleasure. When the day came, You-shi called to invite her again, and she took Lady Wang, Lin Dai-yu, Bao-yu and the others over to watch. At noon Grandmother Jia returned to rest. Lady Wang, who preferred peace and quiet, came back when she saw the old lady leaving.

Xi-feng then took the seat of honour, and they enjoyed themselves until evening, with nothing further to relate. Bao-yu, meanwhile, had escorted Grandmother Jia home. Once she had taken her midday nap, he wanted to go back and enjoy the plays, but feared he might trouble Qin-shi and the others. Then he remembered that Xue Bao-chai had been ill at home lately, and that he had not yet visited her himself. He would go and see her. If he went through the side gate behind the main apartments, however, he might get caught up in some other business, or, worse still, happen to meet his father. Better to take the long way round.

His nurses and maids came to help him change, but he went straight out through the inner gate in the clothes he was wearing. They could only follow, still assuming that he was going to the other mansion for the plays. Once he reached the passage hall, however, he turned east and then north, round the back of the hall. There he came face to face with two of the household’s literary dependants, Zhan Guang and Shan Pin-ren. At sight of Bao-yu both hurried up with beaming faces. One put an arm round his waist, the other took his hand.

‘Our dear little bodhisattva! I knew that dream meant something good. At last we’ve had the luck to meet you!’

They paid their respects, enquired after his health and talked on and on before finally moving away. An elderly nurse called them back.

‘Have you two gentlemen just come from the Master?’

They nodded. ‘The Master is having his midday nap in the little study at Mengpo Studio. There’s nothing to worry about.’

They walked off as they spoke, making Bao-yu smile too. He turned north towards Pear-tree Court. Just then Wu Xin-deng, the head of the silver treasury, and Dai Liang, who was in charge of the stores,

together with several other stewards, seven men in all, came out of the accounts office. Seeing Bao-yu, they hurried over and stood with their hands at their sides. Only the purchasing agent Qian Hua, who had not seen him for some days, stepped forward and made a formal kneeling salute. Bao-yu promptly took his hand and raised him, smiling.

‘We saw a square sheet of your calligraphy somewhere the other day, Master Bao,’ the men said with smiles. ‘Your brushwork is finer than ever. When will you favour us with a few sheets to put up?’

‘Where did you see it?’ Bao-yu asked, smiling.

‘In several places. Everyone praises it to the skies. They’ve even been asking us for some.’

‘It’s nothing much,’ said Bao-yu with a smile. ‘Just tell my pages.’

He walked on as he spoke. The men waited until he had passed before going their separate ways.

But enough of these incidental matters. Bao-yu reached Pear-tree Court and went first to Aunt Xue’s room, where she was arranging sewing work with the maids. He hastened to pay his respects. She caught hold of him at once and drew him into her arms.

‘My dear boy, how good of you to think of coming on such a cold day! Come up and sit on the kang.’

She ordered piping-hot tea.

‘Is Cousin not at home?’ Bao-yu asked.

‘He’s a horse without a bridle,’ Aunt Xue sighed. ‘Off roaming every day. As if he would stay home for a single day!’

‘Is Cousin Bao-chai quite well again?’

‘Yes, indeed. And you were kind enough to send someone to ask after her the other day. She’s in the inner room. Go and see her. It’s warmer in there than here. Sit there, and I’ll come and talk to you when I’ve put these things in order.’

Bao-yu climbed down from the kang and went to the inner doorway, where a soft curtain of somewhat worn red silk hung. Lifting it and stepping inside, he saw Xue Bao-chai sitting on the kang at her needlework. Her glossy black hair was coiled in a bun. She wore a honey-coloured padded jacket, a rose-purple sleeveless coat worked in two shades of gold and lined with ermine, and a padded skirt of spring-onion-yellow figured silk. None of her clothes was quite new or old, and there was nothing ostentatious about her appearance.

Her lips were red without rouge, her brows dark without being painted. Her face was round as a silver basin, her eyes like glistening apricots. She spoke little, which people called hiding her simplicity; she kept her place and accommodated herself to circumstances, which she herself called keeping to her unassuming ways. As Bao-yu looked at her, he asked, ‘Are you quite well again, Cousin?’

Bao-chai looked up and saw him coming in. She rose at once and answered with a smile, ‘Much better now. Thank you for thinking of me.’

She invited him to sit on the edge of the kang and told Oriole to pour tea. While asking after Grandmother, her aunt and the other girls, she looked at Bao-yu. On his head was a purple-gold filigree coronet set with jewels, and across his forehead a gold band with two dragons contending for a pearl. He wore an olive-yellow robe with upright python patterns, close-fitting archer’s sleeves and a lining of white fox underarm fur. At his waist was a five-coloured sash with a butterfly design. Round his neck hung a longevity locket and an amulet of religious dedication, as well as the precious jade that had been in his mouth when he was born.

‘I hear about this jade of yours every day,’ Bao-chai said with a smile, ‘but I’ve never really had a good look at it. Today I mean to.’

She moved closer.

Bao-yu moved towards her too, took it from his neck and put it in her hand. As Bao-chai held it on her palm, she saw that it was the size of a sparrow’s egg, brilliant as radiant sunset clouds, smooth and lustrous as cream, with patterns of five colours winding round it. This was the guise assumed by that unhewn stone beneath Green Ridge Peak on the Mountain of Great Desolation. A later poet once mocked it in these lines:

Nüwa smelting stones was wild enough a tale; From that wild tale unfolds the Great Desolation.

It lost the true realm of the hidden spirit, To take this phantom shape beside a stinking bag of flesh.

Know that when fortune fails, gold loses colour; Lament that when the times go wrong, jade cannot shine.

White bones lie mountain-high, their surnames lost: Nothing but noble youths and girls in crimson dress.

The stone had also recorded this transformed appearance and the seal-script inscriptions engraved by the scabby monk. These are reproduced below according to its drawing. Its actual body was tiny, small enough to be held in the mouth of a child emerging from the womb. Were we to draw it at its true size, the writing would be so minute that readers would have to strain their eyes, which would hardly be agreeable. We have therefore kept only the shape and enlarged the proportions a little, so that it may be read by lamplight or when tipsy. By explaining this now, we forestall such objections as: ‘How large can an unborn child’s mouth be, to hold such a huge, clumsy lump?’

Spiritual Jade: Front

Spiritual Jade

Never lose, never forget

Immortal life, enduring prosperity

Spiritual Jade: Reverse

1. Banishes evil influences

2. Cures grievous afflictions

3. Discerns fortune and misfortune

When she had finished looking, Bao-chai turned it over to examine the front again and read aloud: ‘Never lose, never forget; Immortal life, enduring prosperity.’

After reading it twice, she turned to Oriole with a smile.

‘Why are you standing here in a daze instead of pouring the tea?’

Oriole giggled. ‘Those two lines sound as though they make a pair with the two on your necklace, miss.’

‘So you have eight characters on your necklace too, Cousin!’ Bao-yu exclaimed, smiling. ‘Let me admire them as well.’

‘Don’t listen to her,’ said Bao-chai. ‘There’s no writing on it.’

‘Dear Cousin,’ Bao-yu pleaded with a smile, ‘why did you look at mine, then?’

Unable to resist his pestering, Bao-chai said, ‘Someone gave us a couple of auspicious sayings, so they were engraved on it, and I was told to wear it every day. Otherwise, what pleasure is there in carrying such a weight?’

As she spoke, she undid the row of buttons and drew out from over her scarlet inner jacket a jewelled necklace, its gems glittering and its gold ablaze. Bao-yu promptly took the locket in his hand. Sure enough, there were four seal-script characters on each side, eight altogether, forming two auspicious pronouncements.

Its appearance, too, had been drawn to show the design. The front of the necklace bore, in ordinary transcription: ‘Never part, never forsake.’ The reverse bore: ‘Youth and beauty, ever renewed.’ Bao-yu read these twice, then read his own twice.

‘These eight characters of yours really do make a pair with mine, Cousin,’ he said with a smile.

‘They were given by a scabby-headed monk,’ said Oriole, smiling. ‘He said they had to be engraved on something made of gold—’

Before she could finish, Bao-chai reproved her for not pouring the tea and asked Bao-yu where he had come from. Sitting so close to her, Bao-yu kept catching a delicate scent, sweet and cool, whose nature he could not make out.

‘What incense have you been using on your clothes, Cousin?

‘I’ve never smelt anything like it.’

‘I can’t bear perfuming clothes,’ Bao-chai said with a smile. ‘Perfectly good clothes, and you make them all smoky and singed-smelling.’

‘Then what is this scent?’ Bao-yu asked.

Bao-chai thought a moment, then smiled. ‘Of course. It must be the pill I took this morning.’

‘What sort of pill smells so lovely?’ Bao-yu asked with a smile. ‘Dear Cousin, give me one to taste.’

‘There you go again,’ she said, smiling. ‘Do you think medicines are things to take just as you please?’

She had hardly finished when someone outside announced, ‘Miss Lin is here.’ Before the words were out, Lin Dai-yu came swaying gracefully into the room. At sight of Bao-yu she smiled.

‘Oh dear, I’ve chosen a bad moment!’

Bao-yu and the others rose at once, smiling and inviting her to sit down.

‘What makes you say that?’ Bao-chai asked with a smile.

‘If I’d known he was coming, I wouldn’t have come,’ Dai-yu said, smiling.

‘Now I understand you even less.’

‘Either everyone comes in a crowd, or nobody comes at all. If he comes today and I come tomorrow, and we take turns like that, won’t you have someone here every day? Then you won’t be too lonely, or have too much company either. How is it you don’t understand, Cousin?’

Seeing that Dai-yu wore a scarlet camlet coat fastening down the front, Bao-yu asked, ‘Is it snowing?’

‘Snow pellets have been falling for quite a while,’ the women standing below the kang replied.

‘Has anyone brought my cloak?’ Bao-yu asked.

‘There, you see,’ said Dai-yu. ‘As soon as I come, it’s time for you to go.’

‘When did I say I was going?’ Bao-yu laughed. ‘I only want it here in case I need it.’

His wet-nurse, Nurse Li, said, ‘It’s snowing, and getting rather late. You might as well stay here and amuse yourself with your cousins.

‘Your aunt is laying out tea and refreshments. I’ll send a maid for your cloak and tell the pages they can go.’

Bao-yu agreed. Nurse Li went out and dismissed the pages, of whom no more need be said. Aunt Xue had set out several dainty things to eat and was keeping her visitors to tea. Bao-yu praised the delicious goose feet and duck tongues he had eaten at Cousin Zhen’s wife’s in the other mansion the other day. Aunt Xue promptly brought him some of her own, preserved in wine lees, to taste.

‘These really need wine with them,’ Bao-yu said with a smile.

Aunt Xue sent for some of the very best wine.

Nurse Li came forward. ‘Madam, perhaps no wine.’

‘Mamma, just one cup,’ Bao-yu pleaded.

‘No, that won’t do! In front of Her Old Ladyship or Her Ladyship, I wouldn’t mind if you drank a whole jar. But the other day I took my eyes off you for a moment, and some ill-bred creature, thinking only of pleasing you and not caring what happened to anyone else, gave you a sip of wine. I was scolded for two days because of it. You don’t know, madam, what an aggravating temper he has. Wine only makes him more wilful. One day Her Old Ladyship is in a good mood and lets him drink as much as he likes; another day she won’t let him touch it. Why should I suffer for nothing in between?’

‘You old thing,’ Aunt Xue said, laughing, ‘go and have a drink yourself, and stop worrying.

‘I shan’t let him have too much either. If Her Old Ladyship asks, I’ll answer for it.’

She told a young maid, ‘Come, see that your mammas have a cup too, to keep out the snowy chill.’

Hearing this, Nurse Li could only go with the others to have some wine. Bao-yu now said, ‘There’s no need to warm it. I only like it cold.’

‘That will never do,’ Aunt Xue said at once. ‘Cold wine makes your hand shake when you write.’

Bao-chai smiled. ‘Cousin Bao, with all the odds and ends you pick up every day, surely you know that wine is intensely heating by nature? Drink it warm and it disperses quickly. Drink it cold and it congeals inside you, and your five internal organs have to warm it. How can that fail to do harm? Do give up drinking it cold.’

Bao-yu thought this reasonable. He put down the cold wine and had it warmed before drinking.

Dai-yu sat cracking melon seeds, with only a close-lipped smile. Just then her little maid Snowgoose brought her a small hand-warmer.

‘Who told you to bring that?’ Dai-yu asked, smiling. ‘How very thoughtful! As though I were about to freeze to death!’

‘Nightingale was afraid you’d be cold, miss, and sent me with it,’ Snowgoose replied.

Dai-yu took it and clasped it to her bosom.

‘How good of you to listen to her!’ she said, smiling. ‘Everything I say to you goes in one ear and out the other. Why is it that when she speaks, you obey faster than if it were an imperial decree?’

Bao-yu knew she was using this to make fun of him, but could think of nothing to say in reply. He merely gave a couple of little laughs. Bao-chai knew Dai-yu’s ways of old and took no notice.

‘You’ve always been delicate,’ Aunt Xue said. ‘You can’t stand the cold. Is it wrong of them to think of you?’

‘You don’t understand, Aunt,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘It’s fortunate I’m here with you. If it were someone else’s house, mightn’t they be offended? It looks as though I thought they couldn’t even provide a hand-warmer, so I had to have one specially sent from home. Instead of saying my maids are over-anxious, they would think I was always so ill-mannered and presumptuous.’

‘You do imagine things!’ Aunt Xue said. ‘You may think of that, but I never would.’

During this conversation Bao-yu had already drunk three cups. Nurse Li came forward to stop him again. He was feeling thoroughly content, talking and laughing with Bao-chai and Dai-yu. How could he be willing to stop? He had to swallow his pride and plead.

‘Dear Mamma, just two more cups, and then no more.’

‘Mind yourself,’ said Nurse Li. ‘The Master is at home today. He may ask you about your books!’

This made Bao-yu thoroughly uneasy. He slowly put down his wine and lowered his head. Dai-yu spoke at once.

‘Don’t spoil everyone’s fun! If Uncle sends for you, just say Aunt is keeping you here. This mamma has had a drink herself, and now she wants to amuse herself at our expense!’

She gave Bao-yu a secret nudge to stir him to defiance, and murmured, ‘Don’t mind the old thing. Let’s enjoy ourselves.’

Nurse Li knew Dai-yu’s ways too.

‘Miss Lin, don’t encourage him. Do try to persuade him. He might listen to you.’

‘Why should I encourage him?’ Dai-yu said with a cold laugh. ‘And I’ve no call to persuade him either. You’re far too anxious, Mamma. Grandmother often gives him wine. Surely an extra mouthful here at Aunt’s can’t hurt him. Unless, of course, Aunt is an outsider, and perhaps he oughtn’t to be here at all.’

Nurse Li was both flustered and amused.

‘Really, Miss Lin! Every word you say is sharper than a knife. Where does all that come into it?’

Bao-chai could not help laughing either. She pinched Dai-yu’s cheek.

‘That mouth of yours, Pin-pin! One hardly knows whether to be cross with you or love you.’

Aunt Xue put in, ‘Don’t be afraid, my boy, don’t be afraid!

‘I’ve nothing very good to offer you when you come here. If you’re frightened into letting even this little bit lie heavy on your stomach, you’ll make me feel bad. Go on and enjoy it. I’ll answer for everything. Stay for supper too. If you get drunk, you can sleep here with me.’

She gave orders: ‘Warm some more wine! I’ll have a couple of cups with you, and then we’ll eat.’

Only then did Bao-yu’s spirits rise again.

Nurse Li gave the young maids their instructions.

‘Watch him carefully while you’re here. I’m going home to change my clothes, and then I’ll be back. Have a quiet word with Her Ladyship: she mustn’t give him his way and let him drink too much.’

With that she went home. There were still two or three older women present, but none felt particularly concerned. When they saw Nurse Li leave, they too quietly slipped away to see to their own needs. Only two young maids remained, delighted at the chance to please Bao-yu. Fortunately, after coaxing him again and again, Aunt Xue allowed him only a few cups before hastily having the wine taken away. Soup was made with sour bamboo shoots and chicken skin. Bao-yu drank two bowls with relish and ate half a bowl of green-grained rice gruel. Presently Bao-chai and Dai-yu finished their meal too, and strong tea was brewed for everyone. Only then did Aunt Xue feel at ease. Snowgoose and the other maids, three or four in all, had eaten and came in to attend them.

‘Are you going?’ Dai-yu asked Bao-yu.

Bao-yu looked sideways at her through heavy eyelids. ‘If you’re going, I’ll go with you.’

Dai-yu rose when she heard this. ‘We’ve been here all day. It’s time we went back.

‘Who knows how they may be looking for us over there?’

With that, the two took their leave. A young maid brought his rain-hat. Bao-yu inclined his head a little and told her to put it on. She shook out the scarlet felt hat and had only just set it on his head when he protested.

‘Stop, stop! Clumsy thing, be a little gentler! Haven’t you ever seen anyone put one on? Let me do it myself.’

Dai-yu stood on the edge of the kang.

‘What a fuss! Come here and let me see.’

Bao-yu went over at once. Dai-yu adjusted the hat with her hands, settling it gently over the coronet that held his hair and tucking its brim above his forehead band. She raised the walnut-sized crimson floss tassel on his hairpin so that it quivered outside the hat. When she had finished she looked him over.

‘There. Now put your cloak on.’

Only then did Bao-yu take the cloak and put it on.

‘Your nurses haven’t come back yet,’ Aunt Xue said hastily. ‘There’s no hurry. Wait a little.’

‘Why should we wait for them?’ said Bao-yu. ‘The maids are quite enough to go with us.’

Aunt Xue was uneasy and would not be satisfied until she had ordered two women to accompany the cousins.

The two thanked her for her hospitality and went straight back to Grandmother Jia’s rooms. She had not yet had supper, and was all the more pleased when she heard they had come from Aunt Xue’s. Seeing that Bao-yu had been drinking, she sent him to rest in his room and forbade him to come out again. She ordered that he be carefully looked after. Then, remembering his attendants, she asked, ‘Where is Li, his wet-nurse?’

No one dared say she had gone home. ‘She was here just now,’ they answered. ‘She must have gone off on some errand.’

Bao-yu staggered and turned his head. ‘She has an easier life than you do, Grandmother. What do you want with her? I might live a couple of days longer without her.’

Talking as he went, he reached his bedroom. Brush and ink were on the table. Skybright came forward to meet him, smiling.

‘Well, well! You made me grind all that ink, and in your fine mood this morning you wrote just three characters, threw down the brush and went off. You’ve kept us waiting all day. Come and use up the ink before you do anything else!’

Bao-yu suddenly remembered what had happened that morning.

‘Where are the three characters I wrote?’ he asked with a smile.

‘He really is drunk!’ Skybright said, laughing.

‘Before you went to the other mansion, you told us to put them up over this doorway. Now you ask that! I was afraid someone else would spoil them, so I climbed the ladder and put them up myself. My hands are still stiff with cold.’

‘I forgot,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘If your hands are cold, let me warm them.’

He reached out and took her hand, and they looked up together at the three freshly written characters above the doorway. Presently Dai-yu came in.

‘Dear Cousin, tell me honestly,’ Bao-yu said with a smile. ‘Which of those three characters is best?’

Dai-yu looked up at the three characters newly pasted above the inner doorway. They read ‘Crimson Rue Studio’.

‘They’re all good,’ Dai-yu said with a smile. ‘How have you come to write so beautifully? You must write a name-board for me tomorrow.’

‘You’re coaxing me again,’ Bao-yu chuckled. Then he asked, ‘Where’s Aroma?’

Skybright pointed with her lips towards the kang in the inner room. Bao-yu looked and saw Aroma lying there asleep, still dressed.

‘Well, that’s rather early to be tucked up so snugly,’ he said with a smile. Then he asked Skybright, ‘At breakfast in the other mansion today there was a plate of bean-curd-skin parcels. I remembered you liked them, so I spoke to Mrs Zhen. I just said I wanted to keep them for tonight, and had someone send them over. Did you eat them?’

‘Don’t speak of it. As soon as they came I knew they were for me. But I’d only just eaten, so I put them aside. Then Mamma Li came and saw them. “Bao-yu probably won’t eat these,” she said. “I’ll take them for my grandson.” And she had someone carry them home.’

Just then Snowpink brought in the tea.

‘Have some tea, Cousin Lin,’ Bao-yu said.

The others laughed. ‘Miss Lin left some time ago, and you’re still offering her tea!’

Bao-yu drank half a bowl. Suddenly he remembered the morning’s tea and asked Snowpink, ‘There was a bowl of Maple Dew tea brewed this morning. I said it only comes into its own after three or four infusions. Why have you brewed this instead?’

‘I did keep it,’ said Snowpink. ‘But when Mamma Li came, she wanted to taste it, so I gave it to her.’

At this Bao-yu flung the cup he was holding straight to the floor.

It shattered with a crash, splashing tea all over Snowpink’s skirt. He sprang up and demanded of her:

‘What sort of mamma is she to you, for you all to be so dutiful? All she has to go on is that I drank her milk for a few days when I was little. Now you’ve let her set herself up above my ancestors. I don’t need her milk any more. Why keep an ancestor here doing nothing? Turn her out, and we’ll all have some peace!’

He was about to go that very instant and ask Grandmother Jia to dismiss his wet-nurse.

Aroma, in fact, had not been asleep at all. She had only pretended, to tempt Bao-yu into coming to tease her. When she first heard the talk about the writing and the parcels, there had been no need to get up. But when he smashed the teacup and lost his temper, she quickly rose to explain matters and soothe him. Grandmother Jia had already sent someone to ask what had happened.

‘I was bringing in the tea just now,’ Aroma said quickly, ‘and slipped on the snow. The cup fell out of my hand and broke.’

Then she tried to calm Bao-yu. ‘Very well, if you’ve set your heart on turning her out. We’d all like to go too. You might as well turn us all out together. It would suit us, and you needn’t worry about finding better people to wait on you.’

This silenced Bao-yu at last. Aroma and the others helped him to the kang and changed his clothes. They could not make out what he was still saying: his tongue was growing thick, his eyelids heavier and heavier. They hurried to settle him to sleep. Aroma took the Spiritual Jade from his neck, wrapped it in her own handkerchief and tucked it under the mattress, so that it would not chill his neck when he put it on next morning.

Bao-yu fell asleep as soon as his head touched the pillow. Nurse Li and the others had come back by then. Hearing that he was drunk, they dared not come forward and provoke him further. They quietly made sure he was asleep before dispersing, relieved. When he woke next day, someone reported that Master Rong from the other mansion had brought Master Qin to call. Bao-yu hurried out to welcome them and took Qin Zhong to pay his respects to Grandmother Jia. Delighted by the boy’s good looks and gentle manners, she thought him a worthy companion for Bao-yu’s studies. She kept him to tea and a meal, and had him taken to see Lady Wang and the others. They had always been fond of Qin-shi, and seeing what a fine boy Qin Zhong was, they too were pleased. Each gave him a gift when he left. Grandmother Jia gave him a purse and a gold figure of Kui Xing, to signify ‘harmony under the star of learning’. She also told him:

‘You live a long way off. If ever the heat or cold, or getting your meals, makes things awkward, stay here. There’s no need for a fixed rule. Just keep company with your Uncle Bao, and don’t learn the ways of those good-for-nothing creatures.’

Qin Zhong assented to each instruction, then went home and told his father.

His father, Qin Ye, currently held office as a secretary for building works. He was nearly seventy, and his wife had long been dead. Having had no children in his earlier years, he had adopted a boy and a girl from a foundling home. The boy had died, leaving only the girl, whose childhood name was Ke-er. She grew into a slender, graceful young woman with a charming disposition. Qin Ye had some longstanding connections with the Jias, and a marriage was arranged: she was betrothed to Jia Rong. Qin Ye was over fifty before Qin Zhong was born. The boy’s tutor had died the previous year, and there had been no opportunity to engage another accomplished teacher, so for the time being he had been reviewing his old lessons at home. Qin Ye had been thinking of approaching his daughter’s family by marriage about sending him to their school, so that his studies would not be neglected in the meantime, when this chance encounter with Bao-yu occurred. He knew too that the Jia school was now in the charge of Jia Dai-ru, a venerable scholar of the day. Qin Zhong was bound to improve there, and might hope to make a name for himself. Qin Ye was therefore delighted. His official purse, however, was poorly filled, and everyone in the Jia household, high and low, had eyes only for wealth and rank. A modest sum would hardly do, yet he feared harming his son’s prospects for life. There was nothing for it but to scrape together twenty-four taels, respectfully wrap them as an introductory gift to the teacher and take Qin Zhong himself to pay a call at Dai-ru’s house.

After that they waited to hear which day Bao-yu would begin school, so that the two could start together. As the saying has it:

Had they known the idle quarrels that would follow,

Would they have set out now to study on so false a path?
