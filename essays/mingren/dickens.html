狄更斯，裂缝

Dickens, The Crack

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、两个房间

查尔斯·狄更斯有两个房间。

先走进读者记得的那一个。壁炉在烧，食物上了桌，门终于愿意为另一个人打开。斯克鲁奇醒过来了。小蒂姆没有死。奥利弗有了家。大卫·科波菲尔长大了。雪还在外面，里面有人肯把椅子挪开一点，让你坐下。

狄更斯的小说当然不只有这一间。还有饥饿的孩子、漫长的诉讼、没有人来照料的屋子。正因为外面冷，里面那一点暖才不是装饰。你读过一个孩子怎样被拒绝，才知道一张给他的椅子意味着什么。

再走进他的生活。

1836年，他与凯瑟琳·霍加斯结婚。二十二年后，两人分居。凯瑟琳离开共同的家，她的妹妹乔治娜留在狄更斯身边。报刊上出现了署着狄更斯名字的声明。他不只是在解释自己的生活，也在决定广大读者应该怎样理解另一个人的生活。

一份分居声明装不下二十二年。十个孩子也不是十件搬来搬去的家具。这里曾有亲近，后来有了伤害；记住前者，不会让后者变轻。

两个房间。不是一个真，一个假。

问题在门口：一个能让读者为陌生人挪出椅子的人，怎样对待坐在自己身边的人？

二、鞋油厂

1824年。伦敦。查尔斯十二岁。

家里的钱已经撑不住了，工作先被找好。2月20日，他的父亲约翰因债务被关进马歇尔西监狱。母亲和年幼的孩子后来搬进去陪父亲；姐姐范妮在皇家音乐学院学习，查尔斯则在外寄宿、工作。不是全家只剩下他一个人在墙外。

沃伦鞋油厂。小罐口包上纸，扎紧，剪齐，再贴标签。手要不停地做下去。

他后来记得最难受的，并不只是工作本身。他曾想过上学，想过自己将来能做些什么。现在别人没有因为他有这些念头，就把手里的罐子接过去。工场后来迁到钱多斯街，他坐在临街的窗边干活，路人会围着看。对外面的人，那是熟练的手；对里面的孩子，那还是不愿被看见的自己。

可是同一段回忆里，还有另一个孩子。

鲍勃·费金比他年长，教他怎样做工。有一次查尔斯病得难受，费金照顾他，用热水瓶替他取暖，后来还坚持送他回去。查尔斯却不愿让他知道父亲在监狱里，在别人的门前假装已经到家，等费金走远才离开。

穷孩子已经伸了手。他仍在藏自己的地址。

这不是一个忽然明白人人平等、从此再无隔阂的故事。羞耻、身份的想象、接受过的照料，都在里面。把这一切只写成一种完美同情的诞生，反而把那只伸过来的手遮住了。

多年后，他把这段经历告诉福斯特，并留下自传片段。福斯特在传记中保存了它。《大卫·科波菲尔》又把其中一些东西带进小说：大卫十岁，在酒商的货栈处理瓶子。不是把十二岁的查尔斯、鞋油罐和每一天的经过原样照录。

那间工场留在了文字里。成年人的回忆仍然疼。但一个孩子不必先被放到窗边，才能有一天写出值得读的书。

三、药方

狄更斯给社会开过药方。只是药方不止一句“善良一点”。

《雾都孤儿》第二章，一个孩子拿着空碗走上前，请求再给一些。小说里的成年人没有因此听见饥饿。他们先听见了冒犯：一个已经被安排好份额的人，竟然还要说自己不够。碗那么小，反应那么大。

《圣诞颂歌》的斯克鲁奇不只在清晨流泪。到结尾，他提高鲍勃的工资，答应帮助一家人，还让他多添煤。醒悟落在了别人接下来要过的日子上。屋子会不会暖起来，不能只看屋主感动得有多厉害。

《荒凉山庄》把人放进一场拖延不休的诉讼，也把另一种冷放进了家。杰利比太太忙着远方的慈善事业，小儿子在楼梯上跌伤，女儿满身墨迹地替她写信。她的目光去得很远，近处却有人等不到照料。狄更斯早已写过这一幕：关心远方，可能与疏忽身边同时发生。

《艰难时世》里，西丝的父亲在马戏团与马打交道，她却回答不出课堂要求的“马的定义”。另一个孩子背出四足、食草、四十颗牙齿，定义便算完成了。并不是牙齿数错了，而是背完之后，老师就仿佛已经把她知道和不知道的一切都量完了。

《大卫·科波菲尔》让货栈里的孩子继续长大。他受过的对待很重要，却不是一个把未来提前封死的判词。一个人还有很长的路，不是为了替曾经伤害他的安排证明它做得对。

这些书把一个容易略过去的人，放到你面前。放得近些，再近些。近到“他们都差不多”不够用了。

他也把这份关切带到了讲台上。1858年，狄更斯为大奥蒙德街的儿童医院募款、讲话，又为医院朗读《圣诞颂歌》。听众散场，捐款却能留下，变成病房里的照料。

在这个系列里，托尔斯泰也拿着药方。两个人都把“怎样活”交到别人手里。翻过纸来，还有另一面：写药方的人自己怎样活？

四、他和托尔斯泰

托尔斯泰把这个问题写进了《忏悔录》。他回看自己想教别人、却不知道究竟该教什么的年月。庄园、家人、作品的声名，都不能替他回答那句反复回来的问话：然后呢？

这是把纸转向自己。写的人也在纸上。

福斯特收录了分居前的几段通信。狄更斯在里面承认，凯瑟琳使他不快乐，他使她更不快乐；他不要求免于责备，也承认自己的反复、任性和性格上的困难。这些话确实留下了。它们不容许后人把他写成一个完全不知自己有错的人。

接着看另一张纸。1858年6月12日，《家常话》刊登他的《私人声明》。他请求那些只通过作品认识他的读者相信他，称家事已经友好解决，否认流言。结尾，他不仅以自己的名义，也以妻子的名义作出声明。

读者对书的信任，被带进了一件他们并未亲历的家事。书里有多少温暖，并不能告诉他们凯瑟琳想说什么。

同年，另一封写给朗读经纪人阿瑟·史密斯的信也传到了报上。那封信里有对凯瑟琳母职与精神状态的贬损；它不是六月那篇《私人声明》，两份文字不能混成一份。

更严重的说法，来自另一条较晚的记录。凯瑟琳去世前，把往事告诉邻居爱德华·达顿·库克。库克在1879年1月致威廉·莫伊·托马斯的信里转述：狄更斯曾试图把她送进精神病院，但没有办成。妻子的回忆经由邻居的笔留下来。我们能听见她怎样讲述，却不能假装自己当年也在房间里。

托尔斯泰公开的自我追问，与狄更斯给朋友和公众的不同文字，仍然可以并置。只是不能把一个人的痛苦当作赦免，把另一个人的内心当作我们已经读完的书。站在他们身边的妻子，也不是用来考验作家是否言行一致的道具。

看见裂缝，是一件事。愿不愿意让裂缝另一边的人说话，又是一件事。

五、他和王尔德

王尔德面对假面。狄更斯面对冷漠。这是本系列给两人的照明方向，不是把整个作品分成互不相干的两间屋子。

《道林·格雷的画像》里，道林伤害西比尔之后，发现画中人的嘴发生了变化，添上了残酷的痕迹。他照镜子，自己的脸却没有同样的改变。外面可以照旧好看，受到伤害的人却不会因此没有受伤。

《雾都孤儿》里，拿碗的孩子也使一种体面露出了别的东西。给他定量的人觉得一切安排得很好；他站起来，安排就受到了打扰。

一个让好看的表面不能把事遮住。一个让不愿看的人不能轻易走开。他们都不只是在告诉读者已经愿意听的好话。

也不能说王尔德的人生与美学从无裂缝。他在狱中写成、后来以《自深深处》之名出版的文字，有自责，也有妻子的行动：她身体不好，仍从热那亚来到英国，把母亲去世的消息告诉他。那不是一尊供别人观看的美学形象。有人真的走了那一段路。

道林不是王尔德的自画像。狱中的自责也不意味着对他的惩罚因此正当。我们不需要先把王尔德变成一个毫无矛盾的人，才有理由认真看他；同样，也不必先证明狄更斯所有同情都是假的，才能追问他怎样对待凯瑟琳。

火可以照亮一间屋子。它不会替隔壁的人回答，那里究竟冷不冷。

六、十个孩子

查尔斯和凯瑟琳有十个孩子。

小查尔斯、玛丽、凯特、沃尔特、弗朗西斯、阿尔弗雷德、悉尼、亨利、多拉、爱德华。

十个名字，不是十个位置。多拉在1851年夭折，只有几个月大。1858年分居时，活着的是九个；年长的已经长大，最小的仍在童年。父母分开这件事，落进了九段已经不同的人生。

长子查尔斯与母亲同住。乔治娜留在狄更斯身边，后来与玛丽一道照管他的家。孩子们各有年纪、关系和以后的生活。把他们都写成父亲这一边、母亲那一边，纸上的分界会很整齐，人却被挤没了。

凯瑟琳也没有从此只剩下等待死亡。

博物馆保存着她用过的花束托，可以把花放在桌上，腾出手来。还有她留给凯特的蛇形手镯、留给乔治娜的蛇形戒指。物件不能告诉我们她是否原谅了谁。它们至少留下了她如何保存、选择、把东西交给另一个人的痕迹。

1879年，她去世。后来经女儿凯特的回忆流传下来的一句话，是她希望把丈夫的信交给大英博物馆，“让世界知道，他曾经爱过我”。这句话经女儿回忆，由格拉迪丝·斯托里在1939年的书中记录。隔着两层讲述，我们才听到了她。

但信确实留下了。

大英图书馆的馆藏目录登记着136封狄更斯写给凯瑟琳的信，年代从1835年到1867年。凯瑟琳在去世前把信交给凯特；1899年，凯特把它们赠予当时的大英博物馆。纸上的笔迹属于写信的人，保存它们这件事，却也属于收信的人。

这不是让一百多封旧信证明后来的伤害不算数。也不是说，一个女人只有证明曾被一位作家爱过，才能保住自己的尊严。

她保留了关系中确实存在过的一部分。不肯让后来的解释，把此前的自己一并擦掉。

七、1860年9月

盖茨山庄。屋外的田地。

9月4日，狄更斯写信告诉威尔斯：前一天，他烧掉了二十年来积下的信件和文件。他把烟比成从匣子里出来的精灵；动手时天气好，结束时下起大雨，于是又开玩笑，说自己的通信把天都弄阴了。

烟和雨都在他自己的叙述里。火也在。可是“烧了信”还要问一句：谁写给谁的信？

他能够搬到田地里烧掉的，是自己保存的来信与文件。已经寄到别人手里的信，不会因为这堆火一起消失。凯瑟琳手里的那些，正是反例。

1865年，他又对麦克雷迪说，除纯属事务的来信外，收到的信如今都毁掉。这是一种要把私人通信留在私人之间的做法；我们可以看见它造成的材料缺失，却不能只凭火的存在，就读出每一次销毁的全部动机。

艾伦·特南确实在这段生活里。1857年狄更斯结识她时，她十八岁，比他年轻二十七岁。到了焚信这一年，她已经二十一岁。有材料能告诉我们她在这段生活中的位置，但缺失的信不会自动替每一种传闻作证。

焚信之外，还有别的材料。1869年的遗嘱，第一项遗赠就是给艾伦·劳利斯·特南的一千英镑。它不能让我们重演两人每一次见面，却足以说明：留下的材料并非什么也没有。

火拿走了一部分。收信的人留下了另一部分。

谁来决定后人看见什么，并不始终只握在作家一双手里。

八、最后一天

1870年6月8日。盖茨山庄。

依福斯特收集的记述，狄更斯这天仍在花园的瑞士式小屋里写作。书是《艾德温·德鲁德之谜》。他上午写，午后又回去。傍晚进屋吃饭时，身体出了严重问题，随后倒下。

6月9日，他去世，五十八岁。小说没有写完。前一天留在纸上的句子，停在了他没有替读者写出结局的地方。

6月14日，他葬在威斯敏斯特教堂诗人角。他生前偏好肯特郡的安葬地点，也要求葬礼简朴、不得公开预告。地点改了；私下举行的仪式，却保留了他所要的安静。

墓石很安静。姓名。出生日期。死亡日期。没有替他总结一生的颂词。

几个月前，3月15日，他告别公开朗读的听众。福斯特记下那晚的情景：狄更斯合上《匹克威克外传》，以自己的身份向听众致谢，离开讲台；掌声又把他留住了一会儿。那个在读者面前投入工作的人，也在这些记录里。只是不能把身体的耗损，写成爱得足够深的证明。

读者仍然可以在别处说起他的同情。可以把书翻到孩子举起碗的地方，翻到斯克鲁奇让人添煤的地方。

只是当凯瑟琳走进记述，不能用这些页替她关门。她不只是书外的一位读者。她和他共同生活过，她所经历的，也不能被读者的眼泪代替。

九、裂缝

狄更斯给许多读者留下过一座温暖的房子。

后来知道了他的家事，这座房子不必被拆掉。孩子仍然不该挨饿，人仍然需要照料。可屋子再大，也不能把住在作者身边的人吞进去，叫她为了屋外人的感动，安静地不再说话。

夏洛蒂的简·爱曾当面问罗切斯特：难道因为她贫穷、平凡，就没有灵魂与心？那是小说人物在一个具体处境里的发言，不是凯瑟琳留下的句子。可它让这里的问题更近了一点：被爱不能是一个人获准存在的方式。

凯瑟琳保存的信，也不必是对狄更斯的最后判词。里面有一段关系。那段关系不能只由寄信的人决定，从哪一页开始算，从哪一页起一概不算。

以下桥头相遇，是本篇的文学想象。

桥头上又多了一个人。穿得体面，手里有笔。另一只手握着一盒火柴。

他脚下有一道裂缝。不是亚里士多德铺木板时留下的接缝。是他写下的照料，与有人实际承受过的对待之间，裂开的一道口子。

一只脚在这边，一只脚在那边。他站得并不稳。

苏格拉底站在空地上。柏拉图蹲着画图纸。休谟摆开双陆棋盘。叔本华看桥底下。克尔凯郭尔跳了。图灵看着手里的苹果。契诃夫靠着栏杆。康托尔看天上。哥白尼放下书走了。萨特拿着烟斗转来转去。波伏瓦举着镜子。蒯因轻轻说了一句话。

特斯拉听嗡嗡声。爱迪生拿着没有亮的灯泡。海森堡的位置不确定。玻尔拿着没寄出的信。托尔斯泰拿着药方站在契诃夫对面。莎士比亚不在——他是桥下面的水。斯宾诺莎手里有玻璃粉。亚里士多德蹲着铺地板。法拉第蹲着掀地板。麦克斯韦站着写方程。

贞德带着火飘在桥的上方。王尔德站得很好看，手里拿着那句话。拉马努金从缝隙里冒出半个身子。奥本海默背着灰往前走。夏洛蒂拿着笔。艾米莉在桥外面的荒原上，风从她那里吹来。玻尔兹曼抱着刻有方程的石头。梵高手里有画笔，身上有颜料，走过的地方全是颜色。

狄更斯看了托尔斯泰一眼。

两个开过药方的人。此刻，一个把纸展开，一个把纸卷紧。不是因为我们已经知道谁的内心更诚实；在这场相遇里，他们还没有开口。

狄更斯的纸卷得很紧。火柴盒的边角抵着掌心。他把脚移了一下，裂缝没有跟着合拢。

里面有信。

不是给他新写的判决书。是他写过、凯瑟琳收下并保存的信。没有因为他后来拿起火柴，就从另一双手里消失。

纸在下面。他看见了自己的字。

远处，康德站着。他看着狄更斯，也看着脚下的纸。那座给读者的暖屋仍然亮着。它没有熄灭，裂缝也没有因此合拢。

没有人要求凯瑟琳拿出被爱过的证明，才能在这里占一个位置。她不必替他的小说成为伟大的小说。

狄更斯还拿着笔。笔能继续写，却不能把收信人的一生改成他单方面的附注。

他朝康德的方向走去。并不稳。那盒火柴仍然在手里，却没有划亮。

裂缝在他脚下发出很小的声音。像一封信被折叠的声音。

I. Two Rooms

Charles Dickens had two rooms.

Enter the one his readers remember first. A fire in the hearth. Food on the table. A door that will at last open to someone else. Scrooge has woken up. Tiny Tim lives. Oliver has found a home. David Copperfield has grown up. The snow is still outside. Inside, someone moves a chair to make room for you.

There are other rooms in Dickens, of course: hungry children, lawsuits that outlast the people caught in them, houses where nobody comes to help. It is the cold outside that keeps the warmth from being merely decorative. Once you have read how a child is turned away, a chair offered to him means something.

Now enter his life.

In 1836 he married Catherine Hogarth. Twenty-two years later they separated. Catherine left their shared home. Her sister Georgina stayed with Dickens. A statement bearing his name appeared in print. He was explaining his own life, but he was also telling a large body of readers how to understand someone else's.

A statement about separation cannot hold twenty-two years. Ten children are not ten pieces of furniture moved from one house to another. There had been affection. Later there was harm. Remembering the first does not diminish the second.

Two rooms. Not one true and the other false.

The question waits in the doorway. A writer can persuade a reader to move a chair for a stranger. How does he treat the person already sitting beside him?

II. The Blacking Factory

London, 1824. Charles was twelve.

The family's money had run out, and work had already been found for him. On February 20 his father, John, was imprisoned for debt in the Marshalsea. His mother and the younger children subsequently moved into the prison. His sister Fanny was studying at the Royal Academy of Music. Charles worked and lodged outside. He was not the only member of the family left beyond its walls.

Warren's Blacking. Paper over the pot, string round it, ends trimmed, label pasted on. The hands had to keep going.

What he later remembered as unbearable was not simply the work. He had thought about school, about what he might one day become. None of those thoughts made someone else take the pot out of his hands. When the business later moved to Chandos Street, he worked beside a window overlooking the street, where passersby gathered to watch. They saw a pair of skilled hands. The child behind the glass was also trying not to be seen.

But another boy appears in the same recollection.

Bob Fagin was older. He taught Charles the work. When Charles became ill, Fagin looked after him, warming him with bottles of hot water, and later insisted on seeing him home. Charles did not want Fagin to learn that his father was in prison. He pretended to have arrived at someone else's door, and waited for his companion to leave before walking away.

A poor boy had already offered him help. Charles was still hiding his address.

This is not the story of a child who suddenly discovers equality and never feels another barrier between himself and anyone else. Shame, imagined social standing, and care actually received are all present. Make the episode only the birth of perfect compassion, and the helping hand disappears from view.

Years later Dickens told Forster and left an autobiographical fragment, which his friend preserved in the biography. Some of this material entered David Copperfield in another form. David is ten, working with bottles in a wine merchant's warehouse. The novel is not a day-by-day record of twelve-year-old Charles and the pots of blacking.

The workplace survived in words. Recalling it still hurt the adult who wrote them. A child need not first be put behind that window, however, to earn the right to write a book worth reading.

III. The Prescription

Dickens wrote prescriptions for society. They were more particular than “Be kind.”

In the second chapter of Oliver Twist, a child advances with an empty bowl and asks for more. The adults in the scene do not hear hunger first. They hear an affront: someone whose portion has been decided for him is saying it is not enough. Such a small bowl. Such a large reaction.

Scrooge does more than weep on a Christmas morning. At the end of A Christmas Carol, he raises Bob's wages, promises help for the family, and tells him to put more coal on the fire. His change reaches into the days other people will have to live. Whether a room becomes warmer cannot depend only on how deeply its owner has been moved.

Bleak House places people inside a lawsuit that will not end. It also brings another kind of cold into a home. Mrs. Jellyby is occupied with a distant charitable project. Her little son tumbles on the stairs; her daughter, covered in ink, takes down letters for her. Her attention travels a great distance. Close at hand, someone is waiting for care. Dickens had already written this scene: concern for people far away can coexist with neglect of those nearby.

In Hard Times, Sissy's father works with horses in a circus, but she cannot supply the classroom's required definition of a horse. Another child supplies four legs, a diet of grass, forty teeth. The definition is complete. The problem is not that the number of teeth is wrong. It is that the teacher behaves as though the answer has finished measuring everything Sissy knows and does not know.

David Copperfield allows the child in the warehouse to grow up. What has been done to him matters, but it is not a sentence passed on the whole of his future. The life still ahead of him does not exist to vindicate the arrangements that once hurt him.

These books put someone easily overlooked in front of you. Closer, then closer still, until “people like that” no longer does the work of looking.

He also took that concern onto a public platform. In 1858, Dickens spoke and raised money for the children’s hospital in Great Ormond Street, and read A Christmas Carol for its benefit. The audience went home. Donations could stay behind and become care on a ward.

Tolstoy, too, carries a prescription in this series. Each man places an answer to “How should we live?” in other people's hands. Turn the paper over. There is another side: how does the writer himself live?

IV. Dickens and Tolstoy

Tolstoy made the question part of A Confession. He looked back on his desire to teach other people when he did not know what he ought to teach. His estate, his family, the reputation of his books: none could answer the question that kept returning. And then what?

The paper has been turned toward its writer. He appears on it too.

Forster preserved correspondence written before the separation. Dickens acknowledged that Catherine made him unhappy and that he made her more unhappy still. He did not claim exemption from blame. He admitted his own uncertainties, caprices, and difficulties of disposition. The words remain. They prevent us from making him a man wholly unaware of his faults.

Now consider a different sheet of paper. On June 12, 1858, Household Words published his statement headed “Personal.” Dickens appealed to readers who knew him only through his writing, described the domestic difficulty as amicably settled, and denied the rumors surrounding it. At the end he spoke not only in his own name but in his wife's.

The trust readers placed in books was being brought into a household they had not witnessed. However much warmth they had found on the page, it could not tell them what Catherine wanted to say.

Another letter reached the newspapers that year, this one addressed to Arthur Smith, the manager of Dickens's readings. It disparaged Catherine as a mother and cast doubt on her mental stability. It was not the June statement. The two documents must not be folded into one.

A more serious allegation comes through a later record. Before her death Catherine spoke about the past to her neighbor Edward Dutton Cook. In January 1879, Cook wrote to William Moy Thomas, reporting her account that Dickens had tried, unsuccessfully, to place her in an asylum. A wife's recollection reaches us through her neighbor's pen. We can hear how she described the past without pretending to have stood in the room when it happened.

Tolstoy's public questioning of himself can still be set beside the different things Dickens wrote to a friend and to his readers. But one man's pain is not an absolution, and the other man's mind is not a book we have finished reading. Their wives are not props supplied to test a writer's consistency.

Seeing a crack is one thing. Allowing the person on the other side to speak is another.

V. Dickens and Wilde

Wilde faces the mask. Dickens faces indifference. These are the directions from which this series has chosen to light them, not partitions dividing their entire work into separate rooms.

After Dorian hurts Sibyl in The Picture of Dorian Gray, he notices a change at the mouth of his painted likeness: a trace of cruelty. He looks in a mirror. His own face has not changed in the same way. The surface can remain beautiful. That does not make the hurt disappear for the person who has suffered it.

The child holding the bowl in Oliver Twist also brings something else into view beneath respectability. Those who have decided his allowance consider the arrangements satisfactory. He stands up, and the arrangements are disturbed.

One writer prevents an attractive surface from concealing what has happened. The other makes it harder for someone who will not look to walk away. Neither is merely repeating pleasant things his readers already wish to hear.

Nor was Wilde's life seamlessly identical with his aesthetics. In the prison writing later published as De Profundis, there is self-reproach. There is also an action performed by his wife: despite being ill, she came from Genoa to England to tell him that his mother had died. That is not an aesthetic figure arranged for spectators. Someone made the journey.

Dorian is not a self-portrait of Wilde. The self-reproach of a prisoner does not establish that his punishment was just. We need not turn Wilde into a man without contradictions before taking him seriously. Nor must we first establish that every expression of sympathy in Dickens was fraudulent before asking how he treated Catherine.

A fire may light one room. It does not answer for the people next door whether their room is cold.

VI. Ten Children

Charles and Catherine had ten children.

Charles Jr., Mary, Kate, Walter, Francis, Alfred, Sydney, Henry, Dora, and Edward.

Ten names, not ten allocated places. Dora died in infancy in 1851. Nine children were alive at the separation in 1858. The older ones had grown up; the youngest was still a child. Their parents' separation entered nine lives already different from one another.

Charles Jr. lived with Catherine. Georgina stayed with Dickens and later managed his household with Mary. The children had different ages, different relationships, and lives of their own to follow. Assign all of them to Father's Side or Mother's Side, and the division looks neat on paper. The people have been squeezed out.

Catherine did not become someone who only waited to die.

The museum holds her bouquet holder, with its stand for setting the flowers on a table and freeing the owner's hands. There is also the snake bracelet she left to Kate, and the serpent ring she left to Georgina. Objects cannot tell us whether she forgave anyone. They do retain traces of her keeping things, choosing, passing something into another person's hands.

She died in 1879. A sentence handed down through her daughter Kate's recollections expresses her wish that her husband's letters should go to the British Museum, “that the world may know he loved me once.” Gladys Storey recorded Kate's recollection in her 1939 book. It is through both women's telling that Catherine's words reach us.

But the letters survived.

The British Library's catalogue describes 136 letters from Dickens to Catherine, dating from 1835 to 1867. Catherine gave them to Kate before her death; in 1899 Kate presented them to the British Museum. The handwriting belongs to the person who sent them. Their preservation belongs to the person who received them too.

A hundred old letters are not evidence that later harm did not count. Nor does a woman have to prove that a writer once loved her in order to retain her dignity.

She preserved something that had existed in their relationship. A later account was not to erase the person she had been before it.

VII. September 1860

Gad's Hill Place. The field outside the house.

On September 4 Dickens wrote to Wills about what he had done the day before: burned the letters and papers accumulated over twenty years. He compared the smoke to a genie emerging from its casket. The weather had been fine when he started and it was raining heavily when he finished; perhaps, he joked, his correspondence had clouded the heavens.

The smoke and the rain are in his own account. So is the fire. But “he burned the letters” still leaves a question. Written by whom, to whom?

What he could carry out into the field were the letters and papers he had kept. Letters already sent to other people did not vanish in the same fire. The ones in Catherine's possession are a case in point.

In 1865 he told Macready that he now destroyed incoming letters except those strictly concerned with business. This was a practice of keeping private correspondence private. We can see the gaps it left in the record without claiming that the existence of a fire reveals every motive behind every destruction.

Ellen Ternan was part of this life. She was eighteen when Dickens met her in 1857, twenty-seven years younger than he was. By the year of the fire she was twenty-one. Surviving records tell us something of her place in this life. Missing letters do not supply evidence for every story told about it.

Letters are not the only evidence. The first bequest in Dickens's 1869 will is a thousand pounds to Ellen Lawless Ternan. It cannot replay every meeting between them. It does establish that the surviving record is not a blank.

The fire took something. The recipients kept something else.

What later readers will be allowed to see is not always held in the writer's hands alone.

VIII. The Last Day

June 8, 1870. Gad's Hill Place.

In the account gathered by Forster, Dickens spent the day writing in the Swiss chalet in his garden. The book was The Mystery of Edwin Drood. He worked in the morning and returned after lunch. Back in the house for dinner, he became seriously ill and collapsed.

He died on June 9, aged fifty-eight. The novel remained unfinished. The sentences he had written the day before stopped short of an ending he would never give his readers.

He was buried in Poets' Corner at Westminster Abbey on June 14. He had preferred a burial in Kent, and had asked for a simple funeral with no advance public announcement. The place changed. The private ceremony retained the quiet he had wanted.

The stone is quiet. His name. The date of his birth. The date of his death. No tribute summing up the life.

A few months earlier, on March 15, he had said farewell to the audiences of his public readings. Forster records him closing Pickwick, speaking in his own person to thank his listeners, leaving the platform, then being held there a little longer by the applause. The man who gave himself to this work before an audience is in the record too. Physical exhaustion is not proof that someone has loved enough.

Readers can still speak of his sympathy elsewhere. They can turn to the child advancing with a bowl, or to Scrooge telling Bob to build up the fire.

But when Catherine enters the account, those pages cannot be used to shut the door on her. She was not merely a reader of his books. She had shared his life. A reader's tears cannot stand in for what she experienced.

IX. The Crack

Dickens left many readers a warm house.

Learning about his marriage does not require us to pull it down. Children still should not go hungry. People still need care. But however large the house, it cannot swallow someone who lived beside its author and ask her to fall silent for the sake of everyone moved by what he wrote.

Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre asks Rochester whether being poor and plain means she has no soul or heart. These are a fictional character's words in a particular encounter, not a sentence Catherine left behind. They bring the question closer nevertheless: being loved cannot be what grants a person permission to exist.

The letters Catherine kept need not deliver the last judgment on Dickens either. They hold part of a relationship. Its sender cannot decide alone which page is where the relationship begins, and at which page everything stops counting.

The encounter at the bridge that follows is a work of literary imagination.

Someone else has arrived. A well-dressed man, holding a pen. In his other hand, a matchbox.

There is a crack beneath his feet. Not a joint left between the boards Aristotle is laying. A gap has opened between the care he wrote about and the treatment someone actually received.

One foot on either side. He does not stand easily.

Socrates stands in the clearing. Plato crouches over his blueprints. Hume sets out his backgammon board. Schopenhauer looks beneath the bridge. Kierkegaard has made his leap. Turing looks at the apple in his hand. Chekhov leans against the railing. Cantor looks up at the sky. Copernicus has put down his book and walked away. Sartre paces with his pipe. Beauvoir holds a mirror. Quine has said a quiet sentence.

Tesla listens to a hum. Edison holds an unlit bulb. Heisenberg's position is uncertain. Bohr holds an unsent letter. Tolstoy stands opposite Chekhov, holding a prescription. Shakespeare is not there: he is the water below the bridge. Spinoza has glass dust on his hands. Aristotle crouches to lay the floor. Faraday crouches to lift a plank. Maxwell stands writing equations.

Joan of Arc floats above the bridge, carrying fire. Wilde stands with easy elegance, holding that sentence. Ramanujan has emerged halfway through a gap. Oppenheimer walks on with his burden of ash. Charlotte holds a pen. Emily is out on the moor beyond the bridge; the wind comes from where she is. Boltzmann cradles the stone bearing his equation. Van Gogh holds his brushes, paint on his clothes, color everywhere he has walked.

Dickens glances at Tolstoy.

Two men who have written prescriptions. At this moment, one holds the paper open; the other rolls it tight. Not because we already know whose mind is more honest. Here at the bridge, neither has yet spoken.

Dickens keeps his paper tightly rolled. The edge of the matchbox presses into his palm. He shifts one foot. The crack does not close with the movement.

There are letters inside it.

Not a newly written sentence of the court. Letters he wrote, which Catherine received and kept. They did not disappear from someone else's hands because he later picked up a match.

The paper lies below him. He recognizes his own handwriting.

Kant stands in the distance. He looks at Dickens, and at the paper below his feet. The warm house for readers is still lit. Its light has not gone out. Nor has it closed the crack.

No one asks Catherine for proof that she was once loved before allowing her a place here. She does not have to make his novels great.

Dickens still holds the pen. It can go on writing. It cannot turn the recipient's life into a footnote supplied by him alone.

He walks toward Kant. Unsteadily. He still has the matchbox, but no match is struck.

The crack makes a very small sound beneath his feet. Like a letter being folded.
