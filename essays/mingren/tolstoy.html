托尔斯泰，药方

Tolstoy, The Prescription

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、没有带走的家

1910年11月10日，按俄国当时的旧历，是10月28日。天还没有亮，八十二岁的托尔斯泰离开雅斯纳亚·波良纳。他带上几样东西，与医生一道出发，没有叫醒妻子。一个人可以轻装出门，却很难轻装离开自己的生活。

这座庄园不是他一生唯一住过的地方，却承受了他太多年的生活：写作、孩子、争执、来客，以及读者看不见的日常安排。门关上之后，这些并不会随着主人的脚步消失。留下的人仍然需要过明天。

途中他病倒，在阿斯塔波沃下车。站长奥佐林让出家里的房间。曾在小说里调动千军万马的人，现在需要一张床，需要有人听他的呼吸，需要别人决定什么时候让访客进来。身体把所有大问题暂时缩到一间屋里。

索菲亚赶来，起初被医生和家人挡在病房外。他去世前，她终于获准进去，但那时他已经失去意识。她并非永远停在窗外；真正令人难受的是，门最后打开了，两个人却已经无法再把话说完。

11月20日，即旧历11月7日，托尔斯泰去世。我们知道出走之前围绕日记、遗嘱和作品处置的冲突，也知道他对自己生活的不满。可是，把所有这些压成“逃离妻子”或“奔向自由”，都太省事了。一个人最后的路，不会自动替他一生作证。

他留给人的难题也在这里：如果我终于认定某种生活才是对的，我能否马上照着它活？如果改变牵动妻子、孩子和依靠我的人，他们是等待被纠正的障碍，还是必须与我一起回答这个问题的人？

二、名声回答不了的问题

在《忏悔录》中，托尔斯泰写下一种让旁人很难理解的停顿。生活一切都在向前：土地要经营，孩子要教育，书还能继续写。可他忽然问，然后呢？得到更多，写得更好，赢得更大的名声，然后呢？

这不是一个失败者替失败寻找解释。写过《战争与和平》和《安娜·卡列尼娜》的人，已经获得太多别人梦想的东西。也正因为如此，他不能轻易把心里的空缺推给下一件尚未得到的奖品。

《忏悔录》写作于1879至1882年间，1882年的俄国出版计划遭到审查阻止，1884年在日内瓦出版。这些年份背后，是一个人不得不承认：自己的书可以使别人落泪，却未必能回答自己早晨为什么起床。

他转向普通劳动者的信仰，重新阅读福音书，越来越无法接受教会为财富、战争和权力提供的祝福。他要找的不是一场更体面的仪式，而是一种可以落在手脚上的生活：少占有，自己劳动，不用暴力支撑自己的安稳。

但拿一条线把他切成前期小说家、后期布道者，会错过最值得读的部分。晚年的他仍然写《复活》，写《哈吉·穆拉特》。道德要求进入了小说，小说里具体的人又不断把要求撑开，两种声音一直在同一张书桌上争论。

我们也可能认识这样的时刻。昨天还管用的理由，今天突然不够了。困难不在于找不到一句正确的话，而在于一句话即使正确，也不能代替我们去与仍然生活在旧安排里的人商量，明天究竟怎么过。

三、药方也有它的勇气

把托尔斯泰的主张放在一起，确实像一张很长的药方：简朴，劳动，节制欲望，拒绝剥削，不以暴力对付恶。他还主张素食，对性抱有越来越严厉的看法。这些主张并不因为出自同一个人，就具有同样的理由和同样的分量。

其中最锋利的一问，是我们今天仍会绕开的：如果我承认杀人不对，为什么换上一套制服，接到一道命令，这件事就突然对了？托尔斯泰把良心放回执行命令的人手里。上面有人下令，不意味着下面的人就没有在行动。

他的“不抵抗”主要指不以暴力抵抗恶，并不是闭眼不管。在《天国在你们心中》里，拒绝参军、拒绝参与强制，都是实际的抵抗；拒绝的人可能失去自由，甚至付出生命。这里没有一张可以舒服旁观的椅子。

甘地读到这本书，受到很深的震动。但他不是拿到药方便原样照吃的人。他同时阅读不同宗教，与不同思想者交往，又在具体斗争中改变行动办法。一个思想能够传远，往往因为接到它的人也在思考，而不只是服从。

托尔斯泰的反暴力立场值得认真听，他的禁欲要求却不必因此被一起吞下。我们可以问，谁会因为这条规则得到保护，谁的身体和意愿反而会被怀疑？敬重一个人的勇气，不等于把自己的判断一并交出去。

这也是“药方”这个比喻该保留的地方。它既提醒我们警惕包治一切的承诺，也提醒我们不要嘲笑那个真想止痛的人。托尔斯泰看见了很多被日常体面遮住的伤口。他的急切，首先来自不愿再把伤口当成正常生活。

四、索菲亚也在这张纸上

现在把目光从书桌上的道理挪开一点。旁边还有另一张桌子，上面放着抄写的手稿、出版事务和家里的账目。索菲亚不仅是一个不同意丈夫的妻子，也是他的文学合作者，是维持这个家庭运转的人。

她辨认他的字迹，抄写和整理作品，参与出版，照料孩子，也有自己的写作、摄影和绘画。只把她写成抓住钱财不放的人，我们就会重做一遍她所面对的事：丈夫的精神生活值得叙述，她的工作只配充当背景。

托尔斯泰想摆脱财产与声望对自己的支配，愿望并不虚假。索菲亚担心家庭和作品的处置，关切也不只是庸俗。两人身处同一座房子，却未必握有相同的选择：一个人宣布减轻负担，可能意味着另一个人的负担增加。

想象一个更普通的家庭：有人忽然决定，从此不再追逐收入，要去过真正有意义的生活。这可能是一次诚实的觉醒。可房租、照护、已经许给别人的承诺，并不会因为“意义”两个字而自动取消。它们需要重新谈，不是被一句话赶走。

这里不能用“他太伟大，所以现实容不下”替他开脱。理想使一个人痛苦，不等于理想就不会伤害别人。选择牺牲自己的便利，与替伴侣决定她应当牺牲什么，是两件事；后者不能躲进前者的光里。

索菲亚也不必被换成另一个完美人物。两个人都可能有控制、恐惧和难以退让的时刻。重要的是，他们之间的问题不只是圣人与世俗的争执。她有一段不能被他的精神传记吞掉的人生，这一点不需要她先证明自己从未做错。

五、契诃夫把椅子拉近一点

契诃夫也曾被托尔斯泰深深吸引。1894年3月写给苏沃林的信里，他承认这种影响持续了多年，然后坦率地讲出自己的离开。那不是年轻作家为了出名推翻前辈，而是一个人发现，敬爱已经不能代替判断。

信中一个很具体的比较尤其有意思：电力和蒸汽对人的帮助，在他看来可以超过禁欲和不吃肉。把庄严的道德生活与这些机器放到一起，乍看近乎不敬，却把问题拉回了地面：究竟是什么使人的日子少一点苦？

契诃夫不是没有伦理，也不是看见伤害便保持优雅的沉默。他对暴力和虚伪有判断。只是，他不愿从“战争是恶”直接跳到“所以人人都该穿草鞋、照某一种方式生活”。诊断有道理，整张处方仍然需要一项项看。

我们不妨想象两个医生面对同一个病人。一个急切地谈生活该怎样改变，另一个先把椅子拉近，问昨夜到底哪里疼。这不是谁有爱、谁没有爱；是爱在一种时刻需要远望，在另一种时刻需要忍住替人回答的冲动。

托尔斯泰令人不安的力量，在于他不让我们轻易安于现状。契诃夫的提醒，在于现状中的人也有自己的声音。若只留下前者，别人容易成为改革的材料；若只留下后者的安静，又可能拿理解当作什么都不做的借口。

两个人可以在这里互相打断。我们不必把一个写成永远命令、另一个写成永远不判断。真正的照护也会说“不”，真正的劝告也可以等待回答。问题是，房间里还有没有一张椅子留给那个被劝告的人。

六、不只是换一颗心

把托尔斯泰和马克思放在一起，会看见两种不同的急迫。一种追问：我今天怎样停止参与伤害？另一种追问：什么样的社会安排，让人即使努力做个好人，也不断被推回伤害之中？两问都不是多余的。

一个雇主少拿一些利润，工人也许当月便能过得好一点。但如果工人没有议价的权利，下一任雇主是否仁慈，又会决定他们的生活。改变个人可以立刻有用，却不能因此取消对制度的追问。

反过来，一个人也可以把一切责任都推给制度，等到世界彻底改变再决定今天怎样对待下属。受辱的人却正在眼前，不能靠未来的制度领到今天的道歉。把良心延期，同样可能成为一种很方便的生活方式。

两位思想者都比这个对照复杂。托尔斯泰猛烈批判国家和财产秩序；马克思在《关于费尔巴哈的提纲》中提醒，改变环境的人自己也必须改变，教育者也需要受教育。没有一个人天然站在所有人的生活之外，专门负责开药。

所以，我们不必拿一个国家的兴亡给马克思盖棺，再拿一个运动的成就替托尔斯泰结账。思想被接受、改造、组织成行动，中间有无数人的选择。那些人受过的伤，不能被胜利消除；他们做成的事，也不能被失败一笔抹去。

对普通人而言，这场争论可以收成一个不那么宏大的问题：眼前我能停止哪一种伤害，又能和别人一起改变哪一种安排？两件事也许都很小，却不必等另一件已经彻底完成，才获准开始。

七、理想不是搬家通知

《理想国》里的城邦，与托尔斯泰心中的好生活，都让人看见另一种可能。人不必永远照现在这样争夺、炫耀、互相利用。想象更好的生活，本身不是错误；没有这点不肯认命的想象，我们连批评现实的语言都会变少。

柏拉图在《理想国》第九卷也谈到，一个人可以望着理想的城邦安排自己，即使地上没有这样的城。这幅图先是一种参照，不是一封要求全体居民明天搬进去的通知。理想的用途，未必只能由它是否完整建成来裁定。

难处发生在图纸碰到具体的人时。有人不想住指定的房间，有人需要更多照顾，有人认为设计者所说的幸福不是自己的幸福。这些回答可能麻烦，却不是图纸上的污点。它们来自真正要在里面生活的人。

托尔斯泰对自己要求很高，我们可以看见其中的诚实。但对自己严格，并不自动产生替别人选择的资格。一个人能够承受的清贫、孤独和节制，也不会仅凭他的证明，就成为别人的义务。

同时，做不到也不等于所有要求都该扔掉。说话尖刻的人仍该学习不羞辱别人；依赖特权的人仍可减少特权造成的伤害。问题不是先成为圣人，才准许向好处走，而是走的时候有没有把别人当作自己的垫脚石。

因此，药方要留下可以修改的空白。那不是给敷衍准备的退路，而是留给经验、后果和异议的位置。如果一种好生活必须让所有反对者闭嘴才能成立，我们至少应该再看一眼，“好”究竟是对谁说的。

八、有人托住他的腿

《伊万·伊里奇之死》把这些问题放回一间病房。主人公一直努力过一种得体的生活：职位合适，房子体面，往来的人也合乎身份。他并不觉得自己做过什么惊人的坏事，直到病痛使那些理由一件件失去重量。

最折磨他的，不只是身体疼，而是周围人还在维护“事情会好的”那套礼貌。他快死了，别人却像在处理一个不宜扰乱日程的麻烦。他过去借来保护自己的体面，现在把他一个人关在里面。

年轻仆人盖拉西姆做的事很具体：清理、扶起、托住他的腿，让疼痛减轻一些。他不假装不知道死亡，也不要求病人表现得像个坚强的人。这里的善有重量，有时间，会让照护者的身体一起留在房间里。

到了结尾，儿子捧住父亲的手，吻它，哭起来。伊万看见儿子，又看见妻子的眼泪，开始为他们难过。他不再只问为什么自己遭受这一切，也想让他们少受一点苦。接着，小说写出了光。

这当然是一种回答，并不只是把问题悬在那里。托尔斯泰让人的转变发生在怜惜和关系之中。不过，它不是一份先列好条款、再让人物签字的证明。读者得陪这个人经过恐惧、防卫和疼痛，才走到那个瞬间。

小说的力量，也许正在于此：它使“爱别人”从一句我们早已听熟的话，重新变成一件尚未学会的事。知道应当爱，与愿意托住一个人的腿、看见自己使谁难过，中间仍隔着一生所要练习的距离。

九、纸的另一面

让我们回到这个系列想象中的桥。托尔斯泰走来，手里拿着一张写得密密麻麻的纸。他没有撕掉它。那些关于不伤害、少占有的提醒，仍然使他无法安心；但纸边已经皱了，像被一个人反复拿起又放下。

休谟把双陆棋棋盘合上一半，给他让出地方。柏拉图的图纸被一块小石头压着。远处，爱迪生和特斯拉还在讨论怎么把灯接好。这些人在我们的想象里暂时停下自己的事情，并不是已经同意了同一个答案。

契诃夫接过纸，认真看了看。这里不必安排一个看穿一切的微笑。他也曾急切，也曾对某种回答着迷；如果他问什么，也许会先问，照这张纸生活的人，昨晚睡得好吗？

托尔斯泰正要回答，桥边出现了一张空椅子。我们把它留给索菲亚，不替她决定是否坐下，也不替她写出原谅。关于这个家庭的叙述，到这里仍有一个不能由丈夫代言的位置。

纸上写着爱邻人。椅子离他很近。这种距离，比从俄国到印度更短，有时却更难走。一个人可以真诚地关心远处受苦的人，同时听不清离自己最近的人已经说了很久的话。

契诃夫把纸翻过来。背面还有空白。在这个想象里，他没有另开一张包治百病的方子，只把笔放在旁边。也许要添的不是更严厉的一条，而是一个名字，一件尚未答应的事，一句还没有让别人说完的话。

阿斯塔波沃的火车已经开走了，我们不能把故事写回去，补出一次没有发生的完整和解。但我们还没有坐上自己的最后一班车。今天有人提出异议时，我们仍有可能先不把它诊断成他的病。

托尔斯泰看着纸，又看向那张椅子。桥上没有人催他作结论。药方仍在，笔也在；这一次，空白不只是等他一个人填写。

I. The House He Could Not Pack

Before dawn on 10 November 1910—28 October by the calendar then used in Russia—eighty-two-year-old Leo Tolstoy left Yasnaya Polyana with his doctor. He took a few belongings. He did not wake his wife. Travelling light is easier than leaving a life lightly.

The estate had held decades of writing, children, visitors and quarrels. It also held the less photogenic work that made all those things possible. Once the door closed, that work remained. Whoever stayed behind would still have to arrange tomorrow.

He became ill on the journey and stopped at Astapovo. The stationmaster, Ivan Ozolin, made rooms available in his house. A novelist who had moved armies across the page now needed a bed, someone to listen to his breathing, someone to decide who could enter.

Sofia arrived but was initially kept from him by doctors and family. She was admitted shortly before his death, when he was already unconscious. The door did eventually open. What it could no longer give them was time to finish speaking.

He died on 20 November, or 7 November Old Style. Conflicts over diaries, a will and the future of his writings lay behind the departure, alongside his dissatisfaction with his life. Neither escape from a wife nor escape into freedom contains the whole journey.

A harder question survives the scene. When I decide that a different life is right, may I simply begin? What about the people whose lives are bound to mine? Are they obstacles to my conversion, or people entitled to help decide what change will mean?

II. What Success Could Not Answer

In A Confession, Tolstoy describes a peculiar interruption. His affairs continued to require attention: land to manage, children to educate, books to write. Yet a question kept breaking through. Suppose all these things went well. Suppose he became more famous. What then?

This was not an unsuccessful writer explaining away failure. The author of War and Peace and Anna Karenina possessed many of the things other people postponed happiness to obtain. He could no longer put the missing answer safely inside the next accomplishment.

Written between 1879 and 1882, A Confession was prevented by censorship from appearing as planned in Russia in 1882 and published in Geneva in 1884. Behind that history stood an uncomfortable admission: moving readers did not tell its author why he should get up tomorrow.

He turned towards the faith of working people and read the Gospels anew. Increasingly he rejected religious institutions that blessed wealth, war and obedience. He wanted a belief that reached his hands: less possession, honest labour, no violence underwriting his comfort.

The novelist did not simply resign when the moral thinker appeared. Late Tolstoy still wrote Resurrection and Hadji Murad. Ethical urgency entered the fiction; people in the fiction exceeded the neatness of ethical demands. Both kinds of attention continued working at the same desk.

We may recognise the smaller version of that crisis. Yesterday's reasons no longer carry us through today. Finding a true sentence can matter enormously. It still cannot negotiate, on our behalf, with people living inside the arrangements we suddenly want to leave.

III. The Courage in the Prescription

Taken together, Tolstoy's recommendations resemble a formidable prescription: live simply, work, refuse exploitation, restrain desire, do not answer evil with violence. Vegetarianism and increasingly severe sexual restraint belonged to it too. Sharing an author's signature does not give every item equal force.

One question remains particularly sharp. If killing is wrong, why should a uniform and a command make it right? Tolstoy returns conscience to the person carrying out the order. Someone above you may have decided, but someone below that decision must still act.

Nonresistance, for him, meant refusing violent resistance, not pretending nothing was wrong. The Kingdom of God Is Within You describes refusal of military service and participation in coercion. Such refusals could bring imprisonment or worse. This was no invitation to watch comfortably from a distance.

Gandhi describes the book's profound effect on him. But he was a reader with other teachers, religious traditions and practical encounters, not a blank vessel receiving an entire movement. He adapted what he learned. Ideas often travel because their recipients also think.

We can take Tolstoy's challenge to violence seriously without accepting his sexual ethics wholesale. A rule should face questions about whose safety it protects and whose body or wishes it distrusts. Respect for moral courage does not require surrendering our own judgment.

The prescription metaphor should hold both truths. It warns against promises to cure everything, but should not mock the wish to relieve suffering. Tolstoy saw wounds that respectable society had learnt not to notice. His impatience began in refusing to call those wounds ordinary life.

IV. Sofia Was Not a Footnote

Move your eyes a little from the author's desk. Another surface holds copied manuscripts, publishing business and household accounts. Sofia was not merely the wife who disagreed. She was a literary collaborator and a person doing much of the work that kept this home functioning.

She deciphered his handwriting, copied and organised manuscripts, handled publication and raised children. She also wrote, photographed and painted. Reducing her to a woman protecting money repeats an old division: his inner life becomes literature; her labour becomes scenery.

His wish to escape the authority of property and fame was genuine. Her concern about the family's future and the treatment of his work was not necessarily sordid. Within the same house, they occupied different positions. One person's release from burdens might add to another's.

Imagine a familiar household. Someone announces that earning money has become meaningless and that real life must start now. The awakening may be honest. Rent, care and promises already made do not disappear. They need renegotiation, not banishment by a powerful word like meaning.

Greatness cannot supply an exemption. An ideal that makes its author suffer may still injure other people. Giving up your own comforts and deciding what a partner must give up are different acts. The second cannot borrow the moral glow of the first.

Nor must Sofia become flawless before we listen. Both partners could be frightened, controlling and unwilling to yield. What matters is that her life does not belong wholly inside his spiritual biography. She need not prove herself innocent of every mistake to retain that claim.

V. Chekhov Pulls Up a Chair

Chekhov had felt Tolstoy's influence deeply. Writing to Suvorin in March 1894, he acknowledged years under its spell and described his growing distance. Admiration had ceased to settle his judgments. That is a difficult departure from a teacher, even when no door is slammed.

His comparison was deliberately earthly: electricity and steam could do more for people than chastity and abstinence from meat. Set machinery beside an exalted moral programme and it almost sounds impolite. Yet the comparison asks a serious question: what actually makes living less painful?

Chekhov was not without ethics. He condemned harm and hypocrisy; he did not regard every choice with a beautifully empty smile. He questioned the jump from recognising war's evil to prescribing one costume, diet and manner of life for everyone.

Imagine two doctors at a bedside. One urgently explains how living must change. The other draws his chair nearer and asks where it hurt last night. Neither has a monopoly on love. But love sometimes needs distance to see, and sometimes must stop answering for someone else.

Tolstoy unsettles our readiness to accept things as they are. Chekhov reminds us that the people living there have voices. Keep only the first impulse and people become material for improvement. Keep only the second and understanding can turn into an elegant excuse for doing nothing.

They can interrupt each other without becoming opposites carved in stone. Care sometimes needs to say no. Advice can wait for a reply. The question is whether a room devoted to someone's improvement still contains a chair in which that person may sit and disagree.

VI. More Than a Change of Heart

Tolstoy and Marx bring different urgencies into view. How can I stop participating in harm today? What arrangements keep pushing people back into harm, even when they try to act decently? These questions do not become unnecessary because someone has asked the other one.

An employer who takes less profit may immediately improve a worker's month. But if workers have no bargaining power, their future still depends on the next employer's kindness. Personal change can make a real difference without making institutional change redundant.

The reverse evasion is familiar too. Someone blames the whole system and postpones behaving differently until society has been transformed. Meanwhile, a subordinate stands humiliated in front of him. A future constitution cannot deliver today's apology. Conscience can be deferred for very convenient reasons.

Both thinkers exceed the contrast. Tolstoy attacked states and property arrangements. Marx's third thesis on Feuerbach insists that people change circumstances, and that educators themselves need educating. Neither observation leaves the reformer safely outside the human world, qualified only to prescribe for everyone else.

We need not settle Marx with the fate of one state, or settle Tolstoy with the success of one movement. Between an idea and its consequences stand other people's choices, institutions and adaptations. Success does not erase their injuries; failure cannot erase everything they achieved.

For an ordinary life, the argument yields two manageable questions. What harm can I stop doing? What arrangement can I help others change? The answers may be small. Neither task has to wait until the other has been completed for all humanity.

VII. An Ideal Is Not an Eviction Notice

Plato's imagined city and Tolstoy's good life both refuse the belief that competition, vanity and exploitation are all we can expect. That refusal matters. Without the ability to picture something better, we lose part of the vocabulary with which to challenge the present.

Book IX of the Republic allows a person to look towards the ideal city in ordering a life, whether or not that city exists on earth. A model can be an orientation. It need not arrive as notice that everyone must move into it tomorrow.

Trouble begins when a plan encounters inhabitants. Someone dislikes the assigned room; someone needs care the architect did not anticipate; someone wants a different happiness. Their replies complicate the drawing. They are also the voices of the people who must actually live there.

Tolstoy's severity towards himself contains real honesty. But being demanding of yourself does not confer authority over somebody else's choices. The poverty, loneliness or abstinence you can bear does not become another person's obligation merely because you have demonstrated that it is possible.

Failure is no reason to discard every demand either. A person who speaks cruelly can learn not to humiliate. Someone dependent on privilege can reduce the harm it causes. We need not become saints before making improvements; we must watch whom we step on while making them.

A prescription needs room for revision. That is not a loophole for laziness, but space for experience, consequences and objections. When a good life can be established only by silencing everyone who disputes it, we should ask again whose good it promises.

VIII. Someone Holds His Legs

The Death of Ivan Ilyich returns these questions to a sickroom. Its central figure has spent years arranging a proper life: suitable work, respectable furnishings, appropriate acquaintances. He has done nothing he considers exceptionally wicked. Illness makes those familiar assurances lose their weight.

Pain is not his only torment. People continue maintaining the polite fiction that everything will improve. He is dying; they behave as though an awkward interruption must be managed without disturbing the timetable. The respectability he once used for shelter has shut him inside alone.

Gerasim, the young servant, cleans, lifts and supports his legs, easing the pain. He neither disguises the approach of death nor requires an impressive performance of courage. Here goodness has weight and duration. Someone else's body must remain in the room to practise it.

At the end, Ivan's son takes his hand, kisses it and cries. Ivan looks at the boy and then at his tearful wife, and feels compassion. His attention moves beyond what is happening to him towards what they are suffering. The story gives us light.

That is an answer, not merely a question left suspended. Tolstoy places transformation in compassion and relationship. Yet it is not a list of conditions a character signs to prove an argument. We have travelled through fear, self-defence and physical misery to arrive there.

Perhaps fiction's force lies here. Love other people becomes, once again, something we have not finished learning rather than a sentence we already know. Between that sentence and holding someone's legs, or recognising whom we have hurt, stretches a distance a whole life may have to practise crossing.

IX. The Other Side of the Paper

Let us return to this series' imaginary bridge. Tolstoy arrives holding a sheet covered with instructions. He has not torn it up. The demands to harm less and possess less still trouble him. Its edges are creased from being picked up and put down repeatedly.

Hume half closes his backgammon board to make room. A stone holds down Plato's drawings. Further along, Edison and Tesla are discussing the wiring of a lamp. In this imagined gathering, pausing one's own work does not mean everyone has reached the same answer.

Chekhov takes the paper and reads it carefully. No all-knowing smile is required. He too has known urgency and the enchantment of an answer. If he asks a question, perhaps it is whether the people following these instructions managed to sleep last night.

Before Tolstoy replies, an empty chair appears beside the bridge. We leave it for Sofia. We do not decide whether she sits down, and do not write her forgiveness for her. Her place in the family's story is not one her husband can occupy.

Love your neighbour is written on the page. The chair stands very close. This journey is shorter than the passage from Russia to India, and sometimes harder. A person can sincerely care about distant suffering while failing to hear someone who has spoken beside him for years.

Chekhov turns the sheet over. There is room on the back. In our fiction, he writes no replacement cure for everything; he sets a pen beside it. What belongs there may be a name, an unagreed obligation, or a sentence someone has not been allowed to finish.

The train has left Astapovo. We cannot rewrite the past to provide a complete reconciliation that never happened. But we have not yet boarded our own last train. When someone objects today, we can still refrain from diagnosing the objection as that person's illness.

Tolstoy looks from the paper to the empty chair. Nobody hurries him towards a conclusion. The prescription remains, and so does the pen. This time, the blank space is not waiting for him alone to fill it.
