伽利略，它还是在转

Galileo, And Yet It Moves

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、它还是在转

1633年6月22日，罗马。六十九岁的伽利略在圣母堂修道院宣读悔过书。他被判为有强烈异端嫌疑，必须放弃地球运动、太阳居于中心的主张。一个研究了几十年的人，在别人的要求下，说出自己不再坚持什么。

他签了字。至于起身时有没有低声说一句‘它还是在转’，那是另一回事。这句话没有当时的可靠记录，后来却成了整个故事最响亮的回声。人们愿意替一个被迫沉默的人，留下一句没有屈服的话。

我们可以理解这种愿望，也可以不替他安排最后一刻的英雄表演。那天他害怕到什么程度，权衡了什么，我们不能仅凭一个结局就把他的内心读完。

但传说握住了一件重要的事：宣誓能改变一个人被允许说什么，改变不了天体怎样运动。判决书可以收走他的声音，不能替宇宙签字。

这不是说人受到的伤害不重要。恰恰因为地球不需要他来保卫，我们才更该看见，那个被要求跪下的人，需要有人保卫。

二、望远镜

伽利略1564年出生在比萨，父亲是音乐家。他曾学医，后来转向数学，在比萨和帕多瓦任教。到1609年，他已经做过多年关于运动和仪器的研究，新仪器遇上了他多年积累的经验和问题。

那年，他听说荷兰出现了能把远处东西拉近的装置，便设法制作、改进望远镜。航海者可以用它寻找远处的船，他把它对准了天空。英国的托马斯·哈里奥特也已经用望远镜画过月亮；伽利略的特别之处，不在一个孤零零的‘第一’，而在接下来怎样看、怎样记录，又怎样让更多人知道。

月亮表面有明暗起伏。他观察阴影的变化，推断那里有山谷和高地。天界光滑、完美，与地面截然不同的那幅图景，开始容不下眼前的月亮。

1610年，他连续观察木星附近的亮点，发现它们的位置有规律地变化：四颗卫星绕着木星运行。原来，并不是天上每一个运动，都要把地球作为中心。

同年，金星的完整盈亏变化又带来一个难题：传统托勒密体系解释不了这样的明暗与大小变化。金星绕太阳运行，成了更有力的解释。不过，第谷的体系也让金星绕太阳，同时保留静止的地球。看到金星，并不等于已经看到地球在动。

发现的力量，正在这里。旧图纸上多出了放不进去的东西，你必须重新画；至于新图纸是否已经画完，还得接着看。

三、他凿了什么

哥白尼在1543年出版《天体运行论》，把运动的地球放进一个新的宇宙安排。那不只是方便算数的游戏。他试图说明天体实际怎样运行，只是新的安排仍然有许多问题需要回答。

伽利略让争论多了一种现场感。你不用只在古书和几何图形之间争执，可以在另一个夜晚，把自己的望远镜对准木星，看看那些亮点是否又换了位置。

可‘你自己看’也不是一句使争论自动结束的咒语。镜片可靠吗？亮点会不会是仪器造成的？看见同一个现象，是否只能有一种解释？观测需要反复核对，推断需要接受追问。伽利略自己用潮汐论证地动，就走错了路。

苏格拉底让人把自以为懂得的话再说清楚；马克思追问被说成天经地义的社会关系；伽利略把仪器、记录和数学带进对天空的争论。这是我们在他们之间画出的联系，不是说他们各自拥有一把永不出错的凿子。

伽利略留下的要求更朴素：如果世界交来的东西与你的解释对不上，不能只命令世界闭嘴。这个要求，也必须用在伽利略自己的解释上。

四、谁有权解释天空

争论为什么走到了审判？只说‘教会害怕科学’，会把故事讲得太省事。伽利略自己是天主教徒，教会内部也有认真观察天空、确认他部分发现的人。信仰与观测，没有天然分成两支整齐的队伍。

真正紧张的一处，是谁有权解释经文。当某些经文被读作地球静止的保证，新天文学就不再只是天文学。伽利略认为，谈自然现象应当听取感官经验与论证，不能把经文中的日常说法直接当作天体运行的说明书。

这碰到了解释权，也碰到了当时的宗教冲突、政治处境和服从要求。1616年，他已经被要求不得持守、捍卫哥白尼的主张。十多年后，他能说什么、是否违背过禁令，成为审判的要害。

地球不在中心，并不推出人没有价值，更不自动推翻信仰。危险的地方是，某种制度把自己的一种解释当成不可修改的秩序，于是补上一条事实，也可能被听成拒绝服从。

想到秦始皇控制书籍，我们可以看到一个有限的相似处：当权者试图决定什么可以公开流通。但不同年代的事件不能压成同一个原因。这里要问的，是讨论何时被改造成了服从测试——答错的人，不只需要重新计算，还会失去自由。

五、他跪了

1632年，《关于两大世界体系的对话》出版。三个角色争论，两种体系交锋，读者很容易感到作者站在哪一边。替传统观点辩护的辛普利丘，名字来自古代注释家，也带着容易被讥为愚钝的声音。

教皇乌尔班八世曾经支持伽利略，后来关系破裂。书中把他重视的一个论点放进辛普利丘口中，使冲突更尖锐；但不能把整场审判只写成教皇受了讽刺、恼羞成怒。旧禁令、出版许可和权威问题，都在桌上。

1633年，伽利略受到审讯，也受到酷刑威胁。他最后宣读了悔过书，余生在软禁中度过。我们知道他做了什么，却不能据此宣布，他这个人已经被交了出去。

人们会拿他与苏格拉底比较：一个赴死，一个活了下来。也会想到司马迁，忍受屈辱后继续写作。这些比较能帮助我们理解活下去有多难，不能变成一道考题，要求受压迫的人以死亡证明诚实。

伽利略后来完成并出版了《两门新科学》，继续讨论运动与材料的强度。但即使没有这本书，他也有活下来的资格。后来的成果不是他保全性命必须缴纳的赎金，苏格拉底的思想也不需要由死亡来取得有效性。

地球的运动不取决于他是否认错。一个人的尊严，同样不该取决于他在威胁之下，能不能表演出我们最喜欢的姿态。

六、方法还要有人接住

这个系列常常问：一个人的宏大答案不再能够包办世界以后，他留下的提问方式，还能不能继续帮助我们？牛顿的力学有适用范围，黑格尔的辩证思考、马克思对社会关系的分析，也都可以被后人重新使用、争论和修正。

伽利略留下的，不只是一句‘去看’。他把测量、数学、实验安排和论证放在一起，让对自然的说法承担可以检验的后果。他不是独自发明了全部科学方法，却把许多后来的工作推到了新的起点。

这种做法能够超出一个人的遭遇，但不会自动活下去。书要有人印，记录要有人保存，仪器要有人制作，年轻人要有地方学习。权力改变不了金星的盈亏，却可以使很长一段时间里，少一些人有机会知道它。

到1638年前后，伽利略已经完全失明。晚年的工作依靠书信、来访者和助手继续。他不是一架身体坏了、仍向外输送成果的机器；他会失去亲人，需要照顾，也需要有人听他把一句话说完。

所以，‘方法活了，人碎了’还不是这个故事的终点。我们不能一面庆幸知识传了下来，一面把为它受苦的人当成耗材。让研究能够继续，和让研究的人不必被毁掉，本来就应当是同一份责任。

七、他和哥白尼

哥白尼的书出版于1543年，也是他去世的那一年。他长期在教会体系内工作，书献给教皇，起初没有引起后来那样的官方禁令。不能把这段人生讲成：他逃得快，教会还没来得及审判。

书前那篇把天文理论说成计算假设的匿名序言，是奥西安德加入的，不是哥白尼本人设计的退路。把它还给真正的作者，也就能看清，书来到读者手里时，已经夹带了一种不同于作者意图的阅读方法。

哥白尼提出新安排，伽利略提供重要观测和新的运动论证，两个人的工作相互照亮，却不是一个人负责想、另一个人负责证实那么整齐。第谷、开普勒以及许多观察者也在其中；开普勒的椭圆轨道尤其改变了问题。

布鲁诺1600年在罗马被处死，是这一时代另一件惨痛的事。但他的案件涉及多项神学主张，不能当作‘证明日心说就会被烧死’的简便例证。记住暴力，不需要把历史缩小。

旧宇宙图景的退场，不是一根引信点燃之后的一次爆炸。模型、证据和新的问题，经过几代人的工作，慢慢改变了什么解释能够站住。这里没有一位英雄，替后来所有人把思考做完。

八、世界没有等判决

‘它还是在转’为什么一直打动人？因为它把人的遭遇与世界的运行，放在同一个画面里：这边，一位老人必须改口；那边，木星的卫星还按自己的轨迹移动。

达尔文没有出生之前，自然选择已经发生。杜甫的诗需要人写、需要人读，他笔下那些人的饥饿与失散，却不会因为无人写诗就不存在。苏格拉底没有被处死，他提出的问题也依然可以成立。人与世界的关系，比‘一种余项需要牺牲，另一种不需要’更丰富。

在这里，我们把已有解释装不下、命令也不能抹去的东西叫作余项。这是这篇散文的说法，不是宣称每一个还没解释清楚的现象，都已经构成了某种哲学证明。康德所说的物自体，也不能直接等同于望远镜里看见的天体。

科学不是‘我看见了，所以我永远对’。它更像是愿意让不合预期的结果回来敲门：记录可以重看，模型可以修正，最敬重的人也可以算错。世界不必迁就我们的自尊。

1992年，若望保禄二世在一场讲话中讨论伽利略事件，承认当年神学解释上的错误，并反省学科界限。这种迟来的承认有意义，但它不是地球运动终于获得许可的日期。

地球没有等那三百五十九年。人却等了很久：等一项禁令撤去，等一本书可以公开读，等一个问题不再使提问的人受到惩罚。

九、望远镜朝上

1642年1月8日，伽利略在阿切特里去世，七十七岁，仍在软禁中。按今天通行的公历，次年1月4日牛顿出生；英格兰当时的旧历记作1642年12月25日。真正重要的接力，不在日期的巧合，而在后来者怎样阅读前人的工作。

牛顿读前人的工作，提出新的数学和解释；后来的物理学又发现经典力学的边界。这条路不是预先写好的胜利名单，而是一代代人肯让问题继续存在。

现在，把他带到这个系列想象的桥头。他已经老了，眼睛看不见，手里仍握着一根望远镜。亚历山大有剑，马克思有笔，巴赫有乐谱；他带来的，是请别人再看一次的办法。

这一次，不必让所有人围着他的审判，也不必要求他补说一句勇敢的话。有人接过望远镜，对准天空。有人问起他晚年的生活，愿意听一个老人慢慢回答。

望远镜朝上，关怀留在人间。世界不需要人的批准，人却需要不被强迫交出判断的空间，也需要在害怕、退让、受伤之后，仍被当作一个人。

它还是在转。后来的人，还可以接着看。

资料与说明

九节叙事经科学史资料校订。末节桥头为文学想象；人物间的比较是本文的阅读，不代替各自的历史。观测限制、审判与晚年生平分别参照伽利略博物馆和莱斯大学 Galileo Project；1992年事件参照若望保禄二世原讲话。‘它还是在转’按后世传说处理。

Museo Galileo: Phases of Venus · Museo Galileo: Vita · Rice University: Thomas Harriot · Rice University: Copernican System · John Paul II: 31 October 1992

I. And Yet It Moves

Rome, June 22, 1633. In the convent of Santa Maria sopra Minerva, a sixty-nine-year-old man read his abjuration. Galileo had been found vehemently suspect of heresy. He was required to abandon the claim that Earth moves and the Sun stands at the center. Decades of inquiry had brought him to a room where others prescribed what he must no longer maintain.

He signed. Whether he then murmured ‘And yet it moves’ is another matter. No reliable contemporary record gives us that moment. The later story supplied an irresistible last word: somewhere inside the enforced silence, people wanted a voice that had not yielded.

We can understand the wish without demanding one final heroic performance from him. How frightened was he? What did he weigh against what? The outcome does not make his inward life transparent.

The legend nevertheless holds onto an important distinction. An oath can change what a person is permitted to say. It cannot change the movement of a planet. A judgment may confiscate a voice; it cannot sign on behalf of the universe.

That does not make the injury to the man unimportant. Precisely because Earth did not need him to defend it, we should notice that the man forced to kneel did need defending.

II. The Telescope

Born in Pisa in 1564, Galileo was the son of a musician. He began studying medicine, turned toward mathematics, and taught at Pisa and Padua. By 1609 he had spent years working on motion and instruments. The new device met a store of experience and questions already waiting for it.

Hearing of a Dutch instrument that brought distant objects closer, he set about making and improving telescopes. A sailor could look for a ship. Galileo looked upward. Thomas Harriot in England had already drawn the Moon through a telescope. Galileo’s importance lies less in a solitary claim to be first than in the sustained work that followed: looking, recording, and bringing the results before a public.

The Moon showed changing shadows. Galileo read them as signs of valleys and elevations. The old contrast between an immaculate heaven and a rough, changeable Earth had difficulty accommodating the surface in front of him.

Early in 1610 he followed bright points near Jupiter over successive nights. Their changing positions formed a pattern: four satellites were circling Jupiter. Not every celestial movement needed Earth as its center.

Later that year the full range of Venus’s phases brought another difficulty. The traditional Ptolemaic arrangement could not account for those changes in illumination and apparent size. Venus going around the Sun made better sense. But Tycho Brahe’s arrangement also had Venus circling the Sun while Earth remained still. Seeing Venus was not yet seeing Earth move.

This is what a discovery can do. Something appears that the old drawing cannot accommodate, and the drawing has to change. Whether the replacement is finished is a further question, not a prize awarded with the first observation.

III. What He Opened Up

In 1543 Copernicus published On the Revolutions, placing a moving Earth within a new arrangement of the heavens. This was more than a convenient calculating trick. He was proposing how the heavens actually worked, although his arrangement left much to explain.

Galileo gave the argument a new immediacy. Instead of moving only between old books and geometrical diagrams, another observer could point a telescope at Jupiter on another night and ask whether those small lights had changed position again.

Yet ‘look for yourself’ was no spell that ended disagreement. Were the lenses trustworthy? Could an apparent object be an artifact of the instrument? Might more than one account explain the same sight? Observations needed checking, and inferences needed testing. Galileo’s own attempt to establish Earth’s motion through the tides took him down a wrong road.

Socrates asked people to reconsider words they believed they understood. Marx questioned social arrangements presented as natural necessities. Galileo brought instruments, records, and mathematics into arguments about the sky. That is a connection we draw between them, not a claim that each possessed an infallible tool.

His lasting demand is plainer: when the world delivers something that does not fit your account, you cannot settle the matter by ordering the world to be quiet. That demand applies to Galileo’s account too.

IV. Who May Interpret the Sky?

How did an argument become a trial? ‘The Church feared science’ makes the story too easy. Galileo was himself Catholic. Within the Church were observers who took the sky seriously and confirmed some of his discoveries. Faith and observation did not form two neatly separated armies.

One acute dispute concerned who could interpret Scripture. When certain passages were treated as guarantees of a stationary Earth, astronomy could no longer remain only astronomy. Galileo argued that questions about nature required sensory experience and demonstration; ordinary biblical language should not be used as a manual of celestial mechanics.

The claim touched interpretive authority, religious conflict, political pressures, and obedience. In 1616 he had already been told not to hold or defend the Copernican position. What he was subsequently permitted to say, and whether he had violated that restriction, became central to the proceedings.

Moving Earth away from the center does not logically deprive human beings of worth, nor does it automatically destroy religious belief. The danger comes when an institution identifies its own interpretation with an unalterable order. A new fact can then sound like a refusal to obey.

The comparison with Qin Shi Huang’s control of books offers a limited resemblance: rulers determining what may circulate publicly. It does not give events separated by centuries one common cause. The question here is when discussion becomes a test of obedience—when giving the wrong answer costs not merely another calculation but one’s freedom.

V. He Knelt

The Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems appeared in 1632. Three speakers debate, two cosmic arrangements contend, and the author’s sympathies are not hard to detect. Simplicio, who defends traditional positions, bears the name of an ancient commentator, with an unfortunate echo of foolishness.

Pope Urban VIII had once supported Galileo. Their relationship deteriorated. Putting an argument important to the Pope into Simplicio’s mouth sharpened the conflict, but the trial cannot be reduced to a ruler taking personal offense. Earlier prohibitions, publication permissions, and authority were also at issue.

In 1633 Galileo faced interrogation and the threat of torture. He eventually read the abjuration and spent the rest of his life under house arrest. We know the act. It does not entitle us to pronounce the whole person surrendered.

The comparison with Socrates comes readily: one died, the other remained alive. So does the memory of the Chinese historian Sima Qian, who endured humiliation and continued writing. Such comparisons may help us understand the difficulty of survival. They must not become examinations in which the persecuted are required to prove their honesty by dying.

Galileo went on to complete and publish Two New Sciences, discussing motion and the strength of materials. Even without that book, he would have been entitled to live. A later achievement is not a ransom owed for preserving one’s life. Nor did Socrates’s thought need an execution to become valid.

Earth’s motion did not depend on Galileo’s recantation. A person’s dignity should not depend on producing, under threat, the posture posterity finds most inspiring.

VI. A Method Needs Other Hands

This series repeatedly asks what remains useful when a great answer no longer accounts for everything. Newtonian mechanics has a domain of applicability. Hegel’s dialectical thinking and Marx’s analysis of social relations can be taken up, disputed, and revised. An inheritance need not be either a complete system or a discarded monument.

Galileo left more than an injunction to look. He joined measurement, mathematics, experimental arrangements, and argument, making claims about nature answerable to tests. He did not invent every part of scientific method alone. He helped create a different starting point for much that followed.

That practice can outlive one individual’s fate, but not by magic. Someone must print the books, preserve the records, build instruments, and give younger people somewhere to learn. Power cannot alter the phases of Venus. It can keep people from learning about them for a very long time.

By early 1638 Galileo was completely blind. Letters, visitors, and assistants helped sustain his later work. He was not a damaged apparatus that continued to produce results. He lost people he loved, needed care, and needed someone to hear him finish a sentence.

‘The method survived; the man was broken’ is therefore an inadequate ending. We cannot celebrate the transmission of knowledge while treating those who suffered for it as expendable material. Keeping inquiry alive and allowing inquirers to remain whole belong to the same responsibility.

VII. Galileo and Copernicus

Copernicus’s book appeared in 1543, the year he died. He had long worked within ecclesiastical institutions, dedicated the book to the Pope, and initially encountered none of the later official prohibition. His life cannot honestly be told as a narrow escape from a trial the Church failed to arrange in time.

The anonymous preface presenting astronomical theories as calculating hypotheses was added by Andreas Osiander. It was not Copernicus’s own carefully prepared exit. Restoring its authorship lets us see that the book reached readers accompanied by instructions for reading it that differed from its author’s intentions.

Copernicus supplied a new arrangement; Galileo supplied important observations and arguments about motion. But the division is not simply one man thinking and the other proving. Tycho, Kepler, and many observers also belong in this history. Kepler’s elliptical orbits changed the problem profoundly.

Giordano Bruno’s execution in Rome in 1600 is another terrible event of the period. His case involved multiple theological positions. It cannot serve as a shorthand example of someone burned for proving heliocentrism. Remembering violence does not require making history smaller.

The retreat of the old cosmology was not one explosion after a hero lit the fuse. Models, evidence, and fresh difficulties altered what explanations could survive, through generations of work. No single person completed the thinking on everyone else’s behalf.

VIII. The World Did Not Wait

Why does ‘And yet it moves’ keep its force? It sets a human ordeal and the world’s activity within one frame. On one side an old man must change his words. On the other Jupiter’s satellites continue along their paths.

Natural selection operated before Darwin was born. Du Fu’s poems needed a writer and readers, but the hunger and separation of the people he wrote about did not depend on a poem for their existence. Had Socrates not been executed, his questions could still have mattered. The relation between people and the world is richer than dividing remainders into those that require sacrifice and those that do not.

Here, ‘remainder’ names what an existing account cannot accommodate and a command cannot erase. This is the essay’s way of speaking, not a claim that every unexplained observation supplies a philosophical proof. Kant’s thing-in-itself should not simply be equated with the bodies we see through a telescope.

Science is not ‘I have seen it, therefore I am right forever.’ It is closer to letting an unwelcome result knock again: records can be checked, models revised, and even admired people found mistaken. The world need not accommodate our pride.

In 1992 John Paul II discussed the Galileo affair in an address, acknowledging errors in the theological interpretation of the time and reflecting on disciplinary boundaries. That belated recognition matters. It was not the date on which Earth finally received permission to move.

Earth did not wait those 359 years. People did wait: for a restriction to end, for a book to be read openly, for asking a question to stop putting its asker in danger.

IX. The Telescope, Aimed Upward

Galileo died at Arcetri on January 8, 1642, aged seventy-seven, still under house arrest. In the Gregorian calendar now commonly used, Newton was born the following January 4; England’s calendar recorded December 25, 1642. The important handover lies less in that coincidence than in how later readers took up earlier work.

Newton read earlier work and developed new mathematics and explanations. Later physics encountered the limits of classical mechanics. This is not a prewritten roll of victors. It is a succession of people willing to let a problem remain open.

Bring Galileo now to this series’ imagined bridgehead. He is old and cannot see, but holds a telescope. Alexander brings a sword, Marx a pen, Bach a score. Galileo brings a way of inviting someone else to look again.

This time, nobody needs to gather only around his trial or ask him to supply a missing courageous line. Someone takes the telescope and points it at the sky. Someone else asks about his later life, prepared to listen while an old man answers slowly.

The telescope points upward; care stays here among us. The world needs no human approval. People need room not to be forced out of their judgment, and to remain people after fear, retreat, and injury.

And yet it moves. Those who come after can go on looking.
