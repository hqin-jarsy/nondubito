一百零八种人生 · 062 / 108 · 生命之后留下的工作
Great Lives · 062 / 108 · Work That Outlives a Life

法拉第，手

Faraday, Hands

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、装订学徒

一本书在读者那里是思想，在装订铺里首先是纸、线、胶，是需要压平的书页和不能歪掉的书脊。年轻的法拉第在这两种世界之间工作。他把别人的话订成一本书，下班以后，又把书打开。

1791年，他出生在伦敦南岸，父亲是铁匠。1805年，他开始在乔治·里博的铺子里做装订学徒。他受过的学校教育不多，科学却从工作台边上找到了一个入口：百科全书中的电学文章，还有简·马塞特的《化学谈话》。

许多年后，他写信回忆马塞特，记住的并不只是读懂一本好书的快乐。他会照着书做自己有条件做的小实验，看看书里的说法能不能经得起实物的追问。一段文字因此不再是只有受过高等教育的人才有权理解的东西。它可以拿到桌上，试一试。

这个细节比“穷孩子凭天才逆袭”更值得留下。穷并没有赋予他神奇的眼睛，没上大学也不是发现的捷径。有人把知识写得可以进入，有人让他接触书，有人给了他听讲的机会。他抓住了这些机会，又一点点练出自己的判断。

1812年，他去听汉弗里·戴维的讲座，把笔记整理、装订，后来送给戴维，并请求进入科学工作的世界。书还是那样一本书，纸页却不再只是顾客的货物。一个年轻人把自己能够做到的事放在里面，递了出去。

1813年，皇家研究院雇用了他。起点是实验室助手，不是坐到讲坛上接受掌声。他要准备器材，照管实验，学习日常工作的规矩。但门开了。从此，他可以不只读别人怎样向自然提问，也亲自参加提问。

这双手的重要，不在于手比头脑更诚实，而在于它们没有被固定在一种用途里。装订工的手可以整理证据，学徒也可以形成问题。一个人从原来的位置走出来，不必先把原来的自己丢掉。

二、转起来的导线

1821年，一根通电的导线绕着磁极转了起来。这个动作很小，没有后来电机的外壳和轰鸣，意义却很直接：电与磁的关系不只表现为指针偏一下，还可以产生持续的机械运动。

法拉第并不是独自在一片空白里碰出了这个结果。前一年，奥斯特已经发现电流会使磁针偏转；安培和其他人正积极追问其中的关系。法拉第为电磁研究写综述，重复别人的实验，再改动装置。阅读、复现、想象和手上的调整，在同一张工作台上相遇。

他后来寄出论文，也把小型装置送给别人。发现因此离开了发现者的房间。别人可以接上电源，看看同样的运动是否发生；可以检查哪里需要改进，而不必先相信这个人的眼睛比自己的更可靠。

随着研究推进，他越来越在意物体之间的空间。我们很容易只盯着两端：磁铁在这里，铁针在那里，于是说前者吸引后者。法拉第想追问，这个“之间”发生了什么？改变周围的条件，作用会怎样改变？

铁屑在磁铁附近排列出弯曲的纹路，让这种追问有了可以观看的图形。它们显示的是细小铁屑对磁作用的响应，并不是把空间里原本藏着的绳子抖了出来。“力线”是一种组织观察、提出问题的方式，后来逐渐发展成他对场的理解。

好图像的价值，常常不在于让我们从此不必思考，而在于让原来忽略的地方变得值得思考。对法拉第来说，物体之间不再只是留白。他开始在那里安排实验，而这个新的注意方向，会把许多人带到不同的物理学面前。

三、把看见的交给别人

法拉第没有受过大学数学训练，也不擅长一些同行熟练使用的高等数学。这是真实的限制。然而，翻开他的论文，会遇见大量细致的说明、比较、测量和推论。这个人并不是发现了什么却始终说不出来。

他的电解研究尤其清楚。通入多少电，产生多少化学变化，其中有可以测量的关系。他用数量约束想法，也和别人讨论怎样为新认识命名。“电极”“离子”这样的词背后，有实验，还有与威廉·惠威尔的交流。

我们常把会不会说一种语言，误当成有没有思想。数学当然不只是包装；它能提出日常语言提不出的精确问题。但实验图、步骤、比较和反例也在做真正的思考。一个人尚未掌握前一种语言，并不意味着他在后一种语言里只能等待批准。

法拉第也努力让工作台以外的人明白。他面向公众讲演，给年轻听众讲一支蜡烛：烛液往哪里去，火焰怎样维持，空气参与了什么。一个平常得几乎不值得看的东西，忽然可以领着人走很远。

蜡烛的好处，是听众回家以后仍然认得它。科学没有被留在一场令人自惭形秽的表演里。讲演者当然知道得更多，但知识的距离可以变成路，而不一定变成门槛。

因此，法拉第的手不是无法表达时最后的补偿。它们参与表达：把装置摆好，把变化做给你看，再解释为什么这个变化值得注意。最好的时刻，不是听众惊叹只有他能做到，而是有人开始想，下一次我可以怎样继续问。

四、八月二十九日

1831年8月29日，法拉第使用一个缠着两组绝缘导线的铁环。一组接电池，另一组接检流计。两组导线没有直接接在一起；如果另一边出现电流，便需要解释它怎样发生。

接通电池时，指针动了一下，随即回到原处。电池仍然接着，指针却不再持续偏转。断开时，它又朝相反方向动了一下。真正重要的东西，在接通和断开的瞬间出现，而不是在等待稳稳停住的读数里。

这很容易让人想到自己的工作：我们预先决定成果应该长什么样，随后便把不符合期待的反应当作噪声。法拉第的厉害，不是从未带着期待，而是肯让那个短暂的动作改变问题。也许需要追问的不是“为什么电流没有一直来”，而是“它为什么恰好在这里来了一下”。

在这类闭合回路中，变化的磁通可以产生感应电流。后来把磁铁移入、移出线圈的实验，让关系更容易看清：移动时有响应，停住以后，不能指望同一个静止布置不断送来电流。变化不是插曲，正是关键。

同年，他又用旋转的铜盘研究发电。这里不是从无中取出能量；使圆盘转动需要机械功。实验打开的是能量转换的道路，后来的工程师还要解决规模、效率、输送和可靠性等许多问题。

我们今天可以在发电机和变压器中认出这段工作的后果，但倒着看历史也有危险。那天的铁环不是一座缩小的现代城市，法拉第更不需要预见每一盏灯，实验才算有价值。首先值得解释的，只是一根指针为什么这样动。

发现有时就是这样落在时间里的。把几秒钟放过去，世界似乎仍然安静；把那几秒钟认真留下来，安静本身就有了新的含义。真正持久的变化，从一个不能持久停在那里的偏转开始。

五、戴维打开的门

戴维对他有恩。讲座吸引了他，推荐使他得到工作，欧洲旅行又让他接触更多科学家、实验和讨论。许多年后再回头看，青年法拉第走进科学时，路上确实有这位老师的身影，有他让出来的位置。

但受到帮助，并不等于从此不再受到身份的限制。在1813至1815年的旅行中，科学助手的工作和带有仆役性质的差事交叠在一起。同行可以共享一个科学问题，却仍然在饭桌、车厢和日常安排中被分成不同等级。

这样的经历不必被写成一个励志故事里的必要磨炼。难堪不会自动成就人，也没有必要替难堪寻找功劳。它让我们看见，得到一个机会和被当作能够独立成长的人，还不是同一件事。

后来，电磁旋转研究也牵涉到与沃拉斯顿有关的思想来源和署名争议。关系的紧张可以查考，却不宜被简化成戴维心里只有嫉妒。我们能够确认的是法拉第逐渐成为独立研究者，而这个过程并不总有一条礼貌、顺畅的路。

成年以后，感激一个老师最困难的地方，也许是既不否认当年那只伸过来的手，又不把自己往后的每一步交给那只手决定。法拉第的发现需要来自他自己的判断。老师不能替他完成，也不能因为曾经帮助过他，便成为成果永远的主人。

戴维和法拉第都比一个角色复杂。一个人可以打开门，也可以受自己时代的等级习惯牵制；另一个人可以怀着感激，仍然走得比原来允许的位置更远。保留两面，关系才像人之间发生过的事，而不像为结论搭好的布景。

六、工作台不是孤岛

法拉第的实验记录按段落编号，后来一直编到16041。那不是一万六千零四十一次各自完整的实验，而是一条可以沿着追索的记录链：做了什么，怎样改变，观察到什么，下一次为何接着这样做。

编号很朴素，也很重要。灵感通常以一瞬间的形象流传，记录却留下改口、复查和不顺利的过程。后来的人可以找到一个判断是在哪里形成的，也可以回到同一个地方，对它提出异议。

工作台周围还有别人。查尔斯·安德森长期协助他，法拉第在发表研究时明确感谢过他的细心、稳定和准确。一个装置能够按预期工作，背后有维护、准备、重复和配合。把这些劳动擦掉，剩下的天才故事反而离发现更远。

他也不是靠漫无目的地试东西，把偶然一次次碰成规律。对不同自然作用之间关系的猜想会指导实验；实验的结果又迫使他改变猜想。头脑走在手前面一段，手上的证据又把头脑拉回来。两者不断交换位置。

早年研究光学玻璃，没有得到期待中的实用成功。留下的一些重玻璃，却在1845年的另一类实验中派上用场：当偏振光穿过处在磁场中的适当玻璃时，偏振方向会转动。光和磁之间出现了一种可以检验的联系。

这不是说失败迟早都会得到补偿。许多失败并不会。更值得注意的是，他没有让最初的用途用尽一件东西。做镜片不理想的材料，可以在另一个问题里重新被看见；过去的劳动，也可能带着尚未知道如何使用的经验留下来。

如果把前一篇的亚里士多德请到这里，两人未必需要争论谁推翻了谁的全部世界。一个为解释和分类建立了长久的工具，一个不断让新的观察重新安排问题。思想活得比思想者久，靠的也包括后来的人不必原样照用它。

七、另一种语言来了

1855至1856年，年轻的麦克斯韦发表关于法拉第力线的研究。1857年3月25日，法拉第写信感谢。他承认，看到这么强的数学力量用到这个问题上，起初几乎有些害怕，继而惊讶于这个问题竟承受得这样好。

这封信的动人处，不是一个等待认证的老人终于拿到了合格证。法拉第早已有大量成果、论证和科学声望。他高兴的是自己长期思索的问题，在另一个人的工作里获得了新的展开，而那种展开又不是他最熟悉的方式。

麦克斯韦也不是把图上的线忠实誊写成符号。他选择模型，使用类比，吸收其他研究者的成果，提出新的理论关系。在1860年代的进一步工作中，电磁传播与光的联系被推进到一个新的层次。这样的创造会改变出发点，而不只是保存出发点。

两人的不同，因而不必被排成高低。法拉第擅长把一个模糊的问题带进可操作、可比较的实验，麦克斯韦能用数学发现其中更广的联系。每一种能力都有自己的困难，也都要接受其他证据和思考的检验。

人与人合作时，常有一种不易承认的恐惧：如果你能做我做不到的事，我的工作是不是就变得不够好了？法拉第这封信给出的姿态更宽。他可以承认距离，仍然兴奋；也可以继续寄出自己的论文，期待对方回应，而不只是退到一旁。

让别人把你的工作带到你到不了的地方，需要的不是谦卑表演。它需要你愿意让工作有一个不完全属于自己的将来。麦克斯韦以后还会被别人修正、重写和推进；相遇并没有为科学画上终点。

下一篇将走到那张写着方程的纸前。此刻先记住，两人之间没有一双负责盲做的手和一个负责解释的头脑，而是两种完整的思考，找到了一处可以彼此借力的地方。

八、星期日与实验室

法拉第还是桑德曼派的基督徒。这个人数不多的教会重视《圣经》、共同生活和信徒之间的责任。他的信仰不是成名以后临时安放的装饰，而是家庭、婚姻和日常生活的一部分。

自然具有秩序，各种作用之间可能存在联系，这样的信念可以给一个研究者耐心，却不能替实验决定结果。法拉第对统一性的向往，要在一项项具体关系中经受检验。某种联系没有找到时，虔诚不能让检流计多偏转一点。

这也是他与斯宾诺莎值得相遇、却不宜被写成同一个人的地方。他们对上帝、自然和信仰的理解并不相同。我们可以看见两人都认真安排自己的生活，而不需要宣布他们最终相信的是同一件事。

他和莎拉在1821年结婚。多年后，法拉第在记录荣誉的册子里补上婚姻日期，说这件事带给他的荣誉和幸福远超过那里列下的其他事情。一个人的重要生活，并不都在后来教科书画出的主线上。

廷德尔回忆的法拉第，也会在同事疲倦时来找他喝茶。这一点使实验室不再只是伟大发现的舞台。有人需要休息，有人惦记另一个人是否太累；关心不会因为不能写成科学论文，就变成无关紧要的小事。

把法拉第写得有人情味，不需要削弱他的严格。恰恰是严格和温暖能够住在同一个人身上：在证据面前，不让希望代替结果；在别人面前，不让成果把人全部占满。那双做实验的手，也可以放下器材，招呼人上楼喝茶。

九、手

晚年的法拉第受到健康和记忆问题的困扰，逐渐减少工作。1858年，他得到汉普顿宫附近的住所，1867年8月25日在那里去世。皇家研究院曾容纳了他漫长的工作与生活，晚年的日子又在另一处房屋里慢下来。

铁匠的儿子、做过装订学徒的人，留下的当然不只是几件器材。他留下了许多别人可以接着做的事。今天使用电的人未必知道他的名字，后来修正电磁理论的人也不必服从他的每一种想象。工作走远了，才显出并没有被主人关住。

现在，让我们离开史料，走到这个系列虚构的桥头。那里摆着一张不大的桌子，法拉第把线圈放下，没有先向人群解释自己为什么伟大。他想找一个让大家看得清指针的位置。

亚里士多德走近，先看器材怎样安排，又看法拉第准备怎样说明。两个人可以从不同的地方开始：一个问这是什么关系，一个问我们怎样判断它确实发生了。问题没有因为年代不同，就不能放到同一张桌上。

法拉第移动磁铁，指针偏转；把磁铁停住，指针又回去。他没有把安静的那一刻藏掉。没有动，也属于需要让别人看见的事情。一个好解释，不能只挑选最像自己胜利的片段。

有人想自己试一遍，他便让出位置。第二双手未必像他的手一样熟练，还需要调整，还可能接错。法拉第没有因此把器材立刻收回来。能让别人逐渐学会，和自己一次做得漂亮，是两种不同的耐心。

莎拉站在一旁，他向她望了一眼。她微笑着，等他把椅子挪过来。两个人可以安静地坐一会儿，谈谈桌边的新朋友，也谈些只有他们在意的小事。这一刻属于共同生活过的人，并不需要证明对科学有什么用。

斯宾诺莎也在，但两人不急着把各自的信仰合并。他们可以一起看指针，讨论看到了什么，再把尚未同意的部分留着。共同观看一件事，并不要求先把彼此变成同一个人。

桥的另一端，有个年轻人带着纸走来。麦克斯韦还没有坐下，已经在看桌上的布置。法拉第把地方挪得宽了一点。他知道对方可能会写出自己读得很吃力的东西，也知道这并不妨碍他们从这里开始。

桌上于是多了一张纸。它没有盖住线圈，线圈也没有压住纸。若纸上的关系不合适，可以改写；若实验的安排不充分，可以重做。两个人都不需要靠让对方沉默，来保护自己已经走过的路。

法拉第把手松开。不是因为所有事情已经结束，而是因为另一双手已经伸过来了。

I. The Bookbinder's Apprentice

To a reader, a book offers thoughts. In a binding shop it first presents other demands: paper, thread, glue, pages that must lie flat, a spine that must hold. The young Michael Faraday worked between these two worlds. He bound other people's words together. After work, he opened them.

Born south of the Thames in 1791, the son of a blacksmith, he began his apprenticeship with George Riebau in 1805. His schooling had been limited. Books provided another entrance: an article on electricity in the Encyclopaedia Britannica, and Jane Marcet's Conversations on Chemistry. Knowledge arrived beside the workbench, without requiring him to stop being the person who worked there.

Looking back years later, Faraday remembered more than the pleasure of understanding Marcet. He tried the small experiments his circumstances allowed, checking the explanations against what happened. A printed assertion became something he could question. He did not have to wait for a degree before asking whether it stood up.

This matters more than the familiar tale of poverty overcome by genius. Poverty had given him no supernatural insight, and missing university was no shortcut. Someone made science readable; someone allowed him access to books; someone enabled him to attend lectures. Faraday used those openings and gradually developed judgment of his own.

In 1812 he heard Humphry Davy lecture. He arranged and bound his notes, later sending them to Davy with a request for scientific employment. Binding remained binding. Yet this particular volume carried something beyond a customer's order: a young man's evidence of what he could do, and his hope of being allowed to do more.

The Royal Institution employed him in 1813. He entered as a laboratory assistant, not as a celebrated lecturer. Apparatus needed preparing, experiments needed attention, and there were working routines to learn. Nevertheless, the door was open. He could now participate in questions he had previously encountered on somebody else's page.

The significance of these hands is not that hands are wiser than minds. It is that their purpose had not been settled forever. A bookbinder could arrange evidence; an apprentice could formulate a question. Leaving an assigned position need not mean discarding everything learned there.

II. A Wire Begins to Turn

In 1821 a current-carrying wire revolved around a magnetic pole. The movement was modest, without the casing or roar of a modern motor. Its importance was unmistakable: electricity and magnetism could do more than deflect a needle. Their interaction could produce continuous mechanical motion.

Faraday did not encounter this possibility in an otherwise empty world. Ørsted had shown the effect of an electric current on a magnetic needle the previous year; Ampère and others were investigating its implications. While preparing a survey of electromagnetic research, Faraday repeated experiments and developed arrangements of his own. Reading, replication, imagination, and practical adjustment met at one bench.

He sent out his results and small demonstration models. The discovery could leave the discoverer's room. Elsewhere, someone could supply a current, watch the motion, and examine the arrangement. Understanding would not depend on accepting that Faraday possessed uniquely trustworthy eyes.

Over the following years, his attention increasingly turned to the space between bodies. It is easy to concentrate on the endpoints: here is a magnet, there a needle, and one acts upon the other. Faraday wanted to investigate the between. How did the action vary when the surrounding circumstances changed?

Iron filings formed curved patterns around magnets, giving this attention a visible shape. The filings were responding to magnetic action; they were not exposing hidden strings already lying in space. Lines of force offered a way to organize observations and questions. His thinking about fields developed through sustained work, rather than arriving fully formed in one glance.

A good image does not necessarily relieve us of thought. It makes something previously overlooked worth thinking about. For Faraday, the interval between objects ceased to be a blank in the picture. It became a place where experiments could be made, and where physics might have to change.

III. Making What He Saw Available

Faraday lacked a university mathematical education and did not command some of the advanced techniques familiar to his colleagues. That was a genuine limitation. But his papers contain detailed descriptions, measurements, comparisons, and arguments. Here was no mute discoverer waiting for someone else to explain what his hands had accidentally found.

Electrolysis makes the point particularly clearly. The quantity of electricity passing through a system and the resulting chemical change stood in measurable relations. Numbers constrained his proposals. Naming mattered too: the vocabulary of electrodes and ions developed through scientific work and exchanges with William Whewell. Precision had several tools available to it.

We often mistake fluency in one language for possession of thought itself. Mathematics is certainly more than attractive packaging; it makes possible questions ordinary speech cannot state precisely. Yet diagrams, procedures, comparisons, and counterexamples also perform intellectual work. Difficulty with the first language does not reduce everything done in the others to a request for permission.

Faraday also worked to reach people beyond the laboratory. In public lectures he could begin with a candle: where did its material go, how was the flame sustained, what part did the air play? Something almost too familiar to notice became an entrance into a much larger world.

The candle had the advantage of remaining recognizable when the audience went home. Science had not been confined to an intimidating performance. The lecturer knew much more than his listeners, but the distance between them could become a route to travel, rather than a barrier protecting his position.

His hands participated in that explanation. Arrange the apparatus, make the change visible, then explain why it matters. The best outcome was not merely admiration for something only Faraday could do. It was the listener beginning to wonder what further question might be possible.

IV. August 29

On August 29, 1831, Faraday used an iron ring carrying two separate coils of insulated wire. One was connected to a battery, the other to a galvanometer. The two circuits had no direct electrical connection. Any response in the second circuit required an explanation.

When he connected the battery, the needle moved and returned. The battery remained connected, but the deflection did not continue. On breaking the connection, the needle moved briefly in the opposite direction. The decisive event occurred in the transitions, not in the steady reading someone might have expected to obtain.

It is a recognizable difficulty far beyond laboratories. We decide beforehand what success should look like and classify everything else as noise. Faraday's achievement was not freedom from expectations. It was allowing a short-lived response to alter his question: instead of only asking why the current failed to continue, ask why it appeared at precisely that moment.

In a closed conducting circuit of this kind, a changing magnetic flux can induce a current. Moving a magnet into or out of a coil made the relationship particularly accessible. Motion produced a response; holding the arrangement still did not provide an endless supply. Change was not an interruption of the important part. It was the important part.

Later that year he investigated generation using a rotating copper disc. Energy was not being summoned from nothing: turning the disc required mechanical work. A route for converting energy had opened. Engineers would still have to solve problems of scale, efficiency, transmission, and reliability before such principles could serve ordinary households.

We can recognize the consequences in generators and transformers, but reading history backward has its dangers. The ring was not a modern city in miniature. Faraday did not need to foresee every lamp that might eventually be lit for the experiment to matter. First there was a movement of a needle demanding an account.

Discovery sometimes inhabits time like this. Let a few seconds pass and everything appears quiet again. Preserve them carefully, and the quiet has acquired a new meaning. A lasting change began with a deflection that would not last.

V. The Door Davy Opened

Davy helped him. The lectures drew Faraday in; Davy's recommendation secured employment; continental travel introduced him to scientific people, experiments, and conversations. Looking back across the years, the younger man's entrance into science still contains the figure of this teacher and the space he made available.

Assistance, however, did not abolish social rank. During their travels of 1813–1815, Faraday's duties as a scientific assistant overlapped with more menial service. People could share a scientific problem while occupying sharply different places in the arrangements of daily life. An intellectual opportunity and recognition as an equal were different things.

There is no need to treat humiliation as the necessary tuition charged by greatness. Difficulty does not automatically improve a person. These circumstances instead show how an institution or patron can admit someone's abilities while still restricting the position from which those abilities may speak.

Later, the electromagnetic rotation work became entangled in disputes over acknowledgment and ideas associated with Wollaston. Friction can be investigated; Davy's whole inner life cannot be reduced to jealousy. Faraday's emergence as an independent investigator was real, and its route was not uniformly gracious or straightforward.

Perhaps one of the hardest forms of gratitude is remembering the hand that once helped you without giving it permanent control over your direction. Faraday needed to make judgments of his own. A teacher could not perform them for him, nor could past assistance establish lasting ownership of the pupil's future discoveries.

Both men were larger than their assigned roles. Someone can open a door and remain constrained by the hierarchies of his time. Someone else can remain grateful and still move beyond the place originally available to him. Holding these truths together restores a human relationship where a tidy moral fable might otherwise stand.

VI. A Bench Is Not an Island

Faraday numbered his experimental notes in paragraphs, eventually reaching 16,041. These were not sixteen thousand individually complete experiments. They formed a chain of records: what had been done, what was altered, what appeared, and why the next investigation followed.

Numbering is an unglamorous practice with considerable power. Inspiration survives publicly as a moment; records preserve reconsideration, checks, and disappointment. Another investigator can retrace the formation of a judgment and return to the same point with an objection. Memory alone is relieved of an impossible burden.

Other people worked around that bench. Charles Anderson assisted Faraday for many years, and Faraday publicly acknowledged his care, steadiness, and accuracy. Apparatus depends on preparation and maintenance as well as conception. Erase this labor to improve the portrait of genius, and the portrait becomes less faithful to the discovery.

Nor was his method aimless manipulation rewarded by repeated accidents. Proposals about relations among natural powers guided experiments. Their results compelled him to reconsider those proposals. Thought moved ahead of the hands; evidence obtained through the hands called thought back. Leadership passed between them.

An earlier project to develop optical glass failed to deliver its hoped-for practical success. Some of the heavy glass later became useful in a different investigation. In 1845, polarized light passing through suitable glass in a magnetic field showed a rotation of its plane of polarization. Light and magnetism had acquired an experimentally testable connection.

This is not a promise that every unsuccessful project will eventually pay off. Many do not. What matters here is that the first intended use did not exhaust the material. Something disappointing as an optical product could reappear inside another question. Earlier labor sometimes leaves knowledge whose possible uses are not yet clear.

If Aristotle, the subject of the preceding essay, could visit this bench, their encounter would not need to become a contest over who had destroyed the other's entire world. One built enduring tools of explanation and classification; the other rearranged questions through fresh observations. Thought outlives its makers partly because successors can use it differently.

VII. Another Language Arrives

In 1855–1856 the young James Clerk Maxwell published work on Faraday's lines of force. On March 25, 1857, Faraday wrote his thanks. Encountering such mathematical power brought to bear on the subject had initially frightened him, he admitted; then he was struck by how well the subject stood up to it.

The letter is moving for a reason more interesting than an old man finally receiving certification. Faraday already possessed substantial discoveries, arguments, and scientific recognition. A question he had pursued for years was opening further inside somebody else's work, in a language less familiar to him. He could be delighted without pretending the difference had vanished.

Maxwell, in turn, was doing more than copying drawn lines into symbols. He chose models, used analogies, incorporated other investigators' work, and proposed new theoretical relations. In the developments of the 1860s, the connection between electromagnetic propagation and light reached another level. Creative continuation changes a starting point as well as preserving it.

The difference between the two men need not be arranged into a ranking. Faraday could bring an elusive problem into experiments that could be performed and compared. Maxwell could uncover wider relations mathematically. Each achievement had its own difficulties; each remained answerable to further evidence and thought.

Collaboration sometimes provokes a fear difficult to confess: if you can do something I cannot, has my work become inadequate? Faraday's letter offers a more generous response. He could acknowledge a distance and remain excited. He could send further papers and invite a reply, rather than either withdrawing or demanding that the conversation stay on familiar ground.

Allowing another person to carry your work somewhere you cannot reach is not a performance of humility. It gives the work a future that does not belong entirely to you. Maxwell himself would later be revised, reformulated, and extended. Their meeting supplied no final ending to science.

The next essay will approach the page of equations. For now, remember that there was not one pair of blindly working hands and another man's interpreting brain. Two complete ways of thinking found a place where they could strengthen each other.

VIII. Sunday and the Laboratory

Faraday was also a Sandemanian Christian. His small religious community emphasized Scripture, shared life, and responsibilities among believers. Faith was not a decoration added after scientific success. It belonged to his family background, marriage, and ordinary practices.

Belief in an ordered nature and in possible connections among its powers could sustain a researcher's patience. It could not determine an experimental result. Faraday's hope of unity had to face tests of particular relations. Where a connection did not appear, devotion could not produce an extra deflection of the needle.

Here he can meaningfully meet Spinoza without becoming another version of him. Their understandings of God, nature, and religion differed. We can attend to the seriousness with which both arranged their lives without announcing that they had really believed the same thing all along.

Faraday married Sarah in 1821. Years later, in a volume recording honors, he added the date of their marriage and described its happiness and honor as surpassing the other entries. Important parts of a life do not always fall on the line subsequently drawn by textbooks.

Tyndall also remembered a colleague who would come looking for him when he seemed tired, inviting him upstairs for tea. The laboratory was inhabited by people as well as discoveries. Someone needed rest; someone noticed. An act did not become insignificant merely because it could not be published as a scientific result.

Making Faraday human need not soften his rigor. Rigor and warmth could coexist: hope must not replace evidence, and accomplishment must not consume every part of a person. The hands that arranged an experiment could put down the apparatus and beckon a colleague upstairs.

IX. Hands

In later life, health and memory difficulties restricted Faraday's work. In 1858 he received a house near Hampton Court, where he died on August 25, 1867. The Royal Institution had accommodated decades of work and domestic life. His later days slowed within another home.

The blacksmith's son who had become a bookbinder left more than apparatus behind. He left things other people could continue. Most people using electricity would never need to know his name. Later physicists would not have to accept every aspect of his imagination. The work traveled because its owner had not enclosed it within himself.

Now we leave the historical record for this series' imagined bridge. A small table stands there. Faraday sets down a coil without first explaining why the gathering ought to admire him. He wants a position from which everyone can see the needle.

Aristotle approaches, studying the arrangement and the explanation being prepared. They can begin from different questions: what relation is this, and how shall we establish that it occurs? The centuries between their lives need not prevent the questions from sharing a table.

Faraday moves the magnet; the needle responds. He stops; it returns. He does not conceal the quiet interval. An absence of movement also deserves to be shown. An explanation should not select only those moments that most closely resemble its author's triumph.

Someone asks to try. He makes room. The next pair of hands is less practiced, requires adjustment, and may connect something incorrectly. Faraday does not immediately take the apparatus away. Helping somebody learn and producing a beautiful demonstration oneself require different kinds of patience.

Sarah stands nearby, and he looks toward her. She smiles while he moves a chair into place. They can sit quietly together, discussing their new acquaintances and smaller things that matter only to themselves. This moment belongs to people who shared a life; it need not demonstrate its usefulness to science.

Spinoza is there too, but neither man rushes to merge their beliefs. They can watch the needle together, discuss the observation, and leave disagreements in place. Looking at something in common does not require becoming the same person first.

A younger man approaches from the other end of the bridge, carrying paper. Maxwell is examining the arrangement before he has even sat down. Faraday clears more space. He knows that what arrives on the page may be difficult for him to read. They can begin together nevertheless.

The paper does not cover the coil; the coil does not pin down the paper. A proposed relation can be rewritten, an arrangement improved and tried again. Neither man needs to protect the road already traveled by silencing the other.

Faraday releases his grip. Not because the work is finished, but because another pair of hands has reached the table.
