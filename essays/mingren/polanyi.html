波兰尼：你来

Polanyi: You Come

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、你知道的比你说得出来的多

“我们能知道的，比我们能说出来的多。”

《默会维度》出版于1966年，来自波兰尼1962年在耶鲁的泰瑞讲座。开篇的这句提醒，很短，却会改变我们看待知识的姿势。

你认识一张脸。你能在一千张脸中认出它。但你说不出来你是怎么认出来的。你不能开列一个清单——鼻子的角度，眼距，颧骨的弧线——然后说"这就是为什么我认出了你"。你的认识比你的言语更多。

你会骑自行车。你能骑。但你不能精确地描述你的身体在做什么。梅洛-庞蒂上一篇说了身体先于概念知道。波兰尼在这里收线：那些身体知道但概念说不出来的东西，不是知识的残余。是知识的地基。

他的强主张是：明确表达出来的知识，也需要默会的理解来支撑。不是所有说不清的感觉都叫知识，而是任何说明都不能把理解它所需的本领一起彻底装进去。

你说“地球绕太阳转”。这可以写成命题，也可以继续解释“绕”“空间”“运动”。但再长的说明，仍需要有人会看图、会理解关系、知道怎样把词用到世界上。这里不是反对说明，而是问：说明凭什么能够被理解？

二、从化学到哲学

波兰尼是一个奇怪的人。

他是匈牙利犹太人。在柏林做了多年的物理化学家，研究晶体结构和反应动力学。他是一个一流的科学家——不是那种"还行"的科学家，是真正做出过重要发现的人。

后来，他把越来越多的工作转向知识与社会的问题。从实验室出来，并不等于把科学留在门内。

他经历了两次世界大战。他看到了欧洲文明的崩溃。他问自己：为什么？为什么我们摧毁了欧洲？

他的一条回答，是追问一种知识理想的危险：如果只有完全去掉个人判断的东西才可靠，那一个真实的科学家，究竟还剩多少位置？这不是把欧洲灾难归结为唯一的哲学原因。

他批评把知识想成彻底无人的记录，把直觉、信任、承诺和个人判断都当作必须清除的杂质。这样的标准不只让道德与美感难以安放，也误写了科学工作本身——选择一个问题、判断一条线索，都不是数据自行完成的。

当尊重、判断与承诺被贬成“不过是主观感受”，它们就更容易在权力面前失去说话的资格。但这里不能反过来说：只要我真诚相信，我就有权威。人的判断既需要位置，也需要承担出错的责任。

所以，波兰尼不只是给直觉争取一句好话。他要保住知识中不能被彻底擦掉的那个人。科学的客观追求，不以研究者假装自己不存在为代价；道德判断，也不能因为无法化成一套机械程序就被取消。

三、辅助意识和焦点意识

波兰尼的核心概念之一：辅助意识（subsidiary awareness）和焦点意识（focal awareness）。

你在读这篇文章，注意力朝向句子的意思，却也依靠对字形、语法和上下文的熟悉。这些不是全都消失在无意识里，而是作为通往意义的线索在起作用。椅子压力和窗外声音可能只是背景，不能把所有没有留心的东西一概叫作辅助意识。

关键在“从……到……”：从细节出发，注意一个整体。你可以把某个细节单独拿出来研究，但它这时的角色变了；不是原来的焦点必然自动退成辅助，也不是两个层次不能同时参与认识。

骑自行车时，你看路，身体不断调整平衡和力度。如果把每个细小动作都抓到眼前逐项指挥，熟练的节奏可能被打断。但练习时也可以分析动作，再把学到的东西放回流畅的骑行里；不是一留心身体就必然摔倒。

认脸时也是这样。你通过五官认出这个人，不是在心里逐条核对一份清单。把鼻子单独拿来端详，认识的组织方式就暂时不同了；并不是从此看不见整个人。

波兰尼把这种结构看得很广。它不等于“辅助的永远说不出，焦点的总能说出”：正在辨认的脸，也可能难以描述。两者的区别首先是它们在认识中怎样起作用，不是一道能说与不能说的固定分界。

四、十七篇的收束

现在回看本轮十七篇。

孔德为知识寻找可依靠的规律。本系列拿他的“地板”来提醒一种封闭的危险，却不应把他的一生压成“凡说不出的都不算”。

波兰尼把我们的目光转到另一层：即便你已经写下规律，辨认它、理解它、使用它，仍需要一份不能由规律本身包办的判断。

波普尔划科学的可证伪界线，并不因此否认发现时的想象。波兰尼让我们多看一眼那句“这里也许有东西”：它未必已经是一条可检验的命题，却能引出这样的命题。直觉不能替最后的检验签字。

狄拉克的美感可以引导方程，但漂亮不保证正确。美感与推导、经验检验一起工作，不必被锁死为辅助和焦点的一次固定配对。

屈原的诗不只传递命题，也给人方向。你可能先感到某一句把你叫住，后来才慢慢说清它为什么重要。

费希特关于“我”的论证可以被讲述，但一个人承担自己的行动，不会因为背下论证就自动完成。

麦克林托克对有机体的感觉，来自长期工作，又不断回到证据。不是六十年都结束以后，才从一个感觉里跳出发现。

薇依写过怎样练习注意。然而读完方法，不等于已经会把眼前的人放进来。

撒切尔作出选择时，也要判断哪些代价能承担。但判断难以穷尽地解释，绝不是“不必给公众理由”的豁免。

龙树拆的不是通往一个神秘残余的外壳。连“空”也不能被抓成新的实体。这里留下的提醒，是别让自己最后那个词冒充终点。

伍尔夫写“存在的瞬间”。棉絮为什么在这里裂开，作品能带我们靠近，却不必交出一张可复制那个瞬间的处方。

汤川借助图像与数学追问核力。抛接球可以帮助理解交换，但不能把一种教学图像写成已经证实的灵感起源，更不能让图像独自预言粒子。

阿伦特问人何时停止与自己对话。一个人会说“我在思考”，仍未必真的愿意检查自己的判断。

默多克让红隼打断自我沉迷。我们能说明注意为何重要，却无法保证照着同一套步骤，明天也会真正看见。

赫勒敦的阿萨比亚，需要从共同经历、关系与行动中辨认。置身群体的感觉能提供线索，也可能被热闹或宣传骗过。

维果茨基之后，人们用“脚手架”描述某些支持。扶着后座的手何时松开，要看这个孩子当下的反应，不是背一个年龄表就够了。

斯特劳森分析我们早已会用的概念。说出结构，并不等于从零造出了让我们共同生活的全部本领。

梅洛-庞蒂问手怎样伸向杯子。动作可以被研究，却不必等研究结束，才有第一次伸手。

十七篇，不是十七份证明“凡说不出的都对”的证词。它们让我们看到，明确的知识总在某种生活里发生。波兰尼这条线收回来的，是那份生活不能被当作残渣丢掉。

五、认知循环

接下来，是本系列借波兰尼继续走的一步，不是把下面的整套循环冒充他的原文。

认识既离不开经验，也离不开我们带进经验的预期与本领。这里不用“先验”去称呼从经验学来的直觉；那会把“先前已经学会的”与康德意义上的先验混在一起。

观察没有错，数据没有错。问题是要求一切判断都先变成独立的数据，才允许它参与认识。那样，你连下一组数据该去哪里找，都可能无从决定。

反过来也不行。拿着一个预设去裁剪经验，不合预设的全部扔掉，只是另一种封闭。地图可以引路，不能命令山川改成地图上的样子。

认知是一个循环。

经验探路。你观察，收集材料，遇见世界，也遇见跟预期不一样的东西。材料不是全无解释的石块，却仍可能让原来的想法受阻。

判断整理。你从经历中慢慢辨认出模式、方向、值得追问的地方。不是凭空添一层真理，而是画出一张暂时的地图。

已有的理解再引路。带着地图回去看，你知道哪些差异值得多停留一会儿。

新的经验又来检验。你在那个方向发现了地图上没有的东西，也可能发现当初的方向走错了。地图于是需要修改。

一圈又一圈：经验探路，判断整理，理解引路，再让经验检验。

不是谁永久坐在上面，谁只配提供原料。一个让你接触世界，一个让接触形成方向；方向又必须向世界交代。

麦克林托克的长期观察、熟练操作、对异常的敏感，与遗传分析相互往返。感觉让问题浮出来，实验与记录让这个问题能够被别人检查。不是先闭眼感觉完，再用显微镜补一张证明。

狄拉克也需要分层看。学习与数学判断引导方程，负能量解来自理论推导，不是一次新的实验观察；正电子的发现才带来另一层经验检验。把这些步骤分清，循环才不是一句什么都能套进去的话。

发现常在这样的往返中成长，并不保证每转一圈就产生一个，也不要求所有人的路线完全相同。

六、"我们知道的比我们说得出来的多"的另一个意思

回到开篇那句话。

"我们知道的比我们说得出来的多。"

这句话通常被理解为：有些知识你说不出来。默会知识。身体知识。直觉。

但在十七篇之后，这句话有了一个更深的意思：

“说不出来的那些”，不只可能是表达尚未赶上的部分，也可能是表达得以成立的本领。你从经验中养成的分辨，能引导下一次观看；其中一些以后可以讲得更清楚，却未必能全部装进说明书。

这使默会的理解成为上面那个循环的一个枢纽。你有数据，却还得辨认它们在说什么；你有理论，却还得判断它如何接触眼前这件事。直觉、经验与方向感参与连接，也要接受纠正。

《个人知识》（Personal Knowledge，1958）更早就在追问这件事。“个人的”不等于私人喜好冒充真理，也不意味着凡主观经验都不可靠。它说的是：求知必须经过活人的参与、技能、承诺与判断，而这个人仍在追求不只对自己有效的真。

没有人可以替你学会骑车，也没有人能靠复制一句结论就替你拥有麦克林托克的辨别力、狄拉克的数学判断。但自行车会倒，实验会失败，方程会遇到事实的反驳。个人参与没有取消一个会说“不”的世界。

个人知识不必是客观追求的对立面。波兰尼要我们承认：走向客观，也需要有人真实地走。

七、有方向没有目标

康德说过一个概念：无目的的合目的性（Zweckmäßigkeit ohne Zweck）。

在审美判断里，我们可以感到一种合宜，而不必先规定这东西服务什么具体目的。一朵花不必先交代用途，才让人觉得美。这不是说花在朝一个神秘目的地前进。

借这个提醒，可以靠近一种探索的状态：已经觉得某条路值得走，却还不能完整说出最后会找到什么。这里只是类比，不把康德的美学等同于波兰尼的认识论，也不说所有默会知识都没有目标。

麦克林托克提出遗传因子可以转座时，分子机制还没有完整展开。她有具体问题和研究目标，却没有后来研究才逐步给出的全部答案。方向可以比完整的解释先到。

屈原知道该往哪里走——"路漫漫其修远兮，吾将上下而求索"——但他不知道走到哪里。方向在，目标不在。

所以，有方向而尚无确定终点，并不丢人。危险的是两边各自封口：材料再多也不肯形成判断，或者判断一旦形成就不再听材料。你可以带着问题走，也可以在路上换问题。

方向与终点之间，给尚未说清的理解留一点位置。不是让它免检，是让它有机会长成可以继续讨论的东西。

八、桥头

现在，把波兰尼请到本系列的虚构桥头。他走过来的时候，你也许不会马上注意到。

不是因为他不重要。是因为他看起来太普通了。一个中等身材的老人。穿着不显眼的衣服。没有费希特的紧迫感，没有撒切尔的高跟鞋，没有龙树的静坐，没有屈原的湿衣服。

他走上桥。他不环顾四周。他不找位置。他直接走向——你不知道他走向哪里，但他知道。他有方向。没有目标。但有方向。

他路过孔德。他没有停下来。他没有反驳孔德。他只是从孔德旁边走过。走过的时候，他的脚踩在孔德灌的水泥上面。水泥下面有缝隙。他知道。他不需要说。

他路过波普尔画的线，没有把它擦掉。他看看线这一边的可检验命题，再看看带人走到命题前面的猜测与本领。不是两边的东西都自动为真，而是画一条科学的界线，不必把认识的其余部分都扔进河里。

他路过狄拉克，两人对视。美感怎样参与了推导，不一定能穷尽地写出来；方程仍要算，事实仍要查。一个人的眼光在其中，并没有替世界签收结果。

他路过麦克林托克。她蹲在那里看玉米。他蹲下来看了一眼。不是看玉米。是看她怎么看。她的姿势，她的专注，她的身体跟玉米之间的那个界面——这就是个人知识。没有人能替她做这件事。

他路过薇依。她不忙着把眼前的东西据为己有。波兰尼在这里可以看见一种值得并读的姿态，却不必把她叫作“只剩辅助意识的人”。没有朝向的辅助，不是他的那套从此到彼的认识。

他路过维果茨基，孩子已经在旁边走起来。他微笑。一起做、示范、说话、在适当时候收回一点帮助，都可以让本领逐渐成为孩子自己的。手不是语言的敌人。

他路过斯特劳森重新铺的地板。他的脚踩在上面。地板不完美。但脚踩在上面就知道：够用了。这个"够用了"的判断，说不出来。但脚知道。

他路过梅洛-庞蒂，两人不必先争谁把对方说完了。一个把身体的生活推到眼前，一个追问默会整合怎样让认识成立。两条路相邻，却仍有继续交谈的必要。

他走到了桥的最后面。

他转过身来。面对所有人。

他说了一句话。不是什么深刻的话。不是什么新的理论。是一句你已经知道但从来没有这样听到过的话：

“你说得出来的那些——规律、界线、方程、概念——并不独自悬在空中。它们经过你的身体、经验、判断，也经过你与别人的关系。不能完全写下来的，不一定是应当扔掉的残余；它也可能是让这些话成立的本领。个人的参与不取消客观，反而要求一个人对自己的判断负责。”

然后他又说了一句——不是他自己的话，是一句从十七篇的弧线里长出来的话：

“不要让经验取消判断，也不要让判断封住经验。经验探路，判断整理；理解引路，再让经验检验。这是一个认识与知识的循环。你在里面。从来不是一个站在循环外面的无名眼睛。”

他说完了。

他从口袋里掏出一样东西。很小。看不清是什么。也许是一颗晶体——他做过晶体学研究。也许是一枚纽扣。也许是一粒沙子。不重要。重要的是：他拿着它，你看不到它的细节，但你知道它在他手里。

说不出来。但在那里。

他把手伸出来。掌心朝上。那个东西在他手心里。

你来。[1][2][3]

I. You Know More Than You Can Tell

“We can know more than we can tell.”

The Tacit Dimension appeared in 1966, developed from Polanyi’s 1962 Terry Lectures at Yale. That brief statement near its beginning can change our stance toward knowledge.

You recognize a face. You can pick it out from a thousand. But you can't say how. You can't produce a list — the angle of the nose, the distance between eyes, the arc of cheekbone — and say "that's why I recognized you." Your knowing exceeds your words.

You ride a bicycle. You can do it. But you can't precisely describe what your body is doing. Merleau-Ponty, in the previous essay, said the body knows before concepts. Polanyi draws the line here: what the body knows but concepts can't say is not the residue of knowledge. It is the foundation.

His strong claim is that even explicit knowledge relies on tacit understanding. Not every obscure feeling counts as knowledge. Rather, no explanation fully contains the capacities needed to understand and use it.

“The earth revolves around the sun” is an explicit proposition. We can explain revolution, space and motion further. Yet any explanation still needs someone able to read a diagram, grasp relations and use words in the world. The question is not whether explanations matter, but what makes them intelligible.

II. From Chemistry to Philosophy

Polanyi was an unusual man.

Hungarian Jew. Spent years in Berlin as a physical chemist, researching crystal structures and reaction kinetics. A first-rate scientist — not "decent," genuinely important.

Later he turned increasingly toward knowledge and society. Leaving the laboratory did not mean leaving science behind.

He lived through two world wars. He watched European civilization collapse. He asked himself: why? Why did we destroy Europe?

One response was to question a dangerous ideal: if only what eliminates personal judgment can be reliable, how much room remains for an actual scientist? This does not make a mistaken philosophy the sole cause of Europe’s catastrophes.

He criticized the ideal of knowledge as an entirely impersonal record, with intuition, trust, commitment and judgment treated as impurities. That ideal troubles not only morality and aesthetic discernment; it misdescribes science itself. Data do not choose their own questions or evaluate their own promise.

When respect, judgment and commitment are dismissed as “merely subjective,” they can lose their standing against power. But the reverse would also be wrong: sincere conviction alone does not establish authority. Human judgment needs a place, and responsibility for being mistaken.

Polanyi is doing more than finding something kind to say about intuition. He wants to preserve the person who cannot be erased from knowing. Scientific objectivity need not require a researcher to pretend not to exist; moral judgment need not disappear because it cannot become a mechanical procedure.

III. Subsidiary and Focal Awareness

One of Polanyi's core concepts: subsidiary awareness and focal awareness.

Reading this essay, you attend to its meaning while relying on familiarity with letters, grammar and context. These are not necessarily unconscious; they function as clues through which meaning is reached. The chair’s pressure and noises outside may be mere background. Not everything outside the center of attention is subsidiary awareness.

The key relation is from–to: attending from particulars to a whole. You may examine a detail in its own right, but its role changes. The former focus does not automatically become subsidiary, nor are the two levels unable to participate together in knowing.

On a bicycle you attend to the road through continual adjustments of balance and force. Commanding every adjustment individually can interrupt a practiced rhythm. Yet training may analyze movements and reintegrate them into fluent riding. Paying attention to the body does not inevitably make you fall.

Recognizing a face, you attend through its features to the person, not through a checklist of separate measurements. Studying the nose in isolation temporarily reorganizes your attention. It does not make the whole person permanently vanish.

Polanyi gives this structure wide scope. It does not mean that subsidiaries are forever unsayable while the focus is always expressible: a face we recognize may itself resist description. The distinction concerns their roles in knowing, not a fixed boundary between the sayable and the unsayable.

IV. Gathering Seventeen Essays

Now look back at the preceding seventeen essays.

Comte sought dependable laws. His floor in this series warns against a closed conception of knowledge; it should not compress his entire work into a rejection of everything inarticulate.

Polanyi turns us toward another layer: even a written law requires judgment to recognize, understand and use it. The law cannot perform that work by itself.

Popper’s demarcation of science by falsifiability does not deny imagination in discovery. Polanyi invites us to linger over “there may be something here”: not yet a testable proposition, perhaps, but a way toward one. Intuition cannot sign off the eventual test.

Dirac’s sense of beauty could guide equations without guaranteeing their truth. Beauty, deduction and testing work together; they need not be fixed forever into one subsidiary–focal pairing.

Qu Yuan’s poetry does more than convey propositions; it gives direction. A line may stop you before you can explain why it matters.

Fichte’s argument about the I can be explained. Assuming responsibility for one’s activity is not accomplished merely by memorizing it.

McClintock’s feeling for an organism grew through long practice and returned continually to evidence. Her discovery did not wait for sixty completed years before jumping out of a feeling.

Weil wrote about practicing attention. Reading the instructions does not ensure that we have made room for the person before us.

Thatcher had to judge which costs could be borne. The difficulty of explaining judgment exhaustively never exempts a politician from giving the public reasons.

Nāgārjuna does not strip away a shell to reveal a mysterious remainder. Emptiness itself must not become another substance. The reminder here is not to mistake our last word for the end of inquiry.

Woolf writes moments of being. Her work brings us close to a tear in the cotton wool without supplying a recipe that will reproduce the moment.

Yukawa investigated nuclear forces through images and mathematics. Tossing a ball can illustrate exchange; it is not an established account of the discovery’s inspiration, nor an image that predicted a particle on its own.

Arendt asks when people stop their inward conversation. Being able to say “I am thinking” does not establish willingness to examine one’s judgments.

Murdoch lets a kestrel interrupt self-absorption. We can explain why attention matters without guaranteeing that following the same steps will make us genuinely see tomorrow.

Khaldun’s asabiyya must be discerned through shared experience, relations and action. A group’s felt atmosphere may offer clues, or may mislead through excitement and propaganda.

After Vygotsky, later writers used scaffolding to describe certain forms of support. When to release the bicycle seat requires attention to this child’s response, not merely an age chart.

Strawson analyzes concepts we already know how to use. Articulating their structure does not create all the capacities of shared life from scratch.

Merleau-Ponty asks how a hand reaches for a cup. The movement can be studied without waiting for the study to finish before a first reach.

Seventeen essays are not seventeen witnesses that whatever cannot be said must be right. They show explicit knowledge happening within lives. What Polanyi helps gather is the life that must not be discarded as leftover material.

V. The Cognitive Cycle

The next step belongs to this series’ reading of Polanyi. The cycle described here is not presented as his own formulation.

Knowing needs both experience and the expectations and capacities we bring to it. Calling an acquired intuition a priori would confuse what we learned previously with what is independent of experience in the philosophical sense.

Observation and data are not the enemy. The problem is requiring every judgment to become a freestanding datum before it may contribute. You may then lose the ability to decide where the next data should be sought.

The reverse is equally closed: using a preconception to trim experience and discarding whatever does not fit. A map may guide the journey; it cannot command the landscape to match its lines.

Cognition is a cycle.

Experience explores. You observe, gather materials and encounter what differs from expectation. These materials are not interpretation-free stones, yet they can still resist your existing ideas.

Judgment organizes. Patterns, directions and promising questions emerge through experience. You do not add truth from nowhere; you sketch a provisional map.

Existing understanding guides the next encounter. Returning with a map, you know which differences may deserve another look.

New experience tests the direction. You discover what the map omitted, or that the route was mistaken. The map must change.

Round after round: experience explores, judgment organizes, understanding guides, experience tests again.

Neither side permanently rules while the other merely supplies material. One brings contact with the world; the other gives that contact direction. Direction must answer to the world in turn.

McClintock moved among sustained observation, practiced technique, sensitivity to anomalies and genetic analysis. Discernment brought questions forward; experiments and records made them answerable to other investigators. She did not first intuit with closed eyes and then obtain a confirming microscope image.

Dirac’s case needs distinctions too. Training and mathematical judgment guided the equation; negative-energy solutions were consequences of reasoning, not a new experimental observation. Discovery of the positron supplied another level of empirical testing. Distinguishing these stages keeps the cycle from becoming a slogan that fits anything.

Discoveries often grow through such exchanges. No revolution of the cycle guarantees one, and not every investigator follows the same route.

VI. Another Meaning of "We Know More Than We Can Tell"

Back to the opening sentence.

"We can know more than we can tell."

This is usually understood as: some knowledge is inarticulate. Tacit knowledge. Bodily knowledge. Intuition.

But after seventeen essays, the sentence acquires a deeper meaning:

What resists telling may be more than something not yet expressed: it may be a capacity that makes expression possible. Discernment cultivated in experience can guide the next look. Some of it may later be articulated, without all of it fitting inside a manual.

Tacit understanding is one hinge of the cycle just described. With data, you must still discern what they indicate; with theory, you must still judge how it bears on this case. Intuition, experience and orientation help connect them—and remain corrigible.

Personal Knowledge (1958) had already pursued this question. Personal does not mean a private preference masquerading as truth, nor does subjective experience automatically mean unreliability. Knowing involves a living person’s participation, skill, commitment and judgment while seeking truth valid beyond that person.

Nobody can learn to ride for you. Copying a conclusion will not give you McClintock’s discernment or Dirac’s mathematical judgment. Yet bicycles fall, experiments fail, and equations meet recalcitrant facts. Personal participation does not abolish a world capable of saying no.

Personal knowing need not oppose the pursuit of objectivity. Polanyi asks us to acknowledge that the journey toward objectivity still requires someone actually to make it.

VII. Direction Without Destination

Kant proposed a concept: purposiveness without purpose (Zweckmäßigkeit ohne Zweck).

In aesthetic judgment we can experience fittingness without first specifying a purpose the object serves. A flower need not justify its usefulness before appearing beautiful. It is not traveling toward a secret destination.

This suggests an analogy for inquiry: a route feels worth following before its outcome can be fully stated. It is an analogy, not an identification of Kant’s aesthetics with Polanyi’s account of knowledge, and not a claim that all tacit knowing lacks a definite goal.

When McClintock proposed genetic transposition, its molecular mechanisms had not been fully worked out. She had concrete questions and goals, but not all the answers later research would develop. Direction can precede a complete explanation.

Qu Yuan knew which way to walk — "the road ahead is long and winding; I shall search high and low" — but he didn't know where he would end up. Direction present. Destination absent.

Having direction without a settled endpoint is no failure. Closure threatens from either side: accumulating materials while refusing judgment, or forming a judgment that will no longer listen. You can travel with a question and change it along the way.

Between direction and destination, leave room for understanding not yet fully articulated. Not immunity from testing, but a chance to develop into something that can be discussed further.

VIII. The Bridgehead

Now invite Polanyi onto this series’ imagined bridge. You might not notice him at first.

Not because he's unimportant. Because he looks too ordinary. A medium-built old man. Unremarkable clothes. None of Fichte's urgency, Thatcher's heels, Nāgārjuna's stillness, Qu Yuan's wet robes.

He steps onto the bridge. He doesn't look around. He doesn't search for a spot. He walks directly toward — you don't know where, but he does. He has direction. No destination. But direction.

He passes Comte. Doesn't stop. Doesn't rebut. Just walks past. As he passes, his foot lands on the grout Comte poured. Beneath the grout, cracks. He knows. He doesn't need to say.

He passes Popper’s line without erasing it. On one side are testable claims; around them are the conjectures and skills that bring people to such claims. Neither side is automatically true. But drawing a boundary for science need not throw every other part of knowing into the river.

He and Dirac exchange a glance. Beauty’s contribution to reasoning may resist exhaustive description. Equations still require working through; facts still need checking. A person’s vision participates without signing the result on the world’s behalf.

He passes McClintock. She's crouching, looking at corn. He crouches for a moment and looks. Not at the corn. At how she looks. Her posture, her focus, the interface between her body and the maize — this is personal knowledge. Nobody can do this for her.

He passes Weil, who is not hurrying to possess what she sees. There is a posture worth reading alongside his work, but she is not “pure subsidiary awareness.” A subsidiary with no attended-to whole is not his from–to relation.

He passes Vygotsky; the child is walking beside him now. He smiles. Doing together, demonstrating, speaking and easing support at the right time can help a capacity become the child’s own. The hand is not language’s enemy.

He passes Strawson's relaid floor. His foot lands on it. The floor isn't perfect. But the foot on it knows: good enough. That "good enough" judgment — inarticulate. But the foot knows.

He passes Merleau-Ponty. Neither needs to claim to have said everything the other meant. One brings embodied life into view; the other asks how tacit integration sustains knowing. Neighboring paths still have something to discuss.

He walks to the very back of the bridge.

He turns to face everyone.

He says one thing. Not something deep. Not a new theory. A sentence you already knew but had never heard quite this way:

“The things you can state—laws, boundaries, equations, concepts—do not float alone. They pass through your body, experience, judgment and relations with others. What cannot be fully written down need not be discarded as residue; it may be the capacity that makes those statements possible. Personal participation does not cancel objectivity. It asks someone to answer for a judgment.”

Then he says one more thing — not his own words, but a sentence that grew from seventeen essays' arc:

“Do not let experience abolish judgment, or judgment seal experience shut. Experience explores, judgment organizes; understanding guides, experience tests again. A cycle of understanding and knowledge. You are inside it, never a nameless eye outside.”

He is finished.

He takes something from his pocket. Something small. You can't quite see it. Perhaps a crystal — he did crystallography. Perhaps a button. Perhaps a grain of sand. It doesn't matter. What matters: he is holding it, you can't see its details, but you know it's in his hand.

Inarticulate. But there.

He extends his hand. Palm up. The thing sits in his palm.

Your turn.[1][2][3]
