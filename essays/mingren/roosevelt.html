罗斯福，架构

Roosevelt, Architecture

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、那句话

1933年3月4日。华盛顿。就职典礼。

银行的门关着，工厂的机器停着，找工作的人还在找。按后来常用的年度估计，这一年美国约有四分之一的劳动力失业。数字落到家里，是房租到期，是下一顿饭，是拿着存折却取不出来的钱。

富兰克林·德拉诺·罗斯福说：“我们唯一需要恐惧的是恐惧本身。”

这句话后来被单独摘了出来。可是就在同一篇讲话里，他列举了税负、收入、储蓄和失业，也承认眼前物质生活的严酷。听众并不需要先相信饥饿只是心理问题，才能听他往下说。

他要对付的，是让人连下一步也不敢迈出的恐惧。不是让已经失去工作的人承认：错在你太害怕。

七年后，丘吉尔会把海滩、街道与山丘放进句子。这里，银行、农场和工厂进入了演说。两个人都把一个庞大的危机，说到了听者每天要经过的地方；他们的话也都不能替那里的人把事情做完。

典礼会结束。有人还得回到那扇银行门前。

一句话能够留下来。门怎样重新打开，要靠别的工作。

二、看不见的手

亚当·斯密。1776年。《国富论》。看不见的手。

这几个字太容易被写成一句省事的话：每个人只管自己，市场会把一切安排好，政府不必过问。

斯密自己的书没有这么薄。第四篇谈到“看不见的手”时，讨论的是投资者偏好国内投资，以及私人的打算可能带来未曾预定的公共结果。第五篇又让国家承担某些公共工程和公共机构的职责，还谈到教育。一只手没有把整本书握住。

可站在倒闭的银行门外，储户需要的不只是一套解释。工厂没有订单，一个勤快的工人再勤快，也不能凭空把订单做出来。那些看起来各自努力就能走通的路，忽然一起堵住了。

新政把若干回答写成了法律和机构。它们并不是罗斯福独自在书房画出、第二天便完整立起来的一座房子。

1933年银行立法设立联邦存款保险，保险于1934年初开始运行，最初限额为两千五百美元。这里有参加保险的银行，有限额，也有具体条件；不是从此任何投资损失都由政府填平。罗斯福起初还反对存款保险，国会中的主张与协商却把它留进了他最终签署的法律。1934年成立的证券交易委员会，则涉及交易监管与信息披露，不是替买股票的人保证获利。

1935年《社会保障法》又把老年保险、救助和州失业补偿制度的支持写进不同部分。劳动部长弗朗西丝·帕金斯主持过经济安全委员会，方案经过研究、听证与国会修改。最初的联邦老年保险并没有覆盖全部劳动者，农业劳动与私人家庭里的家政劳动就在排除条款中。

还有工地。1933年的公共工程机构PWA主要通过工程资助和建设安排开展工作；1935年的WPA则以提供工作岗位为中心。两组字母不是同一块招牌，给工程拨款与直接安排就业也不是一回事。道路与桥梁背后，仍有地方机构、工程人员、承包者和劳动者。

于是，所谓“架构”有了纸上的细节：谁缴费，谁能申请，谁负责发钱，谁被写在适用范围之外。拿到一笔钱的人，改变的是接下来几天怎样生活；申请没有被受理的人，面对的也不只是一个法律名词。

这些安排改变了联邦政府参与经济生活的方式。它们各自怎样起作用、付出什么成本、遗漏了谁，仍要分别查看。一个签名不能替所有环节回答。

房子的形状不只在屋顶。也在入口。

三、他

富兰克林·德拉诺·罗斯福。1882年1月30日，生于纽约州海德公园，出身富裕家庭。他读过哈佛，也进入哥伦比亚法学院，后来没有取得那里的法学学位。

1921年，他三十九岁。医生当时诊断为脊髓灰质炎，腿部瘫痪改变了他的日常生活。

支架、拐杖、别人的手臂、轮椅。每一样东西做的事不一样。他可以借支架和协助站立，也练习过借拐杖与身边人的手臂移动。在水里，浮力减轻身体的重量；出了水，又要面对台阶。

不是一个人忽然只剩下“不能”。而是许多过去无需留意的距离，现在需要重新安排。

1928年，他当选纽约州长。1932年、1936年、1940年、1944年，四次赢得总统选举。第四个任期刚开始不久，他便去世了。四次当选，不是四个完整任期。限制当选总统次数的第二十二修正案，1947年由国会提出，1951年获批准；它是后来的宪法程序，不是他一个人替自己改了规则。

轮椅很容易被后来的叙述变成一把钥匙：他受过苦，所以他懂得所有受苦的人；他不能走，所以他发明了给所有人的坡道。

可一段身体经历没有为我们保存一个人全部政策的来源。它也没有保证，遇到别人的困境时，他会作出同样的判断。

能看清的，先是更小的事情。一把椅子能不能转过走廊的弯。门够不够宽。桌子能不能靠近。改变这些条件，不必等坐在椅子上的人证明自己将成为总统。

门外的人已经到了。他缺的，也许只是脚下这一小段路。

坡道。

四、9066号行政令

1942年2月19日。罗斯福签署第9066号行政令。

命令授权划定军事区域，并从中排除一些或全部人员。它没有在文字中直接写出“日裔”。随后，军方的排除令、迁移安排、国会规定的刑事处罚，以及战时迁移局的管理，把这项授权变成了人们必须离开家的现实。

约十二万日裔居民受到强制迁移和拘禁，通常概括为约三分之二是美国公民。把人编入这套安排的，是族裔身份，而不是先分别证明每个人犯了罪。

房屋与生意要处置，随身能带走的东西有限。有人被送到西部荒漠，也有人进入阿肯色州的营地。“迁移中心”这个名称下面，有围栏，有警卫，有不能自行回去处理的生活。不是所有营地都在沙漠里，离开自己家门这件事却并不抽象。

1944年12月18日，最高法院在是松案中维持了违反军方排除令的定罪。多数意见把强制离开特定地区，与此后拘禁是否有效分开，不能将判决改写成法院为所有营地做法发出一张总许可证。墨菲等法官提出异议，质问未经个人审查的族裔推断。

同一天，法院在远藤案中判定，战时迁移局无权继续拘禁被政府承认忠于美国的公民。法院由授权范围作出这个结论，没有就所有相关宪法问题一并裁决。

两份判决落在同一天，回答的却不是同一道问题。对正在等候的人，这些区别会影响他什么时候能够离开。法律上的一行字，仍然要走到营地门口。

翻回那些文件，命令不是自己走到人家门口的。总统的授权、军方的命令、国会规定的处罚、法院接受或拒绝的理由，一道道手续接起来，才使一个人不得不收拾行李。每一道手续后面，都还有可以追问的人。

经过程序，并不等于程序没有需要检查的地方。把全部过程归给一只手，会漏掉其余环节；把环节列全，也不能让签名与命令从文件上消失。

1988年《公民自由法》以美国政府的名义道歉，并为符合资格者规定两万美元支付。法律有生存时间、身份与所受处置等资格条件，并非向所有后代或所有曾受损失的人一律支付。国会在法中将当年的行动认定为对公民与永久居民的根本不公。

几十年后的一张支票，与几十年前被迫离开的日子，不是同一件事。

一个人可能曾经走进某项保障，也可能被另一项命令要求离开自己的家。两份档案都要留下来。不能拿第一份盖住第二份，也不能因为第二份，就假装第一份从未改变过任何人的生活。

五、他和丘吉尔

在这个系列里，丘吉尔带着声音，罗斯福带着架构。

不过，走近一点，也能在屋子里听见声音，在声音后面看见支撑它的梁柱。

1933年3月12日，罗斯福向收音机前的听众解释银行：存进去的钱不会全部留在金库里；银行为什么暂时关闭；重新开门又为什么有先后。讲话里有财政部的检查、现金的运送和国会刚通过的法律。这里同样有声音，只是声音正在领着听众认识几件已经进行的事。

丘吉尔的声音背后，也有内阁、船队、部委与许多执行决定的人。言语与机构并不是谁拥有了一个，便不能再碰另一个。

两人参与战时合作，留下讲话、会议与文件，也留下各自需要解释的决定。旗与轮椅可以放在一起，帝国政策与9066号令却仍须沿着各自的材料去看，不能靠“都有另一面”把差别省掉。

1944年，罗斯福第四次当选。1945年7月，英国选举之后艾德礼接任首相；丘吉尔仍是伍德福德的议员。后一个国家更换政府，前一个国家此前让总统连任，并不能单凭这两个结果，就读出两国所有选民的内心。

更不能说，一个国家的桥已经建完，另一个还没有。英国投票时，对日战争仍在继续；和平时期的住房与工作，也不会在宣布结果那天自动安排妥当。

职位的更换不是一个人被用完，继续任职也不是他已经成为不可替代的零件。办公室里会换人，办公室外的日子还在继续。

罗斯福没有等到自己的任期结束。

他的生命停下的时候，工作仍在许多人的桌上。

六、1945年4月12日

温泉镇，佐治亚州。小白宫。

画家伊丽莎白·舒马托夫正在为罗斯福画肖像。他在这一天突然发病，失去意识。

下午三点三十五分，他去世，六十三岁。死因记录为脑出血。画没有完成。

德国的军事投降到五月才发生，生效时间是5月8日。日本正式签署投降书是在9月2日。罗斯福去世的时候，这两份文件都还没有签下。

联合国旧金山会议4月25日开幕，宪章6月26日签署，到10月24日才生效。总统的参与留在前期筹备中；后来坐进会场、商议条文、签署和批准的，又是许多人。把这项工作称作“他帮助设计的”，不能顺手擦去那些名字。

画像停在那一天，会议却还要开下去。新的人走进房间，拿起文件，添上一句，改掉一句。工作能继续，并不意味着那天下午失去的只是一个职位。画前那个具体的人，已经不能再回来坐一会儿。

贞德也没有看到战争的终点。她死于1431年，百年战争通常以1453年为结束。隔着几百年，这两段生命让人停在同一个地方：一个人已经没有明天，事情却还有。

丘吉尔后来离开首相职位，也没有因此走到历史的句号。罗斯福在任上去世，同样不能让所有公共问题自动变成一份必须照原样执行的遗愿。

他不在了。桌上的纸还有下一页。

谁接着写，怎样改，后来的人仍要自己决定。

七、坡道

罗斯福使用过一把由餐椅改成的轮椅。它较窄，便于在门道和走廊之间移动。椅背、轮子、转弯的空间，这些都不是修辞。

他也安排自己的公共形象，要求记者避免拍摄一些移动和转移身体的场面。许多记者遵从，仍有例外。不能把这种惯例写成他一生从未公开面对过身体的状况。

1945年3月1日，他坐着向国会报告雅尔塔会议，开头解释，腿上承受约十磅钢制支架的重量，使他坐着讲话容易得多。然后，他讲起旅程、战争与尚在商议的国际安排。坐着，并没有让讲话失去内容。

站起来的样子，与把一件事做下去的能力，不是同一个问题。

坡道先回答一个很实在的问题：这把椅子能否从这里过去？它不要求使用者先把腿治好，也不要求他把被帮助的感激写在每一步里。到了另一端，他仍然可以选择自己要去的地方。

把社会保障、失业补偿、存款保险或最低工资比作坡道，便不能只保留那条缓缓上升的线。还要看看它从哪里开始，通向哪里，谁的工作没有被它接住。

1938年《公平劳动标准法》规定最低工资等标准，也规定适用范围和豁免。原法第14节甚至允许凭特别证书，向被认定挣钱能力受损的人支付低于一般最低标准的工资。纸上写着一个“最低”，并不意味着所有人脚下是同一条线。

1935年老年保险没有覆盖的农业劳动者、家政劳动者，仍然在劳动，仍然会老去。职业没有进入那一项保险，不等于生活可以把年老推迟。

坡道因此不只是给予者的一项功劳。对于使用它的人，它首先是一段要亲自走过的路；对于被挡住的人，它也可能使入口的差别变得更清楚。

不要只抬头看房顶。有人还在门边。

八、埃莉诺

埃莉诺·罗斯福，富兰克林的妻子。她也以自己的名字发表文章、旅行、会见别人。

1943年，她访问了亚利桑那州希拉河的日裔拘禁营地。她的公开文字反对因为血统而把邻居预先判定为不可信，也写到被迫处理财产、不能离营料理生意的人。她没有只把自己留在丈夫的办公室里。

但读她同年的《对美国公平精神的考验》，会遇到另一部分。她接受当局对“忠诚”与“不忠诚”的分类，赞许过一部分迁移管理，也把拘禁解释为战时安全措施。反对族裔偏见，与接受这些解释，出现在同一篇文章里。

文章不是一张毫无折痕的白纸。她要求读者把日裔居民当作各自不同的人看待，又把一部分人的离营资格放在忠诚审查之后。被讨论的人，将从这些句子里听到不同的东西。

与此同时，学生的安置工作也在进行。1942年成立的全国日裔美国学生安置理事会，由美国公谊服务委员会与大学方面共同推动，帮助学生到西海岸之外继续学业。学籍、接收学校、费用、离营许可，要一项一项接上。这不是只靠第一夫人向丈夫说一句话就成立的通道。

通道的另一端，也不只有等待被安置的人。太平洋大学所藏一封1943年3月通信的目录摘要，提到马贝尔·杉山觉得自己有责任留在父母身边。能继续上学是一件事，怎样面对还在营地的家人，又是一件事。不能替她把两件事合成一个轻快的离开。

读到这里，总统夫妇的房间之外，多了许多张桌子。埃莉诺写文章，委员会联系学校，学生掂量家人的处境。一条出路，需要这些不在同一间屋子里的人，各自做出下一步。

狄更斯篇里的凯瑟琳保存了信。她不是一张被裂缝吞掉的纸。埃莉诺也不只是替丈夫修补形象的人。要认识她们，得看到她们自己保留了什么、写了什么、去了哪里。

有人写信。有人读。有人安排一个名额。有人暂时不能离开。

一条路要能通行，事情还没有做完。

九、架构

罗斯福留下的“架构”，可以在很小的地方遇见。

一张表格上的职业栏。银行柜台后的一项保险。工程的拨款记录。工资单旁边适用或不适用的条款。它们不必都有砖墙，仍会改变一个人怎样经过一天。

这些安排可以比参与制定的人活得更久，却不会因此变成永远不必检查的东西。纸会被重写，道路需要修整，入口也有人继续询问。

现在，把他想象到这座桥上。

桥头又多了一个人。他坐在轮椅里。手搭在轮圈上，身旁放着一支笔。

笔里有两种墨水。一种让人想起新政的法律，另一种让人想起9066号令。它们在同一支想象的笔里，没有混成一种可以把两份记录冲淡的颜色。

苏格拉底站在空地上。柏拉图蹲着画图纸。休谟守着双陆棋盘。叔本华向桥底下看。克尔凯郭尔跳了。图灵看着手里的苹果。契诃夫靠着栏杆。康托尔看天空。哥白尼放下书走了。萨特拿着烟斗踱步。波伏瓦举着镜子。蒯因轻轻说了一句话。

特斯拉听嗡嗡声。爱迪生拿着没有亮的灯泡。海森堡的位置不确定。玻尔拿着未寄出的信。托尔斯泰拿着药方面对契诃夫。莎士比亚不在——他是桥下的水。斯宾诺莎手上有玻璃粉。亚里士多德蹲着铺地板。法拉第掀起一块木板。麦克斯韦站着写方程。

贞德带着火在桥的上方。王尔德站得很好看，手里拿着那句话。拉马努金从缝隙里探出半个身子。奥本海默背着灰往前走。夏洛蒂拿着笔。艾米莉在桥外的荒原上，风从她那里吹来。玻尔兹曼抱着刻有方程的石头。梵高身上有颜料，画笔还在滴颜色。狄更斯站在裂缝上，笔和火柴盒仍在手里。丘吉尔背着旗，另一只手拿着笔。

罗斯福看向丘吉尔。旗角动了一下。那个曾经隔着大西洋一道应对战争的人，现在就站在桥上。他的手搭回轮圈，椅子向前挪了一点。

远处，康德站着。

轮椅前面有一段坡道。罗斯福参与搭建的这一部分，与别人铺下的桥面接在一起。接缝看得见。没有一块牌子说，路已全部完成。

他把手压在轮圈上，向前推。椅子沿着缓坡移动。坐着的人也在选择方向。

笔还在身旁。那些没有被某项保障接住的人，那些曾被命令迁离家园的人，没有因为椅子经过这里，就从记录里退场。

狄更斯脚下传来一点纸声。休谟挪了一颗棋子。风从艾米莉那边过来，吹动丘吉尔肩上的旗。坡道上不只有一种移动的声音。

罗斯福停了一会儿，又向前推。没有谁要求他先站起来，才算真正走过这段路。康德仍在远处，没有替路上的人宣布结束。

他参与搭起的东西，继续通向他没有到过的地方。后来的人还要走，还要修，也还会选择另一条路。

他参与建起的东西，比他走得远。

I. That Sentence

March 4, 1933. Washington. The inauguration.

Banks with their doors shut. Machines standing idle. People looking for work, still looking. By the annual estimate commonly used afterward, roughly a quarter of the American labor force was unemployed that year. At home, the figure became rent coming due, the next meal, a bankbook that could no longer be turned into cash.

Franklin Delano Roosevelt said, “the only thing we have to fear is fear itself.”

The sentence acquired a life apart from its speech. Yet in that same address he spoke of taxes, income, savings, unemployment. He acknowledged the harsh realities of material life. His listeners did not have to agree that hunger was a psychological problem before they could hear what followed.

He was addressing the fear that could prevent a next step. That is different from telling someone who has lost a job that the real fault lies in being frightened.

Seven years later Churchill would bring beaches, streets, and hills into his sentences. Here, banks, farms, and factories entered a speech. Each brought an enormous crisis close to places a listener had to pass every day. Neither man's words could finish the work waiting there.

The ceremony would end. Someone would return to the bank's closed door.

A sentence could endure. Opening that door again would require something else.

II. The Invisible Hand

Adam Smith. 1776. The Wealth of Nations. The invisible hand.

A few words can become a convenient summary: let everyone pursue their own affairs, the market will arrange everything, government need not intervene.

Smith's book was thicker than that. The invisible hand in Book IV appears in a discussion of investors' preference for domestic investment and of public consequences they had not intended. In Book V, he assigns the state responsibilities for certain public works and institutions, and discusses education. One hand does not hold the whole book.

Outside a failed bank, a depositor needed more than an explanation. If a factory had no orders, a worker could not bring them into being simply by working harder. Routes that seemed open to anyone willing to make an effort had suddenly closed together.

The New Deal put some answers into laws and agencies. They were not a house Roosevelt designed alone in a study and had standing, complete, the following morning.

The banking legislation of 1933 established federal deposit insurance, which began operating early in 1934 with an initial limit of $2,500. There were participating banks, limits, and conditions—not a promise that government would make every investment loss disappear. Roosevelt had initially opposed deposit insurance. Proposals and negotiations in Congress nevertheless kept it in the law he ultimately signed. The Securities and Exchange Commission, created in 1934, dealt with market regulation and disclosure. It did not promise a profit to everyone buying shares.

The Social Security Act of 1935 placed old-age insurance, assistance, and support for state unemployment compensation in different parts of the legislation. Labor Secretary Frances Perkins had chaired the Committee on Economic Security. Research, hearings, and congressional revision stood between a proposal and an act. The original federal old-age insurance did not cover every worker: agricultural labor and domestic service in private homes were among the exclusions.

There were construction sites as well. The public-works organization known as the PWA, originating in 1933, worked chiefly through funding and arrangements for construction. The WPA, established in 1935, centered on providing jobs. The letters did not belong to the same signboard. Funding a project and directly providing employment were different operations. Behind the roads and bridges stood local authorities, engineers, contractors, and workers.

“Architecture” acquired details on paper. Who contributed? Who could apply? Who paid out? Who appeared in an exception to the definition? For a recipient, a payment changed how the next few days could be lived. For someone whose claim was not accepted, the obstacle was more than a legal term.

These arrangements changed the federal government's involvement in economic life. How each worked, what it cost, and whom it left out remained separate questions. One signature could not answer for every stage.

The shape of a house is not only in its roof. It is in the entrance too.

III. The Man

Franklin Delano Roosevelt was born on January 30, 1882, at Hyde Park, New York, into a wealthy family. He attended Harvard and then Columbia Law School, leaving the latter without a law degree.

In 1921, when he was thirty-nine, his doctors diagnosed poliomyelitis. Paralysis of his legs changed the arrangements of daily life.

Braces, a cane, another person's arm, a wheelchair. Each did something different. He could stand with braces and assistance, and practiced moving with a cane and an arm beside him. In water, buoyancy relieved some of his body's weight. Out of the water, there were steps again.

A person had not suddenly become nothing but an inability. Distances he once scarcely noticed now needed to be arranged differently.

He was elected governor of New York in 1928. He won the presidential elections of 1932, 1936, 1940, and 1944. He died early in the fourth term. Four elections were not four completed terms. The Twenty-second Amendment, limiting election to the presidency, was proposed by Congress in 1947 and ratified in 1951. That was a subsequent constitutional process, not a rule he single-handedly rewrote for himself.

Later accounts can turn the wheelchair into a universal key. He suffered, therefore he understood everyone who suffered. He could not walk, therefore he invented a ramp for everyone.

But a bodily experience does not preserve for us the complete origin of a person's policies. It does not guarantee that his judgment will be the same when he confronts another person's predicament.

Something smaller can be seen more clearly. Can this chair turn the corner of the corridor? Is the doorway wide enough? Can it get close to the desk? None of those conditions needs to wait until its user has proved that he will become president.

The person outside has already arrived. Perhaps all that is missing is this short stretch of ground.

A ramp.

IV. Executive Order 9066

On February 19, 1942, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066.

It authorized the designation of military areas from which some or all people could be excluded. The text did not name Japanese Americans. Subsequent military exclusion orders, removal arrangements, criminal penalties enacted by Congress, and administration by the War Relocation Authority turned the authorization into a requirement that people leave their homes.

Roughly 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were subjected to forced removal and incarceration. About two-thirds are generally identified as American citizens. Ancestry placed people within this system; their inclusion did not first require proof that each had committed a crime.

Houses and businesses had to be dealt with. What could be carried was limited. Some people went to the western deserts; others to camps in Arkansas. Beneath the official term “relocation center” were fences, guards, and lives whose affairs could no longer be attended to by going home. The camps were not all in deserts. The loss of access to one's own front door was concrete enough.

On December 18, 1944, the Supreme Court upheld a conviction for violating a military exclusion order in Korematsu v. United States. The majority distinguished exclusion from a particular area from the validity of subsequent detention. Its decision was not a blanket license for every practice in every camp. Dissenting justices, including Frank Murphy, questioned assumptions based on ancestry rather than individual examination.

On the same day, in Ex parte Endo, the Court held that the War Relocation Authority had no authority to continue detaining a citizen whose loyalty the government conceded. It reached that conclusion through the limits of statutory and executive authorization, without deciding every constitutional question surrounding the policy.

Two decisions on one day, answering different questions. For someone waiting in a camp, those distinctions could affect when departure became possible. A line of law still had to reach the gate.

Return to those documents. An order did not arrive at someone’s door by itself. Presidential authorization, military orders, congressional penalties, reasons accepted or rejected in court: one procedure joined the next until a person had to pack. Behind each stage were people who could still be asked to answer for it.

A procedure has not been put beyond examination simply by being followed. Assigning the whole process to one hand loses the other stages. Listing every stage does not remove signatures or orders from the papers.

The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 apologized on behalf of the United States government and provided payments of $20,000 to eligible individuals. Survival, status, and the treatment a person had undergone mattered to eligibility; it was not an identical payment to every descendant or everyone who had suffered a loss. In the act, Congress acknowledged a fundamental injustice done to citizens and permanent residents.

A check received decades later was not the same thing as the days of forced departure.

Someone could enter one system of protection and be ordered out of a home by another. Both records must remain available. The first cannot cover the second, nor does the second establish that the first never changed anyone's life.

V. Roosevelt and Churchill

In this series, Churchill brings a voice. Roosevelt brings architecture.

Look closer, though, and there is a voice inside the house, and beams and columns behind the voice.

On March 12, 1933, Roosevelt explained banking to a radio audience. Money deposited did not all remain in the vault. Why had banks closed temporarily? Why would they reopen in stages? He spoke about Treasury examinations, deliveries of currency, and legislation Congress had just passed. Here was a voice too, taking its listeners through things already being done.

Behind Churchill's voice were a cabinet, fleets, departments, and many people who carried decisions into effect. Words and institutions were not possessions that prevented either man from using the other.

Both took part in wartime cooperation. Both left speeches, meetings, and documents, as well as decisions requiring explanation. A flag and a wheelchair can be placed together. Imperial policy and Executive Order 9066 still need to be examined through their own records. “Each had another side” is not enough to dispense with the differences.

Roosevelt won a fourth election in 1944. In July 1945, after the British election, Attlee became prime minister; Churchill remained the member for Woodford. One country's change of government and the other's earlier reelection of a president do not, by themselves, reveal the minds of everyone who voted.

Nor can they establish that one country's bridge was finished while the other's was not. When Britain voted, the war against Japan continued. Peacetime housing and employment would not arrange themselves on the day the result was announced.

Changing officeholders does not mean that a person has been used up. Continuing in office does not make him an irreplaceable component. Someone else may sit in the office. Outside it, lives continue.

Roosevelt did not reach the end of his term.

When his life stopped, the work remained on many people's desks.

VI. April 12, 1945

Warm Springs, Georgia. The Little White House.

Elizabeth Shoumatoff was painting Roosevelt's portrait. He became suddenly ill that day and lost consciousness.

He died at 3:35 that afternoon, aged sixty-three. The recorded cause was a cerebral hemorrhage. The painting remained unfinished.

Germany's military surrender came in May, taking effect on May 8. Japan's formal instrument of surrender was signed on September 2. When Roosevelt died, neither document had been signed.

The San Francisco conference opened on April 25. The United Nations Charter was signed on June 26 and came into force on October 24. His participation remained in the preparatory work. Many other people entered the conference rooms, negotiated provisions, signed, and ratified. To call it something he helped design cannot also mean erasing their names.

The portrait stopped that day. The meetings would continue. Others would enter the room, pick up the papers, add a sentence, strike one out. Work could go on, but what was lost that afternoon was more than an officeholder. The particular person sitting for that painting could not come back and sit a little longer.

Joan of Arc never saw the end of her war either. She died in 1431; the Hundred Years’ War is conventionally dated to end in 1453. Centuries apart, the two lives bring us to the same pause: a person has no tomorrow left, but the work still does.

Churchill's later departure from the premiership was not history's full stop. Roosevelt's death in office did not turn every public question into a will that the living were obliged to carry out unchanged.

He was gone. There was another page on the desk.

Who would write it, and what they would change, remained for other people to decide.

VII. The Ramp

One of Roosevelt's wheelchairs was adapted from a dining chair. Its narrowness helped it pass through doorways and corridors. A chair back, wheels, turning space: none of these was a figure of speech.

He also arranged his public image, asking the press to avoid photographing certain movements and transfers. Many reporters complied; there were exceptions. The convention cannot be turned into a claim that he never publicly acknowledged the circumstances of his body.

On March 1, 1945, he sat to address Congress about Yalta. At the outset, he explained that not having to carry around ten pounds of steel braces on his legs made speaking while seated easier. Then came the journey, the war, and the international arrangements still under discussion. Being seated did not deprive the address of its content.

The appearance of standing and the ability to carry something forward are different questions.

A ramp first answers something quite practical. Can this chair get through here? Its user need not cure his legs before using it. Nor must each movement be inscribed with gratitude for assistance. At the other end, he can still choose where to go.

If social insurance, unemployment compensation, deposit insurance, or a minimum wage is compared with a ramp, the image must include more than a gently rising line. Where does it begin? Where does it lead? Whose work falls outside its reach?

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 established minimum-wage and other standards, with boundaries and exemptions. Section 14 of the original act even allowed special certificates authorizing wages below the general minimum for people whose earning capacity was considered impaired. A minimum written on paper did not mean everyone stood on the same line.

The farmworkers and domestic workers left outside the original old-age insurance still worked and still grew older. Exclusion of an occupation from that insurance did not allow a life to postpone aging.

A ramp is therefore more than an achievement credited to a giver. For its user, it is first a stretch of ground to travel. For someone unable to enter, it may also make the difference between entrances more visible.

Do not look only at the roof. Someone is still at the door.

VIII. Eleanor

Eleanor Roosevelt was Franklin's wife. She also published, traveled, and met people in her own name.

In 1943 she visited the Japanese American incarceration camp at Gila River, Arizona. Her public writing opposed deciding in advance that neighbors were untrustworthy because of ancestry. She wrote about people forced to dispose of property and unable to leave camp to attend to their businesses. She did not remain only in her husband's office.

But her article from that year, “A Challenge to American Sportsmanship,” contains something else. She accepted the authorities' division between the “loyal” and the “disloyal,” commended aspects of the removal administration, and explained incarceration as a wartime safety measure. Opposition to racial prejudice and acceptance of those explanations appeared in the same piece.

The article is not an uncreased sheet of paper. She asked readers to regard Japanese Americans as individuals, while placing some people's prospect of leaving camp beyond a test of loyalty. The people being discussed would hear different things in those sentences.

Meanwhile, work on student relocation was also under way. Established in 1942 through the American Friends Service Committee and university partners, the National Japanese American Student Relocation Council helped students continue their education outside the West Coast. Enrollment, a receiving institution, expenses, permission to leave: the connections had to be made one by one. This was not a passage that came into existence merely because a first lady said something to a president.

Nor were the people at its other end simply waiting to be placed. The catalogue summary of a March 1943 letter preserved at the University of the Pacific mentions Mabel Sugiyama's sense of obligation to remain with her parents. Continuing an education was one question. Facing the situation of a family still in camp was another. We cannot combine the two for her into an uncomplicated departure.

Beyond the presidential couple’s rooms, other desks come into view. Eleanor writes an article. The council contacts a university. A student weighs what leaving would mean for the family. Making a way out depends on people who are not in the same room each taking a next step.

In the Dickens essay, Catherine kept the letters. She was not a sheet of paper swallowed by a crack. Eleanor was not simply someone repairing her husband's image either. To encounter either woman, we need to see what she kept, what she wrote, where she went.

Someone writes a letter. Someone reads it. Someone arranges a place. Someone cannot yet leave.

Making a route passable is work still under way.

IX. Architecture

The architecture Roosevelt left can be encountered in small places.

An occupation box on a form. Insurance behind a bank counter. A record of a construction grant. A provision beside a pay slip that applies—or does not. None needs brick walls to alter the course of someone's day.

Such arrangements can outlive their makers without becoming exempt from examination. Papers are rewritten. Roads need repair. People continue asking about the entrance.

Now imagine him at this bridge.

Someone else has arrived. He is seated in a wheelchair, his hands resting on its rims. A pen lies beside him.

There are two inks in it. One recalls the New Deal's laws; the other, Executive Order 9066. Within this imagined pen, they have not mixed into a color that dilutes both records.

Socrates stands in the clearing. Plato crouches over his blueprints. Hume sits at his backgammon board. Schopenhauer looks beneath the bridge. Kierkegaard has made his leap. Turing looks at the apple in his hand. Chekhov leans against the railing. Cantor looks up at the sky. Copernicus has put down his book and walked away. Sartre paces with his pipe. Beauvoir holds a mirror. Quine has said a quiet sentence.

Tesla listens to a hum. Edison holds an unlit light bulb. Heisenberg's position is uncertain. Bohr holds an unsent letter. Tolstoy faces Chekhov with his prescription. Shakespeare is not there: he is the water beneath the bridge. Spinoza has glass dust on his hands. Aristotle crouches to lay the floor. Faraday lifts a plank. Maxwell stands writing equations.

Joan of Arc carries fire above the bridge. Wilde stands with easy elegance, holding that sentence. Ramanujan has emerged halfway through a gap. Oppenheimer walks on with his burden of ash. Charlotte holds a pen. Emily is out on the moor beyond the bridge; the wind comes from where she is. Boltzmann cradles the stone bearing his equation. Van Gogh has paint on his clothes, his brushes still dripping color. Dickens stands above the crack, pen and matchbox still in his hands. Churchill carries the flag, a pen in his other hand.

Roosevelt looks toward Churchill. The edge of the flag moves. The man with whom he once faced a war across the Atlantic is standing here on the bridge. Roosevelt’s hands return to the rims. The chair moves a little farther.

Kant stands in the distance.

A ramp lies before the wheelchair. The part Roosevelt helped construct joins the surface laid by others. The joint is visible. No sign announces that the road is finished.

He presses his hands against the rims and pushes. The chair moves along the gentle incline. Someone seated is choosing a direction too.

The pen remains beside him. People not reached by a particular protection, people once ordered out of their homes, have not left the record because the chair has passed this way.

Paper makes a small sound beneath Dickens's feet. Hume moves a piece. Wind comes across from Emily's moor and catches the flag on Churchill's shoulder. More than one kind of movement can be heard on the ramp.

Roosevelt pauses, then pushes again. No one requires him to stand before his passage counts as real. Kant remains in the distance, declaring no ending for the people on the road.

What Roosevelt helped build extends toward places he never reached. Those who come after must travel, repair, and sometimes choose another route.

What he helped build travels farther than he did.
