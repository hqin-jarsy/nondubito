斯宾诺莎，从来就没有墙

Spinoza, There Was Never a Wall

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、一片玻璃要磨多久

先看一片还没有磨好的玻璃。它已经透光，却未必能让人看清。曲面差一点，远处的东西就会在眼前变形。手艺人的工作，不是命令世界清楚起来，而是一遍遍调整自己手里的东西。

斯宾诺莎会做这样的工作。他磨制光学镜片，也同别人讨论望远镜、显微镜。这个后来把整个宇宙写进几何次序的人，知道一片玻璃的弧度不能靠愿望来决定，工具也不会因为使用者思想深刻就自动准确。

1656年7月27日，阿姆斯特丹的葡萄牙犹太社群对二十三岁的他颁下严厉禁令。社群成员不得与他交往、通信，或阅读他的文字。对一个在这里长大的人，这不只是某种观点被否定，也是熟悉的生活忽然向他关门。

那份文书留下了谴责，却没有留下足以重建全部争端的细节。我们也不知道他独处时怎样消化这件事。后来使用的拉丁名字Benedictus，与希伯来名字Baruch都有“受祝福”的意思；仅凭名字，读不出一场完整的内心决裂。

他仍然交朋友，写信，工作，迁居。不能再回到原来的共同体，不等于此后没有人与他来往。朋友会读他的手稿，向他提问，也会在他不在以后，把那些纸张交到印刷者手里。

所以不妨先留下这两个画面：一扇实际关闭的门，一片需要耐心磨制的玻璃。他后来谈的那个没有外部边界的自然，并没有替他取消世上真实的门槛。他仍须找到住处，挣出生活，也找到愿意听他说话的人。

二、神不在另一边

“神即自然。”读到这句话，很容易以为斯宾诺莎只是给山川草木换了一个庄严的名字。其实他追问得更深：所有存在的东西，是否还需要一个站在它们外面、从外面发号施令的主宰？

他的回答是：只有一个无限实体。这里的“实体”，不是一块巨大材料，而是依自身存在、通过自身来理解的东西。他称它为神，也称它为自然。万物都在其中，没有第二个独立的存在领域供它的制造者居住。

不过，一棵树、一张桌子和一个悲伤的人，仍然不是同一个具体东西。它们是有限的“样态”，有各自的关系、力量和遭遇。说它们不在自然之外，并不是把树木、家具和人的眼泪搅成一种没有差别的东西。

思维与广延，是我们认识这个实体的两种属性。斯宾诺莎把心灵与身体理解为同一个人的存在在不同属性下的表达，而不是两位隔墙传令、偶尔沟通失败的房客。身体不是灵魂暂住的坏旅馆。

这会改变看待日常经验的姿势。一个人疲倦、紧张、说不出话，我们不必先把他劈成“身体的问题”和“真正的他”。但理解这种立场，仍须认真讨论他的论证；借来量子物理的名词，并不会替它补上证明。

标题里的“从来就没有墙”，说的是神与自然之间没有那堵外在创造者的墙。它不是宣布一切界限虚假。眼前的玻璃仍有两面，病人仍需要休息，你的身体与我的身体，也仍然不能相互代替。

三、把前面的人请回来

这一乐章从荷马走到斯宾诺莎，共十九篇。一路读下来，许多看似牢固的判断变得不再那么容易：谁有资格讲述，知识凭什么成立，人能否被一个名称说尽，以及我们画下的界线究竟在保护什么。

柏拉图让我们追问，眼前可变的生活之外，是否还有更可靠的尺度。休谟让我们看见，许多确信靠习惯维持。克尔凯郭尔则提醒，解释得再漂亮的体系，到了一个人实际作决定时，也不能替他活。

图灵与康托尔让某些数学问题的边界变得精确；他们没有替所有哲学问题盖上统一印章。哥白尼移动的是天文学的中心。波伏瓦追问的是：社会怎样把一种生活安排，说成了一个人的天性。

蒯因、海森堡、西田、玻尔，分别改变了我们提问的方式。但他们并没有从不同学科共同证明“所有区别都不存在”。把他们排成整齐队伍，反而容易听不见每个人最费力说出的那句话。

斯宾诺莎来到这里，带来的也不是一个可以替大家结案的答案。他问得更根本：如果人从来就在自然之中，我们为什么总把自己想成临时被关进世界的客人，盼着有一天逃往真正属于自己的地方？

这个问题有一种安静的力量。你不必先离开自己的身体，才有资格理解生活。也不必等世界为你另开一道门，才开始思想。不过，已经身在其中，与已经了解全部，仍然是两回事。

四、几何次序里的心跳

翻开《伦理学》，先迎面而来的是定义、公理、命题、证明。读者可能觉得自己走错了门：想读一本教人生活的书，却来到一个需要逐条验算的地方。连悲伤与欲望，似乎也领到了编号。

继续读，人的声音会从附释里传出来。有误认自由的人，有被情感牵着走的人，也有以为只要责骂自己就能摆脱欲望的人。几何次序并没有把生活抽干；它试图让我们看清，自己如何被生活推动。

这套写法也向读者开放了反对的位置。你可以检查推理，可以质疑定义，也可以问某条公理是否真能承担那么大的结论。排列得像几何学，不等于全部前提已经获得了几何学的确定性。

对斯宾诺莎来说，欲望不是理性入住之前必须清空的杂物。一个存在努力保持自己的存在；在人这里，这种努力连着感受、意识和追求。人愿意继续活，愿意变得更有力量，这本来就是伦理学要理解的事。

因此，他没有递给我们一条“想通了就别难过”的捷径。《伦理学》第四部甚至说，一种情感要受到制约，需要有与之相反而且更强的情感。冷冷知道一个道理，并不总能战胜此刻紧攥住你的恐惧。

可以想想一封让你愤怒的信。反复读它，怒气越来越大；多知道一点事情的来由，找可信的人谈谈，再决定怎样回答，局面才可能松动。这是帮助理解的日常例子：理解也要进入生活，成为你真正用得上的力量。

五、自由不是站到因果外面

斯宾诺莎最令人不安的地方，在于他不给意志留下一个完全无原因的起点。我们说“这是我自己决定的”，却常常不知道，决定之前已经有什么推了我们一把：恐惧、习惯、欲求，或者对别人的模仿。

可是，他仍然写自由。自由不是从自然里逃出去，而是更多依自己能够理解的本性行动，少一些被自己不了解的力量摆布。对有限的人来说，这是一种可以增长、也可能缩小的能力，不是拿到一次便永久有效的证书。

受到伤害的人，可能先问自己为什么如此羞耻。看清羞耻怎样被制造出来，会帮助他拒绝某种控制。了解原因，在这里通向抵抗和改变条件，而不是替伤害者说一句“既然发生了，就应该发生”。

后来出生的康德，会以另一种方式追问知识的条件与限度。他区分事物作为经验对象的样子，与不依赖这些认识条件来考虑的事物。那不是把宇宙装修成两层楼，再把人永远锁在楼下。

将两人放在一起，是后来的读者在比较，不是斯宾诺莎已经回答过康德。一个坚持人始终在自然之中，一个追问我们凭什么声称认识；他们让问题保持张力。归属于世界，不会自动给我们一张解释世界一切的通行证。

我们也许可以带走一个更朴素的尺度：多理解一点，是否使你更能行动，更少拿自己出气，也更少伤害别人？如果所谓理解只是让人对屈辱认命，那份平静未必就是斯宾诺莎想要的自由。

六、独立需要有人帮助

磨镜片让斯宾诺莎有了一门手艺，也让他与当时的光学研究发生联系。关于镜片的来信、意见与评价，呈现的是一个参与知识往来的人。那间房不是同世界彻底断开的孤岛。

1673年，海德堡向他发出教授职位的邀请。他拒绝了。在回复法布里修斯的信里，他顾虑教学会占去研究时间，也不知道获许的思想自由，在涉及既定宗教时究竟止于哪里。这是一个具体而审慎的判断。

拒绝这份职位，不等于拒绝一切关系。他有朋友和支持者，也接受生活上的资助。独立的关键不是从未收过别人的帮助，而是帮助是否附带一份要求你交出判断的契约。有人愿意不这样要求，也是一种珍贵的自由条件。

想到居里夫人时，我们可以把注意力放在另一处：抽象的思想和实验，都要靠有限的身体持续做出来。仪器、材料、住处、照料，并不是伟大事业之外那些无关紧要的琐事。身体撑不住，工作就会停。

斯宾诺莎四十四岁死于肺部疾病；磨镜片的粉尘可能加重了问题，但不能替后人提供一份确定的病因诊断。把他的死亡写成“用命购买自由”，太容易使一个人本来值得得到的照料，变成英雄故事的障碍。

他可以坚持思想，也可以需要帮助。可以拒绝教职，也可以接受友人的支持。最动人的独立，未必是不欠任何人，而是人与人之间终于能有一些关系，使受助者不必把自己一起交出去。

七、书从房间里走出去

他不是十五年只写一本书、从不与外面说话的人。1663年，他以自己的名字出版对笛卡尔哲学原理的讲解。1670年，《神学政治论》匿名问世，讨论经文、宗教与思想自由，激起强烈反应。

写作在这里是一种公共行动。人们怎样解释一部经典，谁能以信仰的名义要求服从，国家能否命令人怎样思考——这些问题，会穿过书页，走到市民生活与权力的边界上。

与此同时，《伦理学》在朋友间被阅读、讨论。朋友提出问题，作者解释、修改，通信把看似密闭的体系连到具体读者。我们看见成书时整齐的命题，容易忘记它们也曾是尚未写定的句子。

1677年2月21日，斯宾诺莎在海牙去世。朋友们随后出版了题署1677年的遗著集，其中包括《伦理学》。他不能亲自面对的读者，由这些仍然活着的人替他接续起来。一本书走出房间，也靠友谊。

1678年，荷兰当局查禁了这部遗著集。禁令并没有使他的思想安静沉睡两百年。它继续被传播、反对和争论，后来又进入关于泛神论的激烈争执，并受到歌德、黑格尔等人的认真回应。

一个作者无法支配自己的身后。他的思想可能被误读，也可能被他不曾想过的人重新打开。这和镜片有点像：做镜片的人不能规定后来每只眼睛看什么，但可以尽力让交出去的东西，经得起使用和检验。

八、把镜片重新拿起来

现在回到那片玻璃。磨制得更好，不是它里面装进了更多世界，而是某些妨碍观看的地方被一点点处理了。这个手工活，可以成为理解斯宾诺莎的比喻，但不必把他的哲学说成一件绝不会失真的仪器。

镜片仍然有用途，也有限度。把它放进不合适的装置，或把一点模糊误认成天体的形状，都可能出错。读《伦理学》也一样：顺着作者的眼光看一遍，不等于把自己的判断从此交给他。

他吸引人的，是把许多使人觉得丢脸的经验重新放回可以理解的自然中。害怕、嫉妒、依赖、愤怒，都有来路。一个人不必先假装自己已经没有这些，才配开始学怎样生活。

但理解来路以后，还有方向的问题。我们可以让生活条件帮助人更能思考、相助和行动，也可以使恐惧更深，依赖更重。都发生在自然之中，并不意味着对人的生活同样好。

因此，“一”不能成为抹去具体人的大词。有人被逐出社群，有人替他保存手稿，有人面对伤害决定拒绝。这些差别必须留着，否则我们只记住了体系的辽阔，却把写体系的人和读体系的人都弄丢了。

第四乐章走到这里，留下的不是一张所有思想家共同签字的答案。它更像一张摆满工具的桌子。你可以借用其中一件，让眼前的生活清楚一点；用完以后，还要问自己，它有没有把某个人遮住。

九、桥上的空位

最后，让我们仍然回到这组散文虚构的桥上。斯宾诺莎走来，手里带着一片包在软布里的镜片。他没有把它举到所有人面前，要求大家从此只准用同一种方式看。

荷马留下的声音从人群里传过去。柏拉图还在追问可靠的尺度，休谟愿意听完再去打双陆棋。契诃夫注意到一个站得太久的人，先替他找来椅子。每个人仍在做不同的事。

波伏瓦把镜子挪开一点，让新来的人有地方坐。玻尔把未寄出的信放在手边。图灵不用再拿一个关于他死亡的传说证明自己，康托尔也不用当场把所有无穷解释完。这一刻，没有谁被要求只剩一个标签。

斯宾诺莎坐下。他并不占据一个与别处毫无区别的位置；如果挪开椅子，让一个疲倦的人站着，这个变化对那个人就很重要。共同属于自然，并没有抹去坐下与站着、被照料与被忽略之间的差别。

他把镜片放到桌上。旁边是朋友替他保存下来的书。看着这两样东西，我们也许会想起：一个人让思想走得很远，自己却始终需要空气、食物、住处，以及某个愿意把一叠纸送去印刷的人。

“从来就没有墙”，在这里不必变成一句巨大的命令。它可以轻一点：你不是落在自然之外的废物，你的困惑也不是不配存在的证据。先留下来，看看自己怎样走到了这里。

然后仍然可以不同意。可以追问定义，可以拒绝某种安排，可以为别人留出离开的路。一种足够宽广的理解，应当让差别更能被看清，而不是让所有人都消失在同一个漂亮句子里。

第四乐章，十九种人生，到这里暂时停下。桌上的镜片没有收走。下一个人来了，仍然有位置，也仍然可以用自己的眼睛看。

I. What a Piece of Glass Requires

A piece of glass can admit light and still prevent us from seeing clearly. Give its surface the wrong curve and a distant object swims out of shape. The craft consists of attending to what is in your hands, rather than demanding that the world become more accommodating.

Spinoza knew this work. He ground optical lenses and discussed telescopes and microscopes with other people interested in their construction. The philosopher who would arrange the universe in geometrical order knew that an instrument does not become accurate because its maker has impressive thoughts.

On 27 July 1656, the Portuguese Jewish community of Amsterdam placed the twenty-three-year-old under a severe ban. Its members were forbidden to associate or correspond with him or read his writings. For someone raised within that community, an intellectual condemnation also closed doors in an ordinary, familiar life.

The surviving decree gives us denunciations, not a complete account of the dispute. His private response is harder still to recover. Baruch and the Latin Benedictus both mean blessed; the names alone cannot tell us exactly how a young man experienced the loss of belonging.

He nevertheless continued to work, move house, make friends and exchange letters. Leaving one community did not leave him without human company. Friends read his manuscripts and questioned him. After his death, friends would arrange for the manuscripts to reach a printer.

Keep both things in view: the door that really closed, and the glass that needed patient attention. The natural world he would describe had no outside. That did not spare its inhabitants the difficulty of finding a room, earning a living, or finding somebody willing to listen.

II. God Is Not Across the Divide

God, or Nature. The words can sound like an elaborate compliment to trees and mountains. Spinoza is asking something more demanding: does everything that exists depend on a ruler standing outside it, directing the whole enterprise from a separate realm?

His answer is a single, infinite substance. Substance here is not an enormous quantity of material. It is what exists in itself and is understood through itself. He calls it God and Nature; it requires no second independent realm in which its maker can reside.

A tree, a table and a grieving person are still different finite things. In his vocabulary they are modes, each with its determinate relations and capacities. Their existence within one Nature does not turn timber, furniture and someone's tears into interchangeable items.

Thought and extension are two attributes through which we know this substance. A person's mind and body express the same individual under different attributes, rather than occupying adjoining rooms and trying to pass instructions through a wall. The body is not a defective lodging for a visiting soul.

This gives an ordinary experience a different emphasis. Faced with someone exhausted and unable to speak, we need not divide a bodily inconvenience from the supposedly real person inside. But the philosophical account still needs arguments. Borrowed words from quantum physics cannot do that work for it.

The missing wall in this essay's title separates Nature from an external creator. It is not every boundary whatever. A lens still has surfaces. A sick person still needs rest. Your body cannot take the place of mine simply because neither exists outside Nature.

III. Inviting the Earlier Lives Back

This movement has travelled from Homer to Spinoza: nineteen lives. Along the way, apparently settled questions have become difficult again. Who gets to speak? What supports our knowledge? Can a description exhaust a person? What do the distinctions we make allow us to notice or overlook?

Plato asks whether changing experience can answer to a more dependable measure. Hume finds habit sustaining many of our confident expectations. Kierkegaard insists that, however elegant the explanation, a system cannot take over the actual decision through which an individual has to live.

Turing and Cantor sharpen the boundaries of particular mathematical questions. They do not settle every philosophical problem at once. Copernicus relocates an astronomical centre; Beauvoir asks how an imposed arrangement of life acquires the apparent authority of a person's nature.

Quine, Heisenberg, Nishida and Bohr change different kinds of question. They are not witnesses jointly establishing that every distinction is false. Put them into one perfectly disciplined procession and we may lose precisely what each struggled hardest to make audible.

Spinoza arrives with another question, rather than a verdict on the others: if we have always belonged to Nature, why do we so often imagine ourselves as temporary prisoners of the world, waiting for admission to somewhere more properly our own?

There is quiet strength in that question. You need not leave your body before beginning to understand your life, or wait for the universe to open a special entrance. Yet being within the world is one thing. Having understood everything about it is quite another.

IV. A Heartbeat in Geometrical Order

Open the Ethics and you meet definitions, axioms, propositions, demonstrations. A reader looking for help with living may feel they have entered the wrong building. Even sorrow and desire seem to have been issued numbers and asked to wait in their appointed places.

Keep reading. Human voices emerge in the explanatory notes: people mistaken about their freedom, people driven by feelings, people who suppose that scolding themselves will end their desires. The order is an attempt to understand how life moves us, not to drain the life away.

The form also gives disagreement something to work on. We can examine an inference, dispute a definition, or ask whether an axiom can bear the weight placed upon it. Writing philosophy in the manner of geometry does not automatically give every premise mathematical certainty.

Desire is central. Each thing strives to persist in its being; in human life that striving involves awareness, feeling and pursuit. Wanting to continue, wanting a greater capacity to live, is not debris to be cleared out before reason can enter the premises.

Nor does Spinoza offer the shortcut of understanding an emotion and instantly rising above it. Part IV says an affect must be checked by an opposing, stronger affect. A truth known coldly may have little purchase on the fear currently holding a person fast.

Think of an infuriating letter. Reading it again feeds the anger. Learning more about the circumstances, talking to someone trustworthy, then deciding how to answer may alter the situation. This is an illustration, not his case history: understanding becomes useful by entering the forces of a life.

V. Freedom Inside the Causes

Spinoza's unsettling claim is that willing has no entirely uncaused starting point. We announce that we decided for ourselves, often knowing little about what moved us towards the decision: fear, habit, appetite, admiration, or the wish to resemble somebody else.

Yet he continues to write about freedom. Greater freedom means acting more from our own nature as understood through adequate ideas, less at the mercy of causes we scarcely comprehend. For finite people this is a capacity that can grow or diminish, not a certificate acquired once.

Someone subjected to humiliation may begin by asking where their shame came from. Recognizing how it was produced can help them resist control and change their circumstances. Understanding causes can lead towards opposition; it does not turn whatever has happened into whatever ought to have happened.

Kant, born after Spinoza's death, would ask different questions about the conditions and limits of knowledge. His distinction between appearances and things considered independently of our ways of knowing is not a literal two-storey universe with humanity locked on the ground floor.

It is we, later readers, who put these thinkers together. Spinoza did not answer a philosopher who had not yet been born. One insists on our place within Nature; the other examines our claims to knowledge. Belonging to the world need not make every explanation of it trustworthy.

A modest practical question remains available to us. Does understanding more help us act, torment ourselves less, and do less harm? If an explanation merely persuades somebody to submit to humiliation, its appearance of tranquillity deserves closer inspection. Quietness alone is an unreliable measure of freedom.

VI. Independence Has Friends

Lens work gave Spinoza a trade and a place in contemporary exchanges about optics. Letters, practical questions and assessments of his work show a person participating in knowledge. The room in which he worked was connected to other rooms, other hands, other minds.

In 1673 he declined the offer of a professorship at Heidelberg. His reply to Fabritius names specific concerns: teaching would interrupt his philosophical work, and the promised freedom to philosophize had uncertain limits where established religion was concerned. He was weighing actual conditions, not performing universal withdrawal.

He had friends and supporters and accepted financial help. Independence need not mean never receiving anything from anyone. A more useful question is whether help comes with a demand to surrender judgment. Assistance without that price can be one of the material conditions of an independent life.

Marie Curie offers a comparison at this level: both thought and experiment have to be carried on by finite bodies. Instruments, materials, housing and care are not insignificant details surrounding the great achievement. When the body can no longer continue, the work stops.

Spinoza died at forty-four with pulmonary illness. Lens-grinding dust may have aggravated it, but that possibility is not a secure retrospective diagnosis. Turning his death into freedom purchased with his life can make care seem an obstacle to an attractive story about sacrifice.

He could defend his thinking and still need help, refuse a chair and still accept a friend's support. Independence is sometimes most moving not when a person owes nobody anything, but when relationships allow them to receive something without handing themselves over in return.

VII. How a Book Leaves the Room

Spinoza did not spend fifteen silent years producing his only book. In 1663 he published his exposition of Descartes's Principles of Philosophy under his own name. In 1670 the anonymous Theological-Political Treatise entered public controversy over scripture, religion and freedom of thought.

Writing here is public action. Who can interpret a sacred text? Who can demand obedience in the name of faith? Can a state determine what people think? Such questions travel from pages into the relations between citizens, religious authorities and political power.

Meanwhile, friends read and discussed the Ethics. Questions prompted explanations; correspondence joined an apparently self-contained system to particular readers. Looking at the orderly sequence of the finished propositions, it is easy to forget that these sentences were once still being worked out.

Spinoza died in The Hague on 21 February 1677. His friends subsequently published the posthumous works, including the Ethics, in a volume dated 1677. Living people connected him to readers he could no longer meet. A book's departure from its room depended on friendship as well as arguments.

The posthumous collection was banned by the authorities of Holland in 1678. Prohibition did not put his thinking to sleep for two centuries. It continued to circulate and provoke resistance, later entering the pantheism controversy and the intellectual lives of figures including Goethe and Hegel.

An author cannot govern that future. Misunderstanding is possible, as is an unexpected reader who opens the work afresh. A maker of lenses cannot decide what every subsequent eye must examine. The hope is to make something that can withstand use and scrutiny after it leaves one's hands.

VIII. Picking Up the Lens Again

Return to the glass. Improving it does not involve packing more world inside it. Some impediments to seeing are patiently worked upon. That craft can serve as an image for reading Spinoza, without making his philosophy an instrument guaranteed never to distort.

A lens has a purpose and limits. Put it in an unsuitable arrangement, or mistake a blur for a property of the object, and it can mislead. Following the Ethics through its argument need not mean giving its author permanent custody of our judgment.

Part of its appeal is the return of supposedly shameful experience to an intelligible Nature. Fear, jealousy, dependency and anger have causes. Someone need not pretend to have outgrown all of them before they are permitted to start learning how to live.

Understanding where they come from leaves questions about direction. Conditions can help people think, cooperate and act; they can also deepen fear and dependency. That both occur within Nature does not make their consequences equally good for the lives involved.

Oneness must not become a word large enough to erase everyone. A person was excluded; another preserved his papers; another refused an injury. Keep those differences visible. Otherwise we remember the spaciousness of the system while losing both the human being who wrote it and the human beings who read.

This fourth movement ends without an answer signed by every thinker. Imagine instead a table holding several instruments. You may borrow one to see an aspect of your life more clearly. Afterwards, ask what it illuminated, and whether its use left somebody else out of sight.

IX. A Place on the Bridge

Let us return, finally, to the invented bridge of these essays. Spinoza arrives carrying a lens wrapped in soft cloth. He does not hold it before every face and announce that, from now on, there is only one permitted way of looking.

Voices from Homer move through the gathering. Plato is still asking about dependable measures; Hume is willing to listen before returning to backgammon. Chekhov notices somebody who has stood too long and fetches a chair. Different people are still doing different things.

Beauvoir moves her mirror to make room. Bohr puts his unsent letter beside him. Turing need not carry a disputed story of his death as identification; Cantor need not explain every infinity before he may sit down. No one is required to become a single label.

Spinoza takes his seat. Its location is not interchangeable with every other location: remove it, leave a tired person standing, and the difference matters to that person. Belonging to one Nature has not abolished the difference between receiving care and being overlooked.

On the table he places the lens beside the book his friends preserved. A thought can travel an enormous distance. The thinker still needs air, food, somewhere to live, and perhaps somebody prepared to deliver a bundle of papers to the printer.

“There was never a wall” need not swell into an immense command. Here it can be gentler: you are not waste expelled from Nature, and being confused is no evidence that you have forfeited your place. Stay a little. Look at how you came to be here.

Disagreement remains possible. A definition can be challenged, an arrangement refused, an exit left open for somebody else. An understanding generous enough for human beings should make their differences more visible, rather than dissolving them in one beautiful sentence.

The fourth movement, nineteen lives, pauses here. The lens remains on the table. There is still a place for the next person, and that person is still allowed to look with their own eyes.
