玻尔兹曼，乱

Boltzmann, Disorder

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、墓碑

维也纳中央公墓。一块墓碑。上面刻着一个方程。

S = k log W

S 是熵，k 是玻尔兹曼常数。W 数的，是同一种宏观状态可以由多少种微观安排实现。这里的 log 是自然对数。字很少。字下面藏着的东西很多。

你看见一瓶气体。温度稳定，压力稳定，仪表上的指针几乎不动。可瓶子里面没有一支分子队伍在列队等待。它们一直在运动、碰撞，换了方向，又碰撞。许多不同的内部情形，都能给出近乎相同的读数。

稳定是真的。运动也是真的。那个整齐的数字没有撒谎，只是没有把底下的每一件事都告诉你。

玻尔兹曼把这两层接了起来。不是宣布秩序不存在，而是追问：不必让每一个分子整齐站好，秩序为什么仍然能够出现？

后来，普朗克把这个关系写成了今天熟悉的形式。再后来，人们把它刻在墓碑上。一生的工作，在这里收成了几个字母。

一个方程。留在石头上。

做这项工作的人不在了。

二、他

路德维希·玻尔兹曼。1844年2月20日，生于维也纳。

他在维也纳大学学物理，约瑟夫·斯特凡是他的老师。1866年拿到博士学位，二十二岁。后来在格拉茨、维也纳、慕尼黑、莱比锡之间辗转。有时教数学，有时教实验物理，有时教理论物理。最后回到维也纳，还讲起了自然哲学。

这些地名排起来，像一生没有放稳的行李。职位、研究方向、人际关系，都牵动着去留。换一座城，重新走进一间教室；几年的日子，又在另一个地址展开。

他矮胖，头发卷曲。有关他的记述里，有敏感，有热情，也有严重的情绪低落。同一个人身上，可以有这么不一样的天气。

讲台上又是另一面。

莉泽·迈特纳听过他的课。保罗·埃伦费斯特也是他的学生。埃伦费斯特在1899至1900年听他讲热的机械理论；对这个曾经厌倦学校的年轻人来说，那些课重新打开了物理。后来，他在玻尔兹曼指导下取得博士学位。

一个人可以在争论里疲惫，在课堂上却让另一个人想继续学下去。学生从他那里带走的，不只是一个分布、一条推导，也可能是这样的感觉：原来这些难懂的东西，是可以走进去的。

这也是他留下的工作。没有刻在墓碑上。

三、地板下面是乱的

牛顿力学给了人一种极强的信心：只要知道各个粒子的位置、速度和它们之间的作用，就能沿着运动方程往下算。

拉普拉斯曾把这种信心推到一个想象的极端：一个掌握全部状态与作用、又有足够分析能力的智能，可以把过去与将来一并纳入计算。

但在一瓶气体面前，我们并不是那个智能。粒子太多了。你无法逐个登记它们刚才撞上了谁，下一刻又往哪里去。

玻尔兹曼并不是简单地说，牛顿错了，世界从此没有规律了。他做的是另一件事：不掌握每个分子的行踪，能不能仍然说明一瓶气体会怎样？为什么热会传过去？为什么压力会稳定在这里？

可以换一种问法。不再问这一颗分子下一刻在哪里，而是问：有多少分子落在某一段速度范围里？有多少种内部安排，会让外面的仪表显示同一个结果？

想象地板上放着一个温度计。二十六摄氏度。

掀开地板，下面是分子，各自运动，彼此碰撞。温度计却不用追着每一个分子跑：在经典理想气体模型中，绝对温度对应分子的平均平动动能。分子不断撞向容器壁、传递动量，形成压力。指针的安稳，正从这些不停歇的运动里来。

但同一个温度读数，确实容得下大量不同的分子行踪。

亚里士多德在这个系列里一直铺着地板：分清事物，把它们放到可以理解的位置上。玻尔兹曼掀开的，是我们以为已经铺平的热力学。他没有让地板消失。他让我们看见，地板的可靠，不一定来自底下有什么东西永远不动。

这里还有一个更棘手的问题。一次分子碰撞可以在力学中倒着描述，为什么一整瓶气体的变化，却通常不能在日常生活中倒着发生？洛施密特提出的可逆性异议，就碰到了这个关节。它不是一句坏话，而是玻尔兹曼必须回答的问题。

法拉第把注意力引向场。玻尔兹曼把注意力引向大量粒子的运动。这不是两个人在地板下找到了互相排斥的世界，而是原先觉得足够的说明，又露出了需要追问的地方。

下面很乱。但乱，不等于无法理解。

四、谁相信原子

原子存在吗？

十九世纪末，这不是一道所有物理学家都会给出相同答案的题。

玻尔兹曼把分子运动当作理解物质的一条真实线索。马赫却警惕另一件事：一个模型很方便，并不等于我们已经有权把模型里的东西当成世界的最终实在。他对不可直接观察的原子持强烈怀疑，也追问：描述经验，究竟非要这些东西不可吗？

奥斯特瓦尔德走的是另一条路。他希望以能量及其转化来组织物理学，不再让一群假定的小粒子承担全部解释。马赫与他不是同一套立场。反对玻尔兹曼的人，也并不是只在重复同一句“不存在”。

1895年，吕贝克的一次科学会议上，奥斯特瓦尔德批评机械论解释。玻尔兹曼起来反驳。

索末菲在后来的回忆中，把这场交锋比作公牛与灵活斗士的较量，并认为这一次公牛占了上风。他还记得，克莱因支持玻尔兹曼，当时在场的年轻数学家也站在这一边。那是多年后对现场的回望，不是留下来的逐字速记；但至少在这份回忆里，玻尔兹曼绝不是一个人。

他有反对者，也有读者、学生和同道。1899年，他当选英国皇家学会外籍会员。1904年六十岁生日时，来自多国的物理学家合编论文集向他致敬。

争论没有结束。认可也已经发生。生日的论文集不能替他回答洛施密特，会场上的反对声也不能使那一册论文集变成不存在。

会场里有人反驳他，也有人接着他的论证往下想。玻尔兹曼的声音不是空房间里的回音。有人听见了。听见，并不总是同意；不同意，也并不总是不肯听。

五、他和康托尔

康托尔发现，无穷之间也有大小之别。把每一项都数出来的许诺，并不适用于一切无穷集合。对角线论证把一张看似完整的名单，推到了自己的边界。

玻尔兹曼掀开另一块地板：一个稳定读数的下面，竟有如此多的分子行踪。康托尔要检验一份声称能列尽的名单，他要理解一瓶不能逐个追踪的气体。数学证明与物理实验，各自有自己的路。

他们仍然可以站在一起。

因为在各自的工作里，一种习惯已久的理解，都遇到了以前没有容纳进去的东西。康托尔不能因为克罗内克反对，就让已经提出的数学问题消失。玻尔兹曼也不能因为原子尚有争议，就假装统计方法没有说明任何东西。

但两个人并非直到死后才得到承认。康托尔参加创办德国数学家协会，担任过首任主席；1904年，他获颁西尔维斯特奖章。同一年，玻尔兹曼收到的，是同行为他的六十岁生日编成的论文集。

认可已经在那里。疾病和困难也仍然在那里。两件事可以同时发生。

康托尔1918年在疗养院去世。玻尔兹曼1906年在杜伊诺去世。这是两个人各自生命的终点。协会、课堂、同行编成的论文集，却没有因为后来发生的事，从他们的一生中消失。

我们很容易在这里等一个圆满的结局：有人终于承认你，然后一切都好了。可奖章到了，论文集到了，一个人仍然要过第二天。他需要的，并不只有“你是对的”。

一页数学可以流传下去。一条方程也可以。

那不是一个人全部活过的样子。

六、他和麦克斯韦

麦克斯韦和玻尔兹曼之间，不只是一条供后人画出来的比较线。他们的名字留在同一个分布上。

麦克斯韦—玻尔兹曼分布。说的是在相应的经典平衡条件下，气体分子的速度怎样分布。不是每个分子都以同样的速率运动。有快的，有慢的；在给定条件下，各个速率范围所占的比例却有规律。

麦克斯韦先走了一段。玻尔兹曼接着扩展，把分布与热平衡、碰撞和热力学的问题连得更深。两个人都在学习怎样不逐个追赶分子，却又不把分子丢掉。

在这个系列里，麦克斯韦带来的是光。他把法拉第开启的场的研究推进到电磁理论。玻尔兹曼带来的是“乱”，是热力学整齐读数底下许许多多的运动。

但光的理论并没有一路顺风地被接进来。麦克斯韦1879年去世；赫兹的重要电磁波实验到1880年代后期才出现。今天写在一起的方程与实验证据，当时还隔着没有走完的路。

玻尔兹曼自己就是麦克斯韦理论的支持者。他讲授它，写过关于它的讲义，也帮助它在欧洲大陆传播。那个在“乱”中追问的人，也曾替光的理论开路。

光使人抬头，分子使人俯身。写到这里，我更愿意让他们并肩站着，而不把一种发现留给美，把另一种留给寒冷。

无数分子各走各的路，一条分布却能把它们联系起来。乱的底下，也有可以慢慢读懂的东西。

七、熵

热力学第二定律说，在宏观热力学的描述中，一个与外界既不交换物质、也不交换能量的孤立系统，熵不会减少。达到平衡，并不意味着里面所有运动都停了。

“孤立”两个字不能省。热水放在房间里变凉，热水自身的熵可以减少，周围却接收了热量。计算整体的变化，才知道有没有违背第二定律。不能把整个世界写成每一处、每一刻都只准越来越乱。

想象一只盒子，中间有一道隔板。气体原来只占一半，另一半是真空。把隔板抽掉。

气体散开了。

让气体差不多遍布全盒，有极多的微观实现方式；让所有分子恰好留在原来那一半，所占的可能性小得惊人。宏观平衡状态之所以常见，不是因为每个分子都知道自己应该去哪里，而是因为与它相容的微观状态占了压倒性的多数。

但“可能性多”，也不是一个推着粒子跑的力。还要看粒子怎样运动、碰撞，系统从什么状态开始。玻尔兹曼的统计解释，既留下了微小涨落的位置，也要求我们分清极不可能与绝对不可能。

打碎的杯子不会在我们的日常等待中自行复原。可这不是每片碎片接到了一条“只能往坏处走”的命令。把熵增说成生活只会败坏，便把一个物理问题变成了它没有给出的判词。

热寂也是如此。它令人不安的地方，不在于每个粒子最后都一动不动，而在于可持续利用来做功的差别逐渐消失。没有温差，热机就不能照原来的方式不断工作。至于整个宇宙怎样走向遥远的将来，还涉及宇宙的历史和演化条件，不是墓碑上一行字就能独自写完的结局。

这里仍然能听见一点契诃夫的回声。

《三姊妹》里的姐妹没有走到莫斯科。可是“没有走到”四个字，没有把她们说完。第四幕里，她们还在面对离别，还要决定怎样继续生活。

一个温度读数也没有把瓶子里的每一次运动说完。这里相像的，是简短说明与它所没有逐一说出的东西之间，那一点距离。不是说人物的遗憾就是熵，也不是说分子有人的未竟之志。

契诃夫不替姐妹把日子过掉。一个方程，同样不能替读它的人决定该怎样活。

八、杜伊诺

1906年9月5日。杜伊诺，的里雅斯特附近。

玻尔兹曼与妻女在那里度假。他在这次假期中自杀去世，六十二岁。

生平里的争论是真实的。疾病与生活中的困难也是真实的。可这些材料没有替我们保存那个时刻的全部。它们无法排成一条笔直的因果线，从1895年的会场一直通到这一天。

1905年，爱因斯坦已经发表关于布朗运动的论文。他从分子运动出发，对悬浮微粒可以怎样移动提出可检验的定量预言。论文没有在发表的那一天，就把原子像一张照片一样直接交到每个人眼前。1908至1909年，佩兰的实验测量为分子理论提供了有力支持。玻尔兹曼没有活到这些实验展开的时候。

他也并非只在一堵墙前独自站了一生。

1904年，他参加圣路易斯世界博览会期间的科学会议。1905年，他又到伯克利讲学，并去斯坦福访问。这是两年的行程，不是同一趟旅行。

在写加州之行的游记里，他还会为自己的英语发愁，特意讲起“黑板”和“粉笔”这两个词。一个已经能教别人理解分子运动的人，仍要在另一种语言里学习怎样把课讲下去。

那些写吃饭、旅行、讲课的句子，也属于玻尔兹曼。它们不证明他没有痛苦，只是不让痛苦吞掉整个人。

科学上的墙正在一些地方松动。我们没有足够依据，替他判断他究竟看见了多少。

他没有等到后来。后来，也不能把他还回来。

九、乱

以下桥头相遇，是本篇的文学想象。

S = k log W。

石上的字很整齐。它说的，却不是一群整齐站好的分子。

桥头上又多了一个人。他站着。有点胖。视力不好。抱着一块石头，喘着气。

方程刻在石头上。他把它抱在怀里。很重。

苏格拉底站在空地上。柏拉图蹲着画图纸。休谟摆开双陆棋盘。叔本华看桥底下。克尔凯郭尔跳了。图灵看苹果。契诃夫靠着栏杆。康托尔看天上。哥白尼放下书走了。萨特转来转去。波伏瓦举着镜子。蒯因说了一句话。

特斯拉听嗡嗡声。爱迪生拿着灯泡。海森堡位置不确定。玻尔拿着没寄出的信。托尔斯泰拿着药方站在契诃夫对面。莎士比亚不在——他是桥下面的水。斯宾诺莎手里有玻璃粉。亚里士多德蹲着铺地板。法拉第蹲着掀地板。麦克斯韦站着写方程。

贞德带着火飘在桥的上方。王尔德站得很好看，手里拿着那句话。拉马努金从缝隙里冒出半个身子。奥本海默背着灰往前走。夏洛蒂拿着笔。艾米莉在桥外面的荒原上，风从她那里吹来。

玻尔兹曼站在亚里士多德旁边。他低头看地板。

亚里士多德正在把一块木板放稳。玻尔兹曼看见的，却不止那块木板。那些彼此不同、不断变动的微观情形，没有因为上面放好了一个名称，就一起停下来。

地板仍然承着他们。玻尔兹曼低下头，脚下的安稳和他看见的运动，同时在那里。

法拉第从另一道缝隙望过来。麦克斯韦的笔没有停。玻尔兹曼看看那支笔，又看看怀里的石头。

他把石头稍稍挪了一下。边角硌着手。他停下来喘气。那几个字母随着他的手臂，慢慢低了一点。

然后他抬头。

远处，康德站在那里。

人影太远了。他一时甚至想起统计涨落：会不会只是一个短暂出现的轮廓，过一会儿就散了？

他仍然朝那边走去。走得不快。石头在怀里。桥面在脚下。风从艾米莉所在的荒原吹来。

秩序未必永远稳固。但走向秩序这件事，不是假的。

I. The Tombstone

Vienna Central Cemetery. A tombstone. An equation cut into the stone.

S = k log W

S is entropy. k is the Boltzmann constant. W counts the microscopic arrangements that can give rise to the same macroscopic state. Here, log means the natural logarithm. A few letters. A great deal beneath them.

Look at a bottle of gas. The temperature is steady, the pressure steady, the needle on the gauge hardly moving. Inside, no company of molecules stands at attention. They move, collide, change direction, collide again. Many different things can be happening in the bottle while the instruments give almost the same readings.

The steadiness is real. So is the motion. The neat number is not lying. It simply does not tell you everything that is going on underneath.

Boltzmann connected these two levels. He was not announcing that order did not exist. He was asking how it could exist without every molecule first being made to stand in line.

Planck later gave the relation its familiar form. Later still, others had it carved on the tomb. A lifetime of work comes down, here, to a few letters.

An equation remains in the stone.

The person who did the work is gone.

II. The Man

Ludwig Boltzmann. Born in Vienna on February 20, 1844.

He studied physics at the University of Vienna, where Josef Stefan was his teacher. In 1866, at twenty-two, he earned his doctorate. After that came Graz, Vienna, Munich, Leipzig: sometimes mathematics, sometimes experimental physics, sometimes theoretical physics. Back in Vienna, he also lectured on natural philosophy.

Read the place names in succession: luggage never quite unpacked. Appointments, research, relationships all entered into the decision to stay or leave. Another city, another classroom. A few more years lived at a different address.

He was stocky, with curly hair. Those who wrote about him describe sensitivity and warmth, and periods of profound depression. Very different weather could pass through the same person.

In the lecture room, another side of him appeared.

Lise Meitner attended his lectures. Paul Ehrenfest was a student too. In 1899–1900, Ehrenfest took his course on the mechanical theory of heat. For a young man who had become weary of school, those lectures made physics worth learning again. He later completed his doctorate under Boltzmann's supervision.

A person worn down by arguments can still make someone else want to go on studying. A student may leave with more than a distribution or a derivation. There may also be this discovery: the difficult subject has a way in. Someone has shown you where to begin.

That, too, is work he left behind. It is not carved on the tombstone.

III. Beneath the Floor Is Disorder

Newtonian mechanics gave people a formidable kind of confidence. Know where the particles are, how fast and in what direction they are moving, and how they act on one another; then follow the equations forward.

Laplace carried that confidence to an imagined extreme. An intellect that knew every relevant state and force, and possessed sufficient powers of analysis, could bring the past and the future within a single calculation.

Standing in front of a bottle of gas, we are not that intellect. There are too many particles. We cannot keep a register of what each one has just hit and where it is heading next.

Boltzmann's response was not simply to declare Newton wrong and the world lawless. He asked a different question. Without knowing the path of every molecule, could we still explain what the gas would do? Why heat would pass from one place to another? Why the pressure would settle at a particular value?

Instead of asking where this molecule will be a moment from now, ask how many molecules fall within a given range of speeds. Ask how many internal arrangements could produce the same reading on the instrument outside.

Imagine a thermometer standing on a floor. Twenty-six degrees Celsius.

Lift the floorboard: molecules move and collide below. The thermometer need not follow each one. In the classical ideal-gas model, absolute temperature corresponds to their mean translational kinetic energy. Their continual impacts transfer momentum to the container walls, producing pressure. The steady needle depends on motion that never settles down.

Yet the same temperature can accommodate a vast number of different molecular paths.

Throughout this series, Aristotle has been laying a floor: distinguishing things, finding places from which they can be understood. Boltzmann lifts a plank in the thermodynamics we had thought was already finished. He does not make the floor disappear. He shows us that its reliability need not depend on something underneath remaining forever still.

There is a harder question here. Mechanics allows a molecular collision to be described in reverse. Why, then, do the changes in a whole bottle of gas usually refuse to run backward in ordinary life? Loschmidt's objection about reversibility reached precisely this difficulty. It was not an insult. It was a problem Boltzmann had to answer.

Faraday directed attention toward the field. Boltzmann directed it toward the motion of enormous numbers of particles. They did not discover rival worlds beneath the floor, only one of which could be real. In each case, an explanation that had seemed sufficient revealed something more to ask about.

There is disorder underneath. That does not put it beyond understanding.

IV. Who Believed in Atoms?

Do atoms exist?

At the end of the nineteenth century, not every physicist would have given the same answer.

Boltzmann took molecular motion to be a genuine clue to the nature of matter. Mach worried about something else: the usefulness of a model did not automatically entitle scientists to treat its contents as the world's ultimate reality. He was deeply skeptical about atoms that could not be directly observed. Did a description of experience really have to include them?

Ostwald took another route. He wanted to organize physics around energy and its transformations, rather than make a host of hypothetical little particles bear the weight of explanation. He and Mach were not offering the same argument. Opposition to Boltzmann was not merely a chorus repeating “They don't exist.”

In 1895, at a scientific meeting in Lübeck, Ostwald attacked mechanical explanations. Boltzmann rose to argue against him.

Writing much later, Sommerfeld likened the encounter to a bull confronting an agile fighter, and thought the bull had come off better. He also remembered Felix Klein supporting Boltzmann, with the young mathematicians in the audience on the same side. This was a recollection across many years, not a stenographic record of the meeting. But in that recollection, at least, Boltzmann was anything but alone.

He had opponents. He also had readers, students, and colleagues. In 1899, he was elected a Foreign Member of the Royal Society. For his sixtieth birthday in 1904, physicists from several countries contributed to a volume of papers in his honor.

The arguments were not over. Recognition had already arrived. A birthday volume could not answer Loschmidt's objection for him. Nor could the objections raised at a meeting make that volume cease to exist.

Some people challenged his arguments; others took them further. His voice was not an echo in an empty room. Someone was listening. Listening did not always mean agreeing. Disagreement did not always mean a refusal to listen.

V. Boltzmann and Cantor

Cantor found that infinities could differ in size. The promise of listing every member could not be kept for every infinite set. His diagonal argument brought an apparently complete list up against its own limit.

Boltzmann lifted another floorboard. Beneath a steady reading lay innumerable molecular paths. Cantor tested a list that claimed to include everything; Boltzmann studied a gas whose particles could not be followed one by one. Mathematical proof and physical experiment each had their own work to do.

The two men can still stand beside each other.

In each man's work, a familiar way of understanding things encountered something it had not previously made room for. Kronecker's opposition could not make the mathematical questions Cantor had raised disappear. Nor did uncertainty about atoms oblige Boltzmann to pretend that statistical methods explained nothing.

Neither man had to wait until death for all recognition. Cantor helped found the German Mathematical Society and served as its first president. He received the Sylvester Medal in 1904. That same year, Boltzmann received the collection of papers assembled for his sixtieth birthday.

Recognition was already there. Illness and difficulty were still there too. Both could be true.

Cantor died in a sanatorium in 1918. Boltzmann died at Duino in 1906. These were the ends of two lives. What happened at the end did not erase the society, the classrooms, or the papers their colleagues had written.

At this point we may be waiting for a happy ending: someone finally acknowledges you, and everything becomes all right. But the medal arrives, the tribute arrives, and tomorrow still has to be lived. A person needs more than being told he was right.

A page of mathematics can survive its author. So can an equation.

Neither contains the whole of a life.

VI. Boltzmann and Maxwell

The connection between Maxwell and Boltzmann is more than a line drawn by a later writer. Their names belong to the same distribution.

The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution describes molecular speeds in a gas under the relevant conditions of classical equilibrium. The molecules do not all move at the same speed. Some are faster, some slower, but under given conditions the proportions in the different speed ranges follow a rule.

Maxwell went first. Boltzmann carried the work further, connecting the distribution more deeply with equilibrium, collisions, and the problems of thermodynamics. Both were learning how to stop chasing molecules one by one without abandoning them altogether.

In this series, Maxwell brings light. He developed the investigation of fields that Faraday had opened into an electromagnetic theory. Boltzmann brings “disorder”: all those movements beneath the steady readings of thermodynamics.

But the theory of light did not meet an unbroken welcome. Maxwell died in 1879. Hertz's important experiments with electromagnetic waves came in the late 1880s. The theory and experimental evidence that now sit together on a textbook page were then separated by work still to be done.

Boltzmann himself was a supporter of Maxwell's theory. He taught it, published lectures on it, and helped it circulate on the European continent. The man who asked questions within “disorder” also helped open a way for the theory of light.

Light makes us look up; molecules make us bend closer. Here I would rather leave the two men side by side than reserve beauty for one discovery and coldness for the other.

Countless molecules go their separate ways, yet a distribution connects them. Even here, in the disorder, there is something we can learn to read.

VII. Entropy

In macroscopic thermodynamics, the second law says that the entropy of an isolated system—one exchanging neither matter nor energy with its surroundings—does not decrease. Reaching equilibrium does not mean that every motion inside has stopped.

“Isolated” matters. As hot water cools in a room, the entropy of the water itself can decrease. Its surroundings receive the heat. Only by accounting for the whole change can we tell whether the second law has been violated. We cannot turn the law into a demand that every place, at every moment, must grow messier.

Imagine a box divided in two. Gas fills one half; the other is a vacuum. Remove the partition.

The gas spreads.

There are enormously many microscopic ways for the gas to occupy roughly the whole box. The arrangements in which every molecule happens to remain in the original half make up an astonishingly small proportion. Equilibrium is common not because each molecule knows where it ought to go, but because the microscopic states compatible with it overwhelmingly outnumber the alternatives.

Yet “more ways” is not a force pushing the particles along. Their motions and collisions matter, as does the state in which the system began. Boltzmann's statistical explanation leaves room for small fluctuations. It also asks us to distinguish the extraordinarily unlikely from the absolutely impossible.

A broken cup will not put itself back together while we wait in ordinary life. That does not mean each fragment has received an instruction to make things worse. Turning entropy increase into “life can only deteriorate” gives a physical law a verdict it never delivered.

Heat death can be misunderstood in the same way. Its unsettling prospect is not that every particle must finally be motionless, but that the differences available to sustain useful work disappear. Without a temperature difference, a heat engine cannot keep working in the same way. The remote future of the universe also depends on its history and the conditions of its evolution. One line on a tombstone cannot write that whole ending by itself.

There is still an echo of Chekhov here.

The sisters in Three Sisters do not reach Moscow. But “they do not reach Moscow” does not finish telling us who they are. In the fourth act, they are still facing departures, still confronting how to go on living.

A temperature reading does not tell us every movement inside the bottle either. The resemblance lies in the distance between a brief account and everything it has not individually described. It does not mean that the characters' regrets are entropy, or that molecules have human hopes left unfulfilled.

Chekhov does not live the sisters' lives for them. An equation cannot decide how its reader is to live.

VIII. Duino

September 5, 1906. Duino, near Trieste.

Boltzmann was on holiday with his wife and daughter. During that holiday, he died by suicide. He was sixty-two.

The scientific disputes were real. So were illness and difficulties in his life. But the records have not preserved everything about that moment. They cannot be arranged into a straight line of cause and effect running from the meeting in 1895 to this day.

In 1905, Einstein had already published his paper on Brownian motion. Starting from molecular motion, he made quantitative predictions about the movement of suspended particles that experiments could test. Publication did not deliver atoms to every reader as directly as a photograph. In 1908–1909, Perrin's measurements supplied powerful experimental support for molecular theory. Boltzmann did not live to see those experiments.

But he had not spent his entire life standing alone before a wall.

In 1904, he attended the scientific congress held during the St. Louis World's Fair. In 1905, he went to Berkeley to lecture and also visited Stanford. Two years, two journeys—not a single trip.

In his account of California, he worried about his English and made a point of mentioning the words “blackboard” and “chalk.” A man who could teach people to understand molecular motion still had to learn how to get his teaching across in another language.

The passages about meals, travel, and lectures belong to Boltzmann too. They do not prove that he was free of pain. They simply keep pain from swallowing the whole person.

In places, the scientific wall was beginning to give way. We do not have enough evidence to say how much of that he saw.

He did not live to see what came after. What came after cannot bring him back.

IX. Disorder

The encounter at the bridge in this section is a work of literary imagination.

S = k log W.

The letters on the stone are orderly. They are not describing a company of molecules standing in formation.

Someone else has arrived at the bridge. He stands there, heavyset, his eyesight poor, holding a stone and catching his breath.

The equation is carved into it. He cradles it in his arms. It is heavy.

Socrates stands in the clearing. Plato crouches over his blueprints. Hume sets out his backgammon board. Schopenhauer looks beneath the bridge. Kierkegaard has made his leap. Turing looks at an apple. Chekhov leans against the railing. Cantor looks up at the sky. Copernicus has put down his book and walked away. Sartre paces. Beauvoir holds a mirror. Quine has said a sentence.

Tesla listens to a hum. Edison holds a light bulb. Heisenberg's position is uncertain. Bohr holds an unsent letter. Tolstoy stands opposite Chekhov with a prescription in his hand. Shakespeare is not there: he is the water below the bridge. Spinoza has glass dust on his hands. Aristotle crouches to lay the floor. Faraday crouches to lift a plank. Maxwell stands writing equations.

Joan of Arc floats above the bridge, carrying fire. Wilde stands with easy elegance, holding that sentence. Ramanujan has emerged halfway through a gap. Oppenheimer walks on, carrying ash. Charlotte Brontë holds a pen. Emily is out on the moor beyond the bridge. The wind comes from where she is.

Boltzmann stands beside Aristotle and looks down.

Aristotle is settling a plank into place. Boltzmann sees more than the plank. Beneath it, the different microscopic arrangements go on changing. Putting a name above them has not made them all stop.

The floor still bears their weight. Boltzmann looks down. The steadiness under his feet and the motion he sees beneath it are both there.

Faraday looks across from another gap. Maxwell keeps writing. Boltzmann looks at the pen, then at the stone in his arms.

He shifts the stone a little. An edge presses into his hand. He stops to catch his breath. The letters sink a little as his arms lower.

Then he looks up.

Kant is standing in the distance.

The figure is so far away that, for a moment, he thinks of a statistical fluctuation. Could it be only a passing shape, something that will appear and then dissolve?

He walks toward it nevertheless. Not quickly. The stone in his arms. The bridge beneath his feet. The wind blowing from Emily's moor.

Order may not remain secure forever. But the movement toward it is real.
