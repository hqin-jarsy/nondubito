海森堡，测不准

Heisenberg, Uncertain

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、门外已经不是从前

1941年9月，海森堡来到哥本哈根，见玻尔。丹麦已被德国占领。三十九岁的来访者和五十五岁的主人曾一起工作、争论，把物理学推到陌生的地方。如今，陌生的是两个人见面时所处的位置。

后来的叙述常把那次会面放在一次散步里。仿佛只要走出房间，避开可能的耳目，两个人就能恢复从前谈问题的自由。但占领并没有留在门里。有人来自占领国，有人正在被占领的家园生活。

海森堡战后谈起此行，强调自己想试探原子武器问题能否在科学家之间讨论，是否可能避免这种武器被制造出来。这是他对自己意图的说明，不是一份双方签过字的谈话记录。

玻尔记住的是另一种压力。在后来没有寄出的信稿里，他反对把这次访问写成道德默契：他听见了对德国获胜的信心，也留下了德国正在努力发展原子武器的印象。关于谈话的地点和措辞，两人的回忆也不能无缝拼合。

这些信稿在2002年公开，距离会面已经六十多年。纸张没有把现场重新交给我们，却增加了能查的材料。分歧不是宣布什么都不知道的理由；反过来，也不能挑一位自己喜欢的人，让他的记忆替所有证据作主。

这篇文章要从这里开始：一个把某种物理限制说得非常清楚的人，在自己做过的事情面前，没有同样清楚的公式可用。他必须重新学习怎样回答，而谈话的另一端，有人等着被认真听见。

二、显微镜里的反问

把时间往回拨到1925年。海森堡不再坚持给原子里的电子画一条看不见的小行星轨道。他尝试从可观测的光谱关系出发，寻找新的算法。赫尔戈兰岛的短暂逗留属于这段探索，但发现不是孤岛上一个夜晚独自完成的。

玻恩认出了那些数表与矩阵运算的联系，与约尔丹推进了数学形式；随后三人共同展开矩阵力学。熟悉的图像被放下，不是因为图像不漂亮，而是因为研究对象不肯照着图像给出答案。

到1927年，在哥本哈根工作的海森堡追问：位置和动量究竟能怎样一起被确定？他用伽马射线显微镜作思想实验。为了把电子看得更清楚，需要短波长的光；光与电子的相互作用，又把动量问题带进了观察本身。

这个例子很有力量，却不是全部证明。今天常写的 ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2，说的是同一量子态中位置与动量分布的标准差：把其中一项的分布收得很窄，另一项就不能也任意窄下去。

可以想象许多按同样方式准备的系统。一组测位置，另一组测动量，结果各自铺开。这里不是老师责怪学生手抖，更不是买一台更贵的仪器就能把两个分布都压成一点。限制属于量子态的描述。

海森堡1927年的论证打开问题，肯纳德同年给出位置—动量关系的严格形式，罗伯逊在1929年作了推广。发现也有它的接力。把一串姓名留在这里，是为了看见思想怎样被别人检查、补足，才成为我们熟悉的知识。

海森堡获得1932年的诺贝尔物理学奖，实际颁奖在1933年。那时他还很年轻。能够改写自然的描述是一种罕见的才能；它却不会自动附赠另一种能力，让人在历史中的每个选择都同样准确。

三、观察不是一双神奇的眼睛

显微镜容易让我们想起“看一眼”。实际上，物理学中的测量要有装置、相互作用和记录。光打到哪里，探测器留下什么信号，都属于具体过程。这里的观察者不必是一颗正在凝视宇宙的心。

即使没有人当场看屏幕，仪器也会运行，记录也可以稍后读取。量子力学不是说人的意识想要什么，世界就替他变成什么。把测量说成愿望的力量，会把最有意思的实际问题藏起来。

扰动和态的统计散布也要分开。用工具检查一个系统，可能改变它；但是上面的标准差关系并不只是“第一下碰坏了第二下”。即使分别对同样制备的系统测量，它仍然给出约束。

这一区别听起来细，却保住了问题的形状。否则我们会以为，找到一种更温柔的照明，或让实验者更冷静一点，原理就可以被绕过。真正的理论比“观察总会改变对象”这句格言精确得多。

那种精确也改变了科学家的工作习惯。不能只说想知道电子在哪里，还要说怎样准备系统、测哪个量、如何理解记录。一个看似简单的问题，被要求交代自己到达对象的路线。

我们可以从中学到谨慎，却不用把物理学拉来替所有人生判断作证。至少在走进下一段历史之前，先把两种困难分开：量子态有可计算的约束；一封信为什么没有寄出，要读的是信与它周围的生活。

四、骰子还没有替争论收场

海森堡、玻尔和玻恩的工作，常被放进“哥本哈根诠释”这个名字里。不确定关系、互补性和概率解释确实彼此相连，但这个名字并不是三个人签署过的一份完全一致的宣言。他们内部也有差别，有争论。

对于希望每个粒子都沿着明确轨迹前进的人，新理论令人不安。它能准确给出某些结果出现的概率，却不像一幅缩小了的钟表图，让你看清每个齿轮怎样接着下一个齿轮。

爱因斯坦不肯轻易停在这里。他担心的不只是“我不喜欢概率”，还有理论是否完整，远处的系统之间究竟允许怎样的联系。他与玻尔的争论不是聪明人终于说服一个固执老人，而是把问题逼得越来越具体。

后来的贝尔不等式及实验，使一类满足局域性等条件的隐变量解释受到严格限制。实验的分量正在它的具体：哪些假设放在一起，哪些统计结果就不应出现，而实际又出现了什么。

它们没有因此选出唯一的量子诠释，也没有排除所有可能的隐变量理论。理论预测的成功、形式的使用和对世界图景的解释，并不是三张自动合并的证书。仍可争论的地方，不等于已验证的结果也随意作废。

海森堡属于这场建设，不只是一个喊“不可知”的人。他和同行学会在旧图像失效时继续算、继续试、继续修正语言。承认某种直觉不能原样带过去，和宣布那边什么都没有，是两种不同的勇气。

五、留下来的人

再回到1941年。来哥本哈根的海森堡，不只是玻尔熟悉的年轻合作者。他已是德国铀研究的重要人物。无论他私人怎样理解自己，研究项目与纳粹统治下的国家机构、资源和战争需求有实际联系。

他留在德国。犹太同行遭到驱逐和迫害，有些非犹太学者也选择离开。海森堡本人还受过“德意志物理学”阵营的攻击，现代物理被污名化。他不是在一个没有压力的地方随意挑选人生。

保护德国科学、维持研究和培养年轻人，是他后来解释留下时的重要理由。一个人可能真心爱自己的国家，也不赞同统治者的全部意识形态。承认这种可能，并不困难。困难是继续问：留下以后，具体替什么工作了？

把国家和政权在心里分开，不能使项目自动离开政权的资源系统。觉得自己在保存火种，不等于权力只会按照你的想法使用火。科学家的自我理解值得听，但不能成为唯一的审查者。

德国最终没有造出原子弹。这个结果并不单独证明科学家们进行了成功的道德抵抗；资源、组织、技术判断与优先顺序都要检查。同样，知道他参与核研究，也不授权我们替每一句私下的话编造最恶劣的动机。

人的复杂不是一块免责牌。我们可以暂时不知道他心里每一种愿望的比例，同时知道他承担了什么工作。比较公平的判断，未必是给出最响的称号，而是拒绝让无从证实的好意覆盖已经发生的事。

六、收音机响起的时候

1945年夏天，十位德国科学家被拘留在英国的法姆庄园。盟军秘密监听他们的谈话。8月6日，广岛遭原子弹轰炸的消息传来。世界已经越过一道门槛，房间里的人却先经历了不相信。

海森堡起初怀疑消息。他和同伴讨论美国是否真完成了那样庞大的工作，猜测技术路径，重新衡量自己此前的判断。记录中的停顿、争执和互相反驳，让一段后来容易被写得很整齐的历史重新有了棱角。

8月14日，他作了一次有关炸弹物理的讲解，显示了与最初反应不同的技术理解。这使“早已完全掌握，只是决定不做”的故事难以轻松成立，但也不能凭一段仓促谈话，替所有战时计算和意图作最后鉴定。

更值得听的是他们怎样解释失败。有人倾向于把没做成说成原则上不愿做，另一些人立即不同意。材料显示的不是全体科学家早已拥有同一份秘密道德协议，而是他们也在争夺这段历史该怎样被讲述。

监听记录不是直通灵魂的管道。拘留的环境、同伴的目光、对未来的担心，都可能进入说话方式。正因此，要把记录与报告、书信和项目进展互相对照，而不是因为它不完美，就把它从桌上扫掉。

还有一个位置不能被这些人的争论占满：广岛。对庄园里的物理学家，新闻意味着震惊、专业判断受挫与战后前途；对城市里的人，它首先是身体、家人和生活被摧毁。受害者不是科学家道德传记的背景布。

知道得多，不一定让人更快听见远处的人。法姆庄园留给我们的难题，不只是谁算对、谁算错，还有一个拥有知识的人如何把后果从新闻里的数字，重新听成别人的生活。

七、谈话另一端的人

年轻时，海森堡与玻尔的争论能够生长出新的物理学。意见不同不必立刻成为关系破裂，有时反而说明两个人仍相信对方值得认真回应。那是一种珍贵的工作条件：你可以把未完成的想法交过去。

1941年却不能照搬这份条件。在被占领的哥本哈根，主人没有义务先越过恐惧，替来自德国核研究的朋友辨认最善良的动机。来访者想讨论什么，与听者有理由担心什么，都属于这场谈话。

两人战后仍有接触，1961年玻尔还发去生日贺电，海森堡作了回复。关系没有被一刀切成从此不相往来的两半。可继续来往，也不意味着那次访问的分歧已经得到解决。

玻尔晚年的信稿保留着异议。有些话写下，却没有寄出。我们可以看见他试图表达什么，不能仅凭没寄出，就替他宣布已经宽恕，或者至死只有怨恨。沉默不是由后来的作者随便填满的空格。

解释一件事时，我们往往很急：请你先明白我并不是那样的人。但对方也许首先需要听见的，是你知道他当时身在什么地方。只谈自己的初衷，会让那个人又一次负责修复你的形象。

这不是一条量子定律，只是一段关系里很难的功课。修复不能由想得到谅解的一方单独宣布。对方有权听，有权追问，也有权不把事情收在一个让所有人舒服的结尾里。

八、蒯因递来的不是赦免

蒯因质疑我们给概念与经验划出的根本边界，海森堡参与改变了物理量能够怎样共同被描述。两人都使一种习惯性的确信停下来。但他们提出的论证和所需的证据并不相同。

面对海森堡，我们尤其要小心一种方便的读法：既然自然都不确定，历史也就没有真假，道德也就不必判断。这不是从他的发现得出的结论，而是借一个著名公式，替自己省去困难的工作。

某人的动机还不能确定，意思可能是材料不足、叙述冲突，或者多种愿望同时存在。新证据可以改变判断。它不等于位置与动量满足一个数学关系，也不意味着事实会随我们选择的视角任意改写。

同样，一个人不止一个标签，不等于他的行为没有方向。保留复杂性，是为了判断得更准确，不是把所有选择泡成一样温吞的水。受害者要求说明，并不会因为被要求的人很复杂而失去资格。

蒯因可以帮助我们检查这张人物地图：哪些箭头来自证据，哪些来自我们的偏爱？但地图检查完了，仍然要面对土地上的人。我们不可能只在概念之间来回走动，就把那里的伤害走没了。

海森堡最值得留给普通读者的问题，也许是：当我相信自己在做好工作时，是否还看得见这份工作替什么服务？专业让我更有能力回答某些问题，也可能让我更擅长绕开另一些问题。

九、纸张放低一点

1976年2月1日，海森堡在慕尼黑去世，七十四岁。他的科学留在实验室和课堂里，战时经历留在档案与争论里。现在，让他走到本系列想象中的桥头。两份遗产都不必先被抹掉。

他拿着一张写有公式的纸，走到可以看清别人面孔的地方。纸边在风里轻轻动着。他习惯从一道问题开始谈话，这一次却迟疑了一下：先开口的人，也应该先听些什么。

蒯因让出一块地方。海森堡想起玻尔，望向另一个方向。关于自然，他们曾有那么多没有说完的话；关于彼此，也留下了没有说完的事。这里可以给那场谈话再留一点地方。

假如玻尔走来，他不必按照海森堡希望的方式走来。可以先问一个问题，可以停一会儿，也可以不接受对方准备好的解释。想象不能比真实世界更加急于要求受伤的人配合。

海森堡也不必只剩一句认罪，或者一段为自己辩护的话。他可以讲自己知道的、害怕的、做过的。但要把这些话分清楚。害怕什么不等于做了什么，声称想保护谁，也不等于别人确实得到了保护。

旁边没有人拿诺贝尔奖替他回答，也没有人把他的物理学从书里删去。成就不替责任结账，责任也不要求我们假装成就没有发生。把两件事同时放在桌上，比制造一个完美英雄或纯粹恶人更费力。

他手里的纸可以放低一点。那张纸曾帮助人类清楚地说出一种限度，但此刻面前站着的不是一道待求的物理题。对方的沉默、问题和拒绝，都不能代入公式，然后由他算出一个满意的结果。

桥上还有位置，谈话也可以继续。我们不必先看穿一个人的全部内心，才要求他认真回答。我们需要的不是无所不知，而是在还不知道全部的时候，不把已经知道的责任交出去。

I. Outside Was No Longer Neutral

In September 1941, Werner Heisenberg visited Niels Bohr in Copenhagen. Denmark was under German occupation. The visitor was thirty-nine, his host fifty-five. They had argued their way into unfamiliar physics together. Now their unfamiliar positions belonged to history rather than a blackboard.

Later accounts often place their exchange on a walk. It is tempting to imagine that going outdoors, away from possible listeners, could restore an older freedom of conversation. But occupation did not remain inside the house. One man came from Germany; the other lived under its power.

Heisenberg's postwar account emphasized an attempt to discuss atomic weapons and whether scientists might prevent their development. That account matters. It tells us how he explained his purpose; it is not a transcript agreed upon by both participants.

Bohr remembered a different pressure. In unsent drafts he resisted a story of shared moral restraint. He recalled confidence in German victory and an impression that German scientists were working toward atomic weapons. Even the setting and wording resist being assembled into one seamless scene.

The drafts became public in 2002. They did not restore a recording of the encounter, but they enlarged the evidence. Disagreement does not mean we know nothing. Nor may we choose our preferred participant and let his memory overrule every other document.

Here is where this portrait begins: a physicist who helped describe a limit with extraordinary precision faced choices for which no comparable formula could answer. He had to learn another way of answering. Across from him stood someone who also needed to be heard.

II. A Question Inside the Microscope

Return to 1925. Heisenberg was trying to abandon the picture of electrons following invisible planetary orbits inside atoms. He began instead with relations among observable spectral quantities. His brief stay on Helgoland belonged to this effort, not to a discovery completed alone in one miraculous night.

Max Born recognized the connection with matrix mathematics and, with Pascual Jordan, developed the formalism. The three then extended matrix mechanics together. A familiar picture was surrendered not because pictures were unfashionable, but because the object of inquiry would not obey that picture.

Working in Copenhagen in 1927, Heisenberg asked how position and momentum could be determined together. His imagined gamma-ray microscope brought the problem into focus: resolving an electron's location involved light, and the interaction with light brought momentum into the act of observation itself.

That thought experiment was illuminating, not the whole demonstration. The familiar expression ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2 concerns standard deviations of position and momentum distributions in the same quantum state. Narrow one distribution sufficiently and the other cannot also be made arbitrarily narrow.

Imagine many systems prepared alike, some used for position measurements, others for momentum measurements. Their results spread out. The relation is not a teacher complaining about shaky hands, nor a promise that more expensive equipment will compress both distributions to a point.

Heisenberg opened the question in 1927; Kennard established the precise position–momentum inequality that year, and Robertson generalized uncertainty relations in 1929. Discovery has its relays. Other people check and sharpen an insight before it becomes the apparently settled knowledge we inherit.

Heisenberg received the Nobel Prize for Physics for 1932, awarded in 1933. He was still young. Changing our description of nature is an extraordinary capacity. It does not come with a second capacity guaranteeing equally sound judgment in every historical situation.

III. Observation Is Not a Magical Gaze

A microscope encourages us to think of simply looking. Physical measurement, however, involves an arrangement, an interaction, and a record. Where light travels and what a detector registers belong to a concrete process. The observer need not be a mind staring the universe into existence.

Equipment can operate with nobody watching the screen, and its records can be read afterward. Quantum theory does not say that the world becomes whatever consciousness wishes. Treating measurement as a power of desire conceals the actual questions that make it interesting.

Disturbance and statistical spread also require distinction. An instrument may change a system, but the standard-deviation inequality is not merely a story in which the first measurement damages the second. It constrains distributions even when measurements are made on separate, identically prepared systems.

This apparently small distinction preserves the shape of the problem. Otherwise we might expect gentler illumination or a calmer experimenter to evade the relation. The theory is more precise than the maxim that observation always changes what is observed.

Precision also changes a scientist's habits. It is not enough to ask where the electron is. We must specify preparation, the quantity measured, and how the record is interpreted. An apparently straightforward question must explain the route by which it reaches its object.

There is caution to learn here without making physics certify every reflection on life. Before returning to the history, keep two difficulties distinct: a quantum state obeys calculable constraints; understanding why a letter went unsent requires reading the letter and its circumstances.

IV. The Dice Do Not End the Discussion

The work of Heisenberg, Bohr, and Born is commonly gathered under the Copenhagen interpretation. Uncertainty relations, complementarity, and probability were connected, but the label does not name a single, perfectly agreed manifesto signed by all three. They disagreed among themselves as well.

For someone expecting every particle to follow a definite trajectory, the new account was unsettling. It predicted probabilities with remarkable success without supplying a miniature clockwork picture in which every cog could be watched engaging the next.

Einstein's resistance was not merely a dislike of probability. He pressed questions about completeness and the relation between distant systems. His exchanges with Bohr were not the gradual conversion of an obstinate old man. Their disagreements helped make the difficulties increasingly specific.

Bell inequalities and subsequent experiments placed powerful constraints on hidden-variable accounts satisfying locality and other assumptions. Their force lies in this specificity: certain assumptions imply limits on correlations, while experiments produce results that violate those limits.

Those findings do not select a unique interpretation or rule out every possible hidden-variable theory. Successful predictions, a usable formalism, and an account of what the world ultimately is are not three certificates automatically issued together. Open questions do not make tested results optional.

Heisenberg belonged to the construction of a working theory, not merely to a chorus declaring ignorance. He and his colleagues calculated, tested, and revised their language when old pictures failed. Leaving a familiar picture behind is different from announcing that nothing lies beyond it.

V. The Man Who Stayed

The visitor in 1941 was more than Bohr's former young collaborator. Heisenberg had become a leading figure in German uranium research. Whatever his private understanding of his role, the work had material connections to institutions, resources, and military interests under Nazi rule.

He stayed in Germany. Jewish colleagues were expelled and persecuted; some non-Jewish scientists also left. Heisenberg himself was attacked by proponents of Deutsche Physik, who stigmatized modern physics. He was not choosing his life in an environment without pressure.

Preserving German science, maintaining research, and protecting the next generation featured in his later explanations. A person may love a country without endorsing its rulers' entire ideology. Granting that possibility is not difficult. The harder question is what the person actually worked for afterward.

Separating nation from regime in one's own mind does not detach a research project from the regime's resources. Hoping to preserve a flame cannot ensure that power will use the fire only as intended. A scientist's self-description deserves a hearing, not sole jurisdiction.

Germany did not build an atomic bomb. That outcome alone does not establish successful moral resistance. Resources, organization, technical judgments, and priorities require examination. Conversely, participation in nuclear research does not authorize inventing the worst possible motive for every private remark.

Complexity is not an exemption. We may lack a complete inventory of someone's desires while knowing which work he undertook. A fair judgment need not supply the loudest label. It must prevent unverifiable good intentions from covering actions that can be documented.

VI. When the Radio Spoke

In the summer of 1945, ten German scientists were detained at Farm Hall in England, their conversations secretly monitored. On August 6 came news of Hiroshima. The world had crossed a threshold; inside the house, the first response was disbelief.

Heisenberg initially doubted the report. The scientists debated whether the Americans had accomplished the immense undertaking, speculated about their methods, and reconsidered earlier judgments. Pauses, disputes, and contradictions roughen a history that later accounts can make deceptively smooth.

On August 14 he delivered a technical explanation displaying a different understanding from his first reactions. This complicates the claim that he had already mastered everything and simply withheld it. A hurried conversation nevertheless cannot adjudicate every wartime calculation or intention by itself.

Equally revealing is the disagreement about failure. Some moved toward explaining nonachievement as principled refusal; others objected. The evidence does not reveal a unanimously shared secret moral pact. It shows scientists already contesting how their history should be told.

A surveillance transcript is not an open pipe into a soul. Detention, colleagues, and anxiety about the future can shape speech. That is why transcripts should be compared with reports, correspondence, and project records—not swept aside because their access to the past is imperfect.

One place must not disappear behind their arguments: Hiroshima. For the detained physicists, the news meant shock, overturned professional judgments, and uncertain careers. For people in the city it meant destroyed bodies, families, and ordinary lives. They are not scenery in a scientist's moral biography.

Expert knowledge does not necessarily make distant people easier to hear. Farm Hall leaves us asking more than who calculated correctly. How does someone trained to recognize consequential numbers learn to hear in those numbers a life belonging to somebody else?

VII. The Person Across from Him

In the earlier years, disagreement between Heisenberg and Bohr could produce new physics. It need not dissolve a relationship; it could show that each still considered the other worth answering seriously. They shared the rare freedom to hand over an unfinished thought.

Occupied Copenhagen could not simply reproduce those conditions. Bohr had no obligation to put fear aside and identify the most generous possible intention of a friend involved in German nuclear research. What the visitor wanted to discuss was not the conversation's only reality.

Contact continued after the war. In 1961 Bohr sent a birthday telegram, and Heisenberg replied. Their lives did not divide into two clean halves, fellowship followed by permanent silence. Continuing contact, however, does not establish that the disagreement about the visit was resolved.

Bohr's later drafts retain objections. Words were written and left unsent. We can examine what he tried to formulate without declaring that silence means complete forgiveness or undying resentment. An unsent letter is not a blank invitation for a biographer to finish someone else's feelings.

When explaining ourselves, we often hurry toward one request: understand that I am not that kind of person. The listener may need something earlier—to hear that we understand where they stood. Discussing only our intentions can make them responsible again for repairing our image.

This is not a quantum law but a difficult lesson in relationship. The person seeking reconciliation cannot declare it completed alone. The other may listen, question, or decline to provide the ending in which everybody finally feels comfortable.

VIII. Quine Offers No Acquittal

Quine questioned a fundamental division assigned to meaning and experience; Heisenberg helped change how physical quantities could be described together. Both interrupted a habitual certainty. Their arguments nevertheless concern different problems and answer to different kinds of evidence.

With Heisenberg, one particularly convenient misreading waits nearby: if nature itself is uncertain, history has no truth and moral judgment need not proceed. That is not a consequence of quantum theory. It borrows a famous formula to avoid another kind of difficult work.

Uncertainty about motives may reflect missing records, conflicting testimony, or several desires operating at once. Further evidence can change a judgment. This is not the mathematical relation between position and momentum, and it does not make past events change freely with a chosen perspective.

A person exceeds one label; his actions still have direction. Preserving complexity should sharpen judgment rather than dissolve every choice into the same lukewarm water. People asking for an account of harm do not lose that standing because the person addressed is complicated.

Quine can help inspect our map: which arrows rest on evidence, and which on affection? When the inspection ends, people remain on the ground. Moving indefinitely between concepts cannot walk their injuries out of existence or transfer responsibility somewhere harmless.

Perhaps Heisenberg's most useful question for an ordinary reader is this: while believing I am doing good work, can I still see what that work serves? Expertise improves our answers to certain questions. It can also improve our skill at avoiding others.

IX. Lower the Paper

Heisenberg died in Munich on February 1, 1976, aged seventy-four. His science remained in laboratories and classrooms, his wartime record in archives and disputes. Now let him approach this series' imagined bridge. Neither inheritance must be erased before he arrives.

He carries a sheet bearing an equation and comes close enough to see the faces ahead. The paper stirs in the wind. He is accustomed to opening a conversation with a problem. This time he hesitates: someone preparing to speak should first consider what he needs to hear.

Quine makes room. Heisenberg thinks of Bohr and looks down the approach. There had been so much left to discuss about nature, and things left unfinished between themselves. Here we can leave a little space for that conversation to resume.

If Bohr comes, he need not come in the manner Heisenberg hopes. He may ask a question, pause, or refuse the prepared explanation. Imagination should not be more eager than history to make the injured party cooperate with a satisfying ending.

Heisenberg need not be reduced to a confession or a defense either. He may say what he knew, feared, and did—but keep those statements distinct. Fearing an outcome does not tell us which action followed; claiming to protect someone does not establish that they were protected.

Nobody answers for him with a Nobel medal, and nobody deletes his physics from the books. Achievement does not settle responsibility; responsibility does not require pretending achievement never occurred. Holding both on the table is harder than manufacturing an immaculate hero or a complete villain.

He can lower the sheet a little. It helped humanity state a limit clearly. The person facing him, however, is not a physics problem. Another person's silence, question, or refusal cannot be substituted into the equation to calculate the answer he would prefer.

There is still space on the bridge, and conversation may continue. We need not see every corner of a person's mind before asking for a serious answer. What matters is not omniscience, but refusing to surrender the responsibility already visible because some things remain unknown.
