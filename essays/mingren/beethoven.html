贝多芬，聋了还在写

Beethoven, Deaf and Still Writing

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、四个音

当当当当——

贝多芬第五交响曲的开头。四个音。三短一长。大概是音乐史上最容易被认出的开头之一。

“命运在敲门。”这句相传出自贝多芬的解释，来自安东·辛德勒，出处并不可靠。但不需要借用他的签名，我们也可以听见一种逼近：有什么在敲，在要求回应。

这四个音，在本系列的词汇里，是凿。

它们打断沉默，也打断关于音乐应该怎样开始的预期。没有缓慢的前奏，没有温柔的引入。开头那个极短的休止之后，直接——当当当当。

然后整部交响曲都在发展、拆开、重新组合材料，回应开头的压力。从c小调走向终曲的C大调；从黑暗到光明，从受压到释放。像是从被敲门，走到自己推开门。这个听觉中的故事，有了可以行走的形状。

若把对照暂时拉到最鲜明：巴赫是构——声部交织，在秩序里使张力运动；贝多芬是凿——一个紧迫的动机冲过来，带着人穿过冲突。巴赫也猛烈，贝多芬也精密；这里画的是两种聆听的方向。

在这样的听法里，巴赫的宇宙仿佛已经在那里，邀请你进入；贝多芬的宇宙仿佛正在生成，邀请你同行。一个让人仰望建筑，一个让人感到脚下的路。

二、聋

二十多岁时，贝多芬的听力开始衰退。1801年他写信告诉朋友韦格勒，听力变差已有三年；1802年的自述又回溯得更早。困难逐渐加深，身边的声音一点点变得难以辨认。

对这位钢琴家和作曲家，它威胁着工作、交往，以及他认识自己的方式。痛苦是真实的，但听觉的丧失并不等于一个人的毁灭，更不能替所有失聪者规定人生。

他求医，尝试药物、浴疗和其他治疗，盼望好转。后来也使用助听筒。治疗没有恢复他期待的听力；他仍在寻找能让日常好过一点的办法。

1802年10月，尚未满三十二岁的贝多芬在维也纳近郊海利根施塔特写下给两个弟弟的文书，6日署名，10日补记。它没有寄出，身后才被发现。所谓“海利根施塔特遗书”，既有痛苦的自白，也有给弟弟们的遗产安排。

他解释：自己并非天生冷漠，原本喜欢与人交往；可在人群里听不清，又难以承认这一点。一个被期待拥有敏锐听觉的音乐家，正在失去听力。羞耻与恐惧把他推到人群边上，他写信，希望别人终于能理解。

他写到自己一度接近结束生命，但艺术留住了他。他觉得，在把自己感到应该创造的东西带到世上之前，离开是不可能的。文书里还有德行、对人的爱、对亲友的感谢，不只有一个孤独的“天才”字样。

他觉得还有东西等待写出。还没有写完。这给了他留在世界上的一个理由。

借用本系列的词，这是目的在说话。身体的一种能力正在改变，他仍想作曲。后来的交响曲、奏鸣曲、四重奏尚未存在；他在不确定中选择继续。目的不是向身体发号施令的主人，人也不必靠完成作品来换取活着的资格。

柏拉图笔下的苏格拉底拒绝越狱，以他认为正当的方式面对死亡；贝多芬在自己的文书里找到继续生活的理由。一个坚持不逃，一个坚持留下。我们可以比较两种姿态，却不能把自杀说成和写作一样自由而简单的选项。

它们照亮的是：人会用自己珍视的事物回应处境。不是身体不重要，而是身体遭遇困难时，意义仍可能被重新找到。

三、从黑暗到光明

他活了下来。继续写。写出了许多后来成为音乐生活中心的作品。

所谓“中期”和“晚期”，不是一次失聪把人生切成两半。听力逐步减退，创作也在逐步变化；两条线相交，不能压成一条因果线。

第三交响曲《英雄》，主要写于1803至1804年。留存的誊清谱题页上，波拿巴的名字被刮去，纸上留下破洞。里斯后来讲述了贝多芬听闻拿破仑称帝而愤怒的故事；出版时献给的是洛布科维茨亲王。从纸上的刮痕走进音乐：宏大的篇幅、葬礼进行曲、不断重建的终曲，让我们听见英雄主义、死亡与重新站起。

第五交响曲，1808年首演——当当当当。c小调到C大调。冲突并未因一个漂亮旋律就消失，音乐一步步争取自己的出口。

同场首演的第六交响曲《田园》——溪水、鸟鸣、暴风雨，还有风雨之后的感恩。他的听力已经明显衰退，与自然的关系却还在继续。自然经验、记忆、想象和作曲技艺，共同构成这个声音的世界。

第九交响曲，1824年首演时，他已严重失聪。末乐章把席勒《欢乐颂》的诗句带入交响曲，让独唱、合唱和乐队共同发声：欢乐，神性的火花。

它成为合唱交响曲最有影响力的作品之一。震动不只在于作者的听力状况，也在于这部作品怎样使彼此不同的声音进入同一个空间。

5月7日，维也纳凯恩特纳门剧院。贝多芬参与台上的示意，实际统领演出的是米夏埃尔·乌姆劳夫。后来的见证留下了一个转身：女低音独唱卡罗琳·翁格尔使他转向鼓掌的观众。面对乐队的作曲家，现在面对着向他回应的人群。

在那些回忆中，他需要被提醒，才能看见大家的回应。这里不只有隔绝：也有另一个人把他带回共同的现场。

四、晚期

从1810年代中期展开的晚期创作，是音乐史上最耐人反复接近的世界之一。它延续到他生命的最后几年。

第28至32号钢琴奏鸣曲。第12至16号弦乐四重奏，以及后来独立出版的《大赋格》。《迪亚贝利变奏曲》。《庄严弥撒》。不同体裁，并不只说同一句话。

若把中期的一个强大形象称为“从黑暗到光明”，晚期就把这条路线打开了：胜利之外，还有等待、感谢、迟疑、游戏，以及难以安放的痛苦。不能说他从此不再关心胜利，只能说胜利不再足以概括这些音乐。

长长的慢板，时间仿佛停住。寂静忽然裂成猛烈的声音。赋格再次出现，继承巴赫的技艺，又把声部之间的摩擦推向新的强度。一个近乎天真的旋律，被变奏、扭转、拆开、重组；它回来时，既还是自己，又已经走得很远。

有些听众和评论家困惑，甚至把难以理解归咎于他的失聪；也有人在当时就敬重、学习和演奏这些作品。《大赋格》尤其引起争论。它们不是等到整整一百年后才被听懂的；理解与不理解，从一开始就同时存在。

支撑这些作品的，有长期积累的内在听觉和写作能力，也有草稿上一遍遍修改的劳动。他仍同乐手讨论演奏，同出版商商量作品。《大赋格》另行出版，他又为作品130写了新终曲。那个内在的声音并没有住进一间隔绝世界的密室。

杜甫在挫折和流离中继续成为诗人，诗没有被失意耗尽；贝多芬在听力渐失中继续改变写作，音乐没有被困难耗尽。若说“凿”，凿出的不是自动出现的天才，而是他们持续作出的回应。

杜甫留给我们的有诗。贝多芬留给我们的有晚期四重奏。可以称之为余项，却不能把两个人其余的生命都视作可以削掉的废料。

那些作品极深。深度属于具体的写作，也属于后来一次次阅读、排练和聆听；它不需要苦难是一份礼物，才站得住。

五、他和巴赫

巴赫和贝多芬。把他们暂时放在一幅图的两端，听两个世界怎样互相照亮。

巴赫：构，赋格，对位，在多声部中建立秩序，荣耀归上帝。
贝多芬：凿，冲突，转折，在运动中争取形式，人的声音站到前面。两人都构，也都凿。

巴赫的音乐仿佛说：秩序在这里，进入吧。
贝多芬的音乐仿佛说：秩序还在形成，一起穿过黑暗吧。

打开《赋格的艺术》，我们面对的是一座不断展开、又留下未完成处的建筑。听第五交响曲，则像在时间中同行，从c小调走向C大调。一个有门可以进入，一个有路可以跟随。

巴赫是建筑师——给你一座大教堂，走进去，仰头。
贝多芬是登山者——给你一条路，黑暗，风大，有时你觉得走不下去；音乐还向前，峰顶的光也并非没有代价。建筑里有路，山路也需要建造。

巴赫的S.D.G.，唯独荣耀归上帝，标示一种工作的归向。贝多芬说艺术把他留住，标示一个人继续生活的理由。然而写《庄严弥撒》的也是贝多芬，在海利根施塔特呼告神明的也是他。人的声音和向神明的呼告，在他那里并未分家。

管道与火山：一个像把自己领受的东西传到纸上，一个像把内在压力喷到纸上。而在两张书桌旁，都有学习、修改，也有其他人的帮助。天上的音乐要经手，内心的火焰也要获得形式。

两种音乐，两种构，照亮“音乐从哪里来”的不同方向。从信仰，从自己，从继承的技艺，从人与人之间。哪一个方向都不是全部。

两种回答都可以让人驻足。它们没有穷尽音乐，也没有穷尽这两个人。

六、载体与目的

这个系列不断遇到一个问题：人的能力或生活条件受到损害时，珍视的工作怎样还能继续？“载体”和“目的”是提问的词，不是把人拆成有用部分与无用部分的刀。

苏格拉底面对死刑的姿态，由柏拉图的对话保存；杜甫在贫困与离乱中写下对他人的关切；司马迁在宫刑之后继续完成《史记》，追问天人、古今，形成一家之言。巴赫失明后的工作，与临终口授众赞歌的传说应分开；哥德尔晚年的恐惧、疾病和营养不良，也不该被他的定理解释或抵偿。这些生命不能互相替代，却让我们问同一个问题。

贝多芬的听力从青年时开始衰退，到晚年造成极严重的沟通困难。音乐没有停止，晚期作品打开了新的深度。这是发生过的事实；若说深度必定由失聪造成，就越过了证据。

这里有一个值得追问的可能性，不是一条以受苦换伟大的规律。

一种能力受损，不必使所有目的同时消失。人可以调整方法，接受帮助，也可以改变原来的方向。没有留下杰作的人，生命的价值同样不打折。

贝多芬原来就要与乐器、音响、场地、乐手和听众打交道。音乐从来不是一颗封闭头脑的私产；与世界协商，不等于作品因此不够属于自己。

失聪以后，协商更困难，却仍在进行。助听筒、书信、1818年起使用的谈话册、排练中的讨论，都在连接他与别人。晚期四重奏的慢、静、猛烈、简单，来自一个复杂的创作过程；不是“没有外部输入”便自动获得的纯粹。

若把损失比作凿，余项就是损失之后仍能继续工作的东西。有时它形成了新的力量，但我们不能倒过来说：为了这力量，那一刀就值得落下。

《庄子》写倏与忽凿七窍，混沌死了。贝多芬的音乐让我们想象另一种结尾：受损之后，仍可能有新的开始。这是本文与寓言的对话，不是贝多芬说过的话，更不是让别人去受损的理由。

七、欢乐颂

第九交响曲的最后一个乐章。席勒的《欢乐颂》，由贝多芬选择、重排，加入自己的引入词，成为音乐中的相遇。

欢乐，美丽的神性火花，
极乐世界的女儿！
我们满怀如火的热情，
走入你的圣所。
你的魔力重新联结，
被习俗严厉分开的人。

一个严重失聪的人写下关于欢乐的音乐。一个经历孤独与痛苦的人，仍试着让声音拥抱比自己更大的世界。

“所有的人都成为兄弟”——Alle Menschen werden Brüder。诗句属于席勒，贝多芬使它获得了一种新的公共生命。

欧洲委员会于1972年采用这段主题，欧洲共同体领导人于1985年也将它采用为欧洲的颂歌，后来成为欧盟的象征。正式使用的是没有歌词的器乐版本，不是把这一句德文规定为共同歌词。1824年的旋律，在另一个时代继续进入人与人之间。

《路加福音》叙述中的耶稣在十字架上请求赦免；贝多芬在听力严重衰退后让诗中的全人类共同歌唱。一个向伤害者打开回应，一个向陌生的人发出邀请。

赦免与拥抱不是同一件事。它们的处境、信仰与表达方式都不同。可以相互照亮，不必因此被归为一个公式。

我们所听见的相似，是处境收窄时，关切仍可能向外伸展。

杜甫的屋顶被风吹破，他写“安得广厦千万间”；席勒的诗经由贝多芬的音乐，把兄弟的称呼递向所有人；福音里的祈求则面向正在施害的人。每一种声音都越出了眼前的自己。

但不是苦难自动生出了高处，更不是只有破碎才有空间。是这些人在困难中仍作出回应，也借助语言、传统、技艺和他人。我们可以感谢留下的作品，不必感谢造成痛苦的那一刀。

八、他走的时候

1827年3月26日。贝多芬在维也纳病逝，五十六岁。

许滕布伦纳后来的回忆留下一个著名画面：雷雨、闪电，已经垂危的贝多芬忽然抬起手臂，握拳，随后手落下。人们把这个画面一遍遍讲成与命运的最后交锋。

当时的书信也记下那天的暴风雨；但天气能被旁证，不等于每个动作及其含义都能被旁证。拳头是一段需要谨慎对待的回忆，不能拿来读取临终者的内心。

柏拉图笔下的苏格拉底喝下毒酒。
传说中的老子骑牛离去。
佛典里的释迦牟尼安慰阿难。
巴赫临终口授的故事，需要与手稿分开。
牛顿在病中走向生命终点。
不同层次的记录与叙述，留下了不同的告别。

而在关于贝多芬的那个故事里，手臂举起了。

作为音乐的比喻，它令人难忘：到了最后还在凿，还在回应那四个音。可病榻上的人也会衰弱，会需要照料。贝多芬在疾病中走到了生命的终点；我们听见的反抗，留在他的音乐里。

当当当当——若仍借那扇门来讲，我们愿意听见他的回答：我还没有写完。先让我把这一页写完。

听力渐失，疾病到来。把这一切叫作“命运赢了”，太快；把作品叫作“命运输了”，也太快。生命不是一场能由我们宣布胜负的比赛。

我们知道，第九交响曲留下了，晚期四重奏留下了。那句“所有的人都成为兄弟”，仍能被不同的人重新唱起。他完成了这些作品，不等于一个人的所有目的至此都可被判定完成。

人走了。作品还在寻找下一次相遇。

现在回到本系列想象中的桥头：又多了一个人。风吹过桥面，水在下面流，别人正在说话。他未必能听清；有人面向他，有人拿来纸笔。他站在那里，曾经握紧的手也可以伸向别人。

他的眼睛仿佛在说：路还在。可以继续，也可以停下歇息，可以请别人同行。不必先战胜身体，才有资格走到这里。

当当当当——

I. Four Notes

Da da da daaaa—

The opening of Beethoven’s Fifth. Four notes: three short, one long. Few beginnings in music are more readily recognized.

“Fate knocking at the door.” The explanation attributed to Beethoven comes from Anton Schindler, an unreliable witness. Yet we need not borrow Beethoven’s signature to hear an approach, an insistence, something asking for an answer.

In the vocabulary of this series, the four notes are carving.

They break the silence—and an expectation of how music should begin. No leisurely prelude, no gentle ushering in. After the brief rest at the opening, the demand is already upon us.

The symphony develops, pulls apart, and recombines its material under that pressure. C minor gives way, eventually, to the finale’s C major: darkness toward light, constraint toward release. It is as though someone hears the knock and finally opens the door. This story in the listening has found a shape in which to move.

Sharpen the contrast for a moment: Bach constructs, weaving voices into an order that allows tension to move; Beethoven carves, sending an urgent figure through conflict. Bach can be fierce, Beethoven exact. We are drawing two directions in which to listen.

Heard this way, Bach’s universe seems already present, inviting us inside; Beethoven’s seems to be coming into being, inviting us along. One draws our eyes up into a building. The other makes us feel the road beneath our feet.

II. Losing His Hearing

Beethoven’s hearing began to deteriorate in his twenties. Writing to his friend Wegeler in 1801, he looked back over three years of worsening hearing; his account in 1802 reached further back. The difficulty deepened, and the sounds around him became harder to distinguish.

For this pianist and composer, the loss threatened work, companionship, and his understanding of himself. The distress was real. It does not follow that losing hearing destroys a person, or that his experience defines the lives of everyone who becomes deaf.

He sought doctors, medicines, baths, and other treatments, hoping for improvement. Later he tried ear trumpets. The remedies did not restore the hearing he wanted. He kept looking for ways to make daily life a little easier.

In October 1802, not yet thirty-two, he wrote to his brothers from Heiligenstadt, outside Vienna. He signed on October 6 and added a postscript on October 10. The document was never sent and was discovered after his death. The “Heiligenstadt Testament” contains an anguished explanation of himself as well as arrangements leaving his property to his brothers.

He explained that he was not naturally cold. He had wanted company, yet could not follow conversation and dreaded acknowledging why. A musician expected to possess an unusually acute ear was losing his hearing. Shame and fear pushed him to the edge of gatherings. He wrote in the hope of being understood.

He wrote that he had come close to ending his life, and that art had kept him here: he could not leave before bringing into the world what he felt called to create. The document also speaks of virtue, love for other people, and gratitude to friends. It contains more than the solitary figure of “genius.”

There was still something to write. It was not finished. That gave him one reason to remain.

In this series’ terms, purpose was speaking. A bodily capacity was changing; he still wanted to compose. The later symphonies, sonatas, and quartets did not yet exist. He chose to continue without knowing how things would turn out. Purpose is not a master entitled to command the body, and no one has to earn the right to live by producing a work.

Plato’s Socrates refuses escape and faces death in the way he considers just. Beethoven’s document discovers a reason to go on living. One will not flee; the other will remain. We can place their postures beside each other without pretending that suicide and composition are equally simple, freely available alternatives.

The comparison shows how people respond through what they value. It does not show that bodies are unimportant. Meaning may be found again when embodied life becomes difficult.

III. From Darkness to Light

He lived. He kept writing. Many of the works he wrote would become central to musical life.

The “middle” and “late” periods do not sit on opposite sides of one sudden deafness. His hearing deteriorated over time, and his composing changed over time. The two histories intersect; they cannot be flattened into a single chain of cause and effect.

The Third Symphony, the Eroica, was composed chiefly in 1803–1804. On the surviving corrected copy, Bonaparte’s name has been scraped from the title page, leaving a hole. Ferdinand Ries later described Beethoven’s anger at Napoleon’s assumption of imperial power; the published dedication is to Prince Lobkowitz. From those marks on paper, enter the music: its scale, funeral march, and rebuilding finale allow us to hear heroism, death, and the struggle to stand again.

The Fifth, first performed in 1808: da da da daaaa. C minor to C major. Conflict does not vanish because a pleasing tune appears. The music works its way toward an exit.

The Sixth, the Pastoral, received its premiere at the same concert. A stream, birds, a storm, thanksgiving after the storm. His hearing was already significantly impaired, but his relationship with nature continued. Experience, memory, imagination, and compositional craft together made this sounding world.

By the Ninth’s premiere in 1824, his hearing loss was profound. In the last movement, Schiller’s “Ode to Joy” enters the symphony through soloists, chorus, and orchestra: joy, a spark of the divine.

This became one of the most influential choral symphonies. Its force lies not only in the circumstances of its composer, but in the way different voices are brought into a shared space.

May 7, at Vienna’s Kärntnertor Theatre. Beethoven participated in giving cues, while Michael Umlauf directed the performance. According to later recollections, the alto soloist Caroline Unger prompted him to turn toward the applauding audience. The composer who had faced the orchestra now faced the people answering him.

In those recollections, someone has to alert him to the response he is receiving. There is separation here, but also a person helping him rejoin a common event.

IV. The Late Works

The late music, unfolding from the middle of the 1810s into his final years, is a world listeners can return to without exhausting it.

Piano Sonatas 28–32. String Quartets 12–16, and the Great Fugue later published separately. The Diabelli Variations. The Missa solemnis. These works do not all speak in one voice.

If “darkness to light” names a powerful image of middle-period Beethoven, the late works open that itinerary out. Beyond victory there is waiting, gratitude, hesitation, play, pain without a settled place. We cannot say victory ceased to matter to him. We can say that victory no longer describes enough.

Long adagios make time seem suspended. Quiet can split into a violent eruption. Fugue returns, carrying Bach’s craft into new intensities of friction between voices. A melody almost childlike in its simplicity is varied, bent, dismantled, reassembled. When it comes back, it is both itself and something that has traveled far.

Some listeners and critics were baffled; some blamed what they could not understand on his deafness. Others admired, studied, and performed these works in his own time. The Great Fugue proved particularly contentious. The music did not wait exactly a century to be understood: understanding and incomprehension were present together from the beginning.

His trained inner hearing and compositional skill sustained the work, as did the labor of revision visible in the sketches. He still discussed performance with musicians and publication with publishers. The Great Fugue appeared separately, and he wrote a new finale for Op. 130. That inward voice had not moved into a room sealed against the world.

Du Fu continued becoming a poet through disappointment and displacement; disappointment did not use up the poetry. Beethoven continued changing his writing as hearing diminished; difficulty did not use up the music. If we call this carving, the result is not a genius automatically manufactured by injury. It is a response repeatedly made.

Du Fu left poems. Beethoven left the late quartets. We may call them remainders, but not if the word turns the rest of either life into disposable material.

These works have extraordinary depth. It belongs to particular acts of writing and to subsequent acts of reading, rehearsal, and listening. It does not depend on calling suffering a gift.

V. Bach and Beethoven

Bach and Beethoven. Let us put them at opposite ends of a drawing and listen to the light each world casts upon the other.

Bach: construction, fugue, counterpoint, an order made among several voices, glory to God.
Beethoven: carving, conflict, a turn in the path, form won through movement, the human voice brought forward. Both carve. Both construct.

Bach’s music seems to say: the order is here; enter.
Beethoven’s seems to say: the order is still being made; come through the darkness with me.

Open The Art of Fugue and there is an unfolding architecture, with an unfinished place. Hear the Fifth and we seem to travel together in time, from C minor into C major. One has a door through which to enter; the other a road to follow.

Bach the architect offers a cathedral: walk in and look up.
Beethoven the mountaineer offers a road: darkness, wind, moments when you doubt you can continue. The music moves, and the light at the summit has cost something. There are paths inside buildings, too. Mountain paths must also be made.

Bach’s S.D.G., glory to God alone, marks a dedication of work. Beethoven’s statement that art kept him alive marks a reason to continue living. Yet the composer of the Missa solemnis was also Beethoven, as was the man appealing to divinity at Heiligenstadt. A human voice and an appeal to the divine were not separate households within him.

A conduit and a volcano: one seems to transmit what he has received, the other to release an inner pressure onto paper. At both desks, however, there is study, revision, and help from other people. Music from heaven must pass through hands; the inward fire must also acquire form.

Two kinds of music, two kinds of construction, illuminating different directions in the question “Where does music come from?” From faith, from the self, from inherited craft, from relations between people. None is everything.

Either answer can make us pause. Neither exhausts music. Neither exhausts the person.

VI. Vessel and Purpose

This series keeps encountering a question: when a person’s capacities or circumstances are damaged, how can valued work continue? “Vessel” and “purpose” are ways of asking, not blades for dividing a person into useful and useless parts.

Socrates’ response to his death sentence survives through Plato’s dialogues. Du Fu’s poems preserve concern for others amid poverty and upheaval. After castration, Sima Qian continued the Records of the Grand Historian, investigating heaven and humanity, past and present, and forming a work of his own. Bach’s work after losing his sight must be distinguished from the deathbed-chorale story. Gödel’s late fear, illness, and malnutrition cannot be explained or compensated for by his theorems. These lives are not interchangeable, but they bring us back to the question.

Beethoven’s hearing deteriorated from young adulthood into the profound difficulties of his later years. The music did not stop; the late works opened new depths. That happened. To claim that deafness necessarily caused those depths would exceed the evidence.

Here is a possibility worth considering, not a law that trades suffering for greatness.

The loss of one capacity need not extinguish every purpose. People can adapt their methods, accept help, or change direction. A life that leaves no masterpiece loses none of its value.

Even before severe deafness, Beethoven worked with instruments, acoustics, rooms, performers, and audiences. Music was never the private property of a sealed mind. Negotiating with the world does not make a work less one’s own.

After hearing loss, those negotiations became harder; they continued. Ear trumpets, letters, conversation books used from 1818, and discussions during rehearsals connected him with other people. The slowness, quiet, fierceness, and simplicity of the late quartets belong to a complex creative process—not to purity automatically obtained by removing outside input.

If loss is carving, remainder names what can still go on working afterward. It sometimes finds a new strength. We cannot reason backward from that strength and declare the wound worthwhile.

In the Zhuangzi, Shu and Hu bore seven openings in Hundun, and Hundun dies. Beethoven’s music invites us to imagine a different ending: after injury, a beginning may still be possible. This is the essay’s conversation with a fable, not something Beethoven said, and certainly not a reason to injure anyone.

VII. Ode to Joy

The last movement of the Ninth. Beethoven selects and rearranges Schiller’s “Ode to Joy,” adds introductory words of his own, and makes an encounter in music.

Joy, lovely spark of divinity,
Daughter of Elysium!
With hearts afire we enter,
Heavenly one, your sanctuary.
Your magic joins together again
Those whom custom sternly divided.

A man with profound hearing loss wrote music about joy. Someone who knew loneliness and distress still tried to make sound embrace a world larger than himself.

“All people become brothers”—Alle Menschen werden Brüder. The words are Schiller’s; Beethoven gave them a new public life.

The Council of Europe adopted the theme in 1972; the leaders of the European Community adopted it in 1985, and it became a symbol of the EU. The official anthem is instrumental, without lyrics: it does not prescribe that German sentence as a common text. A melody heard in 1824 continues to pass between people in another age.

In Luke’s account, Jesus asks forgiveness for those crucifying him. Beethoven, after severe hearing loss, makes the people in Schiller’s poem sing together. One opens a response toward his attackers; the other sends an invitation toward strangers.

Forgiveness and embrace are not the same act. Their circumstances, beliefs, and forms of expression differ. They can illuminate one another without becoming a formula.

What sounds alike to us is this possibility: even when circumstances narrow, concern can reach outward.

With his roof torn open by the wind, Du Fu asks for countless rooms to shelter others. Through Beethoven’s music, Schiller’s poem offers the name of brother to everyone. The Gospel’s prayer asks forgiveness for people doing harm. Each voice reaches beyond the immediate self.

But suffering does not automatically produce elevation; brokenness is not the only way to make room. These people responded within difficulty, drawing on language, tradition, craft, and other people. We can be grateful for what remains without being grateful for the wound.

VIII. When He Left

March 26, 1827. Beethoven died after illness in Vienna, aged fifty-six.

Anselm Hüttenbrenner’s later recollection left a famous picture: thunder, lightning, a dying Beethoven suddenly lifting an arm with a clenched fist, then letting it fall. Repeatedly, people have told this as a last encounter with fate.

Contemporary correspondence also records the storm. Corroborating the weather, however, does not corroborate every movement or its meaning. The fist belongs to a recollection we must handle carefully, not a window through which we can read a dying man’s mind.

Socrates drinks hemlock in Plato.
Laozi rides away on an ox in legend.
The Buddha comforts Ananda in Buddhist scripture.
The story of Bach dictating a last chorale must be distinguished from the manuscripts.
Newton approaches the end through illness.
Different kinds of record and narrative have left different farewells.

In the Beethoven story, the arm rises.

As an image of the music, it is unforgettable: still carving, still answering those four notes. But someone in a sickbed can also weaken and need care. Beethoven reached the end of his life through illness. The resistance we hear remains in the music.

Da da da daaaa—if we keep the door as our image, the answer we want to hear is: I have not finished. Let me finish this page.

Hearing diminished. Illness came. To say “fate won” is too quick; to call the works “fate defeated” is too quick as well. A life is not a contest whose result we can announce.

What we know is that the Ninth remains. The late quartets remain. Different people can still take up “all people become brothers.” He finished these works. That does not authorize us to declare every purpose in his life complete.

The person has gone. The works still seek another encounter.

Now return to the imaginary bridge of this series. There is one more person at the bridgehead. Wind crosses the bridge, water runs beneath it, and others are talking. He may not be able to hear them clearly. Someone turns to face him; someone brings paper and a pencil. He stands there, and the once-clenched hand can also reach toward another.

His eyes seem to say: the road remains. We can go on, rest, ask someone to walk with us. No one has to conquer the body before being entitled to arrive here.

Da da da daaaa—
