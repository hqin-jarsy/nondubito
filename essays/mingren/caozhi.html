曹植：不能拥有

Cao Zhi: What Cannot Be Held

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、七步

先从一个几乎人人都听过的故事说起：七步成诗。

它见于曹植身后约两个世纪成书的《世说新语》，不是曹魏宫廷留下的现场记录。我们把宫殿、脚步和沉默放进画面，是在重述传说，不是在补齐某年某日的实录。

想象那座大殿。曹丕已经是皇帝，曹植跪在他面前。一个坐在可以发命令的位置，另一个必须回答。

他们是卞夫人所生的兄弟，曹植比曹丕小五岁。同一个父亲，同一个母亲，一起长大的岁月。现在，这些亲近被皇帝与臣子的距离隔开了。

曹丕看着跪在自己面前的弟弟。

他知道这张脸。

这是他从小认识的弟弟。父亲曹操曾格外欣赏曹植的才华，一度考虑让他继承自己的位置。故事的刀锋，正落在这种熟悉里：不是不知道你是谁，恰恰知道。

最后没立。最后立的是他，曹丕。

但是这张脸还在。

在这个故事里，弟弟的才华也成了皇帝眼中的威胁。

他下了一个命令。

"你给我七步内作一首诗。作不出来，按罪论处。"

曹植站起来。

他开始走。

第一步。第二步。

传说让诗在七步之内到来。后世最熟悉的四句是——

"煮豆燃豆萁，豆在釜中泣。本是同根生，相煎何太急。"

四句，二十个字。《世说新语》所载则是六句的形态，措辞也有差别。短短的一首诗，自己也有流传的历史。

煮豆子用的柴是豆子的茎。豆子在锅里哭。我们本来是同一根上长出来的，你为什么要急着煎熬我。

大殿上安静了一会儿。

故事说，曹丕深感惭愧。

故事可以在这里结束。曹植的真实生活却不能。

二二零年曹操去世以后，曹植先后生活在曹丕、曹叡两朝。封爵有升降，封地有迁换，也有来自朝廷的监控与猜疑。并不是每一次改封都是贬黜，更不是始终关在同一间牢房；但王侯的身份，并没有给他想要的政治活动空间。

二三二年，他去世，史书记为四十一岁，按今天的计龄大约四十。从曾经受宠的少年，到反复陈情的王侯，这一生并不长，等待却占去了很大一部分。

七步诗把兄弟相煎压缩成一个瞬间。接下来要读的，是诗句之外漫长的日子：一个人怎样活在别人为他安排的位置里，又怎样让自己的声音继续出来。

二、争储

要明白曹丕为什么不能放过这个弟弟，得回到他们小时候。

曹操有很多儿子。争储中，曹丕和曹植成为两个重要的人选。

长子曹昂早年已经战死。曹丕有年长的优势，曹植则以才华受宠。继承并不是一道只看出生顺序就再无争议的题。

为什么？

曹植聪明。聪明到一种让父亲惊讶的地步。

《三国志》写他十岁出头便广泛诵读，善于属文。父亲看了他的文章，甚至怀疑是否请人代写；曹植愿意当面受试。铜雀台新成，诸子登台作赋，他很快写成，令曹操惊异。才华在这里，不只是后世给他的名声，是家里早就看见的东西。

曹植的性情，也与这种才华缠在一起。

传记写曹植不拘威仪、饮酒不节，也写曹丕善于经营自己的形象。一个更容易把喜好露在外面，一个更懂得让别人看见什么。可是，把两个人分别贴上“真性情”和“有城府”，还不能代替政治判断。

父亲喜欢一个儿子的文章，和相信他能处理权力，不完全是一回事。

曹植曾有自己的支持者，曹操也多次犹疑。一个家庭的偏爱，渐渐变成了臣僚、继承与政权的事。

曹植确实作过损害父亲信任的事。

他曾乘车走驰道，私开司马门。这不是一场只有少年任性的夜游：禁令被触犯，公车令因此被处死，曹操也加重了对诸侯的限制。一个人的放纵，已经牵连到别人的命。

二一九年，曹仁被关羽围困，曹操一度想派曹植领兵救援；传记记他醉得不能受命，事情作罢。

这一次却不能倒过来解释曹丕为什么在两年前被立为继承人。顺序要放清楚。

公元二一七年，曹丕被立为魏王世子。

公元二二零年，曹操死。曹丕继位，几个月后篡汉建魏。

曹丕成了皇帝。曹植从一个差点是皇帝的人，变成了一个皇帝的弟弟——而且是一个曾经差点取代了这个皇帝的弟弟。

我们可以批评曹植的失误，也可以承认他受到的压制。前一件事不自动使后一件事正当。

也不能因为他犯错，就断定他其实不想要政治上的位置。

聪明的人也会失控，也会误判。才华不是一份每次行动都必定正确的保证。

他的书信和奏表里，有认真想做事、想立功的声音。我们没有理由把它们全改写成“他只想要父爱”。一个人可以既想被父亲欣赏，也想有所作为；愿望不必只准留下一个。

但争储留下的位置，比愿望更难摆脱。无论他后来怎样解释自己，曾经可以取代哥哥的弟弟，已经进入皇帝的政治记忆。

从此，解释自己也变得危险。

三、黄初

公元二二零年。曹丕改元黄初。

黄初二年，也就是二二一年，曹植被降为安乡侯，同年改封鄄城侯。二二二年，立为鄄城王。官爵在变化，一个人能支配自己生活的程度，却不能只看名号。

从侯到王，可以是真正的晋升；改换封地，也不必每次都出于同一个原因。但这些荣号与调动，没有消除朝廷对宗室的戒备。

监国谒者的存在，使言行随时可能成为政治材料。二二一年，灌均曾上奏指控曹植醉酒悖慢、威胁使者，随后便有治罪与降爵。我们不必夸张成每封信、每个脚步都有完整记录，危险已经够清楚：对自己的叙述，并不掌握在自己手里。

他向哥哥，也向后来的侄子上表，想领兵，想报效，想把才华放到诗赋之外。待遇中并非从无褒奖，但他渴望的施展空间，始终与获准的位置相隔很远。

被养着的王侯，有俸禄，有宅第，有服侍他的人；这些不能假装不存在。可生活的优裕，与是否能参与决定自己的方向，是两回事。

宗室的管理，在这里露出一种逻辑：给予名位，同时限制行动。

它不需要杀。

它可以使一个人每天起床、吃饭、读书，却很难把“我想做什么”变成一项真正能商量的事情。不是生命等于死亡，而是生活里一块重要的空间，被别人长期握住。

从曹操去世到曹植去世，十二年。

这十二年并不只是沉默。

他做了一件事——他写。

他在每一个被限制的封地里继续写。他写诗。他写赋。他写表（向皇帝的奏疏）。他用写让自己不消失。

不少文章必须送到皇帝面前：谢恩、陈情、求用，字句要经得起另一种阅读。一个有才华的人，写给一个可能把他当威胁的人，每个字都像走在窄处。

诗赋则能容纳另一种声音。不是奏表里一定没有真话，诗里一定没有修饰；是同一个人，可以用不同的文字给悲伤、渴望、愤懑找地方。后来，那些他看不见的读者，也从这些文字里走近他。

朝廷替他安排了位置，文字没有只剩下那个位置。

四、洛水

《洛神赋》的序，把故事放在黄初三年，二二二年，从京城返回封地的路上。

马车停下，经过洛水的旅人暂时歇息。

洛水是一条不大的河，从洛阳南面流过。在中国传统神话里，洛水有一个女神，名字叫宓妃，是伏羲的女儿。

这里开始，我们进入赋中的世界。叙述者借着行旅遇见神女，不等于历史上的曹植确曾有一场这样的相遇。

但文字会让不可能的相遇，在读者眼前缓缓发生。

先是叙述者看见水边的丽人，惊异地问身边的御者。御者告诉他，这莫非就是洛水的女神宓妃。一个突然的看见，使熟悉的行程停住了。

他再看。

赋于是花很长的篇幅，写这一次看见。

他描写她的形貌——

"翩若惊鸿，婉若游龙。荣曜秋菊，华茂春松。仿佛兮若轻云之蔽月，飘飖兮若流风之回雪。"

她像受惊的鸿雁飞起，像游动的龙转身。她的光彩像秋菊。她的茂盛像春松。她有时候像一片轻云遮过月亮，有时候像一阵风吹动雪花。

这些比喻每一个都不是日常的比喻。每一个都把洛神的形象推到一种超过人类的境地。

他描写她的动作——

"凌波微步，罗袜生尘。"

她踏在水波上轻轻地走，她的罗袜生出尘埃。

这几乎是不能画稳的一笔：水波、轻步、罗袜、尘。人间的身体与不属于人间的动作，落在同一句里。她不是一张可以用比例和尺寸说完的画像。

赋中的旅人看着她。

他写他和她之间发生了什么——

他被她吸引，想托波传意、以玉佩相赠。她也有回应，停留、迟疑、举动与言语。可是双方之间，还有怀疑、礼节、人神之别，不是一句“彼此相爱”就把距离抹去了。

但是。

她是神，他是人。

赋的后段是分别。

离去的队伍在水上展开，神灵、车驾、云气，使这一场分别既华丽又遥远。

旅人只能望着。

在临别的答辞里，洛神自己说——

“恨人神之道殊兮，怨盛年之莫当。抗罗袂以掩涕兮，泪流襟之浪浪。悼良会之永绝兮，哀一逝而异乡。无微情以效爱兮，献江南之明珰。虽潜处于太阴，长寄心于君王。”

举袖掩泪的是她，献明珰的是她，向君王许下心意的也是她。她并不只是站在男性目光里被描写的美；赋在这里给了她自己的悲伤与告别。

而她离开以后，旅人并没有立即平静地上路。他溯流寻望，夜里不能睡；最后即使揽辔策马，仍然盘桓，舍不得离开。

赋写完了。

一千八百年来，读者不断问：

他写的只是洛神吗？

她可以让人想到一种渴望而不可占有的存在。但这种理解，应当从赋里的动作、声音和距离慢慢生长，不必一上来就宣布“其实不是在写她”。

历代注释家有不同的解读。

有的说洛神是甄妃——曹丕的妻子，传说曹植年轻时候喜欢过她。这个说法在文学批评史上很热闹，但缺乏有力的证据。

有的说洛神是曹植的政治理想——他想拥有的那个施展才华的位置。他失去了它。

有的说洛神是某种更抽象的东西——美本身，自由本身，一种不能被这个世界容纳的存在。

我们这一篇不站这些解读的队。

我们只说一件事：

这篇赋使我们看见：一个人可以把渴望写得极深，却不能靠写尽对方的美，就取得对她的所有权。

他能描写她到极致。他能写出她每一个动作的美。他能写出他自己渴望她的程度。但他不能拥有她。

她是他者。

不是说两个人一旦相爱、共同生活，就不再是他者。亲近可以真实，承诺也可以真实；不能成立的是，因为我渴望你，你就只能按我的愿望存在。

我们从洛水边读到的，不是一位已经把感情处理完毕的圣人。

他还在留恋。

赋没有把留恋删掉，也没有用占有来结案。她离开了，他仍然睡不着、走不开。承认对方不是自己的东西，并不会立刻使自己的痛苦停止。

这正是它比一句道理更厚的地方：道理可以说清，情感还在走。

她是她，他是他。距离可以令人悲伤，却不能只为了停止我的悲伤，就被我单方面取消。

五、白马王彪

公元二二三年。

曹丕召集几个兄弟到京城朝见——曹植，任城王曹彰，白马王曹彪。

这一次入朝，成为那首长诗的背景。

曹彰在洛阳期间突然死了。

正史里说是病死。

后来《世说新语》留下曹丕用毒枣害死曹彰的故事。《三国志》正文则记为“疾薨于邸”。传说可以解释后人怎样理解兄弟间的恐惧，却不能因此与较早史书的记载拥有相同的证据分量。

把传说与史实分开，不会减轻曹植面对的丧失。

哥哥曹彰已经死了。曹植与曹彪又将回到各自封地。

死别之后，他们还想在路上多相处一些。

诗序说，有司认为两王归藩，途中应当分开住宿：“道路宜异宿止。”曹植把这道阻隔写在诗前。刚刚失去一个兄长，现在与活着的弟弟也不能依照自己的心意相处。

不久之前还是兄弟相见，此刻又得服从宗室管理的安排。

曹植和曹彪只能服从。

分别的那一天，曹植给曹彪写了一首长诗——《赠白马王彪》。

这首诗有七章，章与章之间有接续，哀伤也不断变换位置：路上的艰难，眼前的阻隔，死去的兄长，活着的弟弟，还有自己必须勉强撑起的话。

中间有一段是他跟曹彪说的告别——

“丈夫志四海，万里犹比邻。恩爱苟不亏，在远分日亲。何必同衾帱，然后展殷勤。忧思成疾疢，无乃儿女仁。”

志向放到四海，万里也可以像近邻；情分没有减少，相隔再远也能相亲。何必同盖一床被，才能表达殷勤？不要让忧思酿成疾病——他说得坚强，甚至带着当时“大丈夫”不该困于儿女情长的口气。

这一段是他给曹彪的安慰——也是给他自己的安慰。

可是最后一章，安慰又开始摇晃——

"苦辛何虑思，天命信可疑。 虚无求列仙，松子久吾欺。 变故在斯须，百年谁能持。 离别永无会，执手将何时。 王其爱玉体，俱享黄发期。 收泪即长路，援笔从此辞。"

苦辛与思虑纠缠，连天命都值得怀疑。神仙长生的许诺，也不能替人保证下次相见。变故只在顷刻之间。于是他从疑问退回一件最小的嘱咐：你要珍重身体，希望我们都能活到白头。擦掉眼泪，上路，提笔告别。

"离别永无会，执手将何时。"

这十个字。

两个兄弟想继续同行，却连住宿都不能自行决定。眼前还有路，未来的相见却没有一张他们能够安排的日程。

“永无会”是诗中极深的恐惧。越是不知道以后还能不能见，那句珍重越没有替代品。

权力在这里做了什么？

它不用每次都下杀人的命令，也能不断收窄一个人的关系。封地、入朝、住宿、交往，每一项都可以成为被批准或不被批准的事情。对管理者而言，只是一条规定；对当事人而言，可能就是下一次握手有没有机会。

曹丕不是不知道自己的弟弟是谁。熟悉并没有自动取消戒备，血缘也没有自动保护自由。

但他选择了。

这也提醒我们，不能把前面列维纳斯的“面容”读成一种心理保证：只要见过脸，就不会伤害。人会压制熟人，也会把亲人的生活排在自己的安全感之后。

我们能判断行为及其后果。

却不能因此声称，已经看清每一次命令背后全部的内心。

把这篇与海德格尔那篇相照，有一个有限而尖锐的问题：认识一个具体的人，为什么仍可能把他放进可以牺牲的位置？

这不是把曹魏宗室政治与纳粹支持写成同一种事件。历史处境、规模、制度都不一样。比较只能照出一个问题，不能代替各自的历史。

亲近，不是不会支配的保证。学问，也不是。

真正要问的，是在恐惧与利益到来的时候，对方还有没有说不的位置。

后来司马氏取代曹魏，皇室也受制于新的权力。这个结果显示权力并不总能保护自己的后代，却不能写成曹丕压制弟弟必然招来的报应。历史不是一台负责替诗人惩罚仇人的机器。

我们可以把这种生活写成暴雨，却不能拿天气代替判断。

六、囚徒王侯

曹植生命最后的十二年里，写作给他留下一条能够出声的路。

安乡、鄄城、雍丘、东阿、陈——名号与封地多次变化。其中有降爵，也有晋爵；不能把每一个地方都涂成同样贫瘠偏远的牢房。贯穿其间的，是他与朝廷权力之间那份很难摆脱的距离。

监控与告发的可能，使表达自己也需要小心。

兄弟的相见受限制，出行与政治活动受安排。他不是没有任何资源，而是有些很重要的决定不能自己作。

哥哥去世以后，他仍向侄子求用，希望立功。诗人的身份是后世最熟悉的他，却不是他自己愿望的全部。

这些愿望没有获得他希望的空间。

但我们赞赏他继续写，不能转身要求所有受压迫的人都必须同样坚强。有人会病，会失去语言，会再也拿不起笔。那不是他失去人的资格，是他受到了伤害。

这一点，要先说清。

曹植留下了诗、赋、表、书，也写过乐府歌辞。他在获准的范围里做事，又让文字越过那个范围，走到别人的耳边。

他的文字，并没有只剩下一个受害的位置。

他的诗里有怨——他不否认怨。"高树多悲风，海水扬其波。利剑不在掌，结友何须多" 这样的句子表明他知道自己处境的真相。但他的诗里更多的是其他东西——对自然的观察，对友人的思念，对历史人物的评论，对人生的思考。

读这些文字，我们遇见一个仍在看、仍在判断、仍在想念的人。

拘束可以夺去自由，也可以伤害一个人表达自己的能力。但一个人即使无法再表达，也没有因此成为无面容的东西。

所以不能拿曹植作尺子，量那些没有留下作品的人够不够完整。没有诗集替他说话的人，也值得被听见和保护。

作品没有抹去他受到的伤害。一个人可以一边写出美的句子，一边承受很重的痛；我们不必在“完整的天才”和“崩溃的失败者”之间，强迫他只准属于一边。

这些文字使我们看见另一件事——

权力没有把他能说的话全部说完。

朝廷可以给他爵位、封地、限令，可以把他记成一个要防范的宗室。但那些安排，没有穷尽《洛神赋》的渴望，也没有穷尽《赠白马王彪》的不舍。

他留给我们的，比别人替他写的档案多。

不是因为他有一种永不受伤的力量。是因为写作这一件具体的事，让某些感受获得了形状。

写一个字，改一句话，选择让哪一个声音留在诗里。这些动作很小，却不是监控者能完全代替他作的判断。

写作没有给他发放尊严。它让本来就有尊严的人，留下了一种可以被后来的人听见的声音。

曹丕二二六年去世，曹植又活了六年。曹叡时期的限制仍在延续。制度有时候比制定它的人活得更久，个人的离去，不自动使它握住的手松开。

皇帝换了，处境没有随之打开。但读诗的时候，我们也知道，那份处境还不是曹植的全部。

七、不能拥有

“不能拥有”，是这些作品里反复出现的一条线。

洛神不可占有，兄长的信任不能强求，政治愿望屡受阻拦，与兄弟相聚也要受命令安排。它们都令人痛，但不是同一种“不能”。

别人的自由要尊重，自己的自由受剥夺却值得争取。不能把后者也美化成应该安然接受的距离。

他怎么对待这件事？

他没有否认渴望。

这很关键。

有时候，人会说“其实我不想要”，借着否认减轻失去。也有时候，放下是真实的变化，是保护自己、重新生活的开始。我们不能替每一个放下的人，断言他是在逃避他者。

曹植这些文字的特点，是没有急着把痛改写成无所谓。

《洛神赋》把渴望写得很深，也让女神自己告别。“长寄心于君王”是她的声音，不是叙述者替自己宣告已经获得她。她说了这句话，离去仍然发生。

《赠白马王彪》也不假装分别没什么。“执手将何时”——不知道何时才能再握手。文字留着那个问号，不替未来写保证。

政治奏表里，他也一再表达想做事的愿望。求用的语气受礼法约束，愿望本身却没有因此不存在。

承认渴望，又不把渴望变成占有他人的权利，这很难。

两个诱惑都很近：为了不痛，把对方贬成不值得；为了满足自己，把对方变成必须属于我的东西。

不走这两条路，不等于必须永远停在无望的渴望里。相爱、协商、拒绝、离开，都可以成为尊重彼此的行动。

但这条路有一个特点：

对方不因我的得失，才获得他自己的分量。

我可以失去一段关系，而不宣布对方一文不值；也可以认真争取自己的生活，而不要求另一个人交出他的生活。

“你不是我的东西”，不是痛苦的唯一答案，却是一条不能轻易跨过去的线。

一千八百年后，曹植仍能让人感到这种张力：想接近，却不能替对方决定；想做事，却被另一个人的安排挡住。前者要求我不占有，后者要求权力不占有我。它们不相同，却在一个人的生命里相遇。

前面几篇，我们用伦理、关系、哲学的语言接近他者。

曹植给了我们另一种语言——

渴望的语言。

他者也会出现在想念里、分别里，在一个怎么也无法平静离开的夜晚。不是每一种渴望都因此正当，但渴望可以让我们更清楚地听见自己，也听见对面的声音。

留位置，不是取消自己，是不让自己的渴望替所有人写完结局。

康德不让人仅仅成为手段，列维纳斯让面容质问我的自由，布伯让“你”打断我已知的关系。我们从曹植的诗里读到的，则是渴望与不占有之间那条细路。

这不是四套完全相同的思想。是我们在四种不同的语言之间，听见的一点回声。

那个方向上有一种风。一种温和的风。

八、桥头

想象的桥上，又到了夜里。曹植走过来。

我们让他穿着一件旧袍子，腰带松着，脚步放得很轻。此刻不需要朝见的仪仗，也没有人在旁边记录他该怎样站。

他手里拿着一卷东西。

不是希帕蒂娅手里的星盘，也不是柏格森的拐杖。

他手里拿的是一卷竹简，或者绢帛——上面有他写的字。

他走得不快。他去世时还很年轻，四十岁上下。我们让多年受拘束的疲惫留在他的姿态里，也让他终于不必急着解释自己。

他到了桥的中段。

希帕蒂娅在那里。星盘在她手里。 阿奎那在另一边，手里没有东西。 柏格森拄着拐杖。 列维纳斯站着。 布伯站在不远处。

桥的中段不止这五个人。桥上是几代几代累积下来的人——画方程的，看玉米的，写诗的，读星图的，写小说的，画图纸的，蹲着记笔记的，坐着发呆的，跟旁边人小声谈话的。

曹植看了一圈。

眼前的希帕蒂娅、阿奎那、柏格森、列维纳斯和布伯，都生在他以后。他没有读过他们的书，不知道他们后来怎样谈面容、相遇、目的。康德更要等一千多年才出生。

但他看见了一件事——

他们在做的事，他懂。

不是他已经懂得他们的全部哲学。是他们站在这里的姿态，使他觉得不必先证明自己有用。手里的卷子可以展开，也可以不展开。

他对他们点了点头。

他们也对他点了点头。

他没有走过去深谈。他在桥的中段找了一个位置站下。他把手里的卷子放在身边——那是他写的诗赋。他不需要拿在手里。这些字已经写完了。它们留在世界上一千八百年。已经在做它们该做的事。

桥头远处那一头，康德站着。今晚的康德比之前更清楚一点——能看见他大致的轮廓，他的头发的颜色。

桥外那条路上——能看见暗的天空，雷光。

那条路上有人在走。

走得最远的那些已经看不见了。 近处那个穿教授袍子的人——海德格尔——又走远了一些。

曹植朝那条路看了一眼。

他认出那条路。

在他自己的时代，兄弟也曾被权力推远。曹丕二二六年去世，比他早六年；曹叡继位，限制没有随之消失。后来曹魏又被司马氏取代。一个人以为握牢的位置，也并不能替子孙永久握牢。

曹植看了一会儿。

他不必把伤害说成已经不痛，也不必靠想象哥哥受罚来安放自己。桥外的雨不是替他完成复仇的工具。他转身时，那些命令造成的失去仍然存在，但它们不再规定他只能向哪里看。

曹植转回身。

希帕蒂娅手里的星盘在风里反着月光。

他对希帕蒂娅微微一笑。希帕蒂娅笑了一下。两个人语言完全不通——曹植不会希腊语，希帕蒂娅不会汉语。但他们都看见了对方手里的工具。星盘和竹简。两件不同时代的工具，做的是相似的事——让人看见星星，让人看见字，让人看见一些他们看不见但应该看见的东西。

阿奎那从另一边走过来。他用拉丁语说了一句什么。曹植听不懂。但是阿奎那的语调里有一种他熟悉的东西——一个写过很多字然后停下来的人的语调。曹植自己也是写过很多字的人。曹植对阿奎那点了点头。

布伯走近一点。长袍，白胡子，手里没有工具。一个童年经历了母亲离开，一个成年后受到兄长、侄子所代表的权力拘束。两种生活不是同一种伤，但亲近的人也可能使人孤单，这一点，他们不用先争谁更痛。

桥的中段人群里月光是温和的。

桥头最远那一头那个一直看着远方的人，看了希帕蒂娅，看了阿奎那，看了柏格森，看了列维纳斯，看了布伯。

这次他看的是曹植。

曹植和那个人的目光交汇了一瞬间。

曹植没有低头。这里没有一个皇帝在等他俯首。他只是抬眼，看向一个同样站着的人。

但是他的眼睛里有一种承认。

仿佛在说：我不懂你的全部语言，但我看见，你没有把我的位置只留在一份诏书里。

那个一直看着远方的人也轻轻点了点头。

曹植站在桥的中段。他的一生并非一无所有，却失去了许多深切想要的东西。他留下的诗赋穿过一千八百年，让后来的人听见洛神的告别，听见向曹彪说的珍重，也听见一个没有被爵位写完的人。

这不是用不朽赔偿他的一生。是一个人的声音，仍然能走到另一个人面前。[1][2][3]

I. Seven Steps

Let us begin with a story almost everyone who knows Cao Zhi has heard: the poem composed in seven steps.

It appears in A New Account of Tales of the World, compiled roughly two centuries after his death, not in a contemporary palace transcript. The hall, footsteps, and silence below belong to a retelling of the anecdote, not to a reconstructed day in the court record.

Imagine the great hall. Cao Pi is emperor; Cao Zhi kneels before him. One occupies the place from which commands are issued. The other must answer.

They were sons of the same mother, Lady Bian; Cao Zhi was five years younger. The same father, the same mother, years of growing up. Now the distance between emperor and subject cuts through that closeness.

Cao Pi looked at the brother kneeling in front of him.

He knew this face.

This is the brother he has known since childhood. Their father, Cao Cao, had particularly admired Cao Zhi’s gifts and considered making him his successor. The story’s sharpness lies in this familiarity: not ignorance of who you are, but knowledge.

In the end he had not. In the end the heir had been him, Cao Pi.

But this face was still here.

In this story, the brother’s gift has also become a threat in the emperor’s eyes.

He gave an order.

"Compose a poem within seven steps. If you cannot, you will be punished."

Cao Zhi rose to his feet.

He began to walk.

One step. Two steps.

The anecdote makes the poem arrive within seven steps. The four-line version best known today runs—

"Beans being cooked, with bean-stalks for fuel — The beans inside the pot weep. We grew from the same root. Why are you so urgent to burn me?"

Four lines, twenty Chinese characters. The version in A New Account has six lines and some different wording. Even this brief poem has a history of transmission.

The fuel beneath the pot of cooking beans is the stalks of the bean. The beans inside the pot are weeping. We grew from the same root. Why are you so urgent to burn me?

The hall was quiet for a moment.

The anecdote says that Cao Pi was deeply ashamed.

The story can end there. Cao Zhi’s actual life cannot.

After Cao Cao’s death in 220, Cao Zhi lived under Cao Pi and then Cao Rui. Titles rose and fell; fiefs changed; surveillance and suspicion came from the court. Not every change was a demotion, nor was he continuously locked in one cell. Yet princely rank did not give him the political scope he wanted.

He died in 232, forty-one in traditional reckoning, about forty by modern counting. From the favored youth to the repeatedly petitioning prince, the life was not long. Waiting had occupied a large part of it.

The seven-step poem compresses fraternal persecution into an instant. What follows concerns the long days beyond that instant: living inside a place assigned by someone else, and continuing to find a voice of one’s own.

II. The Succession

To understand why Cao Pi could not let his brother go, one has to go back to their boyhood.

Cao Cao had many sons. Cao Pi and Cao Zhi became two major contenders in the succession.

The eldest son, Cao Ang, had died in battle. Cao Pi had the advantage of seniority; Cao Zhi enjoyed favor for his gifts. Succession was not a question rendered uncontroversial merely by birth order.

Why?

Cao Zhi was brilliant. Brilliant in a way that astonished even his father.

The Records of the Three Kingdoms describes extensive reading and a gift for composition from early adolescence. His father even wondered whether someone else had written his work; Cao Zhi offered to be tested in person. When the sons composed rhapsodies on the newly completed Bronze Bird Terrace, he wrote rapidly and impressed Cao Cao. His talent was not merely a reputation granted by posterity. It was already visible at home.

His temperament was tangled with that talent.

The biography describes Cao Zhi as informal and excessive in drinking, Cao Pi as skilled in managing appearances. One exposed his inclinations more readily; the other better understood what people should be allowed to see. Labels such as authentic and calculating cannot, however, substitute for political judgment.

Admiring a son’s writing is not quite the same as trusting him with power.

Cao Zhi had supporters; Cao Cao hesitated more than once. A father’s preference became a matter of advisers, inheritance, and rule.

Cao Zhi did take actions that damaged his father’s confidence.

He drove on the reserved roadway and opened the Sima Gate without authorization. This was more than an unruly young man’s excursion. A prohibition had been violated; the responsible transport official was executed, and Cao Cao tightened restrictions on the princes. One person’s conduct had reached another person’s life.

In 219, when Guan Yu besieged Cao Ren, Cao Cao intended to send Cao Zhi with a relief force. The biography reports that he was too drunk to receive his instructions, and the appointment was abandoned.

That episode cannot explain Cao Pi’s appointment as heir two years earlier. The order of events matters.

In 217, Cao Pi was named Heir of the King of Wei.

In 220, Cao Cao died. Cao Pi succeeded him as King of Wei. A few months later, he forced the abdication of the last Han emperor and founded the Wei dynasty.

Cao Pi became emperor. Cao Zhi went from being a man who had nearly become emperor to being the brother of an emperor — and a brother who had once nearly displaced him.

We can criticize Cao Zhi’s failures and acknowledge his subsequent repression. The first does not automatically justify the second.

Nor does making mistakes prove that he never wanted a political role.

Brilliant people lose control and misjudge situations too. Talent does not guarantee that every action will be sound.

His letters and memorials contain a serious wish to serve and accomplish something. There is no warrant for rewriting all of it as a desire for paternal affection alone. Someone can want a father’s approval and want to act in the world. Only one desire need not be allowed to remain.

The position left by the succession struggle was harder to shed than a wish. However he later explained himself, the brother who might have displaced him had entered the emperor’s political memory.

From then on, even explaining himself became dangerous.

III. Huangchu

220 CE. Cao Pi changed the era name to Huangchu.

In Huangchu’s second year, 221, Cao Zhi was demoted to Marquis of Anxiang and then reassigned as Marquis of Juancheng. In 222 he became Prince of Juancheng. Titles changed; a title alone could not measure his power to direct his own life.

Promotion from marquis to prince could be real, and changes of fief need not all have had the same motive. Neither honors nor transfers, however, removed the court’s suspicion of the imperial clan.

A supervising envoy could turn conduct into political evidence. In 221 Guan Jun accused Cao Zhi of drunken insolence and threatening an emissary; proceedings and demotion followed. We need not imagine a complete record of every letter and footstep. The danger is already clear: the account of his own life was not solely in his hands.

He addressed memorials to his brother and later his nephew: he wanted command, service, a use for his abilities beyond poetry. His treatment was not devoid of honors. Yet the scope he sought remained far from the place permitted him.

A maintained prince had income, a residence, people serving him. Those advantages must not be erased. Material privilege and a part in deciding one’s direction are nevertheless different things.

The management of the imperial clan reveals a logic here: grant rank while restricting action.

It does not require killing.

It can leave a person waking, eating, and reading each day, while making what I want to do almost impossible to negotiate. Life is not equivalent to death. But an important part of living remains in someone else’s keeping.

Twelve years separated Cao Cao’s death from Cao Zhi’s.

They were not years of silence alone.

He did one thing — he wrote.

He kept writing, in each constrained fief. He wrote poems. He wrote rhapsodies. He wrote memorials (formal addresses to the emperor). He used writing to keep himself from disappearing.

Many texts had to reach the emperor: thanks, petitions, requests for service. Their words had to survive a dangerous reading. A gifted man writing to someone who might regard him as a threat had little room for error.

Poetry and rhapsody could hold another voice. Memorials were not necessarily false, or poems free of artifice. The same person could find different forms for grief, desire, and anger. Later, readers he could never see would approach him through them.

The court assigned him a place. His writing did not contain only that place.

IV. The Luo River

The preface to the Rhapsody on the Luo Goddess places its story in Huangchu’s third year, 222, on a return from the capital to his fief.

The carriage stops. A traveler passing the Luo River rests.

The Luo River is not large, flowing south past Luoyang. In Chinese tradition, the Luo has a goddess named Mi Fei, said to be the daughter of the legendary Fuxi.

Here we enter the rhapsody’s world. A traveler’s encounter with a goddess does not establish that the historical Cao Zhi experienced such a meeting.

Writing lets an impossible meeting unfold slowly before the reader.

The narrator first sees a beautiful figure beside the water and asks his driver in astonishment. The driver identifies the possible presence of Mi Fei, goddess of the Luo. A sudden sight interrupts an ordinary journey.

He looks again.

The rhapsody spends a long stretch of language on that act of seeing.

Of her form —

"Light as a startled wild goose taking flight, sinuous as a coiling dragon. Bright as autumn chrysanthemums, flourishing as spring pines. At moments, like a soft cloud veiling the moon; at moments, drifting, like wind-driven snow."

The images move between a startled bird, a dragon, flowers, trees, cloud, and snow. No single comparison holds her still.

Each of these comparisons is not an everyday comparison. Each pushes the image of the goddess to a place beyond the human.

Of her movement —

"She walks with light steps on the waves, her silken stockings stirring dust."

The light steps seem scarcely to touch the surface; the silk belongs to a body and yet does not obey the weight of a human body.

It is a stroke almost impossible to hold still: waves, light steps, silk, dust. A bodily image and a motion beyond the human inhabit one sentence. She is not a portrait that dimensions and proportions can exhaust.

The traveler in the rhapsody watches.

He writes what passes between them —

He is drawn to her, wishes to send his feelings across the water and offer a jade pendant. She responds too, in pauses, hesitation, movement, and speech. Yet suspicion, propriety, and the difference between mortal and divine remain between them. Mutual attraction does not simply erase the distance.

But.

She is a goddess. He is a man.

The end of the rhapsody is parting.

A departing procession unfolds over the water: spirits, a chariot, clouds. The farewell is splendid and remote at once.

The traveler can only watch.

In her speech of farewell, the goddess herself says—

“I grieve that human and divine must follow different ways, that our prime cannot be joined. I lift a silken sleeve to hide my tears; they stream over my collar. I mourn the end of our fine meeting, the parting that sends us to different places. With little to express my love, I offer a bright jewel from the south. Though I dwell in the dark depths, my heart will remain with you, my prince.”

She raises the sleeve, offers the jewel, and promises remembrance to the prince. She is not only beauty described under a male gaze. Here the poem gives her grief and farewell a voice of their own.

After she leaves, the traveler does not calmly resume his journey. He searches upstream, passes a sleepless night, and finally takes the reins but still lingers, unable readily to depart.

The rhapsody is finished.

Across eighteen centuries, readers have kept asking:

Is the goddess all he is writing about?

She can suggest an existence desired but not possessable. That reading should grow from the movements, voices, and distances of the poem, not begin by announcing that he is not really writing about her.

Generations of commentators have read it differently.

Some say the goddess is Empress Zhen — the wife of Cao Pi, whom Cao Zhi was rumored, in his youth, to have loved. This reading has had a vigorous life in literary criticism but lacks strong evidence.

Some say the goddess is Cao Zhi's political ideal — the place from which he could have used his gifts. He lost that.

Some say the goddess is something more abstract — beauty itself, freedom itself, an existence the world cannot contain.

This essay does not take a side among these readings.

It says only one thing —

The rhapsody lets us see someone write desire with extraordinary depth without acquiring ownership by describing another’s beauty.

He can describe her to the limit. He can write each gesture of her beauty. He can write the depth of his own longing. But he cannot hold her.

She is other.

This does not mean that love or a shared life makes people cease to be other. Intimacy and commitment can be real. What does not follow is that, because I desire you, you must exist only as I wish.

What we read beside the Luo is not a saint who has completed the management of his feelings.

He is still longing.

The poem neither deletes the longing nor resolves it through possession. She has gone; he remains sleepless and reluctant to leave. Acknowledging that the other is not one’s property does not immediately end the pain.

That is what makes it thicker than a maxim. The thought can become clear while the feeling is still finding its way.

She is herself; he is himself. Distance can hurt. I cannot cancel it unilaterally merely to stop my hurt.

V. To Cao Biao, Prince of Baima

223 CE.

Cao Pi summoned several brothers to the capital — Cao Zhi; the Prince of Rencheng, Cao Zhang; and the Prince of Baima, Cao Biao.

That visit to court became the setting of the long poem.

Cao Zhang died suddenly while in Luoyang.

The official histories say he died of illness.

A later anecdote in A New Account of Tales of the World has Cao Pi poisoning Cao Zhang with dates. The main text of the Records says that he died of illness at his residence. The anecdote can illuminate how later readers imagined fear between the brothers without acquiring the same evidential weight as the earlier account.

Distinguishing anecdote from record does not diminish Cao Zhi’s loss.

Cao Zhang was dead. Cao Zhi and Cao Biao were about to return to their separate fiefs.

After a parting in death, they wished for a little more time together on the road.

The preface says officials required separate lodging on the return to their fiefs. Cao Zhi places that separation before the poem. Having just lost one brother, he cannot now spend time with a living brother as he wishes.

A meeting of brothers had once again become subject to the arrangements governing imperial princes.

Cao Zhi and Cao Biao could only obey.

On the day of parting, Cao Zhi wrote a long poem to Cao Biao — To Cao Biao, Prince of Baima.

The poem has seven linked sections, and grief keeps changing its position: difficulty on the road, enforced separation, the dead brother, the living brother, words the speaker must somehow make strong enough to bear.

In the middle, there is a section that is what Cao Zhi says to Cao Biao in farewell —

“A man’s resolve reaches the four seas; ten thousand miles can be next door. If affection does not fail, distance can deepen closeness. Must we share one coverlet to show our care? To grieve until grief becomes illness—is that not a tenderness too small for our resolve?”

Let the aspiration range across the world; ten thousand miles can seem next door. Affection need not diminish with separation; sharing a coverlet is not its only proof. Do not let sorrow become illness, he urges, drawing on his time’s ideal of masculine resolve against tender attachment.

This passage is his comfort to Cao Biao — and his comfort to himself.

But in the final section, consolation begins to tremble again—

"What is the use of dwelling on suffering and toil? Even the mandate of heaven seems doubtful now. The pursuit of empty immortality — Master Red Pine and his kind have deceived me too long. A change of fortune comes in an instant; who can hold a hundred years? This parting will never be undone. When will I take your hand again? My prince, take care of your jade body. May we both reach white-haired age. Wipe away the tears, set out on the long road, take up the brush and end the poem here."

Suffering and anxious thought entangle each other; even heaven’s mandate becomes doubtful. Promises of immortality cannot guarantee another meeting. Everything can change in an instant. He returns to the smallest wish: take care of yourself; may we both grow old. Wipe the tears, set out, lift the brush to say goodbye.

This parting will never be undone. When will I take your hand again?

Ten Chinese characters, holding an uncertainty no consoling argument can settle.

Two brothers wish to continue together and cannot even decide where to stay. A road lies ahead; their next meeting has no timetable they can arrange.

Never meeting again is the poem’s profound fear. The less certain the future meeting, the less replaceable the injunction to take care becomes.

What has power done here?

It need not order a killing every time in order to keep narrowing someone’s relations. Fiefs, visits to court, lodging, association: each can become a matter of permission. To an administrator it may be a regulation. To those affected it may decide whether another handshake is possible.

Cao Pi knew who his brother was. Familiarity did not automatically cancel suspicion; kinship did not automatically protect freedom.

But he chose.

This also warns against reading Levinas’s face as a psychological guarantee: having seen someone does not prevent harm. People suppress those they know and put a relative’s life behind their own sense of security.

We can judge actions and their consequences.

That does not mean we have seen every inward motive behind every command.

Reading this beside the Heidegger essay raises a limited, sharp question: why can knowledge of a particular person coexist with placing them among the expendable?

This does not make Wei dynastic politics and support for Nazism the same event. Context, scale, and institutions differ. Comparison can illuminate a question; it cannot replace either history.

Closeness is no guarantee against domination. Neither is learning.

The question is whether, when fear and interest press in, the other still has a place from which to refuse.

The Sima family later replaced Wei, and the imperial house fell under another power. Power does not necessarily protect its own descendants. But that outcome cannot be presented as inevitable retribution for Cao Pi’s treatment of his brother. History is not a machine assigned to punish a poet’s enemies.

We can picture that life as a storm without letting weather substitute for judgment.

VI. A Prince in Captivity

In his last twelve years, writing left Cao Zhi a way of making himself heard.

Anxiang, Juancheng, Yongqiu, Dong’e, Chen: titles and fiefs changed repeatedly. Some changes were demotions, others promotions. The places cannot all be painted as equally impoverished prisons. What runs through them is his difficult distance from the power of the court.

Surveillance and the possibility of denunciation made self-expression precarious.

Meetings among brothers were restricted; travel and political activity were regulated. He was not without resources. But important decisions were not his to make.

After his brother’s death, he continued seeking service under his nephew, hoping to accomplish something. Poet is how posterity best knows him. It was not the whole of what he wanted.

Those wishes did not obtain the scope he sought.

Admiring his continued writing cannot become a demand that everyone under oppression be equally strong. Some become ill, lose words, cannot pick up a pen again. That is injury, not the loss of their standing as human beings.

That needs to be clear first.

Cao Zhi left poems, rhapsodies, memorials, letters, and lyrics for music. He acted within what was permitted and let writing travel beyond those permissions into other people’s hearing.

His writing did not contain only the position of a victim.

His poems contain resentment — he does not deny it. Lines like "A tall tree has many sorrowful winds; the sea raises its waves. With no sharp sword in my hand, what good is a great roster of friends?" make clear that he saw the truth of his condition. But his poems contain more than this. Observations of nature. Thoughts of friends. Reflections on figures of the past. Reflections on the human life.

Reading him, we meet a person still seeing, judging, and missing others.

Confinement can take freedom and damage the capacity to express oneself. Even someone no longer able to express themselves has not become a faceless thing.

Cao Zhi cannot become a ruler for measuring whether people who left no works were sufficiently whole. Someone with no book to speak for them still deserves hearing and protection.

The works did not erase his injuries. Someone can write a beautiful line while carrying great pain. We need not force him to choose a place between the intact genius and the broken failure.

The writing lets us see something else—

Power did not say everything he could say.

The court could assign title, land, and restrictions, and record him as an imperial kinsman requiring vigilance. Those arrangements did not exhaust the longing of the Luo rhapsody or the reluctance to part from Cao Biao.

He left us more than the file others kept on him.

Not because he possessed an invulnerable strength. The specific work of writing gave certain feelings a form.

Writing a character, revising a line, choosing which voice will remain in a poem: small acts, but judgments the watcher could not wholly make on his behalf.

Writing did not issue him dignity. It allowed someone who already had dignity to leave a voice that later people could hear.

Cao Pi died in 226; Cao Zhi lived another six years. Restrictions continued under Cao Rui. Institutions sometimes outlive those who establish them. A person’s departure does not automatically loosen their grip.

The emperor changed without the circumstances opening up accordingly. Reading the poems, we also know those circumstances were not all of Cao Zhi.

VII. What Cannot Be Held

What cannot be possessed is a recurring thread in these works.

The goddess cannot be possessed; a brother’s trust cannot be compelled; political wishes are obstructed; meetings among brothers are regulated. All can hurt. They are not the same kind of cannot.

Another person’s freedom deserves respect; one’s own denied freedom deserves to be sought. The second must not be romanticized as a distance one ought peacefully to accept.

How did he meet this?

He did not deny longing.

This is the key.

Sometimes someone says I never wanted it to soften a loss. Sometimes letting go is a genuine change, the beginning of self-protection and a new life. We cannot tell everyone who lets go that they are evading otherness.

What marks these writings is their refusal to hurry pain into indifference.

The rhapsody deepens desire and lets the goddess say goodbye herself. The promise that her heart will remain with the prince is her voice, not the narrator’s declaration that he has obtained her. She speaks; departure still happens.

The poem to Cao Biao likewise refuses to call parting trivial. When will I take your hand again? The writing keeps the question open instead of guaranteeing the future.

In political memorials he repeatedly expresses his wish to serve. The language is constrained by courtly forms; the desire does not thereby cease to exist.

Acknowledging desire without turning it into a right to possess another person is difficult.

Two temptations lie nearby: to devalue the other in order not to hurt, or turn the other into something that must belong to me in order to satisfy myself.

Refusing those roads does not require remaining forever in hopeless longing. Loving, negotiating, refusing, and leaving can all be acts that respect each person.

But this road has one feature:

The other’s weight is not bestowed by my success or failure.

I can lose a relationship without declaring the other worthless. I can work seriously for my own life without requiring someone else to surrender theirs.

You are not my property is not the only answer to pain. It is a line not to be casually crossed.

Eighteen centuries later, Cao Zhi still makes the tension felt: wishing to approach without deciding for the other; wishing to act while another person’s arrangements stand in the way. The first asks that I not possess; the second that power not possess me. They differ, yet meet within one life.

In the preceding essays, ethics, relation, and philosophy helped us approach the other.

Cao Zhi gives us another language —

The language of longing.

The other also appears in missing someone, in parting, in a night from which one cannot calmly depart. That does not justify every desire. Desire can, however, make us hear ourselves more clearly and hear the voice across from us.

Making room is not canceling oneself. It is refusing to let one’s own longing write everyone’s ending.

Kant refuses the person reduced merely to a means; Levinas lets the face question my freedom; Buber lets the Thou interrupt the relation I thought I knew. What we read in Cao Zhi is the narrow path between longing and possession.

These are not four identical philosophies. They are echoes we hear between four different languages.

In that direction there is a wind. A mild wind.

VIII. The Bridge

Night again on the imagined bridge. Cao Zhi approached.

We gave him an old robe, a loose belt, a light tread. No ceremonial retinue was needed here. Nobody stood beside him recording how he ought to stand.

He carried something in his hand.

Not an astrolabe like Hypatia’s, nor a cane like Bergson’s.

What he carried in his hand was a roll of bamboo slips, or of silk — written with his own hand.

He walked unhurriedly. He had died young, around forty. We let the fatigue of constrained years remain in his bearing and allowed him, finally, not to hurry an explanation of himself.

He reached the middle of the bridge.

Hypatia was there. The astrolabe in her hand. Aquinas, on the other side, his hands empty. Bergson, leaning on his cane. Levinas, standing. Buber, standing not far off.

The middle of the bridge was not just these five. The bridge held people accumulated from generation to generation — those drawing equations, those watching corn, those writing poems, those reading star charts, those writing novels, those sketching diagrams, those crouching aside taking notes, those sitting and looking into the distance, those speaking quietly with their neighbors.

Cao Zhi looked around.

Hypatia, Aquinas, Bergson, Levinas, and Buber had all been born after him. He had not read their books or known how they would speak of the face, encounter, ends. Kant would not be born for over a millennium.

But he saw one thing —

What they were doing, he understood.

Not that he understood their philosophies in full. Their way of standing there let him feel no need to prove his usefulness first. The scroll in his hand could be opened, or left unopened.

He nodded to them.

They nodded back.

He did not approach for further talk. He found a place in the middle of the bridge and stood. He set the scroll beside him — those were his poems and rhapsodies. He did not need to hold them. The words had been written. They had been in the world for eighteen hundred years. Doing the work they were meant to do.

At the far end of the bridge, Kant stood. Tonight Kant was a little clearer than before — you could see his rough outline, the color of his hair.

Across from the bridge, on that road, the sky had darkened. Lightning.

There were people on that road, walking.

The ones farthest along were no longer visible. That man in the professor's robe — Heidegger — had walked further than last time.

Cao Zhi glanced once toward that road.

He recognized the road.

In his own age, power had also pushed brothers apart. Cao Pi died in 226, six years before him; Cao Rui succeeded without the restrictions disappearing. Later the Sima family replaced Wei. A position one person believed firmly held could not be held forever for descendants.

Cao Zhi looked for a while.

He did not have to say the injury no longer hurt, or settle himself by imagining his brother punished. The rain outside was not an instrument for completing his revenge. When he turned, the losses caused by those orders remained. They no longer dictated the only direction in which he could look.

Cao Zhi turned back.

The astrolabe in Hypatia's hand caught the moonlight.

He smiled at her, slightly. Hypatia smiled back. The two could not understand one another's languages — Cao Zhi did not know Greek, Hypatia did not know Chinese. But they each saw the instrument the other carried. An astrolabe and a scroll of bamboo slips. Two tools from different times, doing similar work — making people see the stars, making people see the words, making people see something they cannot otherwise see but should.

Aquinas walked over from the other side. He said something in Latin. Cao Zhi did not understand. But there was something in the tone of Aquinas's voice that he recognized — the tone of a man who has written many words and then stopped. Cao Zhi was such a man too. He nodded back.

Buber drew a little closer: dark robe, white beard, empty hands. One had experienced his mother’s absence in childhood; the other had lived under the constraining power of brother and nephew. Their wounds were not identical. They did not need to decide whose pain was greater to recognize that those nearest can also leave one alone.

The middle of the bridge — many people, and the moonlight was mild.

The figure who had always been looking into the distance, the one at the far end of the bridge, looked at Hypatia, at Aquinas, at Bergson, at Levinas, at Buber.

This time he looked at Cao Zhi.

Cao Zhi's eyes met the eyes of that figure for an instant.

Cao Zhi did not lower his head. No emperor awaited his submission. He looked up toward another person who was also standing.

But there was an acknowledgment in his eyes.

As though to say: I do not know your whole language, but I see that you have not left my place solely inside an imperial decree.

The figure who had always been looking into the distance nodded, lightly.

Cao Zhi stood in the middle. His life had not been without possessions or relationships; he had lost many things he deeply wanted. His surviving writing crossed eighteen centuries. Later readers heard the goddess’s farewell, the wish for Cao Biao’s safety, a person not exhausted by his title.

Immortality does not compensate for his life. A person’s voice can still reach another person.[1][2][3]
