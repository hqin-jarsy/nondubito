贞德，火

Joan of Arc, Fire

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、先有人替她备马

十七岁的贞德要去见国王。把这句话放进传奇，下一步通常就是奇迹：少女出现，众人让路，历史忽然换了方向。但在一四二九年的道路上，她先得说服具体的人。谁肯听她把话说完，谁肯提供马匹和护送，谁愿意冒险，把这个没有显赫家世的年轻姑娘送到希农去？

她来自栋雷米，战争并不是远处的消息。村庄会受到威胁，人要带着牲畜避难，邻近地方的孩子也会因为大人的阵营打得头破血流。后来受审时，她说自己大约十三岁开始听到声音，并相信那是上帝的指引。听见声音的是她；我们能够核对的是她怎样讲述，以及她随后做了什么。

她去沃库勒找守将罗贝尔·德·博德里库尔，并非第一次开口就获得通行。拒绝之后还要再去，还要找到愿意同行的人。道路最后通向希农，也通向普瓦捷的宗教审查。她并没有跳过人与人之间的信任，直接从天上降落在军队中央。她走过了一道道门，有些人把门关上，也有人终于把门打开。

到奥尔良时，城里的围困已经持续数月。一四二九年五月八日，英军撤走，围城解除。贞德带来的信心、催促进攻的决心和在战场上的出现，成为扭转局势的重要力量；军官的部署、士兵的战斗和城里人的坚持，也都在这场胜利之中。一个人的到来能够改变许多人怎样行动，并不意味着许多人从此不存在了。

随后是卢瓦尔河一线的战事、帕提的胜利，以及前往兰斯的行程。七月十七日，查理七世在那里加冕。数月前还需要别人允许她进门的姑娘，如今举着旗站在王权最庄严的仪式旁边。她追求的事情，确实发生了一部分。这足以令人惊异，也足以让人忘记：站在那里的人，才十七岁。

二、门槛一直都在

她是农家女，年轻，没有大学里的训练，也没有凭爵位取得的发言权。这些身份放在当时，每一种都可能成为拒绝她的理由。更难的是，它们会叠在一起：军官可以说她不懂战争，学者可以说她不懂神学，年长者可以说她不懂世事。还没轮到她把事情讲清楚，结论似乎已经写好了。

我们都见过这种门槛的较小版本。一个人提出办法，大家先问他是什么职位；一个年轻女人表达判断，别人先赞她勇敢，再把判断放到一边。资格当然有用，经验也确实重要。但资格原来是帮助我们识别能力的，久而久之，却可能变成保证某些人永远不用被认真听见的办法。

贞德知道自己需要争取什么。她寻找引荐，要求护送，向王室说明任务，在军中催促行动，也在审讯中拒绝回答某些问题。她的勇气里有坚持、有判断，也有逐渐学会与人周旋的部分。一个从小地方来的人，并不因此活在历史之外，更不会因为没有读过大学，就不知道别人正在关门。

支撑她的语言来自信仰。祈祷、弥撒、圣徒、合法的国王，这些都属于她熟悉的世界。她不是为了摆脱一切传统才出发，而是在传统里听到一个不能交给别人替她承担的召唤。同样的钟声，有人听见日常的秩序，她听见自己必须动身的理由。

今天的读者未必分享她对声音的理解。信徒、怀疑者和历史研究者，可以在这里保留分歧。认真对待她，不必先替她完成医学诊断，也不必先宣布神迹已经获得人人认可的证明。至少可以承认：她对自己经历的理解，不能只因为说话者年轻、出身农家、是女性，就被省略掉。

不过，愿意听她说话，与必须服从她，是两回事。她深信自己被派遣，同行者仍然有需要判断的风险，城里的居民也有自己的生命。一个人的信念可以打开道路；如果它要求所有人把判断交出去，那条道路同样会变窄。尊重贞德，也包括让她进入这个共同承担后果的世界。

三、王冠旁边的人

拿破仑戴上王冠，贞德站在王冠旁边。这个画面上的区别很鲜明，却不能替我们完成全部判断。她没有建立个人帝国，但她也并非什么都不想建立。她要解围，要恢复查理的王权，要让一个被争夺的政治秩序重新取得力量。兰斯的仪式，就是她工作的一部分。

她信赖那位国王，并把他的加冕看得极重。后来的人常常更喜欢一个完全孤独、只服从内心的贞德，仿佛她与国王、教会和军队都没有关系。但那样的姑娘无法走到兰斯。真实的她在这些关系里行动，依靠它们，也与其中的人争执。她既能够改变局面，也会受到局面的限制。

她写给英方的信要求对方退出，并带着战争的威胁。她催促攻击，参与的战役有人死去。信仰使她有力量，也使她能够把自己的军事目标理解为上帝的旨意。这一点值得同情地理解，也值得认真追问：当我确信自己站在正义一边时，对面的人还会以怎样的面目出现？

复审时，她的侍从路易·德·库特讲过一个细节：一名法国人击伤英军俘虏，贞德下马，托住伤者的头，设法让他告解，并安慰他。这是多年后的证词，我们不能把它当作随身摄像记录；但它保存了一种与凯旋很不相同的记忆。一个原本属于“敌军”的人，突然近到需要有人托着他的头。

她没有因此停止战争。一次俯身也不能替一场战争清账。可这个动作让问题有了重量：当目标要求我们向前冲的时候，是否还看得见倒在路边的人？如果只能在自己人的身上辨认出痛苦，所谓崇高很容易成为一种分配视力的方式。

拿破仑与贞德的不同，可以让我们继续想权力怎样改变一个人，却不必把两个人排成纯洁程度的名次。对贞德真正严肃的尊重，是容许她的行动接受判断，同时不让判断吞掉她整个人。她不是因为每一个决定都正确，才有权免于被当作工具。

四、问题已经准备好了

一四三〇年五月二十三日，贞德在贡比涅附近被勃艮第方面俘获，后来落入英方手中。到鲁昂，她面对的已不是需要她鼓舞士气的军队，而是要判定她有罪的审判机器。这座城当时处于英方控制下；主审皮埃尔·科雄支持英方，宗教程序与王位争夺紧紧缠在一起。

一四三一年开始的审讯，把她的声音、衣着、行动和对教会权威的服从放到案头。记录留下许多问答，也留下权力的不对称。对面可以规定问题的形式，可以整理她说过的话，可以决定哪些回答算回答；她则得在囚禁之中，一次次解释自己为什么有资格相信自己。

有一次，审讯者问她是否知道自己处在上帝的恩宠中。她的回答大意是：若不在，愿上帝使我在其中；若在，愿上帝保守我。今天读来仍然灵敏。她没有接受对方递来的两个危险格子，而是把回答放回祈祷，放回她与上帝之间尚未被法庭全部占据的关系。

更让我留意的，是她会说不知道，会要求延后，会指出某些事不准备告诉他们。证人也记得她纠正记录者：自己说的不是那个意思。一个没有受过经院训练的姑娘，仍然知道话被改写以后，变成的可能是另一桩罪。她守护的不只是结论，还有自己究竟说了什么。

这并不是一个现代无神论者面对整个宗教世界的故事。她想参加弥撒，希望得到圣事，也曾提出向教皇申诉。支持过她的神职人员，与审判她的人同属基督教世界。裂缝穿过制度内部：谁可以代表它，谁有权解释她的信仰，谁又能让自己的敌意披上最终裁决的外衣？

一个问题如果只允许你在自我否定与有罪之间选择，它就已经不是为了理解你而提出的。这里令人不安的，不只是贞德答得多聪明，而是她必须如此聪明，才能暂时保住一点说话的地方。普通人答得慢、记错一句、忍不住哭出来，也不应因此失去被公正对待的资格。

五、一件衣服与一间牢房

五月二十四日，在死亡的威胁面前，贞德作了撤回声明，通常称作弃绝或悔过宣誓。后来她承认自己害怕火。读到这里，英雄故事常常急着解释：那不算软弱，她很快又恢复坚定。可恐惧本来就不需要这样被赦免。她十九岁，她不想被烧死，这是一个完整的理由。

她换上女装，却仍被送回英方看守的牢房。几天后，她重新穿上男装；五月二十八日的审讯又把她带回此前的指控。原审记录说她选择恢复这种衣着，同时也记下她的解释：自己与男人关在一起，男装更合适；关于弥撒、解除镣铐和改善处境的承诺没有兑现。

后来的证词补充了受到侵害的危险，也有人说女装被拿走，使她只能穿上男装。这些叙述在细节上并不完全一致。能够看清的是，那件衣服从来没有独立于牢房存在。看守是谁，她能否安全入睡，能不能获得女性陪伴，这些问题与所谓“再次违抗”被放在了极不相称的位置。

她也重新肯定声音的来源，说先前否认是出于怕死。因此，最后的定罪不能只简化成穿错衣服；信仰、先前的宣誓与被判定再次陷入异端，都进入了程序。但程序越完整，越需要问：一个人在怎样的条件下说了那些话，又是在怎样的条件下撤回？把恐惧删掉，文件就会显得格外整齐。

五月三十日，她在鲁昂被处以火刑。有关临终的证词记着十字架、祈祷和呼唤耶稣，也记着在场的人哭泣。我们没有必要靠延长那一刻的痛苦来增强文章的力量。火烧死了一个年轻人；它没有把杀死她的行为变成一场为后人准备的高贵仪式。

后世给她画出光环，无法改变她当时需要的东西：可靠的保护，能够认真听她说话的人，不拿死亡逼迫她签字的审理。她的信念值得理解，她的身体也值得保全。两者之间，本不该要求她用焚毁后者来证明前者。

六、当她不再能够带来胜利

查理七世没有把她救出来。这一结果令人难以绕过，尤其想到兰斯的加冕。我们无法凭这个结果读出王室里每一个人的私心，也不能把分裂中的法国写成一个合谋者。然而，从被期待创造奇迹到被关在异方牢房之间，确实露出一道残酷的落差：一个人的功用可以被迅速认识，她的脆弱却未必因此有人承担。

战争后来还延续了多年。她没有独自完成法国的胜利，也没有活到后来那幅地图成为事实。可国家记忆很擅长把漫长的过程压进一个人的身影，把她需要的援助挤到画框之外。旗帜越鲜明，持旗人的饥饿、疼痛和想回家的愿望，越容易变成不重要的细节。

读到这里，会想起这个系列中别的人。特斯拉的发明需要资金、同事和市场，他的晚年却不能用商业结果估价；康托尔的数学遭遇争论，一个人的精神痛苦也不该被缩写成某位对手的一次否定。每个故事都有自己的因果，不能因为读起来相似，就把它们压成同一种受难。

图灵遭到刑事惩罚时，他对国家的贡献没有保护他的私人生活。但即使他没有那些贡献，惩罚同样是不公的。这一点放在贞德身上，也一样重要。若我们只会说“她立过那么大的功，怎么可以这样待她”，就还把人的资格押在功劳上。另一个没有解救过任何城市的女囚，也不应被这样对待。

真正困难的承认，发生在一个人不再有用的时候，甚至发生在他判断错误、令我们失望的时候。我们仍然可以拒绝他的主张，追究他的责任，却不能随之把他的恐惧、身体和活下去的愿望取消。否则，人只是暂时表现出色的资源，随时等待下一次估价。

贞德可以失败，可以求助，可以希望事情停下来。允许这些可能性，并不削弱她曾经做过的事；它让那些行动终于属于一个活人。勇敢不是取消退路的合同，更不是旁人有权代替她签署的合同。

七、母亲重新走进法庭

贞德死后，故事没有立刻只剩国家和圣像。她的母亲还活着。伊莎贝尔·罗梅知道那个名字在旗帜和判决书之外意味着什么：自己养大的女儿，被说成异端，被处死，还留下牵连家人的耻辱。公众能够把时间算成二十五年，母亲经历的是女儿一直没有回来。

政治局势变化以后，早先的审判开始受到调查。经教皇加里斯都三世授权，正式的平反程序于一四五五年展开，贞德的母亲与兄弟是申诉者。十一月七日，巴黎圣母院听到母亲要求恢复女儿名誉的请求。她首先带来的不是关于民族象征的理论，而是一个家庭不肯接受的判决。

复审召来许多证人：儿时的邻居、同行者、神职人员、参与过原审的人。他们记得的贞德不完全相同，有的记得战场，有的记得祈祷，有的记得记录被改动。证词会受到岁月、政治变化和自我辩护的影响；但让这些声音重新进入案卷，本身就在打破原审关于“全部已经说清”的封口。

一四五六年七月七日，原来的判决被撤销。这是重要的修正，既关乎贞德的名誉，也关乎家人继续生活时背负的名字。迟到的正义仍然有意义，只是它不能要求受害者替迟到本身说没关系。纸上的污名可以取消，母亲失去的年月不能补发。

一九二〇年，天主教会将贞德封圣。封圣是另一种宗教程序，与十五世纪判决无效的认定不同。此后也好，此前也好，人们用不同方式认领她：信仰、爱国、政治运动，都曾站到她的形象旁边。那里既可能有真诚的敬意，也可能有借用一个不会再反驳的名字的方便。

因此，纪念她时可以保留一道小小的检查：这一次，我们是在让人听见她，还是又需要她替我们举旗？如果她活着，提出不合时宜的问题，要求休息，不愿为我们的主张作证，我们仍然愿意给她留位置吗？比起塑像放得多高，这个问题更接近她母亲重新走进法庭的那一步。

八、她也可以想活下去

苏格拉底七十岁左右被处死，贞德十九岁。把他们放在一起，首先遇到的是两个截然不同的人：一个用追问度过漫长的一生，一个用信仰走进战争；一个留下由学生写成的对话，另一个的许多声音保存在敌对审讯的记录里。相似处不必抹掉这些差别。

柏拉图的《克力同》写苏格拉底拒绝朋友安排的逃亡，并给出关于公正与法律的理由。那是值得认真读的选择，却不是一道要求后来者照做的考题。一个人受到不公审判以后，不会因为想逃出去、想多活几年，就自动比留在牢里的人低一等。

贞德在审讯中会回避，会争取时间，在火刑威胁面前会撤回声明。她的信仰也曾给她重新肯定自己的力量。这些摆动让人看见的是处境的压力，而不是供后人扣分的瑕疵。一个十九岁的人，不欠观众一条毫无迟疑、直通死亡的线。

他们都让人问，裁决一个人的机构，是否足以占有这个人关于自己的全部理解。可我们也需要反过来问：我对自己的确信，是否足以要求别人停止判断？苏格拉底的诘问和贞德的信仰，不能被熔成同一套答案。他们可以在同一个空间里被认真对待，而不必彼此证明。

这也使康德所说的“人是目的”有了一种更实际的读法。它不只是替伟大人物追授勋章，而是限制我们如何使用每一个人。不能为了国家的胜利、宗教的声誉，甚至为了写出一个动人的故事，就把具体的人当作可以耗尽的材料。

因此，我们可以敬佩贞德，也可以不同意她某些信念或行动。我们可以研究她的军事作用，也可以承认档案尚未让我们知道的一切。留在这些分歧下面的，并非一张永远正确的证明书，而是一件更简单的事：她本来有权继续活着。

九、把火留在炉子里

接下来仍然是这个系列的想象：第五乐章的桥头，已经有许多人坐下。亚里士多德正在看脚下的木板，讨论怎样才算稳固。贞德走来时，大家先注意到她很年轻。这里没有准备好的刑台，也没有等着她完成的加冕。

有人挪开一张椅子，让出足够的地方。有人把水放在手边，而不是直接塞给她。她愿意坐下就坐下，愿意站一会儿也可以。历史替她安排过太多紧迫的时刻，这一次，几分钟不必立刻拿去改变任何人的命运。

休谟合上双陆棋盘，似乎想问她关于声音的事。苏格拉底也有问题。但问题可以等到她愿意谈的时候。这里的耐心，不是把怀疑取消，而是不让怀疑先取得看守的钥匙。我们可以不相信同一件事，仍然不把对方关进必须证明自己的牢房。

她把旗放在椅子旁。拿破仑看着那面旗，没有人要求他们比较哪一种胜利更大。旗可以让许多人朝同一个方向走，也可以挡住路边的人。讨论会继续，可她此刻不必替整个法国发言，也不必代表所有相信自己听见召唤的人。

远处还留着几张空椅子。那里可以坐她的母亲，也可以坐一个曾经站在另一方的普通士兵。想象并不能抹去战争与责任，只是替一个问题腾出位置：如果不急着决定谁可以被忽略，我们会怎样重新谈论那场战争？

康德没有递给她一张毕业证书。她也不需要把自己的信仰改写成别人的哲学，才能留在这里。她可以祈祷，别人可以不祈祷；她可以讲述声音，别人可以继续追问。共同相处的困难还在，所幸，没有谁因此自动取得处置另一个人的权利。

炉子里有火。木头轻轻响了一声，光落在杯沿上。贞德把手伸近一点，又收回一点，自己找到合适的距离。这是一种极小的自由，过去却有人连这一点都没有留给她。

让火留在炉子里。它可以烧水，可以暖一间屋子。今晚不需要任何一个人，通过被烧掉来证明自己有光。

I. Someone had to find her a horse

At seventeen, Joan wanted an audience with the king. A legend can move straight from that sentence to a miracle: the girl arrives, the doors open, history changes direction. A journey in 1429 needed rather more. Someone had to listen, find horses, provide an escort, and take the risk of bringing an obscure young woman to the court at Chinon. Before she could alter the fortunes of a kingdom, she needed particular people to help her cross particular roads.

She came from Domrémy, where war was not merely news from somewhere else. People fled threats to their village; animals had to be brought to safety; the quarrels of rival allegiances reached even the children. At her trial, Joan said she had begun hearing a voice at about thirteen and believed it came from God. The experience was hers. What the surviving record gives us is her account of it, and a remarkable sequence of actions that followed.

She approached Robert de Baudricourt at Vaucouleurs and persisted after being turned away. Eventually there were companions, horses, and a journey through dangerous country. At Chinon she gained access to Charles; at Poitiers churchmen examined her claims. She did not descend into an army from outside human relationships. She worked her way through them, finding people whose confidence could turn a conviction into a practical possibility.

The siege of Orléans had lasted for months when she arrived. On 8 May 1429 the English withdrew. Her presence, confidence, and insistence on action mattered enormously. So did commanders, soldiers, provisions, and the people who had endured the siege. The extraordinary thing is not that everyone else became useless when Joan appeared. It is that her arrival helped change what many other people believed they could do.

The Loire campaign and the victory at Patay were followed by the march to Reims. On 17 July, Charles VII was crowned there. The girl who had recently needed permission to enter a room now stood with her banner beside the most solemn ceremony of kingship. A substantial part of what she had set out to achieve had happened. The achievement can almost prevent us from noticing the person standing beside it: she was still seventeen.

II. The thresholds were real

A young woman from a farming family had no inherited command and no university training to display. Each fact could become a reason not to hear her; together they made a formidable barrier. A soldier could dismiss her understanding of war, a theologian her understanding of faith, an older person her understanding of life. The verdict might be ready before she had finished explaining what she wanted to do.

Most of us have encountered smaller versions of that room. Someone proposes a solution and is asked for their job title. A young woman offers a judgment and receives a compliment on her confidence instead of an answer. Qualifications and experience matter. The trouble begins when a means of recognizing competence becomes a means of ensuring that certain people never receive a serious hearing at all.

Joan was not simply unaware of the obstacles. She sought introductions, secured companions, pressed her case at court, argued with commanders, and later refused to answer questions she considered improper. Her courage included persistence and an increasing ability to deal with people. Coming from a village did not place her outside history. Lacking a scholar's education did not prevent her from recognizing a door being shut in her face.

Faith furnished the language in which she understood her task. Prayer, Mass, saints, and the rightful king belonged to her world. She had not set out to abolish every tradition. Within a tradition she heard a call that, as she understood it, no one could answer on her behalf. The bells that marked an ordinary day for others accompanied, for her, a reason to leave home.

A reader need not settle the origin of the voices before taking her seriously. A believer, a skeptic, and a historian can retain their differences here. Neither a retrospective medical label nor a declaration of publicly established miracle supplies everything we need to understand. At the very least, her account of her experience deserves more than dismissal on the grounds of her age, background, or sex.

Listening to Joan, however, is not identical with obeying her. Her certainty did not remove the risks faced by companions or the lives of people in a besieged town. Conviction can open a road; it can also narrow that road if everyone else must surrender their judgment. Respecting her means allowing her actions to enter a shared world of consequences, not exempting them from it.

III. Beside the crown

Napoleon wore a crown; Joan stood beside one. The contrast is memorable, but it does not finish the argument. She sought no personal empire, yet she had a definite political project: relieve the siege, advance Charles's cause, and restore strength to a contested kingship. The coronation was not an accidental decoration attached to her spiritual independence. It was part of the work she had come to do.

She placed immense trust in her king. Later readers sometimes prefer a Joan answerable to nothing but an entirely private conscience, detached from court, Church, and army. Such a figure could never have reached Reims. The historical Joan acted through these relationships, depended on them, and disputed with the people inside them. They made action possible while also setting limits to what she could accomplish.

Her letters demanded English withdrawal and threatened force. She urged attacks; people died in the campaigns in which she took part. Her faith sustained her and allowed her to understand military objectives as divinely commanded. There is a difficult question here for anyone who has felt sure of being on the right side: once my cause becomes sacred, what can I still see in the person standing opposite me?

At the later rehabilitation inquiry, her page Louis de Coutes remembered a Frenchman striking an English prisoner. Joan dismounted, supported the wounded man's head, arranged for his confession, and comforted him. This is testimony recalled years afterwards, not an untouched window into a battlefield. Yet it preserves a memory quite different from a triumphal procession. The enemy has become close enough to need someone to hold his head.

That gesture did not end her participation in war, nor could it settle the account of a campaign. Its force lies elsewhere. A forward movement has been interrupted by a particular body. If we can recognize suffering only when it wears our own colors, noble language becomes a way of distributing eyesight: some people remain visible, while others are allowed to disappear.

The contrast with Napoleon can help us think about ambition and power without becoming a ranking of immaculate souls. A serious regard for Joan permits judgment of what she did while refusing to let any judgment consume the whole person. She did not have to be correct in every decision to possess a life that other people were not entitled to spend.

IV. The questions were waiting

On 23 May 1430 Joan was captured near Compiègne by Burgundian forces and subsequently transferred to English hands. In Rouen she faced an apparatus with very different purposes from an army hoping for deliverance. The city was under English control. Pierre Cauchon, the leading judge, supported the English interest; ecclesiastical proceedings and the struggle over the French crown were intimately entangled.

The examinations of 1431 placed her voices, clothing, conduct, and submission to ecclesiastical authority under scrutiny. The surviving questions and answers also preserve an imbalance. Her questioners could set the terms, summarize her words, and decide whether an answer counted as an answer. A prisoner had to explain repeatedly why she was entitled to trust what she understood to be her own experience.

Asked whether she knew herself to be in God's grace, she answered, in effect, that if she was not, God should place her there, and if she was, God should preserve her there. The answer still feels wonderfully alert. Instead of entering either of the dangerous boxes offered to her, she returned the question to prayer, to a relationship the tribunal had not entirely succeeded in occupying.

Equally striking are her requests for time, admissions of uncertainty, and refusals to disclose certain matters. Witnesses later recalled her correcting the clerks when their wording did not match what she had said. Without a scholastic education, she understood that a rewritten sentence might become another accusation. What she defended included not only her conclusions but the more elementary question of whose words were being entered in the record.

This was not a modern unbeliever confronting an undivided religious world. Joan wanted Mass and the sacraments; she sought an appeal to the pope. Clergy who had supported her belonged to the same Christian world as her judges. The fracture ran inside institutions: who might speak for them, who could interpret her faith, and who could give political hostility the appearance of a final spiritual judgment?

A question that allows only self-renunciation or guilt is no longer primarily seeking understanding. What troubles me is not merely how quick Joan could be, but how quick she had to be to preserve a little room for speech. Someone slower, more confused, or unable to stop crying would have been no less entitled to justice.

V. A garment and a prison

On 24 May, under threat of death, Joan made an abjuration. She later acknowledged her fear of the fire. Heroic narratives are often impatient with this moment, hurrying ahead to restored resolution. But fear does not need to be pardoned by a subsequent display of courage. She was nineteen. She did not want to be burned alive. That is already a complete human reason.

She put on women's clothing but was returned to a prison guarded by English men. A few days later she was again wearing male dress. The examination of 28 May records her saying she had resumed it willingly; it also records her explanation that it was more appropriate among men and that promises concerning Mass, the removal of her irons, and better conditions had not been fulfilled.

Later witnesses described the danger of sexual assault, and one account said the women's clothes had been removed, leaving the male clothing available. These accounts do not agree in every detail. They do make it impossible to understand the garment apart from the cell. Who guarded her? Could she sleep safely? Could she have women with her? The proceedings gave these questions a very different weight from the alleged return to disobedience.

She also reaffirmed the divine source of her voices and said fear of death had led her to deny them. The final condemnation therefore cannot be reduced to a clothing violation: belief, the abjuration, and a finding of relapse into heresy were involved. Yet a complete-looking procedure must still answer for the conditions in which a person spoke and recanted. Remove the fear, and the paperwork becomes remarkably tidy.

On 30 May 1431 she was burned at Rouen. Testimony remembers a cross, prayer, repeated calls to Jesus, and people weeping. Prolonging the spectacle on the page adds nothing that we need. A young person was killed. The fire did not transform the killing into an elevated ceremony arranged for the moral benefit of later generations.

A halo painted centuries afterwards could not supply what she had needed: protection, a serious hearing, and a process that did not extract a declaration under threat of death. Her convictions deserved consideration; her body deserved safety. Destroying one should never have been the price of establishing the sincerity of the other.

VI. After usefulness

Charles VII did not secure her rescue. It is hard to read past that outcome with the coronation still in view. We cannot use it to inspect every private motive at court, or turn a divided France into a single conspirator. But the distance between a welcome deliverer and an abandoned prisoner exposes something recognizable: a person's usefulness can become public knowledge much sooner than responsibility for their vulnerability.

The war continued for years. Joan neither accomplished the eventual French victory alone nor lived to see it. National memory is adept at compressing long processes into a single figure. In that compression, the help the figure required can fall outside the frame. The banner becomes sharper; the person carrying it grows hungry, frightened, or homesick somewhere beyond the edge of the picture.

Other lives in this series come to mind. Tesla's inventions required money, collaborators, and markets, but the value of his later life cannot be measured by commercial outcomes. Cantor's mathematics met with dispute; his psychological suffering cannot responsibly be reduced to one opponent's rejection. These histories have different causes. Similarities invite a question, not a single formula for suffering.

Turing's service did not protect him from criminal punishment for his private life. Yet without that service the punishment would still have been unjust. The same distinction matters for Joan. If our objection is only that someone who did so much should not have been treated that way, we still make protection conditional on an outstanding contribution. A prisoner who had saved no city deserved safety too.

The harder recognition comes when someone ceases to be useful, makes a mistake, or disappoints us. We may reject their claims and hold them accountable without canceling their fear, their body, or their wish to remain alive. Otherwise a person is simply a resource enjoying a period of favorable assessment, always waiting to be valued again.

Joan could have failed, needed help, or wanted everything to stop. Those possibilities do not diminish what she accomplished; they return her accomplishments to a living person. Courage is not a contract surrendering every possible retreat. It is certainly not a contract an admiring audience may sign on someone else's behalf.

VII. Her mother returns to court

After Joan's death, the story did not immediately belong only to nations and sacred images. Her mother was alive. Isabelle Romée knew what the name meant before it appeared on banners or judgments: a daughter she had raised, condemned and killed, with the stigma reaching the family. The public could count twenty-five years. A mother lived through years in which her child had not come home.

As political conditions changed, the condemnation came under investigation. A formal nullification process, authorized by Pope Callixtus III, opened in 1455 with Joan's mother and brothers as petitioners. On 7 November, at Notre-Dame in Paris, her mother's plea sought restoration of her daughter's name. Its starting point was not a theory of national symbolism. It was a family's refusal to accept what had been pronounced over someone they loved.

Neighbors, companions, clergy, and participants in the original trial were called to testify. Some remembered a battlefield, some a prayer, some words inaccurately recorded. Time, changed politics, and a witness's need to defend himself could all affect recollection. Even so, admitting these voices reopened what the first judgment had attempted to close: the assertion that everything necessary had already been heard.

On 7 July 1456, the condemnation was annulled. That correction mattered for Joan's reputation and for the name her family continued to carry. Belated justice remains meaningful, but it cannot demand that the injured forgive its lateness. A sentence on paper can be canceled. The years a mother lost cannot be issued again.

In 1920 the Catholic Church canonized Joan. Canonization was a different religious process from the fifteenth-century annulment. Before and afterwards, people claimed her for faith, patriotism, and political movements. Some of that attachment was sincere; some made convenient use of a figure no longer available to object. Reverence and appropriation can stand uncomfortably close together.

A small test may help when we commemorate her. Are we making room to hear her, or asking her to carry our banner? If she were alive, asked an inconvenient question, requested rest, or refused to endorse our position, would there still be a place for her? The answer matters more than the height of a statue. It brings us closer to the step her mother took back into court.

VIII. She was allowed to want to live

Socrates was about seventy when he was executed; Joan was nineteen. Put them together and the differences arrive first. One spent a long life questioning; the other entered war through faith. One reaches us through dialogues written by followers; much of the other's speech survives in the record of hostile interrogation. A resemblance need not erase these unequal circumstances.

Plato's Crito presents Socrates refusing an escape arranged by his friends, with arguments about justice and his obligations to the laws. It is a choice worth reading closely, not an examination that all later prisoners must pass. Someone unjustly condemned does not become inferior by wanting to get away and have more years of life.

Joan evaded questions, sought time, and made a retraction under threat of burning. Her faith also gave her strength to reaffirm what she believed. These movements reveal the pressure she was under. They are not flaws left for a distant audience to score. A nineteen-year-old owed posterity no perfectly straight, hesitation-free line toward death.

Both lives ask whether an institution judging a person can possess the whole of that person's understanding of themselves. The reverse question also remains: does my certainty about myself entitle me to stop everyone else's judgment? Socratic inquiry and Joan's faith do not melt into one answer. They can be taken seriously in the same space without being made to validate one another.

Kant's insistence on humanity as an end acquires a practical edge here. It is not simply a medal awarded retrospectively to exceptional people. It limits what we may do with anyone. National victory, religious reputation, even a moving piece of writing cannot make a particular life into material that we are entitled to exhaust.

We can admire Joan and disagree with some of her beliefs or actions. We can investigate her military role and admit what the documents do not enable us to know. Beneath those disagreements remains something simpler than a certificate of permanent correctness: she had a right to go on living.

IX. Leave the fire in the hearth

Now we return to this series' imagined meeting place. At the bridge in the fifth movement, many people have already sat down. Aristotle is looking at the wooden boards and discussing what makes a floor secure. When Joan approaches, what first strikes the company is her youth. No scaffold has been prepared here. No coronation is waiting for her to make it happen.

Someone moves a chair to give her room. Someone leaves water within reach instead of pressing it into her hands. She can sit, or remain standing for a while. History has assigned her so many urgent moments. This time a few minutes need not be spent immediately changing anyone's fate.

Hume closes his backgammon board. He seems about to ask about the voices; Socrates has questions too. They can wait until she wants to speak. Patience does not abolish doubt. It simply refuses to give doubt the jailer's keys. We may believe different things without locking one another inside a permanent obligation to prove ourselves.

She rests her banner beside the chair. Napoleon looks at it, and nobody asks them to rank their victories. A banner can bring people into a common movement; it can also conceal someone at the roadside. There is plenty to discuss. For the moment she need not speak for all France, or represent everyone who has ever believed they heard a call.

A few chairs remain empty farther along. One might hold her mother, another an ordinary soldier who fought on the other side. Imagination cannot erase war or responsibility. It can make room for a question: how would we speak about a war if we were slower to decide whose experience could be left out?

Kant hands her no certificate of graduation. She does not have to rewrite her faith as someone else's philosophy to remain in the company. She may pray while others do not. She may describe the voices while others continue to question. Living together will still be difficult. Fortunately, difficulty does not automatically grant one person the right to dispose of another.

There is a fire in the hearth. A piece of wood cracks softly; light catches the rim of a cup. Joan brings her hands a little closer, then draws them back, finding the distance that suits her. It is a very small freedom. Even that has not always been left to her.

Leave the fire in the hearth. It can heat water and warm a room. Tonight nobody needs to be burned to prove that they have light.
