伊本·赫勒敦：群体怎么认知自己

Ibn Khaldun: How Does a Group Know Itself?

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、堡垒里的书

1377年，北非。今天阿尔及利亚境内的伊本·萨拉马堡。

伊本·赫勒敦终于离开宫廷旋涡一阵子。他在突尼斯、非斯任过职，也为格拉纳达的统治者出使。任职、入狱、离开、重新结盟，他见过忠诚怎样变得不可靠，胜利怎样埋下下一场争斗。

这一年，他四十五岁。政治生活没有给他一本现成的答案，只给了他许多互相纠缠的问题。

1375年至1379年前后的隐居中，《历史序言》成形。按他自己的记述，1377年的初稿用了约五个月，后来仍不断修订。它是《教训之书》的导论，却逐渐拥有了独立的读者。

这本《穆卡迪玛》不满足于记下发生过什么，还追问：这些记载为什么可信？这样的事情，在这样的社会里可能发生吗？

谁打败谁之外，还有另一组问题。为什么一些群体能建立统治？共同打天下的人，为什么后来不能继续共同生活？

不是一个人凭空发明了全部社会科学，而是一位从政治与学术传统里走来的人，试着为历史叙述添上一套检验和解释的办法。

二、阿萨比亚

其中一个重要的词是阿萨比亚，asabiyyah。

常译作群体凝聚力、团结感。它不只是“我们是谁”的观念，还关乎保护、互助和共同行动的能力。亲缘很重要，联盟与依附也能形成类似的纽带。

在本系列里，我们借它把镜头从“我”移向“我们”。这是本文的阅读路线，不是宣布赫勒敦首先发现了集体认知。

孔德的社会、波普尔的批评共同体、薇依对他人的注意，都已经不止一个人。现在，我们把几个人之间的联系放到画面中央。

一群人怎样知道，自己不只是偶然站在一起？

遇到危险，谁会来？分到资源，谁不会被忘记？需要承担代价时，谁愿意留下？“我们”并不只活在一句自我介绍里，也活在这些可以被反复验证的关系里。

待过有凝聚力的群体，就会懂得那种温度。但温度也有边界：对内团结的群体，可能对外残酷。凝聚力说明力量怎样形成，不替力量保证善。

三、三代的模型

赫勒敦描画过一种代际变化。

第一代建立者，熟悉艰苦、互相依靠，也共同争夺权力。起初分享的荣誉和风险，使人愿意一起行动。不能只看他们的节俭，也要看到征服与暴力。

第二代进入更安定、更富足的生活。他们与建立者有直接接触，还保留一些旧习惯；但荣誉开始被少数人独占，共同行动渐渐让位于服从。

到了第三代，在他的模型里，奢华与依赖加深，旧日的互助能力变弱。统治者转而寻找新的追随者和力量支撑自己。

这时，另一股更能共同行动的力量可能出现。循环的威胁，不只来自道德变坏，也来自权力怎样重新分配关系。

他以一代约四十年，谈到三代约一百二十年；也谈到没有挑战者时，衰老的王朝仍可能延续。这是带条件的历史概括，不是一到年份就会响的钟。

“富不过三代”让中文读者觉得熟悉，却不是验证。罗马、奥斯曼等漫长历史不能被塞进同一张年表。值得带走的是问题：成功以后，维系共同生活的能力有没有被成功本身耗掉？

四、共同经历留下什么

如果借一个今天的比喻，群体的记忆像一种有损压缩。

许多事情不再被逐件复述，却沉淀成一句“我们一起走过”。这不是阿萨比亚的原典定义，而是理解共同经验的一条旁路。

个人也会把长久经验带进一瞬间的判断。麦克林托克的熟悉感不能只化成公式，薇依的注意也不能化成一个信息百分比。经验留下的东西，多于说出口的摘要。

迁徙、饥饿、庆祝、相互救助，在群体里也会留下习惯。某个人迟到，大家会不会等；某个人倒下，是否有人回头。这些动作，让“我们”有了分量。

后来的人可以听故事、学习制度，并不一定非要重受一次苦才懂得团结。但如果只剩纪念词，再没有互信的行动，继承就会变成空壳。共同生活需要不断添进今天的经验。

撒切尔之后的政策延续可以作为另一种类比：一个建立过的框架怎样变成默认背景？但布莱尔不是阿萨比亚的“第二代”实验证据。英国政治与中世纪王朝之间，仍隔着制度和历史的差别。

五、他和孔德

赫勒敦的《历史序言》，比孔德开展社会学计划早了数百年。

孔德使社会学这一名称进入自己的体系。赫勒敦所说的，则是研究人类社会生活的学问，ilm al-umran。可以把他视为重要先驱，不必假装两个时代已经在做同一门现代学科。

两人都试着在零散事件里找秩序。差别不是一人只有理论，另一人只有眼睛。

孔德的阶段图式有把历史排得太整齐的风险；但他也知道观察需要理论引导。不能用一句“从来不看事实”替他结案。

赫勒敦的政治经历提供了材料。他也读前人的书、比较记载，用关于社会生活的判断检验传闻。一支军队有那么多人，它吃什么、怎样行军？数字不能因为写在古书里就免于追问。

这是一种有自觉的方法，不是毫无理论地看一看，再如实抄下来。他有自己的概括，也带着时代的局限；同样需要后来读者检验。

把两人放在一起，问题因此更有用：理论让我们看见什么，又什么时候开始替我们挡住不合图式的事实？

六、从我到我们

本篇把镜头移向关系，而不是把个人从画面里抹掉。

“我们”由人组成，却不能只靠人数说明。共同承诺、习惯和相互依靠，会形成一个人独自拥有不了的行动能力。

这里可以借费希特追问：一个群体怎样说出“我们”？但阿萨比亚不是放大版的自我设定。群体也不是一个长着许多身体的巨人，可以替每个人把话说完。

时间让这个问题变得具体。建立、继承、遗忘，并非只能按三代发生，却都提醒人：曾经有过的联系，不保证永远在场。

伍尔夫写过往怎样进入当下，可以与这里的群体记忆对读。文学的意识经验与历史社会的机制不同，但都让我们发现：眼前这一刻，带着看不见的来路。

下一篇维果茨基还会把镜头推近：共同拥有的语言和做法，怎样经由一次谈话、一份帮助，进入一个孩子正在长成的能力？

七、城墙下的帖木儿

1401年，大马士革。六十八岁的赫勒敦被绳索从城墙上缒下，去见帖木儿。

城池被围，苏丹已经离开。求见不是一场安全的学术参访。他要顾及自己和城中同行的安全，也在面对一个改变地域政治的人。

接下来的会面中，帖木儿询问北非，赫勒敦提供地理与政治知识。他在自传中留下这段经历；谈话里的赞语也须放回征服者面前的处境，不能径直当成无保留的认同。

写文明兴衰的人，面对一位制造征服的人。这里有可以观察的权力，也有无法控制的危险。大马士革后来遭到洗劫，不能简单写成“城里人不够团结，所以活该失败”。

绳索向下放时，理论并没有保护他站在历史之外。他也在那场历史里，试图寻找一条能活着走回去的路。

八、桥头

在这座想象的桥上，赫勒敦带着沙漠的气息走来。

那并非他的全部来历。他出身城市的学者家庭，在突尼斯受教育，往来于宫廷。沙漠也不是天然善良的故乡，只是他书里一条重要的视线。

他走上桥，先看看人们之间的距离。

孔德在分类，波普尔在追问，费希特谈自我，薇依听别人，默多克刚从鸟的方向收回目光。每个人都有自己的事情。

他想知道的还不止这些：桥若摇晃，谁会伸手？有人落在后面，谁会发现？

光看一眼，还不知道。

争论不必证明没有“我们”，同声附和也不一定就是团结。有些联系要到行动中才看得出来。

他见过聪明人同处一座宫廷，却无法互相信任。也见过共同进退的力量如何成为统治。力量值得解释，不必因此受到赞美。

他站到桥边，望向我们替这个场景画出的远方。那里有一群人在走。

他们走了很远，很渴。有人放慢脚步，等一等另一个人。他们知道彼此的名字，也知道不能只把“我们”留在嘴上。

这让阿萨比亚不再只是一个陌生的词。

它有一部分，留在一起走过的脚印里；还有一部分，要看下一步愿不愿意继续等人。[1][2][3]

Ibn Khaldun — The Muqaddimah, translated by Franz Rosenthal · The Muqaddimah — online contents

I. The Book in the Fortress

North Africa, 1377. Qalʿat Ibn Salama, in what is now Algeria.

Ibn Khaldun has stepped away from court politics for a while. He served in Tunis and Fez and undertook a diplomatic mission for Granada’s ruler. Office, imprisonment, departure, renewed alliances: he had seen loyalty become unreliable and victory prepare the next struggle.

He is forty-five. Political life has given him no ready-made answer, only questions entangled with one another.

During his retreat around 1375–1379, the Muqaddimah takes shape. He reports completing its initial draft in about five months in 1377, with revisions continuing afterward. An introduction to his larger Kitab al-Ibar, it would find readers of its own.

The book asks more than what happened. Why should we believe a report? Could that event have happened under those social conditions?

Beyond who defeated whom lie further questions. How do groups acquire the capacity to rule? Why do those who won power together cease to be able to live together?

This is not one man inventing all social science from nothing. It is a thinker working through political experience and inherited scholarship to develop ways of testing and explaining historical accounts.

II. Asabiyyah

One important word is asabiyyah.

Often rendered as group feeling or solidarity, it concerns more than an idea of who we are. It includes capacities for protection, mutual assistance and concerted action. Kinship matters, but alliances and attachments can produce comparable bonds.

In this series, we use it to move the camera from I toward we. That is our reading path, not a claim that Ibn Khaldun first discovered collective cognition.

Comte’s society, Popper’s community of criticism and Weil’s attention to others already exceed a solitary person. Here, the relations between people move into the foreground.

How does a gathering know it is more than people who happened to stand together?

Who comes when danger arrives? Who is remembered when resources are shared? Who stays when there is a cost? We exists not only in a description but in relationships that these occasions repeatedly put to the test.

Anyone who has belonged to a close group knows its warmth. But warmth has a boundary: solidarity within can accompany cruelty without. Cohesion helps explain how power forms; it does not certify that power as good.

III. A Model of Three Generations

Ibn Khaldun describes a pattern of generational change.

Founders know hardship and mutual dependence, as well as competition for power. Shared risks and honor enable common action. Their austerity is only part of the story; conquest and violence belong to it too.

The next generation enters greater security and abundance. It has direct contact with the founders and retains some older habits. But honor becomes concentrated, while obedience begins to replace participation.

In his model, the third generation grows more dependent on luxury and established rule. Older capacities for mutual defense weaken. The ruler seeks fresh clients and other forces to sustain the dynasty.

Another power better able to act together may then emerge. The threat comes not merely from moral decline but from the ways successful rule rearranges relationships.

He reckons a generation at roughly forty years and three at about 120. He also allows an aging dynasty to persist without a challenger. This is a conditional historical generalization, not a clock guaranteed to ring on schedule.

The Chinese saying about wealth lasting no more than three generations sounds familiar, but familiarity is not verification. Roman and Ottoman histories cannot be squeezed into one timetable. Keep the question instead: can success consume the capacities that made shared life possible?

IV. What Shared Experience Leaves Behind

Borrowing a present-day image, collective memory resembles lossy compression.

Many events cease to be recounted separately yet settle into a sense that we went through something together. This is our analogy, not the original definition of asabiyyah.

Individuals, too, bring long experience into brief judgments. McClintock’s familiarity cannot be reduced to a formula, nor Weil’s attention to an information percentage. What experience leaves exceeds the summary we can give.

Migration, hunger, celebration and assistance leave group habits. Does everyone wait for the late arrival? Does someone turn back when another falls? These actions give we its weight.

Later generations can learn through stories and institutions; they need not suffer everything again to understand solidarity. But commemorative language without acts of trust becomes a hollow inheritance. A common life needs the addition of present experience.

Political continuity after Thatcher offers another analogy: how does an established framework become the assumed background? Blair is not experimental evidence for a second generation of asabiyyah. British politics and medieval dynasties remain separated by institutions and history.

V. Beside Comte

The Muqaddimah precedes Comte’s sociological project by several centuries.

Comte gave sociology a name and place in his system. Ibn Khaldun spoke of ilm al-umran, a study of human social life. Calling him an important precursor need not pretend both men already practiced the same modern discipline.

Both searched for order among scattered events. The difference is not that one had only a theory and the other only eyes.

Comte’s stages risk arranging history too neatly. Yet he also understood that observation needs theoretical guidance. “He never looked at facts” is not an adequate verdict.

Ibn Khaldun’s political career supplied material. So did earlier books and competing reports, which he tested against social possibilities. If an army was that large, what fed it, and how could it march? A number in an old book is not beyond questioning.

This was a deliberate method, not theory-free looking followed by faithful transcription. He made generalizations and carried the limits of his age. Later readers must examine him as well.

Together the two give us a more useful question: what does a theory enable us to see, and when does it start concealing the facts that fail to fit?

VI. From I to We

The camera now attends to relations without removing individuals from the picture.

We is made of people, but numbers alone do not explain it. Commitments, habits and mutual reliance create capacities for action that no one possesses alone.

Fichte can prompt the question of how a group says we. But asabiyyah is not self-positing enlarged. Nor is a group a giant with many bodies, entitled to finish every member’s sentences.

Time makes the question concrete. Founding, inheriting and forgetting need not occur on a three-generation schedule. All remind us that a relation which once existed is not guaranteed to stay.

Woolf’s writing about the past entering the present can illuminate collective memory. Literary consciousness and historical mechanisms differ, but both let us notice the invisible journey inside the present moment.

Vygotsky, next, will bring the camera closer. How do shared languages and practices enter a child’s growing capacities through a conversation or a moment of help?

VII. Timur Below the Wall

Damascus, 1401. Sixty-eight-year-old Ibn Khaldun is lowered by ropes over the wall to meet Timur.

The city is besieged; the sultan has departed. This is no safe academic visit. Ibn Khaldun has his own and his colleagues’ safety to consider while confronting someone who is remaking regional power.

During their meetings, Timur asks about North Africa. Ibn Khaldun supplies geographical and political knowledge, later recording the encounter in his autobiography. Praise spoken before a conqueror must be read within that predicament, not automatically as unqualified approval.

A writer on civilizations’ fortunes faces a maker of conquests. Power can be observed here, but danger cannot be controlled. Damascus would be sacked; that cannot be reduced to a moral tale in which insufficiently united residents deserved defeat.

As the rope descends, his theory gives him no place outside history. He is inside it too, trying to find a way to return alive.

VIII. At the Bridge

On this imagined bridge, Ibn Khaldun arrives with something of the desert about him.

It is not his whole background. He came from an urban scholarly family, was educated in Tunis and moved among courts. Nor is the desert a naturally virtuous homeland. It is one important line of sight in his writing.

He steps onto the bridge and first notices the distances between people.

Comte classifies, Popper questions, Fichte discusses the self. Weil listens; Murdoch has just turned back from the bird. Each has something to do.

He wants to know something further. If the bridge shakes, who will offer a hand? If someone falls behind, who will notice?

One glance cannot tell him.

Argument need not mean there is no we; agreement need not establish solidarity. Some bonds become visible only in action.

He has seen clever people share a court without trusting one another. He has also seen concerted action become domination. Power deserves explanation, not necessarily admiration.

He stands at the edge and looks into the distance we have drawn for this scene. A group is walking there.

They have come a long way and are thirsty. Someone slows to wait for another. They know each other’s names and know that we cannot remain only a word.

Asabiyyah becomes less alien.

Part of it lies in the footprints they have left together. Another part depends on whether, at the next step, they will still wait.[1][2][3]
