默多克：看见就是道德

Murdoch: Seeing Is Morality

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、红隼

默多克请我们想象一个窗前的瞬间。

一个人正在生闷气。也许刚受了轻慢，也许自尊被碰了一下。事情在脑子里反复播放，越想越委屈。窗外一直有世界，但此刻几乎看不见。

然后，一只红隼出现了。

它悬停在窗外的空中，在风里寻找自己的平衡。它不是为安慰谁而来的，也不知道窗内那个人遇到了什么。

注意力忽然转了过去。烦恼没有被解决，再想起时，却没那么大了。不是痛苦变成了假的，而是它暂时不再占据整个世界。

这个片刻给默多克提供了一个道德生活的入口。

我们常把道德想成最后那一下：选哪条路，遵守哪条规则。她把目光往前移。在决定之前，你已怎样看待别人？哪些细节被你看到了，哪些被你的故事挡住了？

二、婆婆

她还有一个更日常的思想实验。

婆婆M不喜欢儿媳D。她觉得D没有教养，太随便，举止不够庄重。连口音和衣着都碍眼。她觉得儿子娶低了。

不过，M在行为上很得体。她客气、克制，不把敌意流露出来。只看举止，很难挑出毛病。

为了把问题看清，默多克甚至让我们设想：D已经远走，或者已经去世。这样，M后来的变化不能简单算成一次新的相处行为。这里是思想实验的变式，不是必须发生的一段情节。

M开始怀疑自己的眼光。也许自己太势利，太想把儿子留在身边。她原先叫作粗俗的东西，会不会是率真？叫作不庄重的东西，会不会是活泼？这不是给D换一套好听的标签，而是肯花力气重新辨认。

她的外在举止未变。但那个被她称为D的人，不再只是她原先想象中的D。

这个变化，算不算道德上的变化？

如果只把道德放在可见的行动和选择上，就很难说明M做了什么。默多克挑战的是这种过窄的图景，不是说所有分析哲学家或存在主义者都会给出同一个答案。

一个人愿意更公正、更有爱地看待另一个人，本身就在做道德工作。行动仍然重要；只是行动到来以前，眼光已经在为它准备道路。

三、去我

默多克把某些这样的经验叫作unselfing，去我。

她警惕那个不断膨胀、不断要求被照顾的自我。它并不总是大声发号施令，更多时候只是悄悄替一切加上解释。

你觉得一个人无聊，也许因为他不奉承你；觉得一个人不懂规矩，也许因为她没有按你的规矩生活。偏见、恐惧和自我保护像一层滤镜，你以为自己在看对方，其实不断遇见自己的需要。

去我不是把人消灭，而是让这层滤镜松动。

红隼使那段围绕自尊的独白暂停。看鸟的人没有消失，只是不再要求鸟来证明自己的重要。世界不必围着我转，反而终于有东西可以看见。

这里会想起伍尔夫笔下棉絮裂开的瞬间：熟悉的生活忽然露出未被留意的纹理。两者可以互相照亮，却不是同一个心理事件，更不是每个清醒的瞬间都自动使人善良。

薇依的影响则是默多克自己承认的。她从薇依那里接过注意力的问题，但不是替薇依把一个原本没有道德意义的概念变成道德。薇依也始终关心怎样听见受苦的人。

宗教、爱、他人，贯穿薇依的注意力；默多克在自己的柏拉图主义里重新安放这些问题。

可以从祈祷读到日常的凝视，但不要把一人留给上帝，另一人留给邻居，好像她们互不相通。

我们站在婆婆和儿媳之间，看这条思想怎样落到一个小问题上：我愿不愿意承认，眼前的人可能不是我已经讲好的那个故事？

四、善的主权

她的书叫《善的主权》。

“主权”在这里不是让某个声音替人发号施令。它先打破一种自满：不能因为这是我的选择，就宣布它已经是善。人的欲望不是衡量一切的最后尺度。

默多克批评把孤立意志放在中心的道德图景。选择并未被取消，只是不再凭空发生。平日看见什么、忽略什么，会决定到了关键时刻，我们甚至能不能看见另一种选择。

柏拉图的洞穴提供了一幅图像：转身，走向光，逐渐辨认原先看不清的东西。太阳不是一件握在手里的奖品。借到默多克这里，转身也可以发生在M对D的重新注视中。

本系列曾担心柏拉图的理念会不会把世界封住。默多克让我们从另一条路回去：她并未丢掉善的超越性，却把趋近它的工夫放进窗前、艺术和人与人的相处。善不是把别人安置在我的完美图纸里。

五、她是小说家

默多克还写了二十六部小说。

哲学家与小说家并非互斥的身份。但这两种写作放在她身上，显出一种耐心：提出一个关于人的观念，还要让具体的人来打乱它。

好的小说可以成为练习注意力的地方。

人物做了一件让你不耐烦的事。继续读，才看见他的羞耻、误会和不肯说的愿望。理解不是免除责任，而是让判词不那么轻易替一个人过完一生。

小说也可能只喂养我们的幻想。读得多，不保证看得公正。那项练习是否发生，要看我们肯不肯让人物超出自己预先安排的角色。

想到孔德与波普尔，可以追问知识的范围，但不能说他们的框架已证明小说没有认识价值。科学的划界不是对一切意义的判决；一个人物也不是等待实验推翻的假说。

默多克让我们在阅读中练习一件难事：别人不是我的理论的例子。书合上之后，这件事还要回到生活里继续做。

六、她和阿伦特

上一篇阿伦特让我们问：命令和套话有没有替我判断？默多克把问题往日常里推了一步：即使我一直在想，我有没有真正留意别人？

艾希曼的责任不能被写成里面空无一人。他的行为、信念和自我辩护都要受检验。阿伦特提出的问题，不是一张把人分成思考者与空壳的表。

M的情形当然不是同一种罪。她有一整套关于D的看法，甚至觉得自己已经很克制。问题恰恰是这套看法太周全，周全到D本人没有多少地方可以进来。

可以想象两种房间：一间把判断的工作推到门外，另一间堆满自己的投影。这是帮助我们提问的比喻，不是两个哲学体系的完整定义，也不代表两个案例的严重程度相同。

不要把判断交出去，也不要把自己的判断误认成对方本人。两个要求放在一起，才能让心里的房间有一扇朝外的窗。

七、遗忘

1997年，默多克被诊断出阿尔茨海默病。

记忆和语言越来越难以依靠。熟悉的生活需要更多帮助。一个长期用文字辨认人的人，开始经历难以用文字说明的困难。

丈夫约翰·贝利在《Iris》等回忆文字里记录了他们最后几年的生活。那是一个亲近者的记述，不是能从外面穷尽她内心的镜头。

不能因此说她不再看见任何人，或者那个“我”已经蒸发了。我们不知道另一个人的所有经验，尤其不能把他说不清，当成他已经不在。

伍尔夫的结局也不该被拉来排成一张认知崩溃的对称图。疾病和痛苦属于具体的生命，不是为了让哲学篇章收束得漂亮。

此刻，问题转到照护者和读者身上：当一个人不再能以熟悉的方式回应，你还看不看见她？她不必继续写书，才值得被耐心对待。

二十六部小说还在，《善的主权》还在，M和D、窗前的红隼也还在。但即使这些都没有留下，一个人的价值也不需要靠作品来续期。

八、桥头

在这座想象的桥上，默多克拿着一本小说走来。不是自己写的，是别人的。她还在读。

她抬头，看见桥，也看见桥上的人。

她没有先找位置，先看人。

孔德在那里。她没有急着把他收进“实证主义者”的格子。认真和固执有时很像，她想多看一会儿，不替他断定固执背后一定藏着什么。

她看薇依。也许先问她冷不冷，需不需要坐下，而不是把她的身体变成去我的寓言。听一个人说话，不等于赞美她承受的一切伤害。

阿伦特还在写。默多克在旁边停了一下。一个追问判断怎样失守，一个追问眼光怎样变窄；两人都不只属于光或影。桥可以让她们继续谈。

天上有东西掠过。一只鸟。不必是红隼。

默多克暂时停下，看着它。

哲学家、论争和自己的读书心事退到后面。不是那些事不重要，而是此刻还有一只不为这些事而飞的鸟。

鸟飞远了。她把小说夹在腋下，在桥上站定。

她不知道同一只鸟会不会回来。也不能保证下一次，自己就一定能从心事里抬起头。

但可以继续看。也继续容许眼前的人和世界，打断自己。[1][2][3]

I. The Kestrel

Murdoch asks us to imagine a moment at a window.

Someone is brooding. Perhaps there has been a slight, perhaps an injury to pride. The scene keeps replaying, the grievance growing. There has been a world outside the window all along, but scarcely any of it registers.

Then a kestrel appears.

It hovers in the air, finding its balance in the wind. It has not come to console anyone. It knows nothing about the trouble indoors.

Attention moves outward. The trouble has not been solved, but when it returns it seems less large. The pain has not become unreal. It has briefly stopped occupying the whole world.

For Murdoch, this gives us an entrance into moral life.

We often imagine morality as the final moment: which path to choose, which rule to obey. She looks further back. Before deciding, how have you been seeing the other person? Which details have registered, and which has your story kept out?

II. The Mother-in-Law

Her other famous example is closer to the dinner table.

A mother, M, dislikes her daughter-in-law, D. D seems unpolished, too familiar, insufficiently dignified. Even her accent and clothes offend. M feels that her son has married beneath him.

Nevertheless, M behaves impeccably. She is courteous and restrained, never displaying her hostility. Look only at conduct and there seems little to criticize.

To isolate the question, Murdoch even invites us to suppose that D is far away or dead. M’s later change cannot then be reduced to a new interaction. These are variations in a thought experiment, not obligatory episodes in a story.

M begins to distrust her own gaze. Perhaps she is snobbish, perhaps too possessive of her son. Could what she called vulgarity be spontaneity, what she called undignified behavior be liveliness? The work is not to exchange hostile labels for flattering ones. It is to look again, seriously.

Her outward behavior has not changed. But D is no longer confined to the D she had already imagined.

Has anything morally significant happened?

A picture that locates morality solely in observable acts and choices struggles to say what M has done. Murdoch challenges that narrow picture, not every analytic philosopher or existentialist as if they all gave the same answer.

Trying to see someone more justly and lovingly is itself moral work. Conduct still matters. But before an action arrives, a way of seeing has already been preparing its path.

III. Unselfing

Murdoch calls certain experiences of this kind unselfing.

She distrusts the ego that keeps expanding and demanding consideration. It need not issue loud commands. More often it quietly supplies the explanation for everything.

Someone seems dull because he does not flatter you; someone seems ill-mannered because she does not live by your manners. Prejudice, fear and self-protection become a filter. You think you are meeting the person, while repeatedly meeting your own needs.

Unselfing loosens that filter. It does not abolish the person looking.

The kestrel interrupts the monologue about injured pride. The observer remains, but no longer requires the bird to certify the observer’s importance. Once the world need not revolve around me, there is finally something else to notice.

We might recall Woolf’s moments when the cotton wool of ordinary life tears and unnoticed textures appear. The scenes illuminate one another without describing an identical mental event. Nor does every vivid moment automatically make someone good.

Weil’s influence is one Murdoch herself acknowledged. She took up Weil’s account of attention; she did not give moral significance to a concept that had previously lacked it. Weil, too, cared about hearing the person who suffers.

Religion, love and other people run through Weil’s attention. Murdoch repositions these concerns within her own Platonism.

We can move from prayer to an everyday gaze without assigning God to one woman and the neighbor to the other, as if neither crossed that border.

Between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law, the question becomes small and difficult: will I allow that the person here may not be the story I have already finished telling?

IV. The Sovereignty of Good

Her book is called The Sovereignty of Good.

Sovereignty here does not authorize a voice to order people about. It first unsettles a complacency: choosing something cannot by itself make it good. My desire is not the final measure of everything.

Murdoch challenges a moral picture centered on an isolated will. Choice remains, but it does not happen out of nowhere. What we habitually notice or ignore helps determine whether, at the decisive moment, we can even perceive an alternative.

Plato’s cave offers an image: turn, move toward the light, learn to distinguish what could not be seen. The sun is not a trophy held in the hand. Read through Murdoch, a turn may also happen in M’s renewed attention to D.

Earlier in this series we worried that Plato’s Forms might close the world. Murdoch offers another route back. She does not abandon the Good’s transcendence, yet places the work of approaching it at a window, in art, between people. Goodness does not consist in fitting others into my perfect drawing.

V. The Novelist

Murdoch also wrote twenty-six novels.

Philosopher and novelist are not mutually exclusive identities. In her, however, their conjunction reveals a particular patience: after proposing an idea about people, let actual particularity disturb it.

A good novel can be a place to practice attention.

A character does something that irritates you. Reading on, you encounter shame, misunderstanding, an unspoken wish. Understanding does not remove responsibility. It prevents a verdict from too easily living a whole life in the person’s place.

Fiction can also feed our fantasies. Reading many books guarantees no justness of vision. The exercise happens only if we allow a character to exceed the part assigned in advance.

Comte and Popper invite questions about the scope of knowledge, but their frameworks do not simply establish that fiction has no cognitive value. Demarcating science is not judging all meaning; a character is not an experimental hypothesis awaiting refutation.

Murdoch gives the reader a difficult practice: another person is not an illustration of my theory. After the book closes, the practice must continue in life.

VI. Beside Arendt

Arendt, in the previous essay, asked whether orders and clichés were doing our judging. Murdoch brings the question into ordinary encounters: even when I am thinking constantly, am I attending to anyone else?

Eichmann’s responsibility cannot be described as an empty interior. His actions, convictions and self-defense require examination. Arendt’s question is not a chart dividing humanity into thinkers and vacant shells.

M’s case is plainly not an equivalent crime. She has an elaborate account of D and thinks herself remarkably restrained. The difficulty is precisely that her account is so complete that D herself has little room to enter it.

Imagine two rooms: one sends the work of judgment outside; the other is crowded with projections. This can help us ask questions. It is neither a complete definition of two philosophies nor an equation of the cases’ gravity.

Do not surrender judgment; do not mistake your judgment for the person judged. Together, these demands give the room within us a window facing outward.

VII. Forgetting

In 1997, Murdoch was diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease.

Memory and language became harder to rely on. Familiar life required increasing assistance. Someone who had spent so long finding words for people was encountering difficulties that words could not easily convey.

John Bayley recorded their last years in Iris and other memoirs. His was the testimony of someone close to her, not a camera capable of exhausting her experience from outside.

We cannot conclude that she could no longer see anyone, or that her self had evaporated. Another person’s experience is never entirely available to us. An inability to explain it does not mean the person is absent.

Nor should Woolf’s ending be arranged beside hers as a symmetrical diagram of cognitive collapse. Illness and suffering belong to particular lives, not to an essay’s need for an elegant conclusion.

The question now turns toward carers and readers. When someone can no longer respond in familiar ways, do you still see her? She need not keep producing books to deserve patient company.

The twenty-six novels remain, as do The Sovereignty of Good, M and D, and the kestrel. But even without those works, a person’s worth would not require a literary legacy to renew it.

VIII. At the Bridge

On this imagined bridge, Murdoch arrives carrying a novel. Not her own: someone else’s. She is still reading.

She looks up and sees the bridge and its people.

Before finding a place, she looks at them.

Comte is there. She does not immediately file him under positivist. Earnestness can resemble obstinacy. She wants to look a little longer, without deciding what anxiety must be hidden underneath.

She looks at Weil. Perhaps she first asks whether she is cold, whether she needs to sit down, rather than turning her body into an allegory of unselfing. Listening to someone does not require celebrating everything that hurt her.

Arendt is still writing. Murdoch pauses beside her. One asks how judgment gives way, the other how vision narrows; neither belongs exclusively to light or shadow. The bridge can let their conversation continue.

Something passes overhead. A bird. It need not be a kestrel.

Murdoch stops for a moment to watch.

The philosophers, arguments and her own preoccupations recede. Not because they do not matter, but because a bird is also here, flying for none of their reasons.

It flies away. She tucks the novel under her arm and takes her place.

She does not know whether that bird will return. Nor can she guarantee that next time she will look up from her concerns.

But she can keep looking. And keep allowing people and the world to interrupt her.[1][2][3]
