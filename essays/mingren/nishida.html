西田几多郎，无の场所

Nishida Kitarō, The Place of Nothingness

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、在"有"之前

西方哲学有一条很长的主线，从一个字开始：有（Being）。

巴门尼德说：有是有的，无是没有的。你只能思考"有"。"无"不可思考——因为你思考"无"的时候，"无"已经变成了你思考的对象，变成了某种"有"。

此后两千多年，从柏拉图的理念、亚里士多德的实体，到笛卡尔的"我思故我在"与黑格尔的辩证法，关于存在的问题不断改写。这并不是西方哲学的全部，却是一条极强的主线。

西田几多郎说：你们漏了一个东西。

在"有"之前，还有一个更根本的东西。他叫它"无"。不是"什么都没有"的无。是让"有"成为可能的无。就像舞台不是演员，但没有舞台演员就没地方演。"无"是舞台。"有"是演员。

西田让读者暂时把视线从演员移到舞台。这个比喻不是他对全部西方哲学的判决，而是进入他自己问题的一扇门。

二、善的研究

1911年。西田几多郎四十一岁。他出版了《善の研究》（《善的研究》）。

这是现代日本哲学的标志性著作。明治以来，西周等人已经在翻译、创造术语并思考西方哲学；日本更早也有自己的思想传统。西田的重要，不是凭一己之力从零开始，而是写出一套后来能够进入世界哲学讨论的原创体系。

《善的研究》的核心概念：纯粹经验（純粋経験）。

什么是纯粹经验？在你把世界分成"主体"和"客体"之前——在你说"我在看花"之前——有一个更原始的时刻：看。不是"我"在看"花"。只是看。还没有分出"谁在看"和"看的是什么"。主客未分。

你弹钢琴弹到忘我的时候——没有"我"在弹"钢琴"。只有弹。运动员说的"zone"，禅宗说的"三昧"，都是这个意思。主体和客体还没分开。经验是纯粹的。

西田说，纯粹经验并非还没学会思考的低级状态，也不是简单地高于一切反省。它在主客划分之前；反省从那里发生，又会回头解释它。切出"我"和"世界"以后，我们获得判断，也失去了一部分尚未分开的整体。

William James也讲"纯粹经验"。James的激进经验论本来就不只是心理学；西田受其启发，却把问题带向自己的宗教与本体论方向。两个人不能被压成一个提供材料、一个完成升级的关系。

三、他凿了什么

西田凿了什么？

他凿了一个假设：只有西方的方式才叫哲学。

明治维新以后，日本知识分子面对一个巨大的压力：西方的一切都是先进的——科学，技术，制度，包括哲学。日本自己的思想传统——禅，儒，神道——被当成"前现代的"，不是"真正的哲学"。你要做哲学，你得用西方的概念，西方的逻辑，西方的语言。

西田做了一件更复杂的事：他使用当时大学哲学的概念、逻辑和体系，也让禅与日本思想改变这些工具所能提出的问题。他不是替一个固定的"东方"向一个固定的"西方"递交证明；他在两边都仍会变化的接触处工作。

那个东西就是"无"。

苏格拉底凿假知识——用对话。 马克思凿假自然——用分析。 伽利略凿假宇宙——用望远镜。 西田凿假边界——"哲学只能是西方的"——用西方自己的工具。

用你的剑斩你的锁。这是西田最精彩的地方。

四、绝对无の场所

《善的研究》之后，西田继续深入。他发展出了一个更成熟的概念：场所（場所 / basho）。

世界上的一切"有"——这棵树，这个人，这个想法——都需要一个场所来存在。树在森林里。人在社会里。想法在意识里。但意识在哪里？意识在一个更大的场所里。那个更大的场所在哪里？在一个更更大的场所里。

你一直追问下去，最后到达的是：绝对无の場所。一个不是任何"有"的场所——它自己什么都不是，但一切都在它里面发生。

这不是虚无主义。"绝对无"不是"什么都没有"。它是所有"有"的条件。就像画布——画布上什么图案都没有的时候，它不是"空的画布"。它是"让一切图画成为可能的画布"。

用这个系列的语言，可以把绝对无想成一种不让任何既成结构占满全场的场所。这是本文的对读，不是把西田的术语直接换算成"余项"。

构是我们已经说清、已经建立的东西；余项是仍未被它妥善纳入的部分。西田提醒我们的，是连"里面"和"外面"这条边界也可能需要追问。绝对无不是边界外另放着的一只容器。

康德为认识划定限度；西田则从主客尚未分开的经验追问限度怎样形成。弹琴忘我可以帮助我们接近"纯粹经验"的直觉，却不能据此断言，每次沉浸都已经等同于西田后期所说的绝对无。概念之间还有一段漫长的发展。

五、他和慧能

慧能说"本来无一物"。

西田说"绝对无の場所"。

两个"无"放在一起会发出回声，却不是同一概念的体验版与理论版。

慧能传统中的"本来无一物"出现在偈语、修行与传承的具体语境里；它并不意味着禅只要一次体验、从此不需语言或实践。顿悟也没有取消此后的生活。

西田的"无"经过纯粹经验、场所逻辑与宗教哲学的长期变化。他确实借用佛教思想资源，也与康德、黑格尔和詹姆斯交锋；绝对无不是把慧能的一次体验装进西方术语。

这是一个翻译的行为。不是语言的翻译——是思维方式的翻译。把一种只能体验的东西变成一种可以讨论的东西。

这种翻译当然会有丢失。禅宗也有经论、问答和解释传统，却警惕语言被误当成抵达本身。概念能使讨论继续，也可能把活的修行压成一张术语表。

翻译同时有所创造："无"进入现代大学哲学的论证，欧洲哲学的范畴也被迫在日本语境中改变。这里不是西田替整个东方取得哲学资格，而是一个思想家让既有资格标准本身受到追问。

慧能与西田之间不是一扇门和一把锁，更像两条在某处相遇、又各自继续的路。把距离留下，比较才不会变成吞并。

六、他和康德

康德画了一条线：物自体在线那边，你过不去。

西田画了一个场所：你以为你过不去，其实你一直在那里面。你不是在线的"这边"试图看"那边"。你在一个场所里，那个场所包含了线的两边。

康德从认识的条件区分现象与物自身；把他的全部框架画成主体在此岸、物自身在彼岸，只是一幅便于入门的草图。

西田说：主客二分本身就是后来的。在主客分开之前，你就已经在那个场所里了。纯粹经验就是主客未分的状态。你不是从"这边"去够"那边"——你在一个比主客二分更根本的地方。

这跟康德的区别是什么？

康德说：你的认知框架限制了你。你只能看到现象，看不到物自体。 西田说：你的认知框架（主客二分）确实限制了你。但在这个框架"之下"，有一个更原始的经验层。在那个层面上，限制还没有发生。

可以说，康德提醒我们墙不能被认识轻易穿透；西田追问墙与两侧怎样一同出现。两人并不是在同一张地图上争论一堵墙该不该拆。

七、他和维特根斯坦

维特根斯坦说："凡是不能说的，就必须沉默。"

西田没有留下这样一句对维特根斯坦的直接回答。本文把两人并置：不能用主客二分的语言说的，是否能由另一种哲学语法接近？

维特根斯坦的Tractatus画了一条语言的边界——边界之内是可以说清楚的，边界之外是必须沉默的。伦理，美学，生命的意义——都在边界之外。

从西田的工作出发，可以追问：所谓"不能说"是否也与所用的语言有关？场所与纯粹经验不是边界外事物的说明书，却让我们重新观察说话者、对象与语言如何一起形成。

维特根斯坦后来在Investigations里也发现了语言的多样性——语言不只是描述事实，语言是游戏，有很多种用法。但他没有走到西田的位置——他没有说"在主客二分之前有一个更根本的层面"。

维特根斯坦发现了语言的多种用法。 西田发现了语言之前的东西。

一个拓宽了语言的用法；一个试图把哲学带到语言与反省成形之前。两条路都没有真正绕过语言——他们仍然靠写作把路留下来。

八、京都学派

西田不是一个人。他创建了京都学派（京都学派）。

田边元、西谷启治、久松真一等人接过问题，却没有沿着同一条直线前进。西谷让佛教的空与尼采之后的虚无主义相遇，讨论的不是一句"什么都没有，所以什么都不怕"，而是虚无如何穿过自我，改变人与世界的关系。

这可以和苏轼不把官位当作全部自我作有限的对照，却不能说成同一个结构。一次贬谪中的生活实践，与一套宗教哲学对虚无主义的分析，各自有不能互换的历史与问题。

京都学派有争议——二战时期有些成员和日本军国主义有暧昧关系。"超越现代性"的讨论被用来为"大东亚共荣圈"辩护。这是哲学被权力收编的老故事——马克思的理论变成了斯大林的工具，同一个逻辑。

西田本人的政治文字至今有不同解释。他反对法西斯主义与抹去个人的总体主义，也接受国家官员的请求，写下有关日本文化与"新世界秩序"的文字；其中的普遍主义既可被读成抵抗帝国，也留下被帝国语言利用的暧昧。不能因为绝对无看似开放，就宣布整套思想与政治无关。

九、哲学的小路

京都。银阁寺附近。有一条路后来被叫作"哲学之道"。西田和田边元等京都学者曾在这一带散步、思考；樱花季时，两边都是花。道路与哲学的关系后来也长成了城市记忆。

这条路今天还在。游客来走。大部分人不知道西田是谁。他们来看樱花。

1945年6月7日，西田几多郎去世，七十五岁，距日本宣布投降约两个月。他没有看到战后围绕京都学派责任的长期争论，也没有看到自己的哲学后来被不断翻译、批评，进入更广的世界哲学讨论。

桥头又多了一个人。他是桥头上第一个日本人。他很安静——但他的安静跟八大山人不一样。八大山人的安静是拒绝说话。西田的安静是在说话之前。他在那个主客未分的地方待着。他不在"这边"也不在"那边"。他在场所里。

桥头上所有人都站在某个位置——维特根斯坦站在语言的边界上，康德站在物自体的线这边，黑格尔试图站在终点。

西田不站在任何位置。他站在让所有位置成为可能的地方。

那个地方什么都没有。

但一切都在那里发生。[^1][^2]

注释

[^1]: 本文将西田几多郎的"绝对无の場所"与Self-as-an-End理论中的余项、构不可闭合并置，但不把术语直接等同。西田并未把绝对无定义为构边界外的一堆余项，纯粹经验与后期场所逻辑之间也有思想发展。与康德、慧能、维特根斯坦的段落都是受限比较：不宣称康德只看见墙、慧能只需一次体验，也不把"换一种语言"冒充西田对维特根斯坦说过的话。

[^2]: 西田几多郎生平主要依据藤田正胜《西田几多郎：生きることと哲学》（2007）及Yusa Michiko, Zen and Philosophy: An Intellectual Biography of Nishida Kitarō (2002)。《善の研究》（1911年出版）。"纯粹经验"概念受William James影响（Essays in Radical Empiricism, 1912），但西田将其发展为本体论概念。"場所"（basho）概念见西田《働くものから見るものへ》（1927年）。"绝对無の場所"为西田后期核心概念。京都学派主要成员：田边元（1885-1962），西谷启治（1900-1990），久松真一（1889-1980）。西谷启治与虚无主义的对话参考《宗教とは何か》（Religion and Nothingness, 1961/英译1982）。京都学派与战时政治的关系参考James Heisig, Philosophers of Nothingness (2001)。"哲学之道"（哲学の道）在京都左京区。西田去世（1945年6月7日）。系列第二轮第十七篇。前四十篇见nondubito.net。

I. Before Being

One long current of Western philosophy begins with a single word: Being.

Parmenides said: what is, is; what is not, is not. You can only think about Being. Nothingness cannot be thought — because the moment you think about nothingness, it has already become the object of your thought, which makes it a kind of Being.

For more than two millennia, Plato's Forms, Aristotle's substance, Descartes's “I think, therefore I am,” and Hegel's dialectic kept rewriting the question of existence. This is not the whole of Western philosophy, but it is one of its strongest currents.

Nishida Kitarō said: you have missed something.

Before Being, there is something more fundamental. He called it Nothingness. Not the nothingness of "there is nothing here." The nothingness that makes Being possible. A stage is not an actor, but without the stage the actor has nowhere to perform. Nothingness is the stage. Being is the actor.

Nishida asks us to move our eyes, for a moment, from the actors to the stage. The image is not a verdict on an entire tradition. It is an entrance into his question.

II. An Inquiry into the Good

1911. Nishida Kitarō was forty-one. He published Zen no Kenkyū (An Inquiry into the Good).

It became a landmark of modern Japanese philosophy. Meiji thinkers such as Nishi Amane had already translated, coined terms, and thought with European philosophy; Japan also had older intellectual traditions of its own. Nishida's importance lies not in creating thought from nothing, but in building an original system that later entered global philosophical debate.

The core concept of An Inquiry into the Good: pure experience (junsui keiken).

What is pure experience? Before you divide the world into "subject" and "object" — before you say "I am looking at a flower" — there is a more primitive moment: looking. Not "I" looking at "a flower." Just looking. "Who is looking" and "what is being looked at" have not yet been separated. Subject and object are undivided.

When you play the piano and lose yourself — there is no "I" playing "the piano." There is only playing. What athletes call "the zone," what Zen calls samādhi — this is what it means. Subject and object have not yet split. Experience is pure.

For Nishida, pure experience is not an inferior state that has not yet learned to think; nor is it simply higher than every act of reflection. It precedes the subject-object division. Reflection grows from it and then looks back. By cutting out “I” and “world,” we gain judgment and lose something of the still-undivided whole.

William James also wrote of pure experience, and his radical empiricism was never merely psychology. Nishida learned from him but carried the question toward his own religious and ontological concerns. Their relation is not a story in which one supplied raw material and the other completed it.

III. What He Carved

What did Nishida carve?

He carved an assumption: that only the Western way counts as philosophy.

After the Meiji Restoration, Japanese intellectuals faced enormous pressure: everything Western was advanced — science, technology, institutions, including philosophy. Japan's own intellectual traditions — Zen, Confucianism, Shinto — were treated as "pre-modern," not "real philosophy." If you wanted to do philosophy, you had to use Western concepts, Western logic, Western language.

Nishida did something more complicated. He used the concepts, logic, and system-building of academic philosophy while letting Zen and Japanese thought change the questions those tools could ask. He was not pleading for a fixed “East” before a fixed “West.” He worked where both sides could still be altered by contact.

That something was Nothingness.

Socrates carved false knowledge — with dialogue. Marx carved false nature — with analysis. Galileo carved a false universe — with a telescope. Nishida carved a false boundary — "philosophy can only be Western" — with the West's own tools.

Using your sword to cut your lock. This is Nishida's most brilliant move.

IV. The Place of Absolute Nothingness

After An Inquiry into the Good, Nishida went deeper. He developed a more mature concept: place (basho).

Everything that "is" in the world — this tree, this person, this thought — needs a place in which to exist. The tree is in the forest. The person is in society. The thought is in consciousness. But where is consciousness? Consciousness is in a larger place. Where is that larger place? In an even larger place.

Follow the question all the way down and you arrive at: the place of absolute nothingness (zettai mu no basho). A place that is not any kind of Being — it itself is nothing, yet everything happens inside it.

This is not nihilism. "Absolute nothingness" is not "there is nothing." It is the condition for everything that is. Like a canvas — when the canvas has no image on it, it is not "an empty canvas." It is "the canvas that makes every painting possible."

In this series' vocabulary, absolute nothingness can suggest a place no settled construction can occupy completely. That is a comparison made here, not a direct equation of Nishida's term with “remainder.”

A construction is what we have articulated and built; a remainder is what it has not adequately included. Nishida's work asks us to question even the border between inside and outside. Absolute nothingness is not another container waiting beyond that border.

Kant marks limits to knowledge; Nishida begins with experience before subject and object divide and asks how such limits arise. Losing oneself at the piano can offer an intuition of “pure experience,” but it does not prove that every absorbed moment is identical with Nishida's later place of absolute nothingness. A long philosophical development separates the two.

V. Nishida and Huineng

Huineng said "originally there is nothing."

Nishida said "the place of absolute nothingness."

The two nothings echo one another, but they are not experiential and theoretical editions of the same concept.

Huineng's “originally not one thing” belongs to a specific setting of verse, practice, and lineage. It does not mean that Zen needs only one experience and can then dispense with language or discipline. Sudden awakening does not abolish the life that follows.

Nishida's nothingness changed through pure experience, the logic of place, and religious philosophy. He drew on Buddhist resources while arguing with Kant, Hegel, and James. Absolute nothingness is not one experience of Huineng repackaged in European terminology.

This is an act of translation. Not translation between languages — translation between modes of thought. Turning something that can only be experienced into something that can be discussed.

Such translation loses something. Zen has scriptures, debates, and traditions of explanation, yet it warns against mistaking words for arrival. Concepts let a discussion continue; they can also flatten a living practice into a glossary.

Translation also creates. Nothingness entered the arguments of modern academic philosophy, while European categories had to change in Japanese use. Nishida did not win philosophical credentials for an entire East. He made the credentialing rules themselves open to question.

Huineng and Nishida are less like a door and its new lock than two paths that meet and then continue separately. Comparison needs the distance, or it becomes annexation.

VI. Nishida and Kant

Kant drew a line: the thing-in-itself is on the other side; you cannot cross.

Nishida drew a place: you think you cannot cross, but you have been inside it all along. You are not on "this side" of the line trying to see "that side." You are in a place that contains both sides.

Kant distinguishes appearances from things in themselves by asking after the conditions of knowledge. Drawing his entire framework as a subject on one bank and a thing-in-itself on the other is only an introductory sketch.

Nishida said: the subject-object split itself came later. Before the split happened, you were already in that place. Pure experience is the state before subject and object divide. You are not reaching from "here" toward "there" — you are in a place more fundamental than the division.

What is the difference from Kant?

Kant said: your cognitive framework limits you. You can only see phenomena, not the thing-in-itself. Nishida said: your cognitive framework (the subject-object split) does indeed limit you. But "beneath" this framework, there is a more primitive layer of experience. At that level, the limitation has not yet occurred.

Kant reminds us that knowledge cannot casually pass through its own limit. Nishida asks how the wall and its two sides appear together. They are not simply disputing whether to demolish the same wall on the same map.

VII. Nishida and Wittgenstein

Wittgenstein said: "What we cannot talk about we must pass over in silence."

Nishida left no such direct reply to Wittgenstein. The comparison here asks instead whether what escapes subject-object language can be approached through another philosophical grammar.

Wittgenstein's Tractatus drew a boundary of language — inside the boundary is what can be said clearly; outside is what must be passed over in silence. Ethics, aesthetics, the meaning of life — all outside.

Nishida's work lets us ask whether “unsayable” also depends on the language being used. Place and pure experience are not instruction manuals for objects beyond the boundary. They let us reconsider how speaker, object, and language arise together.

Wittgenstein later, in the Investigations, also discovered the diversity of language — language is not only for describing facts; language is a game with many uses. But he did not go where Nishida went — he did not say "before the subject-object division there is a more fundamental level."

Wittgenstein discovered the many uses of language. Nishida discovered what comes before language.

One broadened the uses of language; the other tried to take philosophy toward what precedes language and reflection. Neither truly bypassed language: both had to write in order to leave a path.

VIII. The Kyoto School

Nishida did not work alone. He founded the Kyoto School.

Tanabe Hajime, Nishitani Keiji, Hisamatsu Shin'ichi: they inherited Nishida's questions without walking one straight road. Nishitani brought Buddhist emptiness into conversation with nihilism after Nietzsche. His argument is not the slogan “own nothing and nothing can hurt you,” but an inquiry into how nihilism passes through the self and changes one's relation to the world.

A limited comparison can be made with Su Shi refusing to let office define his whole self. But an exiled official's practical life and a religious philosophy's analysis of nihilism are not the same structure. Each has a history and a problem the other cannot replace.

The Kyoto School is controversial — during the Second World War, some of its members had ambiguous relationships with Japanese militarism. The discourse of "overcoming modernity" was used to justify the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere. This is the old story of philosophy co-opted by power — Marx's theory became Stalin's instrument. The same logic.

Nishida's political writings remain contested. He opposed fascism and any totalitarianism that erased the individual, yet he also answered state officials' requests and wrote about Japanese culture and a “new world order.” His universalism can be read as resistance to empire, but its ambiguities could also be recruited by imperial language. The openness of absolute nothingness does not make the whole philosophy politically innocent.

IX. The Philosopher's Path

Kyoto. Near the Silver Pavilion, a route later came to be called the Philosopher's Walk. Nishida, Tanabe, and other Kyoto thinkers walked and talked in this area. In blossom season, flowers line both sides. The road's relation to philosophy has itself become part of the city's memory.

The path is still there. Tourists walk it. Most of them do not know who Nishida was. They come to see the cherry blossoms.

Nishida Kitarō died on June 7, 1945, aged seventy-five, about two months before Japan announced its surrender. He did not see the long postwar argument over the Kyoto School's responsibility, or the later translations and criticisms that carried his thought into a wider philosophical world.

One more at the bridgehead. He is the first Japanese person there. He is quiet — but his quiet is different from Bada Shanren's. Bada Shanren's quiet is a refusal to speak. Nishida's quiet is what comes before speech. He is in the place before subject and object have divided. He is not on "this side" or "that side." He is in the place.

Everyone else at the bridgehead stands in a particular position — Wittgenstein stands on the boundary of language, Kant stands on this side of the thing-in-itself, Hegel tried to stand at the endpoint.

Nishida does not stand in any position. He stands in the place that makes all positions possible.

That place has nothing in it.

But everything happens there.[^1][^2]
