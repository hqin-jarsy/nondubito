丘吉尔，声音

Churchill, Voice

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、那段话

1940年6月4日。英国下议院。

敦刻尔克的撤退接近结束。丘吉尔向议员报告，三十三万五千多名英法军人已经被接了出来。人回来了，大量装备留在海峡另一边。法国仍在作战，结局还没有写完。

他没有把撤退说成已经获胜。“战争不是靠撤退打赢的。”接下来的话，要从这一点说起。

然后，那些后来被反复念起的地名出现了。海滩。登陆场。田野。街道。山丘。

“我们将在海滩上作战。”

“我们决不投降。”

这是一种把地图念到人身边的说法。海滩不只在军事报告里。街道是人每天经过的街道，田野是有人耕种的田野。话说到这里，战争不再只是从海峡对岸传来的消息，也可能来到听者脚下。

今天听到的那段熟悉录音，是他在1949年补录的。1940年6月4日，他没有在当晚亲自向全国重讲这篇演说。广播听众听到的是播音员转述的片段，更多人通过报道接触它。当时有人受到鼓舞，也有人因它预告的漫长战事而更加不安。声音不是同一时刻落进每个人耳朵里的同一种东西。

演说结尾还没有停在这座岛上。他说到帝国、舰队，以及大洋另一边可能到来的援助。那是对未来的呼唤，不是一份已经兑现的保证。

在这个系列里，王尔德曾让一句不容易说出的话留在法庭上。丘吉尔把一连串尚未抵达的战场放进句子里。两种声音，相隔着不同的处境。这里的说话者要承担的，还不只是自己说过什么：听完之后，会有许多人去做事，去等待，去冒险。

话说完了。船还要开，飞机还要修，守在海峡两边的人还没有回家。

二、他

温斯顿·丘吉尔。1874年11月30日，生于布伦海姆宫。父亲是伦道夫·丘吉尔勋爵，母亲是珍妮·杰罗姆。

桑德赫斯特军校之后，是印度、苏丹和南非。在不同的行程中，他当军人，也写战地报道。战事进入他的经历，很早也进入了他的文字。笔不是等到他离开战争才拿起来的。

1900年，他当选奥尔德姆议员，次年进入下议院活动。往后的职务一项接着一项：内政、海军、财政。名字旁边换了很多头衔；纸上的决定，却会在办公室以外继续发生。

一战时，他在海军大臣任上推动达达尼尔海峡行动。后来与加里波利登陆相连的战事失败，造成大批伤亡。他不是全部作战安排的唯一制定者，也不是没有参与决策的旁观者。1915年，他离开海军部，随后辞去内阁职务，去了法国前线，指挥皇家苏格兰燧发枪团第六营。职务从内阁换到了前线，仍有一批人依他的命令行动。

后来他又回到议会。

到了1930年代，他坐在后座，反复谈德国重整军备，要求加快飞机生产。他的声音与政府的政策有冲突，却没有处在一个完全无人听见的真空里。军备的调整、同事的消息、部门的工作，都在继续。1940年能用来作战的飞机，不是等他成为首相以后才开始制造的。

但他对英国独立的坚持，和他对印度自治的立场，并没有顺着同一条线展开。谈到英国，他要求不受别国支配；谈到印度，他仍要保留帝国政府安排其政治前途的位置。这种差别，后来会在他自己的议会解释中变得清楚。

同一个人说出的“自由”，走到不同地方，边界可能不同。

这里不能只听音量。还得听，他在替谁说话。

三、1940年

5月10日，丘吉尔成为首相。德国也在这一天向法国及低地国家发动进攻。

五月底的战时内阁里，问题还没有变成后来纪念演说中的一句话。哈利法克斯提出，是否可以通过墨索里尼探询调停的可能。他谈的不是已经拿到手的投降书，而是能否探得不损害英国独立的条件。丘吉尔担心，一旦沿这条路走下去，就会在连续让步中丢掉舰队、重新武装的能力和继续自主行动的余地。

争执发生在不完整的消息中。法国还能支持多久？意大利会不会参战？空袭将造成什么后果？海军、空军与海外的联系能够支撑多久？这些都是他们要讨论的问题。没有一张地图已经替他们保证结局。

哈利法克斯与丘吉尔的分歧，也不是“一个要自由，一个不要”的简单对话。他们对可能得到什么条件、询问条件会产生什么后果，判断不同。艾德礼、格林伍德、张伯伦也都参与了讨论。到5月28日，内阁没有接受当时向意大利寻求调停的提议，继续作战的决定被保留下来。

后来，一个声音最容易留在录音和记忆里。会议桌上却不只坐着一个人。

苏格拉底在这个系列里曾面对是否离开的选择。把两个人并置，能让我们看见“不肯放弃”里面的分量。但这里多了一道不能跨过去的区别：一个人决定自己承担什么，与一个政府决定许多人将承担什么，不是同一件事。

一个人可以说，某件事值得自己冒险。说出“我们”以后，他还要面对没有坐在会议桌旁的人。对那些人，理由、物资、防护、允许质问的地方，都不能由他的决心代替。

因此，声音的力量不只在于把句子说到底。

也在于句子说完以后，仍然有人可以问：接下来怎么办？

四、帝国

那句“我们”有多大？

6月4日演说自己就把帝国和舰队放了进去。海峡边的岛屿并不是与世界断开的一块石头。海外的军队、粮食、港口和运输线，都进入了战争。

但把地图上这些地方涂成同一种颜色，容易让人忘记它们的区别。加拿大、澳大利亚、新西兰等自治领，与仍受殖民统治的印度，不处在同一种政治关系里。共同参战不等于每个地方都以同样的方式决定参战。

到1945年战争结束时，英属印度陆军已扩大到约二百五十万人。这个数字包括不同语言、宗教和出身的人。他们是士兵，不是地图向伦敦自动输送的一串整数。兵员统计可以说明规模，却不能替每个人说出他为何入伍、家里等着谁、又怎样理解这场战争。

1941年9月9日，丘吉尔在下议院解释《大西洋宪章》。面对关于人民选择政府的原则，他把纳粹占领下欧洲国家恢复主权的问题，与印度、缅甸等地的政治发展分开处理，表示原有的帝国政策没有因此改变。

同一个“自由”，在演说里有了不同的时间表。

英国的独立需要现在保住。印度怎样决定自己的前途，却仍被放在由帝国政府解释的进程里。对于等待的人，“以后”不是一句没有重量的话。他也正在过自己的现在。

“帝国”两个字在地图上铺得很平，它的厚度却藏在名字后面。地图能够画出港口、铁路和边界，画不出一个人何时愿意合作，何时说自己不愿再接受安排。

甘地在本系列所带来的不合作，也在这个问题旁边。拒绝配合，是让一个惯于发出命令的人发现，对面不是命令的延长线。印度的政治要求又远不止甘地一个人的声音，不能由一个人包办，更不能由伦敦代为完成。

自由合作之后，会不会获得更多士兵、更多粮食、更多支持？历史没有替另一个未发生的过程出具保证书。尊重一个人的选择，也不能以他将来一定更加有用为条件。

把人请到桌边，意味着答案可能不是你预先写好的答案。

五、代价

1943年，孟加拉。

八月的一份美国外交备忘录转录当地报告：饥饿的人从乡村来到加尔各答，有人死在街上，有人未能进入医院。报文里有街道，也有门。前一节的地图在这里变成了很短的一段路，一个已经没有力气的人还要走过去。

这场饥荒及相随的疾病夺去了以百万计的生命。人数估计有差异；死亡本身不因数字尚需考订而变得模糊。

缅甸失陷后输入米粮中断，1942年的风灾与稻作损失，战时采购、物价上涨、运输受扰和救济迟缓，交织在一起。对一个家庭，粮食在某处存在，还不等于它能买到、运到，或者及时领到。原来买得起一顿饭的钱，可能已经不够买同样的一碗米。

为应对日本入侵威胁，当局曾实施稻米与船只的“拒敌”措施。船被征用、移走或毁坏时，失去的也可能是当地人的运输和生计。安全措施与实际损害在同一条水道上发生，不能只从伦敦的海图看它。

1945年的调查报告批评救济开展过晚、组织不足。委员会内部也留下了分歧。纳纳瓦蒂特别强调，乡村居民的收入没有跟上粮价上涨；对于加尔各答与乡村之间的粮食分配，他没有完全接受多数委员的解释。报告并非只有一种声音。

再看一份较晚的文件。1944年4月29日，丘吉尔向罗斯福请求运输粮食所需的船只。美国外交文献集保存的电文转述说，韦维尔总督一直强调需要进口；丘吉尔也承认，自己曾一度没有同意总督向美国求援的请求。此时他提出了请求，并列明英国已经安排的运输。

美方以军事运输压力为由拒绝提供所求援助。到七月，英国驻美使馆又报告，为另调船只，英国方面同意削减一些其他军事运输安排。求援、拒绝、调整，都留在通信里。船舶优先用于什么，并非一个没有人参与决定的自然现象。

但这是1944年的通信。它到来时，1943年已经死去的人不会重新走回街上。纸面上的吨数还要变成航次，航次还要变成卸下的粮食，粮食还要抵达需要它的人。每一段都有自己的时间。

追问责任，需要沿着这些具体决定走：谁掌握消息，谁能够安排船只，谁制定采购与分配办法，谁在等待中失去了活下去的条件。省政府、印度中央政府与伦敦之间的关系不能抹成一个名字；分出不同环节，也不是让责任在机构之间消失。

欧洲听过的鼓舞，不能替孟加拉的一户人家吃饭。饥饿的人没有义务把自己的经历改成另一段历史的注脚。

六、他和奥本海默

奥本海默在桥上背着灰。丘吉尔将带来一面旗。

先把道具放下，看看纸上的名字。

1945年6月16日，奥本海默代表四人科学小组提交关于核武器立即使用的建议。报告记录了科学家之间的分歧，小组自身则倾向直接军事使用。决定怎样使用武器不只由他们作出，但他们确实向决策者提供了这份意见。

同年11月，在洛斯阿拉莫斯的讲话里，奥本海默谈到科学家参与制造武器的责任。他也说，将来的和平应用并不能消除眼前的问题。两份文字相隔着几个月。后来的忧虑，没有使前面的建议从档案中消失。

丘吉尔的纸上留下的，是另一类行动。内阁的决定、议会的演说、致罗斯福的求援电文。它们有时面向不同的人，回答不同的问题；同一个声音不能替这些文件完成全部说明。

旗与灰可以在一座桥上相遇，却不能把两个人合成同一种自责。我们没有一张可以读出全部内心的纸。能够追问的是他们参加了什么，提出过什么意见，谁接受了决定，又是谁承受了后果。

这里还有一个不容易说清的地方。后来的人仍会拿起他们留下的知识与话语。拿起，不等于接过一份不许再问的遗嘱。可以从一段讲话得到力量，同时回头查看说话者的文件。

灰不是承认看见了，就会少一点。旗也不是背得很重，就已经替旗帜下面的人回答了什么。

它们提醒读者把目光移到纸外：谁还没有说过话？

七、1945年7月

欧洲的战争已经结束，对日战争仍在继续。

7月5日，英国大选投票举行。为等候海外选票，结果到26日公布。工党取得下议院多数，艾德礼成为首相，丘吉尔离开政府首脑的位置。

但伍德福德仍选他做议员。他没有随着首相职位一起从议会消失。1951年，他再次出任首相。一个人的政治经历，并没有在一次选举之后被收进用完即弃的箱子里。

1941年向美国国会讲话时，他曾说明，自己是下议院的公仆，议会的一次表决就能使他离职。说这句话时，战争仍在继续。职位可以被交还，并不只是在离职以后才出现的说法。

1945年的选举也有战争以外的问题：住房、就业、复员和社会保障。为下一段生活选择政府，与抹去上一段生活的记忆，不是同一件事。选票没有要求投票的人先假装自己从未受过鼓舞。

另一方面，选民也不欠一位演说者永久的职位。听进过他的话，不等于此后的日子只能继续由他主持。

这是声音与座位的区别。声音可以留下来。座位却要由另一套公开的程序决定。

离开座位的人仍然是人。决定换一个政府的人，也仍然有自己的生活要过。

八、诺贝尔文学奖

1953年，丘吉尔获得诺贝尔文学奖。

获奖理由把历史与传记叙述、演说放在一起。他的写作也确实不只剩下一段战时录音。《世界危机》《马尔伯勒传》《第二次世界大战回忆录》把很长的时间放进了书里。写书的人曾参加其中一部分事情，后来又拿起笔，安排它们如何被讲述。

这使他的文字有一种特殊的近，也留下了一种需要读者辨认的距离。参与过，并不等于知道一切；记得自己的会议，也不等于已经记下每一个受影响的人。

再把6月4日的演说翻开。不放录音，只看文字。

一句“我们将”不断回来。后面跟着的地方不断改变。句式给人一种可以接下去的节奏，地名又把节奏拉回实物：海滩、田野、街道。你不能在这些地方真正站住，只靠修辞不行。可语言能够把一个还没有到来的处境，变成眼下可以共同谈论的东西。

有人会从这样的句子里得到继续做事的力量。另一个人也可以问：这个“我们”有没有包括我？两种阅读不必由一本书替它们选定胜负。

狄更斯给读者留下过温暖的房间，凯瑟琳的经历不能被房间里的炉火盖住。丘吉尔留下的声音也需要有窗有门：后来的人能够进来，能够出去，能够把另一份记录带进来。

文学奖表彰的是文字工作，不是一枚可以压住其他文件的镇纸。

读者把书翻过一页。下一页还在那里。

九、声音

1965年1月24日，丘吉尔去世，九十岁。

他活到了战争结束后的第二十年。印度与巴基斯坦已在1947年独立，帝国地图上的关系发生了变化；他也曾离开首相职位，再回来，后来再离开。那句1940年的话，一直留在这些变化之中。

人不再说话，录音还可以继续播放。打开，声音来到房间里；关上，房间又安静下来。活着时的说话者无法这样被装进一个按钮。那声音曾经离不开一个会走出议会、接过文件、继续作决定的人。

现在，把他想象到这座桥上。

桥头又多了一个人。身形结实，有点矮胖。雪茄在手边。两根手指举起，做出V字，然后慢慢放下。

他带来一支笔和一面帝国的旗。许多颜色缝在一起，卷得很厚。他把它搭上肩头，一只手扶住旗杆，另一只手仍拿着笔。回声先到，脚步在后。

苏格拉底站在空地上。柏拉图蹲着画图纸。休谟守着双陆棋盘。叔本华向桥底下看。克尔凯郭尔跳了。图灵看着手里的苹果。契诃夫靠着栏杆。康托尔看天空。哥白尼放下书走了。萨特拿着烟斗踱步。波伏瓦举着镜子。蒯因轻轻说了一句话。

特斯拉听嗡嗡声。爱迪生拿着没有亮的灯泡。海森堡的位置不确定。玻尔手里是没有寄出的信。托尔斯泰拿着药方面对契诃夫。莎士比亚不在——他是桥下面的水。斯宾诺莎手上有玻璃粉。亚里士多德蹲着铺地板。法拉第掀起一块木板。麦克斯韦站着写方程。

贞德带着火在桥的上方。王尔德站得很好看，手里拿着那句话。拉马努金从缝隙里探出半个身子。奥本海默背着灰往前走。夏洛蒂拿着笔。艾米莉在桥外的荒原上，风从她那里吹来。玻尔兹曼抱着刻有方程的石头。梵高身上有颜料，画笔还在滴颜色。狄更斯站在裂缝上，笔与火柴盒都还在手里。

丘吉尔看了奥本海默一眼。灰没有变成旗，旗也没有散成灰。两个肩头各自负着重量，没有一架秤移到他们中间。

他又看向贞德。火在上面动，风把旗的边角吹开了一点。他把旗角收回肩边。布面没有展开，桥上各人的身影仍在原处。

远处，康德站在那里。

丘吉尔听见自己从桥面上回来的声音。那段话可以传得很远。他向前挪了一步，旗杆碰到了肩。

前面不是只有一句话在等待。休谟移动了一颗棋子，契诃夫没有离开栏杆；狄更斯脚下，纸轻轻响了一声。

丘吉尔停了一下。把肩上的旗重新放稳。没有人因为这次停顿，替他擦掉文字；也没有人因为文字还在，就替他把旗拿走。

他向康德的方向走去。声音能够先到，肩上的东西却还得由脚步带过去。

风吹过来。旗角动了一下。

那声音留下的空隙里，还有别人的声音。

I. Those Words

June 4, 1940. The House of Commons.

The evacuation from Dunkirk was drawing to a close. Churchill told the House that more than 335,000 British and French servicemen had been brought out. The men had returned; much of their equipment remained across the Channel. France was still fighting. The ending had not yet been written.

He did not describe the evacuation as a victory already won. “Wars are not won by evacuations.” What followed had to begin there.

Then came the places that would be repeated so often afterward. Beaches. Landing grounds. Fields. Streets. Hills.

“We shall fight on the beaches.”

“We shall never surrender.”

It was a way of reading a map aloud until it came close to people's lives. A beach was not only a place in a military report. The streets were streets people walked through every day; someone worked those fields. By this point in the speech, war was no longer merely news arriving from across the Channel. It might come to the ground beneath the listener's feet.

The familiar recording heard today was made by Churchill in 1949. On the evening of June 4, 1940, he did not personally deliver the speech again to the nation over the radio. A newsreader read extracts, and reports brought the words to further readers and listeners. Some were encouraged. Others became more apprehensive about the long struggle the speech anticipated. A voice does not enter every ear at the same moment, carrying exactly the same thing.

The speech did not end at the boundaries of the island either. Churchill spoke of the Empire, the fleet, and help that might come from across the ocean. It was a call into the future, not a guarantee already fulfilled.

In this series, Wilde has left in a courtroom words that were difficult to say. Churchill placed a succession of battlefields that had not yet arrived inside his sentences. Two voices, in different circumstances. Here the speaker would have to answer for more than his own words: after hearing them, many other people would work, wait, and take risks.

The speech was over. Ships still had to sail. Aircraft still needed repairs. The people keeping watch on either side of the Channel had not come home.

II. The Man

Winston Churchill. Born at Blenheim Palace on November 30, 1874. His father was Lord Randolph Churchill; his mother, Jennie Jerome.

After Sandhurst came India, Sudan, and South Africa. On different journeys he served as a soldier and worked as a war correspondent. War entered his experience early, and his writing almost alongside it. He did not wait until he had left war behind to pick up a pen.

In 1900 he was elected for Oldham, taking his place in the Commons the following year. The offices came one after another: the Home Office, the Admiralty, the Treasury. The titles beside his name changed. Decisions written on paper continued to have consequences outside the office.

During the First World War, as First Lord of the Admiralty, he pressed for the Dardanelles operation. The campaign, subsequently involving the Gallipoli landings, failed and caused extensive casualties. He was neither the sole author of every operational decision nor a spectator uninvolved in making them. He left the Admiralty in 1915, later resigned his remaining Cabinet post, and went to the front in France, where he commanded the 6th Battalion of the Royal Scots Fusiliers. His position had changed from Cabinet office to front-line command. There were still people acting on his orders.

Later he returned to Parliament.

On the back benches in the 1930s, he repeatedly raised German rearmament and urged faster aircraft production. His voice challenged government policy, but it did not sound in a vacuum where nobody ever listened. Changes in armaments, information from colleagues, and the work of government departments continued. The aircraft available for combat in 1940 had not all waited for him to become prime minister before someone began to build them.

His insistence on British independence and his position on Indian self-government did not, however, follow the same line. For Britain, he demanded freedom from another country's domination. For India, he retained a place for the imperial government in determining its political future. The difference would become explicit in his own explanation to Parliament.

“Freedom,” spoken by the same person, could have different boundaries in different places.

Volume is not the only thing to listen for. We also have to hear whom the speaker is speaking for.

III. 1940

Churchill became prime minister on May 10. On the same day, Germany attacked France and the Low Countries.

In the War Cabinet at the end of May, the issue had not yet become a single sentence in a commemorative speech. Halifax proposed exploring the possibility of mediation through Mussolini. He was not presenting a surrender document already in hand. He wanted to know whether terms compatible with Britain's independence could be found. Churchill argued that entering such a process risked a succession of concessions: the loss of the fleet, the ability to rearm, and the room to continue acting independently.

They argued with incomplete information. How long could France hold out? Would Italy enter the war? What damage would air attacks do? How long could the navy, the air force, and overseas connections sustain them? These were questions for discussion. No map had already guaranteed an outcome.

Nor was the disagreement between Halifax and Churchill a simple exchange in which one wanted freedom and the other did not. They differed over what terms might be available and what asking about them would set in motion. Attlee, Greenwood, and Chamberlain also took part. By May 28, the Cabinet had not accepted the proposal to seek Italian mediation at that point. The decision to continue fighting remained in place.

Afterward, one voice would be especially easy to preserve in recordings and memory. More than one person had sat at the table.

Socrates, in this series, has faced the choice of whether to leave. Setting the two men beside each other brings some of the weight of refusing to give something up into view. But a distinction remains that the comparison cannot cross. Deciding what one will bear oneself is not the same as a government deciding what many people will bear.

A person can say that something is worth risking his own life for. Once he says “we,” he also faces people who were not at the meeting table. His resolve cannot take the place of reasons, supplies, protection, or room for those people to ask questions.

The force of a voice therefore lies in more than carrying a sentence through to its end.

There must still be someone able to ask, after the sentence is finished: what happens next?

IV. The Empire

How large was that “we”?

The June 4 speech itself included the Empire and the fleet. The island beside the Channel was not a stone cut off from the world. Overseas troops, food, ports, and shipping routes were part of the war.

Color all these places the same shade on a map, however, and their differences become easy to forget. Dominions such as Canada, Australia, and New Zealand did not stand in the same political relationship to Britain as India, which remained under colonial rule. Fighting in the same war did not mean that every place had entered it through the same kind of decision.

By the end of the war in 1945, the British Indian Army had grown to about two and a half million. Within that number were people of different languages, religions, and backgrounds. They were soldiers, not a sequence of integers automatically dispatched from a map to London. A personnel total describes scale. It cannot say for each person why he enlisted, who was waiting at home, or how he understood the war.

On September 9, 1941, Churchill explained the Atlantic Charter to the Commons. Addressing the principle that peoples should choose their government, he distinguished the restoration of sovereignty to European countries under Nazi occupation from political development in places such as India and Burma. Existing imperial policy, he said, had not thereby changed.

The same “freedom” now had different timetables within the speech.

Britain's independence had to be preserved now. How India would determine its future remained within a process interpreted by the imperial government. For someone waiting, “later” was not a weightless word. That person, too, was living through a present of his own.

“Empire” lies flat on the map. Its depth is hidden behind the names. A map can show ports, railways, and borders. It cannot show the moment when a person agrees to cooperate, or says that he no longer accepts arrangements made for him.

Gandhi's non-cooperation, as it appears in this series, belongs beside this question. Refusing to comply makes someone accustomed to issuing orders encounter a person who is not merely an extension of the command. India's political demands included far more than Gandhi's voice. One person could not speak them all; London could not settle them on everyone's behalf.

Would freely chosen cooperation have brought more soldiers, more food, more support? History has issued no guarantee for a course that did not take place. Respect for someone's choice cannot depend on a promise that he will become more useful afterward.

Inviting people to the table means allowing for an answer you have not written in advance.

V. The Cost

Bengal, 1943.

An American diplomatic memorandum from August reproduced local reports. Starving people were coming from the countryside into Calcutta. Some died in the streets; some were not admitted to hospitals. The report has streets in it, and doors. The previous section's map has become a short stretch of road that someone with almost no strength left must still get across.

The famine and the diseases that accompanied it took millions of lives. Estimates differ. Uncertainty over the count does not make the deaths themselves indistinct.

The interruption of rice imports after the fall of Burma, the cyclone and crop losses of 1942, wartime procurement, rising prices, disrupted transport, and delayed relief became entangled. For a household, the existence of food somewhere did not mean it could be bought, transported, or received in time. Money that had once paid for a meal might no longer buy the same bowl of rice.

Facing the threat of Japanese invasion, the authorities had implemented “denial” measures for rice and boats, intended to prevent their use by an invading force. Requisitioning, removing, or destroying boats could also take away local transport and livelihoods. A security measure and its practical damage occurred on the same waterway. A naval chart in London was not the only place from which to see it.

The inquiry report published in 1945 criticized the late start and inadequate organization of relief. Disagreements remained within the commission itself. Nanavati particularly emphasized that rural incomes had failed to keep pace with food prices. He did not fully accept the majority's explanation of the distribution of food between Calcutta and the countryside. The report did not speak with only one voice.

Now turn to a later document. On April 29, 1944, Churchill asked Roosevelt for ships to carry food. The paraphrased telegram preserved in the American diplomatic record states that Viceroy Wavell had repeatedly stressed the need for imports. Churchill also acknowledged that he had for a time resisted the Viceroy's request to seek American help. Now he made the request and set out the shipping Britain had already arranged.

The American side declined the requested assistance, citing the pressures of military shipping. By July, the British Embassy was reporting that Britain had agreed to curtail some other military shipping programs in order to release additional vessels. Request, refusal, adjustment: all remain in the correspondence. The priority given to one cargo rather than another was not a natural event in which nobody made a decision.

But these letters belong to 1944. By the time they arrived, people who had died in 1943 could not walk back into the streets. Tonnage on a page still had to become voyages, voyages had to become grain unloaded, and grain had to reach those who needed it. Each stage had its own time.

To ask about responsibility is to follow these particular decisions. Who had the information? Who could allocate ships? Who determined procurement and distribution? Who lost the means to stay alive while waiting? The provincial government, the central government in India, and London cannot all be erased into a single name. Separating the stages does not mean letting responsibility vanish between the institutions either.

Encouragement heard in Europe could not feed a household in Bengal. People who were starving had no obligation to turn their experience into a footnote to another history.

VI. Churchill and Oppenheimer

Oppenheimer carries ash on the bridge. Churchill will bring a flag.

First set down the props. Look at the names on the papers.

On June 16, 1945, Oppenheimer submitted recommendations on the immediate use of nuclear weapons on behalf of a four-person scientific panel. The report recorded disagreement among scientists; the panel itself favored direct military use. The decision about using the weapon was not theirs alone, but they did provide this advice to those making it.

In a speech at Los Alamos that November, Oppenheimer discussed the responsibility of scientists who had helped make the weapon. He also said that future peaceful applications did not dispose of the immediate problem. A few months separate the two texts. The later concerns did not make the earlier recommendation disappear from the archive.

Churchill's papers record a different kind of participation: Cabinet decisions, parliamentary speeches, the appeal for shipping sent to Roosevelt. They addressed different people at different times and answered different questions. One voice cannot provide the whole account on behalf of all these documents.

Flag and ash can meet on a bridge without merging two people into a single form of self-reproach. There is no page from which we can read everything in either man's mind. We can ask what they participated in, what they advised, who accepted the decisions, and who bore the consequences.

Something else remains difficult. Later generations will continue to take up the knowledge and words they left. Taking them up is not accepting a will that forbids further questions. A reader can draw strength from a speech and still return to the speaker's papers.

Acknowledging the ash does not reduce it. Nor does carrying a heavy flag mean that one has already answered for the people beneath it.

The props direct the reader's attention beyond the paper. Who has not yet had a chance to speak?

VII. July 1945

The war in Europe was over. The war against Japan continued.

Britain's general election was held on July 5. To allow time for overseas ballots, the results were announced on the 26th. Labour won a majority in the Commons. Attlee became prime minister, and Churchill left the head of government's office.

Woodford, however, still elected him as its MP. Leaving the premiership did not remove him from Parliament. He became prime minister again in 1951. An election had not packed the whole of his political life into a box marked used and discarded.

In his address to the United States Congress in 1941, he had described himself as a servant of the Commons and explained that a vote of the House could remove him from office. The war was still in progress when he said it. The possibility of relinquishing office was not an idea that first appeared after he had lost it.

The election of 1945 also concerned matters beyond the war: housing, employment, demobilization, and social security. Choosing a government for the next part of one's life is not the same as erasing the memory of the last. A ballot did not require its voter first to pretend that no words had ever offered encouragement.

Conversely, voters did not owe an orator a permanent office. Having listened to him did not mean that only he could preside over their lives from then on.

There is a difference between a voice and a seat. The voice can remain. The seat is decided through another, public procedure.

The person leaving it remains a person. Those choosing a different government also have lives of their own to live.

VIII. The Nobel Prize in Literature

In 1953 Churchill received the Nobel Prize in Literature.

The award's stated grounds brought historical and biographical writing together with oratory. His work did extend beyond a single wartime recording. The World Crisis, Marlborough: His Life and Times, and The Second World War placed long stretches of time inside books. The writer had participated in some of what happened. Later he took up the pen again and arranged how it would be told.

That gives the writing a particular closeness, and also a distance that the reader has to discern. Having taken part is not knowing everything. Remembering one's own meetings is not the same as having recorded every person they affected.

Open the June 4 speech again. Leave the recording off. Look only at the words.

“We shall” keeps returning. The places that follow it keep changing. The repeated form offers a rhythm one can carry onward; the places pull that rhythm back toward things: beaches, fields, streets. Rhetoric alone cannot hold those places. But language can make a situation that has not yet arrived into something people can discuss together now.

Someone may find in those sentences the strength to continue working. Someone else may ask whether this “we” includes them. A book need not choose a winner between the two readings.

Dickens left his readers warm rooms. Their hearths cannot cover Catherine's experience. The voice Churchill left also needs windows and doors: people who come later must be able to enter, to leave, and to bring another record inside.

A literary prize recognizes work with words. It is not a paperweight that can hold all the other documents shut.

The reader turns a page. The next page is still there.

IX. Voice

Churchill died on January 24, 1965. He was ninety.

He lived into the twentieth year after the war ended. India and Pakistan had become independent in 1947; the relationships on the imperial map had changed. He had left the premiership, returned, and left it again. The words of 1940 remained amid these changes.

When a person no longer speaks, a recording can go on playing. Switch it on and the voice enters the room; switch it off and the room becomes quiet. The living speaker could not be contained in a button that way. The voice had belonged to someone who walked out of Parliament, received another document, and went on making decisions.

Now imagine him on this bridge.

Someone else has arrived. A solid, rather short and stocky man. A cigar close at hand. He raises two fingers in a V, then slowly lowers them.

He has brought a pen and an imperial flag. Many colors sewn together, rolled into a thick bundle. He rests it on his shoulder, steadying the pole with one hand, still holding the pen in the other. The echo arrives first. The footsteps follow.

Socrates stands in the clearing. Plato crouches over his plans. Hume keeps watch over a backgammon board. Schopenhauer looks beneath the bridge. Kierkegaard has made his leap. Turing looks at the apple in his hand. Chekhov leans against the railing. Cantor looks at the sky. Copernicus has put down his book and walked away. Sartre paces with his pipe. Beauvoir holds a mirror. Quine has said a quiet sentence.

Tesla listens to a hum. Edison holds an unlit bulb. Heisenberg's position is uncertain. Bohr holds an unsent letter. Tolstoy faces Chekhov with a prescription in his hand. Shakespeare is not there: he is the water beneath the bridge. Spinoza has glass dust on his hands. Aristotle crouches to lay the floor. Faraday lifts a plank. Maxwell stands writing equations.

Joan of Arc is above the bridge, carrying fire. Wilde stands with easy elegance, holding that sentence. Ramanujan leans halfway out of a gap. Oppenheimer walks forward with his burden of ash. Charlotte holds a pen. Emily is out on the moor beyond the bridge; the wind comes from where she is. Boltzmann cradles a stone carved with an equation. Van Gogh has paint on his clothes; his brushes still drip color. Dickens stands over the crack, pen and matchbox still in his hands.

Churchill glances at Oppenheimer. The ash has not turned into a flag; the flag has not fallen apart into ash. Each pair of shoulders carries its own weight. No set of scales moves into the space between them.

He looks toward Joan. The fire moves above him. The wind loosens a corner of the flag, and he gathers it back beside his shoulder. The cloth does not unfurl. The figures on the bridge remain where they are.

Kant is standing in the distance.

Churchill hears his own voice returning from the surface of the bridge. The words can travel far. He moves one step forward. The pole touches his shoulder.

There is more than a sentence waiting ahead. Hume moves a counter; Chekhov has not left the railing. Beneath Dickens's feet, paper makes a faint sound.

Churchill pauses and settles the flag more firmly on his shoulder. No one erases his words because he has stopped. No one takes the flag from him because the words remain.

He walks toward Kant. A voice can arrive first. What rests on his shoulder still has to be carried by his steps.

The wind comes across. A corner of the flag stirs.

In the spaces that voice has left, there are other voices.
