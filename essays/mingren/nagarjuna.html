龙树：连地板都是空的

Nāgārjuna: Even the Floor Is Empty

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、八个"不"

不生亦不灭。不常亦不断。不一亦不异。不来亦不出。

八个“不”。四对否定。《中论》开篇的归敬偈。

龙树通常被放在公元二至三世纪，传说把他的出身连到南印度，确切生平却很难落实。《中论》约五百颂，这八个“不”像一粒种子：往后的因果、运动、时间与存在之辨，都从这里伸展开来。

不生不灭，不是说孩子没有出生、花不会凋谢。不常不断，不是说今天和昨天没有联系。不一不异，不来不出，也不是禁止我们辨认同一把椅子，或说一个人进了门。它们追问的是：把条件全拿走之后，还有一个靠自己成立的“生”“灭”“同”“异”，可以独自在那里吗？

你习惯了的范畴——生灭，常断，同异，来去——都被重新拿起来检查。不是随手判一句“这个对，那个错”。是问：这些帮助你思考的工具，凭什么又变成了不需要条件的终极地基？

前面八篇讨论的都是认知的内容和方式——什么算知识（孔德），怎么检验知识（波普尔），方程能不能先于观察到达（狄拉克），美算不算认知（屈原），认知者在哪里（费希特），感觉能不能认知（麦克林托克），注意力是不是认知（薇依），什么时候该停止认知（撒切尔）。

龙树把问题再往下移了一层。认知的内容、方式、认知者，都要问；连用来问它们的范畴与概念，也不能例外。他不是让人从此不能说话、不能判断，而是不让任何一个工具冒充最后不动的地板。

连地板都是空的。你以为站在地板上讨论缝隙，龙树却让你低头看：脚下这块板也靠许多条件托着，不是自己把自己撑起来的。

二、龙宫

关于龙树的生平，传说远多于史实。

五世纪初，鸠摩罗什把《中论》译入汉语。题署他译的《龙树菩萨传》，则留下另一种文字：一个聪颖的婆罗门青年和三个朋友学了隐身术，潜入宫中侵害宫人；事情败露，朋友被杀，他逃过一劫，转而出家。这是带有警诫意味的传说，不是已核实的履历，也不只是天才少年“闹事”的趣谈。

传说继续说，他读了很多，却又以为佛教还有未尽之处，甚至打算另立教法。这时大龙把他接到海底的宫殿，打开经藏。他在那里读到远超过自己所知的经典，回来以后弘扬大乘、写作论书。

不必把龙宫当作地图上的地点，也能读出这个转折：一个以为自己已经学完的人，遇到了自己尚未想到的广大。龙宫不只奖赏他的聪明，也使他的自满停了一下。

这个故事让人想起前面的狄拉克。方程曾把他带到当时观察尚未抵达的地方，指向反物质——不是物理世界之外，而是那个世界尚未被看见的一部分。龙宫属于传说，方程属于可以继续检验的理论；不能把两种证据混在一起。但作为阅读的意象，它们都让“我已知道的”不再等于“全部”。

再回到《中论》，龙树并不靠一句“我去过龙宫，你们听我的”让人信服。他一个一个追问人们的框架。因果？要问。运动？要问。变化？要问。存在？也要问。并不是把这些日常经验删除，而是拆它们被当成自性实有的那一层。

他不给你另一块永远不会松动的地板。拆到最后，说：空。连这个“空”也不能拿来当地板。

三、四句否定

龙树常用的一种论辩方式，是把一个问题的四个角都检查一遍。梵文叫catuṣkoṭi，通常译作“四句”。在针对某些自性见的论证里，四句都会被遣除。

拿“一个东西能否凭自性成立”来作通俗示意，四个落脚点是：

它自性而有。
它自性而无。
它自性地亦有亦无。
它自性地非有非无。

你先抓住“有”，被问住了，退到“无”；再退到“都有”，最后退到“都不是”。但只要你仍想替同一套自性前提找一个绝对安全的位置，换答案也未必走出了原来的问题。

看到四句，容易联想到佛教的四无色处：空无边处、识无边处、无所有处、非想非非想处。那些名称讲的是禅定经验及相应的生存境界，不是四个逻辑真值；空无边处也不是“一切法空”的同义词。不能仅凭都有四层，就把它们认作四句的来源。

两边可以产生文学上的回声：你以为终于到了一个地方，却还不能把它当成最后的居所。但禅定的次第不是论证的四步，非想非非想也不等于已经解脱。龙树不是给人画四层天的哲学地图；他是在追问，你凭什么把某一个回答钉死。

中文还有“想入非非”。今天多指不切实际的想象，辞书却把它的佛教语源连到“非想非非想处”。日常的一句调侃，原来带着修行词语的旧回声。这个语言转折很有意思，却不能反过来证明四句与四无色处是同一套东西。

也不能把它简单写成“西方有排中律，龙树把排中律推翻了”。这里不是对任意一句话都连说四次不，更不是宣布真假从此无用。他常常追问的是：这个问题让你选择的四个答案，会不会共同带着一个站不住的前提？

这就不只是玩文字了。你以为可以站在“有”上讨论世界；如果那是一个不依条件的有，脚下会松动。你退到“无”，也未必就安全。退到“亦有亦无”或“非有非无”，仍要接受同样的检查。

你找不到一块不需要任何条件的落脚石。

但龙树不是要你摔下去。他要松开的，是那个误会：好像非得找到一块绝对不动的地板，人才可以活。脚下有木板，有支撑，有彼此借力；没有的是一块脱离一切仍能自己站住的地板。

把误会去掉。你还在。你可以站，也可以走。只是不必再把支撑你的东西，误认成了永远不用支撑的东西。

四、他和释迦牟尼

前面的008篇写过释迦牟尼。佛陀教人看缘起：事情随条件生起，也随条件止息。龙树沿着这条路，把对自性的追问推得很深。

佛教在传承中发展了精密的分析：五蕴，十二处，十八界，以及对苦与解脱的种种说明。这些分类并非一概到后世才出现，也不是每个部派都把每个概念当成实有。龙树面对的危险更具体：本来用于分析经验的格子，被当成了自身成立的最后单位。

这让人想起本系列读亚里士多德时遇到的风险：分类太好用了，人会忘记它也是一种工作。这是本文的对照，不是说亚里士多德与佛教论师持有同一种学说。

龙树把追问送回这些格子。你用缘起来说明世界，不能到了说明世界的工具这里就停下。格子也有条件。格子也无自性。格子也是空的。

空也是空的。

这是最难松手的一步。如果“空”又成了新地板，你站上去说“这回总是真的了”，不过是换了一种执著。“空”不该变成宇宙背后的材料，也不是把概念全拆完后留下的神秘东西。它所指出的无自性，同样不能再被捏成自性。

众因缘生法，我说即是无，亦为是假名，亦是中道义。

这是《中论·观四谛品》第十八颂的汉译文字，常被引述为“因缘所生法，我说即是空”。这里的“无”不是一概没有，而是无自性。依条件成立，所以说空；“空”也不能脱离所说明的事物，独自变成一个绝对。中道不是在“有”和“无”之间折中，而是不被这两个极端抓住。

五、最彻底的否定

回到本轮的弧线。

孔德灌缝——宣布缝隙不存在。但孔德承认地板存在。波普尔画线——承认缝隙存在，但在缝隙和科学之间划了界限。他也承认地板存在。狄拉克在地板边缘听到了声音。屈原在水里写诗。费希特问认知者在哪里。麦克林托克用身体感觉。薇依用注意力等待。撒切尔关上了认知的门。

把这些人放进这幅文学图景，好像人人脚下都有一处可以暂时用力的地方：分类、检验、方程、诗、身体、注意或决断。这不意味着他们各自的哲学都相信同一种实体地基。只是接到龙树这里，问题要再往脚下落一层。

龙树不让这个地基自动获得豁免。

没有独立自存的地板，没有自性成立的框架。不是不能获得知识，而是认识的人、被认识的事物和认识的过程，都不能脱离条件独自成立。知识可以发生，却不是两个封闭实体之间搬运一件东西。

在本轮的写法里，这是最彻底的一次往下追问。前面八个人让我们讨论缝隙有没有、在哪里、里面有什么。龙树让我们低头：讨论了八篇，也别忘了看看脚下。你踩的那个东西，也是空的。

六、他不是虚无主义

到这里，大多数人的反应是：这不就是虚无主义吗？一切都是空的，那什么都不重要了。

龙树会说：你又执著了。你执著于"空等于不重要"。这个执著本身也需要被空掉。

空不是"没有"。空是"没有自性"。什么叫自性？自性是独立的，不依赖其他东西的，不变的，自己就是自己的存在方式。龙树说：没有任何东西有这种存在方式。一切事物都是因缘和合的——依赖其他东西才存在，随条件变化而变化。

但"因缘和合"的东西不是"不存在"。你坐的椅子是因缘和合的——木头，钉子，工人，设计——但你确实坐在上面。它不是"没有"。它只是没有"自性"——它不是独立的，不变的，自己把自己撑起来的。

空不是把椅子拿走了。空是把你对椅子的误解拿走了。你以为椅子是一个独立的，永恒的，不依赖任何东西的实体。空说：不是。椅子是一堆条件的临时聚合。条件变了，椅子就变了。条件散了，椅子就散了。但只要条件在，椅子就在。

这与“什么都不存在”相去很远。不是所有想象出来的东西都因此成立，而是实际起作用的事物，不必先拥有自性才可以起作用。没有永恒的椅子，仍然可以坐下。

区别巨大。

七、他和薇依

龙树和薇依。一个通常被放在公元二至三世纪的印度佛教思想家，一个二十世纪出身犹太家庭、深受基督教吸引的法国思想家。

他们不是到了同一个终点。可是隔着这么远，某些动作仍然会相互照亮。

薇依的注意，让“我”的急切解释先退后一点，另一个人才能显现。把龙树放到她旁边，他还会追问：这个退后的“我”，以及作为对象的“他”，会不会又被想成两个各自独立成立的东西？

薇依谈重力，也谈不能由自我制造的恩典。她的上帝不是龙树的“空”。不过，“里面”和“外面”能否各自成立，仍可成为一次对读：我们一说恩典从外而来，会不会马上把“外面”画成了另一间房？

在这个对照里，即便“无我”也不能变成新的奖章：我已经没有我了，所以我比你更高。龙树的追问会继续落到这里。连这个可供自满的位置，也不是自足的。

两条路。一条有柏拉图与基督教的回声，一条从佛教缘起与般若传统走来。可以交叉着读，不必把两条路并成一条。

薇依让我们看见“我的撤退”。读龙树，又会多一层不安：我会不会连“撤退”都据为己有？这里没有东方胜过西方的排名。只是刚刚放松的手，也可能换个东西重新抓紧。

八、桥头

在这座想象中的桥上，龙树走过来的时候，没有人注意到他是怎么上桥的。

他就在那里了。好像一直在。

他不像其他人——其他人走上桥，找位置，站下来。龙树没有"站"的姿势。他坐着。盘腿坐着。在桥面上。就坐在那里。没有特别的位置。没有靠左。没有靠右。没有靠边。坐在中间。

孔德的日历翻到了他的页面。格子里写着什么？"哲学/宗教"。龙树看了一眼。微笑了。分类。又是分类。格子是空的。日历也是空的。分类是空的。"哲学"是空的。"宗教"是空的。连"空"也是空的。

波普尔走过来。"你的'空'可以被证伪吗？"

龙树没有立刻回答。在这场想象的相遇里，他也许想先问：你要检验的是哪一个具体主张？说一切无自性，不会让一句话自动免于质疑。就连“可以”和“不可以”的区分，也要看它依什么条件成立。

费希特站在缝隙上，说："我就是缝隙。"龙树看着他。缝隙也是空的。"我"也是空的。站在缝隙上的那个"我"——空。缝隙——空。"站在"——空。全部空掉之后，还剩什么？

还剩龙树坐在那里。

不是作为一个靠自己独立成立的实体。而是条件暂时聚合在这里。条件散了，他不能原样留下。条件还在，他就在。

薇依站在不远处。她在看，没有急着让自己的解释挤到前面。龙树朝她点了点头。不是两套思想终于成了同一种“空”，也不是他们真在历史上相识。是这座桥允许我们想象：两个不肯把自我放在世界中央的人，能够认出彼此的一个动作。

释迦牟尼在很远的地方。前面的008篇就写过他。相隔许多代，龙树仍向那个教人看缘起的方向行了一个礼。不是可核实的三代师承，而是一种致意：我沿着你打开的问题，走到了这里。

桥面。缝隙。地板。全部是空的。但桥还在。人还在。风还在吹。

龙树坐在那里。不生不灭。不来不去。

桥下面，没有一块自立不动的终极地基。桥上面，也没有一个可以替一切盖章的最后答案。两者之间，人们在走。

人们在走。这就够了。[1][2]

I. Eight Negations

Not born, not destroyed. Not permanent, not interrupted. Not one, not different. Not coming, not going.

Eight negations. Four pairs. The opening dedication of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, the Middle Way Verses.

Nāgārjuna is usually placed in the second to third centuries CE. Tradition connects him with southern India; the details of his life are much less secure. His Middle Way Verses runs to roughly five hundred stanzas. These eight negations are like a seed: the arguments about causation, motion, time and existence unfold from here.

“Not born, not destroyed” does not mean that no child is born or no flower fades. “Not permanent, not interrupted” does not erase the connection between today and yesterday. The other pairs do not forbid us to recognize the same chair or say someone has entered a room. They ask something else: take away every condition, and is there a birth, a death, a sameness or a difference that can stand entirely on its own?

The familiar categories—arising and ceasing, permanence and interruption, sameness and difference, coming and going—are all brought back for examination. This is not a quick ruling that this one is right and that one wrong. How did the tools that help us think become an ultimate foundation that supposedly needs no conditions at all?

The previous eight essays discussed the content and methods of cognition — what counts as knowledge (Comte), how to test it (Popper), whether equations can precede observation (Dirac), whether beauty is cognition (Qu Yuan), where the knower stands (Fichte), whether feeling can cognize (McClintock), whether attention is cognition (Weil), when to stop thinking (Thatcher).

Nāgārjuna takes the question down another level. What we know, how we know, who knows: all remain open to questioning. So do the concepts used to ask about them. He is not forbidding speech or judgment. He will not let any tool pass itself off as the final, immovable floor.

Even the floor is empty. You thought you were standing on it discussing the cracks. Nāgārjuna asks you to look down: this board, too, is held up by conditions. It does not support itself.

II. The Dragon Palace

Nāgārjuna's biography is more legend than history.

Kumārajīva translated the Middle Way Verses into Chinese in the early fifth century. A biography traditionally attributed to him as translator tells another kind of story. A brilliant young Brahmin and three friends learn invisibility, enter the royal palace and violate its women. Discovered, the friends are killed; he escapes and enters religious life. This is a cautionary legend, not a verified résumé—and the harm it describes is not merely a clever boy’s mischief.

The legend continues. He reads widely, yet thinks Buddhist teaching still incomplete and even plans a teaching of his own. A great nāga takes him to a palace beneath the sea and opens its treasury of scriptures. What he reads there far exceeds what he had known. He returns to teach Mahāyāna Buddhism and compose treatises.

We need not locate that palace on a map to recognize the turn in the story. A man who thought he had finished learning encounters a vastness he had not imagined. The palace does not merely reward his intelligence. It interrupts his satisfaction with it.

It recalls Dirac, earlier in this sequence. Equations took him beyond what observation had yet reached, pointing toward antimatter—not outside the physical world, but toward an unseen part of it. The palace belongs to legend; the equations belong to a theory open to further testing. These are not interchangeable kinds of evidence. As images, though, both stop “what I already know” from meaning “everything.”

Back in the Middle Way Verses, Nāgārjuna does not ask us to believe him because he has visited a dragon palace. He questions our frameworks one by one. Causation? Examine it. Motion? Examine it. Change? Existence? Those too. Not to delete ordinary experience, but to dismantle the claim that it rests on things existing through their own intrinsic nature.

He offers no replacement floor guaranteed never to move. At the end of the dismantling: empty. And even “empty” must not become another floor.

III. The Tetralemma

One of Nāgārjuna’s characteristic ways of arguing examines all four corners of a question. In Sanskrit, catuṣkoṭi; in English, the tetralemma. In arguments against certain claims of intrinsic existence, all four positions are rejected.

As a plain-language illustration, imagine four places to stand when asking whether something can be established through an intrinsic nature:

It inherently exists.
It inherently does not exist.
It inherently both exists and does not exist.
It inherently neither exists nor does not exist.

You seize on existence, meet an objection and retreat to non-existence. Then to both; then to neither. But if each retreat is another attempt to find a safe home for the same assumption of intrinsic nature, changing the answer may not get you out of the original difficulty.

Four corners may recall Buddhism’s four formless attainments: infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither perception nor non-perception. These concern meditative experience and corresponding realms of existence, not four logical truth values. Infinite space is not another name for the emptiness of all phenomena. Sharing the number four does not establish where the tetralemma came from.

There can be a literary echo between them: you seem to have arrived somewhere, yet cannot make it your final home. But a sequence of meditative attainments is not a four-step argument, and neither perception nor non-perception is not itself liberation. Nāgārjuna is not drawing a philosophical map of four heavens. He asks what entitles an answer to be fixed beyond questioning.

Chinese has an expression, xiǎng rù fēifēi, now often meaning to indulge in wild fantasy. Dictionaries trace its Buddhist background to “neither perception nor non-perception.” A casual remark carries an old echo of contemplative language. That journey is interesting without turning it into proof that the four attainments and the four corners are one system.

Nor can we reduce this to “the West has the excluded middle; Nāgārjuna overturned it.” He is not saying no four times to any arbitrary sentence, or declaring truth and falsehood useless. Often the question is whether all four offered answers carry a shared premise that cannot bear the weight put on it.

That is more than a word game. You thought existence offered a secure place from which to discuss the world. If it means existence independent of conditions, the ground shifts. Retreat to non-existence and you are not automatically safe. Both and neither must undergo the same examination.

You cannot find a foothold that needs no conditions at all.

But Nāgārjuna does not want you to fall. He loosens a misunderstanding: that we cannot live until we find a floor incapable of moving. There are boards, supports, things holding one another up. What is missing is a floor that stands entirely apart from everything and still holds itself up.

Remove the misunderstanding. You are still here. You can stand; you can walk. What supports you need no longer be mistaken for something that will never need support.

IV. Nāgārjuna and the Buddha

Essay 008 introduced the Buddha. He taught people to attend to dependent origination: things arise with conditions and cease with conditions. Nāgārjuna follows this path, pressing the inquiry into intrinsic nature very far.

Buddhist traditions developed precise analyses: five aggregates, twelve sense bases, eighteen elements, accounts of suffering and release. These classifications did not all first appear centuries later, nor did every school treat every concept as inherently real. The danger at issue is more specific: the cells of a grid used to analyze experience can become its supposedly self-established final units.

This recalls a risk encountered in this series’ reading of Aristotle. A classification works so well that we forget the work involved in making it. That is our comparison, not a claim that Aristotle and Buddhist thinkers held one and the same doctrine.

Nāgārjuna turns the inquiry back on those cells. If dependent origination helps explain the world, why stop when we reach the tools of explanation? The grid, too, has conditions. It, too, lacks intrinsic nature. It, too, is empty.

Emptiness is also empty.

Here comes the hardest thing to let go. If emptiness becomes a new floor, and you stand there saying “at last, something truly real,” you have exchanged one attachment for another. Emptiness is not the material behind the universe or a mysterious residue left when all concepts are dismantled. The absence of intrinsic nature cannot itself be made into an intrinsic nature.

What arises through conditions is empty of intrinsic nature. This is a dependent designation; this is the middle way.

That is a plain-language rendering of chapter 24, verse 18. The Chinese verse uses wú, “absence,” where a familiar citation uses “empty”; its commentary makes the connection explicit. Absence here is not the denial of everything. What depends on conditions lacks intrinsic nature. Emptiness cannot then detach itself from what it explains and become an absolute. The middle way is not a compromise halfway between existence and non-existence. It refuses to be caught by either extreme.

V. The Most Radical Negation

Back to this round's arc.

Comte grouted the cracks — declared them nonexistent. But Comte acknowledged the floor exists. Popper drew a line — acknowledged the cracks exist, but drew a boundary between cracks and science. He also acknowledged the floor. Dirac heard sounds at the floor's edge. Qu Yuan wrote poetry in the water. Fichte asked where the knower stands. McClintock used bodily feeling. Weil waited with attention. Thatcher closed the door of cognition.

Within this literary picture, each seems to have somewhere to put a foot: classification, testing, equations, poetry, the body, attention or decision. That does not mean their philosophies share a belief in the same substantial foundation. It means that, on reaching Nāgārjuna, our question moves down toward the ground again.

He will not let that foundation enjoy an automatic exemption.

No independently self-existing floor; no framework established through intrinsic nature. This does not make knowledge impossible. The knower, the known and the activity of knowing all depend on conditions. Knowledge can happen without being an object passed between two sealed, self-sufficient entities.

In the story this sequence tells, this is the deepest downward turn. Eight figures have led us to ask whether cracks exist, where they are and what they contain. Nāgārjuna asks us to look down. After eight essays, remember to examine what you are standing on. That, too, is empty.

VI. He Is Not a Nihilist

At this point, most people react: isn't this nihilism? Everything is empty, so nothing matters.

Nāgārjuna would say: you're clinging again. You're clinging to "empty means not important." That clinging itself needs to be emptied.

Empty doesn't mean "doesn't exist." Empty means "has no inherent existence." What is inherent existence? It is independent, not dependent on anything else, unchanging, self-sustaining being. Nāgārjuna says: nothing has this kind of existence. Everything is dependently arisen — existing only through conditions, changing as conditions change.

But what is dependently arisen is not "non-existent." The chair you're sitting on is dependently arisen — wood, nails, a carpenter, a design — but you are indeed sitting on it. It is not "nothing." It simply has no inherent existence — it is not independent, not permanent, not self-sustaining.

Emptiness doesn't remove the chair. Emptiness removes your misunderstanding of the chair. You thought the chair was independent, eternal, self-standing. Emptiness says: no. The chair is a temporary convergence of conditions. When conditions change, the chair changes. When conditions disperse, the chair disperses. But as long as conditions hold, the chair holds.

That is very far from “nothing exists.” It does not make every imagined thing real. It means that things which actually function do not first need an intrinsic nature in order to function. There is no eternal chair. You can still sit down.

The difference is immense.

VII. Nāgārjuna and Weil

Nāgārjuna and Weil. An Indian Buddhist thinker usually placed in the second to third centuries. A twentieth-century French thinker born into a Jewish family and deeply drawn to Christianity.

They did not reach an identical destination. Across that distance, though, certain gestures can illuminate each other.

Weil’s attention lets the “I,” so eager to explain, step back enough for another person to appear. Read Nāgārjuna beside her and a further question arises: might the withdrawing self and the other it attends to still be imagined as two independently established things?

Weil writes of gravity and of grace that the self cannot manufacture. Her God is not Nāgārjuna’s emptiness. Yet inside and outside can still become questions in a reading across the two. Say grace comes from outside, and do we immediately picture that outside as another room?

In this comparison, even “no-self” must not become a new medal: I have no self now, so I am above you. Nāgārjuna’s inquiry reaches that point of satisfaction too. It cannot sustain itself any more than the position it replaced.

Two paths. One carries echoes of Plato and Christianity. The other comes through Buddhist dependent origination and the wisdom tradition. We can read them across one another without turning them into one road.

Weil shows us the retreat of “I.” Reading Nāgārjuna adds an uneasiness: could I make even my retreat into something I possess? This is no ranking of East above West. The hand that has just loosened can always close around something else.

VIII. The Bridgehead

On this imagined bridge, no one notices how Nāgārjuna arrives.

He is simply there. As if he always has been.

He doesn't do what the others do — walk onto the bridge, find a spot, stand. Nāgārjuna doesn't "stand." He sits. Cross-legged. On the bridge surface. Just there. No particular spot. Not left. Not right. Not to the side. In the middle.

Comte's calendar turns to his page. What does the grid say? "Philosophy/Religion." Nāgārjuna glances at it. Smiles. Classification. More classification. The grid is empty. The calendar is empty. The classification is empty. "Philosophy" is empty. "Religion" is empty. Even "empty" is empty.

Popper walks over. "Can your 'emptiness' be falsified?"

Nāgārjuna does not answer immediately. In this imagined encounter, perhaps he wants to ask first: which particular claim are you testing? Saying that things lack intrinsic nature cannot exempt a sentence from criticism. Even the distinction between “can” and “cannot” must be considered in the conditions that make it work.

Fichte stands on the crack. He says: "I am the crack." Nāgārjuna looks at him. The crack is also empty. "I" is also empty. The "I" standing on the crack — empty. The crack — empty. "Standing on" — empty. After everything is emptied, what remains?

Nāgārjuna, sitting there, remains.

Not as an entity established entirely by itself. Conditions have converged here for a while. When they disperse, he cannot remain unchanged. While they hold, he is here.

Weil stands nearby. She is looking, not hurrying to put her explanation in front of what she sees. Nāgārjuna nods. Not because two philosophies have become one emptiness, or because they met in history. The bridge lets us imagine two people unwilling to place the self at the center of the world recognizing a gesture in each other.

The Buddha is far away; we met him in Essay 008. Many generations separate them. Nāgārjuna bows toward the one who taught people to see dependent origination. Not a documented chain of three teachers, but an acknowledgment: I followed a question you opened, and it brought me here.

The bridge surface. The cracks. The floor. All empty. But the bridge is here. People are here. The wind is still blowing.

Nāgārjuna sits there. Not born, not destroyed. Not coming, not going.

Below the bridge, no self-supporting ultimate foundation. Above it, no final answer authorized to put a seal on everything. Between the two, people are walking.

People are walking. That is enough.[1][2]
