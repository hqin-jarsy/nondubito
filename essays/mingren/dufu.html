杜甫，被凿成诗的人

Du Fu, Carved into Poetry

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、会当凌绝顶

杜甫年轻的时候，写过一首关于泰山的诗。

岱宗夫如何，齐鲁青未了。
造化钟神秀，阴阳割昏晓。
荡胸生层云，决眦入归鸟。
会当凌绝顶，一览众山小。

“会当凌绝顶，一览众山小。”——总有一天，我要登上最高峰，看见众山在脚下变小。

这是一个二十多岁的年轻人的声音。他漫游齐赵，天地之间，似乎没有什么是他不敢想的。家世追溯到晋代名将杜预，祖父杜审言是初唐诗人。他饱读诗书，后来把自己的志向写得很清楚：“致君尧舜上，再使风俗淳。”辅佐君王超越尧舜，让天下恢复淳朴。

他想做官。想进入庙堂。想让孔子所向往的政治理想，在自己的时代落地。

往后三十余年，现实一刀一刀，凿向这首诗里昂扬的自信。

二、野无遗贤

他考进士，落了第。

一般把这次应举系于735年。他二十出头。落榜以后，他继续漫游；一次失意，还没有终结他的前程。

747年，唐玄宗下诏征求有一技之长的人才。已经三十多岁的杜甫，又赴长安应试。这不是前一次进士考试的简单重来，而是皇帝特召所开的一道门。

《资治通鉴》记载，宰相李林甫担心草野之士直言朝政，使考试层层筛选，最后竟无一人及第。然后，他向玄宗道贺：“野无遗贤。”民间已经没有遗漏的人才了。

门关上了，关门的人却把这当作太平盛世的证明。

对抱着用世之志来长安的人，这个笑话并不好笑。杜甫此前已经来到京城；落选之后，等待还要继续。

三、长安十年

从大约746年到755年，杜甫在长安困了将近十年。

他写诗投赠权贵，希望得到引荐。这叫“干谒”——带着自己的作品，去敲有权势的人的门。长安前期写的《奉赠韦左丞丈二十二韵》，已经有了后来这段岁月的苦味：

致君尧舜上，再使风俗淳。
此意竟萧条，行歌非隐沦。
骑驴三十载，旅食京华春。
朝扣富儿门，暮随肥马尘。
残杯与冷炙，到处潜悲辛。

“致君尧舜上，再使风俗淳”——这是他的用世理想。紧接着，就是“此意竟萧条”：理想落空了，他却还不是一个甘心隐退的人。

早上敲富贵人家的门，晚上跟在肥马扬起的尘土后面。残酒，冷肉，寄人篱下的悲辛。诗把这些经历压在一起，压得人喘不过气。

那个说要登上绝顶的人，在长安的马蹄尘土里，等了一年又一年。

755年，他终于得到一个小官：右卫率府胄曹参军，掌管太子卫府的兵甲等事务。不是治国的中枢，而是庞大官僚机构的一角。

一个想“致君尧舜上”的人，苦等多年，等到了兵甲簿籍。

他接了。此前获授河西尉，他没有赴任；县尉的奔走与折腰让他畏惮。《官定后戏赠》写得直白：“老夫怕趋走，率府且逍遥。”他需要一点俸禄，也想留住一点自己。

这就是现实给他的第一刀。不是一下砍落，是十年的钝刀慢慢磨。

四、幼子饥已卒

755年岁末，安史之乱爆发前后。杜甫刚得到那份小小的俸禄，便从长安去奉先县，看望寄居在那里的妻儿。

《自京赴奉先县咏怀五百字》把两个世界放在同一条路上：骊山宫廷的暖意、宴饮与丝绸，路边的寒冷、饥饿与死亡。那些华美织物，又出自辛苦劳作的人手里。

他到了家。

“入门闻号咷，幼子饥已卒。”

推开门，听到哭声。他的小儿子，饿死了。

“吾宁舍一哀，里巷亦呜咽。所愧为人父，无食致夭折。”——我岂能不为孩子哀哭？邻里也在呜咽。最难承受的，是做父亲的羞愧：没能让孩子吃饱，没能让他活下去。

他已经是帝国的一个小官。他的孩子，还是饿死了。

同一首诗里，还有那两句：

“朱门酒肉臭，路有冻死骨。”

朱门里面是酒肉的气味，道路上是冻死的白骨。丰盛与死亡，隔着一扇门。

诗中，这两句出现在抵家以前；痛苦却不按行程表分隔。一个丧子的父亲，把宫廷、道路与自己的家写进同一幅图景。社会的不平，不再只是一个在远处讨论的问题。

这是第二刀。这一刀比仕途的困顿更深。它凿进了他的家。

五、国破山河在

756年，长安陷落，玄宗逃往四川，肃宗在灵武即位。杜甫把家人安置在鄜州，自己投奔肃宗。

途中，他落入叛军手中，身陷长安。京城还在，却已不是原来的京城。

757年春天，他写了《春望》。

国破山河在，城春草木深。
感时花溅泪，恨别鸟惊心。
烽火连三月，家书抵万金。
白头搔更短，浑欲不胜簪。

山河还在，人的秩序碎了。春天照常来临，草木深长，花与鸟也成了伤心之物。

战火延绵，一封家书重过万金。消息为什么这么贵？因为不知道亲人还活着没有。

白发越搔越短，几乎插不住簪子了。天下的破碎，最后落在一个人不自觉摸向头发的手上。

后来，他逃到了凤翔。“麻鞋见天子，衣袖露两肘。”麻鞋，破袖，露在外面的胳膊肘。肃宗授他左拾遗，一个可以向皇帝进谏的职位。

这是他离“致君尧舜上”很近的时刻。终于可以说话了。

他果然说了。房琯被罢相，杜甫上疏替他辩护。肃宗震怒，命人审问；宰相张镐以不能堵塞言路相救，他才免于获罪。

事情没有当天就以贬官收场。但信任已受损。758年，他出任华州司功参军；759年，关辅饥荒，他弃官，带着家人西行。

这不是他从此再不任职——后来他还会进入严武幕府。可是通向理想的那条大路，再一次在脚下断了。

这是第三刀。最靠近庙堂的时候，他也看见了庙堂怎样对待一句不愿吞回去的话。

六、安得广厦千万间

759年末，他到了成都。在亲友帮助下，次年在浣花溪边营建草堂。后来，严武也给了他重要的支持。一个流离的人，终于有了一段可以安顿的日子。

761年农历八月，秋风来了。

八月秋高风怒号，卷我屋上三重茅。
茅飞度江洒江郊，高者挂罥长林梢，下者飘转沈塘坳。
南村群童欺我老无力，忍能对面为盗贼。
公然抱茅入竹去，唇焦口燥呼不得，归来倚杖自叹息。

屋顶飞走了。诗里的老人看着孩子抱茅跑进竹林，气得骂他们是盗贼，喊得口干，也喊不回来。他拄杖回家，叹气。

接着，天黑了，雨落下来。

俄顷风定云墨色，秋天漠漠向昏黑。
布衾多年冷似铁，娇儿恶卧踏里裂。
床头屋漏无干处，雨脚如麻未断绝。
自经丧乱少睡眠，长夜沾湿何由彻！

旧被子冷得像铁，里面的布让孩子睡觉时踢破了。床边没有一处干的地方。自从丧乱以来，就很少睡好；这一夜又湿又长，什么时候才能熬到头？

写到这里，已经足够苦了。失去屋顶的人，有理由只想着自己的屋顶。

但诗在最低处，突然转向了最高处：

安得广厦千万间，大庇天下寒士俱欢颜，风雨不动安如山！
呜呼！何时眼前突兀见此屋，吾庐独破受冻死亦足！

怎样才有千万间大屋，庇护天下贫寒的读书人，让他们都展颜，在风雨里安稳如山？什么时候眼前能出现这样的房子？倘若真能如此，哪怕只剩我的屋子破了，我受冻而死，也甘愿。

自己的屋子破了，他的眼光却越过了自己的屋子。“寒士”首先是贫寒士人；我们还可以从这个转身里，看见一种能够继续向所有受苦者打开的关怀。

最后一句，是诗人把愿望推到极处的声音，不是要求每个受苦的人牺牲自己。令人震动的，不是冻死才算善，而是他没有让自己的寒冷成为看不见别人的理由。

这是我在杜甫身上读到的“仁”。孔子对不同的人给出不同的回答；杜甫用一间漏雨的屋子，作了自己的回应。抽象的字，忽然有了湿被子的重量。

七、天地一沙鸥

765年，严武死了。杜甫失去一个重要的依靠，离开成都，沿江东下。

《旅夜书怀》通常就系于这一年。夜色里，有岸边细草，有船上高高的桅杆：

细草微风岸，危樯独夜舟。
星垂平野阔，月涌大江流。
名岂文章著，官应老病休。
飘飘何所似？天地一沙鸥。

“名岂文章著，官应老病休。”声名难道只系于文章？做官难道真只因为老病而结束？两句自问里，有不能说尽的不平。

他并非不愿写诗。他长久经营诗艺，甚至说过“语不惊人死不休”。只是，诗名不能替代他盼望的用世。一条路越走越窄，另一种声音越写越深。

“飘飘何所似？天地一沙鸥。”那么，我像什么？辽阔天地之间，一只无所依的沙鸥。

766年到768年，他在夔州住了近两年。767年的《登高》，后来得到明代胡应麟“古今七言律第一”的极高评价。再以后，他继续漂向湖南。

风急天高猿啸哀，渚清沙白鸟飞回。
无边落木萧萧下，不尽长江滚滚来。
万里悲秋常作客，百年多病独登台。
艰难苦恨繁霜鬓，潦倒新停浊酒杯。

“万里悲秋常作客，百年多病独登台。”十四个字，两句诗。远在万里，长久为客；一生多病，独自登临。

“万里”，把空间拉远；“百年”，把一生铺开；“悲秋”，带来季节的哀意；“多病”，落到身体；“常作客”，没有安居；“独登台”，没有同伴。

这样读，六层苦叠在一联里。不是苦难自己长成了诗，而是诗人把它们安排得如此紧密，又让大江在旁边滚滚流着。

八、无成涕作霖

770年，杜甫五十多岁，按当时的虚岁算是五十九。他晚年的诗里，陆续出现“右臂偏枯半耳聋”“病渴”“老年花似雾中看”：衰弱的手臂，听不清的耳朵，干渴，模糊的眼睛。

湖南的水路上，一个久病的人，仍在写诗。

《风疾舟中伏枕书怀三十六韵奉呈湖南亲友》，是他最晚的一批作品之一。题目把病、船、伏枕与亲友都留在了纸上。不是一个已经与世界断开的人；他还在向人说话。

诗的末尾写道：

战血流依旧，军声动至今。
葛洪尸定解，许靖力还任。
家事丹砂诀，无成涕作霖。

血还在流，军声还在响。身体已经这样疲惫，战争却没有随他的疲惫停下来。

葛洪的仙去，许靖老而任事，家传的丹砂之诀，都挤在最后几句里。求长生也好，想有所用也好，“无成”接住了这些未遂的愿望；泪下如雨。

这里有晚年的失落，却不能把两个字当作他对整个人生已经签字的判决。诗说出了疼痛，不等于替人生结了账。

他死于770年。具体地点与死因，后世有不同说法。《新唐书》留下了在耒阳饮酒吃牛肉后去世的故事；更早的元稹墓铭只说他在旅居中去世，暂殡岳阳，并没有给出病因。

他当然看不见自己的后来：宋代以来不断升高的地位，“诗圣”与“诗史”的声名，以及千百年以后仍有人念着“安得广厦千万间”。

他想入仕，想治国，想把君王推向尧舜。现实一次次凿伤他的前程、家与身体，却没有把他凿成一个只能看见自己的人。

许多未能实现的东西，留在了诗里。

九、诗圣

后人称李白为诗仙，王维为诗佛，杜甫为诗圣。三个称号，像三束不同的光。

《诗人玉屑》引杨万里说：“李神于诗，杜圣于诗。”这是诗学上的评价，不是说李白有天才而杜甫没有，也不是说只要受够苦，就自然能成为杜甫。

在后来的文化称号里，孔子是文圣，关羽是武圣；“圣”常常承载着一种值得人追随的典范。杜甫之所以被我们这样阅读，不只因为作品精严，也因为那份精严没有把人的痛苦关在门外。

“安得广厦千万间，大庇天下寒士俱欢颜。”这里有心，也有让心抵达读者的技巧。二者不是敌人。

所以，“被凿成诗”不是说他没有选择、没有创造。现实凿掉的，不是一个人的全部能动性，而是他原来以为可以安放志向的道路。他亲手把剩下的经验、愤怒与牵挂，构成了诗。

他想构成的，还有仕途、政治与一个更好的天下。诗接住了其中无法按原样实现的余项。

沿着本系列的比喻：庄子被推回混沌——凿开以后，余项大到不能封口；杜甫被推向诗——凿开以后，余项深到需要另一种形式来盛放。

庄子的余项，让我们重新面对混沌。杜甫的余项，让我们听见诗。

孔子问：“天何言哉？”杜甫写：“安得广厦千万间。”这不是后人终于修补了前人的失败，而是两种声音彼此照见：一种不断问仁怎样落实，一种把问题带进寒夜的屋子。

仁，未必只有一种定义。这里有一种不能轻易忘掉的姿态：我正在受冻，仍愿意让你有地方避寒。

于是，在这篇文章想象的桥头，又多了一个人。一个瘦削的老人，从漂泊的船上走来。不用金色外套或粗布衣裳表明身份；我想起他旧诗里的破袖，胳膊肘露在外面。

但他的诗在。一千两百多年了。还在。

注释

本文以杜甫诗作、元稹《唐故工部员外郎杜君墓系铭》及《新唐书》卷二〇一为基本材料，参照香港中文大学《译丛》作家资料等。诗是经过构思的文学作品，不是逐日录音；标题中的“凿”、三次“刀”及桥头场景，属于本文的解释与文学想象。

735年进士落第、747年特召试、746—755年长安时期采用常见编年；747年的政治背景见《资治通鉴》卷二一五。年龄不混用周岁与虚岁。奉先之行一般系于755年岁末安史乱爆发前后，不据诗的叙述顺序认定每个场景的实际见闻时间。757年房琯事件、758年华州任职、759年弃官与764年入严武幕应当分开。

诗句据传世杜集、《全唐诗》等核对，保留本文选用的文字。“骑驴三十载”在《全唐诗》卷二一六有据，另有“十三载”等异文；《望岳》“层”亦有“曾”本。“朱门酒肉臭”的“臭”有香气与腐臭等不同读法，正文不将其中一种说成定论。《茅屋为秋风所破歌》补全风定云黑两句；“寒士”先指贫寒士人，向所有受苦者的延伸是本文的阅读。“吾宁舍一哀”的“宁”按反问读，亦有让步式解读。外语诗文均据中文独立译写。

《旅夜书怀》通常系于765年，《登高》系于767年夔州；“古今七言律第一”是胡应麟《诗薮》的评价，不是可客观排名的事实。“李神于诗，杜圣于诗”见《诗人玉屑》卷十四引诚斋杨万里；它不能简化为天才与非天才的区别。杜甫“语不惊人死不休”见《江上值水如海势聊短述》。

晚年诗中的“风疾”“病渴”等不能直接确诊为现代所称中风或糖尿病。《风疾舟中伏枕书怀三十六韵奉呈湖南亲友》是晚期诗作，并非已经证实的绝笔；“无成”紧接丹砂求仙的语境，也可引发更广的人生感慨，不宜等同于明确的临终心理记录。新、旧史与后世传说对死地死因各有叙述；元稹“竟以寓卒，旋殡岳阳”并没有确证某种病死原因。

仁、余项与“被凿成诗”是本系列的解释框架，不是杜甫自述的理论。诗中愿意独自受冻的极言，不构成要求受害者自我牺牲的义务；对杜甫的理解也不以贬低孔子、李白、王维或否认诗人的技艺为前提。

I. I Shall Reach the Summit

When Du Fu was young, he wrote a poem about Mount Tai.

What is this mountain, greatest of the east?
Its green runs on through Qi and Lu.
Creation gathers here its wondrous beauty;
shade and sunlight cut dusk from dawn.
Clouds rise in layers, stirring my breast;
I strain my eyes after birds coming home.
One day I shall reach the very summit
and see how small the other mountains are.

One day I shall reach the summit. From there, the mountains will look small. The future is still something he can climb.

This is the voice of a man in his twenties, travelling through Qi and Zhao. Nothing seems beyond imagining. His ancestry reached back to the Jin general Du Yu; his grandfather Du Shenyan was an early Tang poet. He had read deeply. Later he would state his ambition plainly: to raise his ruler above Yao and Shun, the ancient sage-kings, and restore simplicity to the people's ways.

He wanted office. A place at court. He wanted the political hopes associated with Confucius to take shape in his own age.

Over the next thirty-odd years, reality would take a chisel to the confidence of that mountain poem.

II. No Talent Left Outside

He sat the jinshi examination and failed.

The attempt is generally dated to 735, when he was in his early twenties. Afterwards he went on travelling. One disappointment had not yet brought his prospects to an end.

In 747, Emperor Xuanzong summoned men with exceptional abilities for a special examination. Du Fu, now in his thirties, tried again in Chang'an. This was a new imperial opening, not simply a repeat of the earlier jinshi examination.

The Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government records that the chief minister Li Linfu feared criticism from candidates outside official circles. He arranged successive screenings; in the end, no one passed. Then he congratulated the emperor: no worthy talent remained undiscovered among the people.

The door was shut. The man who shut it called that proof of an enlightened reign.

For those who had come hoping to serve, the joke was bitter. Du Fu had already settled in the capital. After the examination, the waiting continued.

III. Ten Years in Chang'an

From about 746 to 755, Du Fu spent nearly a decade struggling in Chang'an.

He addressed poems to powerful men, hoping for a recommendation. To seek patronage was to take your work to somebody else's door. A poem offered to Vice Director Wei, written early in these years, already has the taste of what followed:

To raise my ruler above Yao and Shun,
to make the people's ways simple again—
this hope has withered; though I sing as I go,
I am no man content to hide from the world.
Thirty years riding a donkey;
spring in the capital, eating at others' tables.
At dawn I knock on rich men's doors;
at dusk I follow the dust of well-fed horses.
Leftover wine and cold scraps of meat:
everywhere, sorrow bitterly concealed.

The ambition comes first; immediately after it comes desolation. The hope has failed, but he cannot yet become a willing recluse.

Rich men's doors in the morning. Horse-dust at night. Stale wine, cold meat, dependence. The poem presses these experiences together until there is hardly room to breathe.

The man who meant to climb the highest peak waited, year after year, in the dust of the capital's horses.

In 755 he finally received a minor appointment, administering arms and related affairs in a guard office of the crown prince. Not the centre of government: a corner of its great machinery.

He had hoped to make a ruler greater than the sage-kings. After years of waiting, there were arms and registers to look after.

He accepted. He had declined an earlier appointment as county lieutenant at Hexi, recoiling from its exhausting errands and humiliations. His poem on the appointment is blunt: the old man fears all that running about; let him find a little ease in the guard office. He needed a salary. He also wanted to keep something of himself.

This was reality's first cut. Not one sudden blow, but a blunt blade grinding for ten years.

IV. My Youngest Child Had Starved

Late in 755, around the outbreak of the An Lushan rebellion, Du Fu left Chang'an for Fengxian, where his wife and children were staying. He had only recently secured that small official income.

His long poem on the journey sets two worlds along one road: the warmth, feasting and silks of the court at Mount Li, and the cold, hunger and death outside. Those splendid fabrics had themselves come from the hands of people working hard to survive.

He reached home.

Through the door I heard the wailing;
my youngest child had starved to death.

A door opens. Crying. His little son is dead.

How could I withhold my mourning?
The neighbours, too, are sobbing.
Ashamed to be his father—
no food, and a young life ended.
The shame is almost harder to bear than the loss: he had not been able to feed the child, had not kept him alive.

He was already a minor servant of the empire. His child still starved.

In the same poem stand two other lines:

Behind vermilion gates, the smell of wine and meat;
along the road, the bones of those who froze.

Abundance and death. Between them, a gate.

These lines occur before the homecoming in the poem. But grief does not obey a travel schedule. A bereaved father brings court, road and household into one picture. Inequality is no longer something to discuss from a safe distance.

This was the second cut. Deeper than frustrated advancement. It cut into his home.

V. The Country Broken, Hills and Rivers Remain

In 756 Chang'an fell. Xuanzong fled towards Sichuan; Suzong assumed the throne at Lingwu. Du Fu left his family in Fuzhou and set out to join the new emperor.

Rebels intercepted him. He found himself trapped in Chang'an. The capital was still there, but it was no longer the same capital.

In the spring of 757, he wrote Spring Prospect.

The country broken, hills and rivers remain;
spring in the city, grass and trees grow deep.
Grieving for the times, tears fall at flowers;
hating separation, birds startle the heart.
War-beacons burn through month after month;
a letter from home is worth a fortune in gold.
I scratch my white hair, thinner each time—
soon it will scarcely hold a pin.

The landscape survives; the human order is shattered. Spring arrives on time. Plants flourish. Even flowers and birds have become occasions for pain.

War goes on. Why should a letter be worth so much? Because you do not know whether those you love are still alive.

White hair grows thinner under the hand. The world's breaking comes to rest in one person's unconscious gesture.

Eventually he escaped to Fengxiang. In hemp sandals I came before the emperor; my sleeves left both elbows bare. Suzong appointed him Left Reminder, an official entitled to remonstrate with the ruler.

Now he was close to the ambition of guiding a sovereign. At last he could speak.

He did. When Fang Guan lost the chief ministership, Du Fu submitted a defence. Suzong ordered an investigation in anger. Another minister, Zhang Gao, argued that punishing him would close the channel for criticism; Du Fu escaped condemnation.

He was not demoted to Huazhou that very day. But trust had suffered. In 758 he took a provincial post there; in 759, amid famine in the metropolitan region, he left office and headed west with his family.

This was not a final renunciation of all employment: he would later join Yan Wu's staff. Yet the broad road towards his ideal had broken beneath him again.

The third cut. At his nearest approach to court, he learned what the court could do to a sentence its speaker would not swallow.

VI. How Can We Find Ten Thousand Great Houses?

He reached Chengdu late in 759. With help from relatives and friends, he built a thatched cottage beside Huanhua Stream the following year. Yan Wu later became an important supporter. For a while, a displaced man had somewhere to settle.

In the eighth lunar month of 761, the autumn wind came.

High autumn, eighth month: the wind roars in anger,
sweeps three layers of thatch from my roof.
Thatch flies over the stream, scatters across its banks;
some hangs high in the tops of tall trees,
some whirls down into hollows by the pond.
The village children mock my age and weakness,
bold enough to rob me before my face.
They carry the thatch openly into the bamboo.
My lips are scorched with shouting; I cannot call them back.
I return, lean on my stick and sigh.

The roof has flown away. In the poem, the old man watches children carrying the thatch into the bamboo and angrily calls them thieves. His mouth dries out from shouting. Nothing comes back. He leans on his stick and goes home.

Then darkness, and rain.

Soon the wind falls; the clouds turn ink-black.
Autumn's vast dimness thickens into night.
The old cloth quilt is cold as iron;
my restless child has kicked its lining apart.
The roof leaks by the bed: nowhere dry.
Rain falls, unbroken, thick as hemp.
Since war began, sleep has been rare—
how shall I live through this long, sodden night?

The old quilt is cold as iron, its lining torn by a child who kicks in sleep. There is nowhere dry by the bed. War has already made sleep difficult. Now this wet night seems endless.

That would be enough suffering for a poem. Someone without a roof has every reason to think about his own roof.

But at its lowest point, the poem suddenly turns towards its greatest height:

How can we find ten thousand great houses,
sheltering all the world's poor scholars, their faces bright,
unmoved by wind or rain, secure as mountains?
Ah, when shall I see those houses rise before me?
Let my own hut alone fall apart;
I would accept death in the cold, if that could be.

Let them have shelter. Let them smile. Let their homes stand firm in the storm. If such houses could truly appear, he would accept the loss of his own.

His roof is broken, yet his gaze travels beyond it. The poem first names impoverished scholars. In that turning outward, we can also read a concern capable of reaching other sufferers.

The last line drives a personal wish to its furthest point. It does not command every victim to sacrifice themselves. What moves me is not the notion that freezing to death makes a person good, but that his own cold has not made everyone else invisible.

This is one shape of Confucian humaneness, ren, that I find in Du Fu. Confucius answered different people differently. Du Fu makes his own reply with a leaking house. An abstract word suddenly takes on the weight of a wet quilt.

VII. One Gull Between Earth and Sky

Yan Wu died in 765. Losing an important source of support, Du Fu left Chengdu and travelled downriver.

Thoughts Written on a Night Journey is usually dated to that year. In the darkness: grass along the bank, a tall mast over a boat.

Fine grass, a breeze along the bank;
a towering mast, one boat at night.
Stars hang low over the widening plain;
moonlight surges with the great river.
Can writing alone have made my name?
Did age and illness truly end my service?
Drifting, drifting—what am I like?
One gull between earth and sky.

Is writing all that made his name? Was old age really the reason for leaving office? Beneath those questions lies more grievance than they can explicitly say.

It was not that he never wanted to write. He worked at poetry throughout his life, declaring that he would not rest even in death unless his words could astonish. But literary fame could not replace public service. As one road narrowed, another voice deepened.

What am I like? One gull, with nowhere to rest, between the vast earth and sky.

From 766 to 768 he lived for nearly two years in Kuizhou. Ascending the Heights, written there in 767, would later be acclaimed by the Ming critic Hu Yinglin as the foremost seven-character regulated poem of all time. Afterwards, Du Fu wandered on towards Hunan.

Fierce wind, high sky, the apes' cries grieving;
clear islets, white sand, birds wheel home.
Endless leaves fall, rustling, rustling;
the Yangtze rolls on, never exhausted.
Far from home, grieving in autumn, always a guest;
a lifetime of illness, I climb the terrace alone.
Hardship and bitter sorrow thicken my frosted temples;
worn down, I have lately set aside my cloudy wine.

Two lines, fourteen Chinese characters: far away, always a guest; a lifetime of illness, climbing alone.

Great distance stretches space. A lifetime opens time. Autumn brings the season's sadness. Illness brings it into the body. Always a guest: no settled home. Climbing alone: no companion.

Read this way, six layers of suffering lie inside one couplet. Suffering has not spontaneously become poetry. A poet has placed these things together, so tightly, while letting the great river run beside them.

VIII. Nothing Accomplished, Tears Like Rain

In 770 Du Fu was in his late fifties—fifty-nine by traditional Chinese reckoning. His late poems speak of a withered right arm and failing hearing, of thirst and illness, of flowers seen through a mist of ageing eyes.

On the waterways of Hunan, a chronically ill man was still writing.

Lying Ill on a Boat with Wind Sickness: Thoughts in Thirty-Six Rhymes, Presented to Relatives and Friends in Hunan belongs among his latest works. The title holds illness, boat, sickbed and friends together. He has not severed his connection with the world. He is still addressing someone.

The poem ends:

Blood flows in battle as before;
the sounds of armies shake us still.
Ge Hong surely slipped his mortal body;
Xu Jing in old age could still bear office.
My family's art: the cinnabar elixir—
nothing achieved, tears falling like long rain.

Blood still flows. Armies still sound. His body is exhausted, but war does not end because he is tired.

Ge Hong's immortality, Xu Jing's service in old age, the family's cinnabar secrets: all crowd into the closing lines. Longer life, continued usefulness—nothing accomplished gathers these unfulfilled hopes, and the tears fall.

There is disappointment here. But two words are not a signed verdict on an entire life. The poem voices pain; it does not settle the accounts of existence.

He died in 770. The precise place and cause remain disputed. The New History of the Tang tells a story of death after drinking wine and eating beef at Leiyang. Yuan Zhen's earlier epitaph says only that he died away from home and was provisionally buried at Yueyang; it supplies no medical cause.

He could not see his afterlife: the stature he would acquire from the Song onward, the names poet-sage and poetry-history, the people still repeating his wish for ten thousand houses centuries later.

He had wanted office, government, a sovereign raised towards the sage-kings. Reality wounded his prospects, his family and his body again and again. It did not turn him into someone who could see only himself.

Much that could not be accomplished remained in the poems.

IX. The Poet-Sage

Later readers called Li Bai the poet-immortal, Wang Wei the poet-Buddha and Du Fu the poet-sage. Three names, three kinds of light.

The anthology Jade Fragments of Poets quotes Yang Wanli: Li is divine in poetry; Du is sagely. This is a critical judgement about poetry, not proof that Li Bai had genius and Du Fu did not, or that enough suffering will automatically produce a Du Fu.

In later cultural honorifics, Confucius is the civil sage and Guan Yu the martial sage. Sage can name an exemplary life. We read Du Fu this way not merely because his verse is finely wrought, but because that precision does not shut human suffering outside.

Ten thousand great houses; the poor sheltered, their faces bright. There is heart here, and the art that allows a heart to reach another person. They are not enemies.

Carved into poetry does not mean he had no agency or creative choice. What reality cut away was the road on which he had expected to place his ambition—not every power to act. He himself made experience, anger and concern into poems.

He had also wanted to build a public career, a political order, a better world. Poetry received the remainder of what could not be realised in its intended form.

In this series' metaphor, Zhuangzi is driven back towards Hundun, the undivided: the remainder is too large for the opening to close. Du Fu is driven towards poetry: the remainder is so deep that it needs another form to hold it.

Zhuangzi's remainder returns us to the undivided. Du Fu's remainder lets us hear a poem.

Confucius asks, What does Heaven say? Du Fu asks for ten thousand great houses. This is not a later man repairing a predecessor's failure. Two voices illuminate each other: one keeps asking how humaneness can take effect; the other brings that question inside a house on a cold night.

Humaneness need not have only one definition. Here is a gesture not easily forgotten: I am cold, and I still want you to have shelter.

At the imagined bridgehead of this essay, another person arrives. A thin old man comes from a boat that has carried him from place to place. No golden coat or rough-spun robe need announce his identity. I remember the torn sleeves of his earlier poem, his elbows showing through.

But his poetry remains. More than twelve hundred years. Still here.

Notes

The basic sources are Du Fu's poems, Yuan Zhen's epitaph for him and the New History of the Tang, chapter 201, with reference to institutional accounts such as the Chinese University of Hong Kong's Renditions author profile. Poems are composed works, not daily transcripts. The chisel, three cuts and bridgehead belong to this essay's interpretation and literary imagination.

The dates 735 for the jinshi failure, 747 for the special examination and approximately 746–755 for the Chang'an years follow standard chronologies. The political account of the 747 examination appears in the Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government, chapter 215. Traditional and modern age reckoning are distinguished. The Fengxian journey is usually placed around the end of 755, near the outbreak of rebellion; the poem's order does not establish the exact sequence of witnessed events. The Fang Guan affair of 757, Huazhou appointment of 758, resignation of 759 and service under Yan Wu in 764 are separate episodes.

Chinese texts were checked against transmitted collections, including the Complete Tang Poems. Thirty years in the poem to Wei is supported by chapter 216; thirteen years is among the variants. The mountain poem also has variants for layered clouds. The word chou/xiu in the vermilion-gates couplet has been read as an odour or as putrefaction; no single reading is asserted here. The thatched-hut poem includes the two lines about the darkening clouds. Its poor scholars should not silently become all humanity: that wider reach is this essay's interpretation. The mourning question reads ning interrogatively; a concessive reading also exists. All non-Chinese verse renderings were independently made from the Chinese.

The night-journey poem is usually dated to 765 and Ascending the Heights to 767 in Kuizhou. Its superlative ranking is Hu Yinglin's judgement in Shisou, not an objective league table. The comparison of Li's divinity and Du's sageliness appears in Jade Fragments of Poets, chapter 14, attributed to Yang Wanli; it does not reduce to genius versus non-genius. Du Fu's vow to find astonishing words occurs in his short poem on a river rising like the sea.

Terms such as wind sickness and thirst illness cannot establish a retrospective diagnosis of stroke or diabetes. The long boat poem is a late work, not a proven final poem. Nothing accomplished follows the cinnabar-elixir allusion; it can suggest broader frustration without being treated as a verbatim record of his dying thoughts. Accounts of his death differ. Yuan Zhen's statement that he died while away and was provisionally buried at Yueyang establishes no particular medical cause.

Humaneness, the remainder and being carved into poetry are this series' interpretive terms, not a theory Du Fu formulated. His extreme wish to accept the cold himself creates no obligation for victims to sacrifice themselves. This reading does not depend on diminishing Confucius, Li Bai or Wang Wei, or on denying the poet's deliberate artistry.
