玛丽·斯克沃多夫斯卡，不止是一个名字

Maria Skłodowska, More Than a Name

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、她的名字

我们习惯叫她“居里夫人”。先把那个名字说完整一点：玛丽·斯克沃多夫斯卡—居里。Maria是她的波兰名字，在法国，她用Marie。1867年，她出生在华沙。

居里并不是别人强加给她、我们有权替她删掉的姓。她自己也使用它。问题不在于一个女人婚后用什么姓，而在于，我们听见“夫人”两个字的时候，是看见了一个研究者，还是只看见某个人的妻子？

1903年的诺贝尔物理学奖，一度可能只表彰贝克勒尔与皮埃尔。瑞典数学家米塔格—莱夫勒提醒皮埃尔，皮埃尔回信强调玛丽在研究中的贡献。最终，三个人一起获奖。她的工作并不是等丈夫承认之后才存在，但承认能否进入制度，竟仍需要这样的一封信。

1911年，她独自获得化学奖，成为第一个两次获得诺贝尔奖的人。那时皮埃尔已经去世五年。我们当然可以继续说“居里夫人”，只是别让这四个字把她重新放回某个人的身后。

她是这个系列写到的第一位女性。这首先暴露的是我们这份名单的局限，不是历史上直到她才有值得记住的女人。教育、职位、出版与纪念的门开给谁，决定了哪些名字更容易来到我们面前。

这一篇，从把人看见开始。奖章放在后面。

二、华沙

玛丽出生时，波兰已经失去独立，华沙处在俄国统治之下。学校受到俄化政策压制，波兰语与本国历史的学习常常需要另找空间。对年轻女子来说，华沙大学的大门又关着。

不过，大门关着，不等于里面的人什么也没做。玛丽参加过秘密办学的“流动大学”，也在华沙获得过实验训练。老师、亲人和同学，在正式制度之外把学习继续下去。她并不是凭空造出了自己。

她与姐姐布罗尼斯瓦娃约好：先由玛丽工作，帮助姐姐去巴黎学医；等姐姐站稳，再帮助妹妹。几年家教和家庭教师的工作，几年自学，钱一点点存起来，也一点点寄出去。梦想没有停在脑子里，它变成了一份两个人共同承担的安排。

1891年，她到巴黎求学，二十四岁。起初住在姐姐家，后来搬到大学附近简陋的房间。生活拮据，冬天难熬，吃饭也常常被学习挤到一边。不能把贫困写成天才的必修课；她需要的是时间、食物和一间可以安心读书的屋子。

1893年，她以第一名取得物理学学位；次年，数学第二名。支持她的还有家人、奖学金和愿意给她机会的人。那个阁楼里的身影很容易被画成孤独的英雄，真实的人生却有很多只递来一点帮助的手。

想到杜甫在长安求仕，会听见一种遥远的回声：想做的事，迟迟找不到容身之处。但他的处境不能简单归结为考试腐败，她的处境也不是他的翻版。她面前多了一道明确的门槛：因为是女人，连申请同一条路的资格都没有。

我们赞叹她终于走进去的时候，也该问一句：为什么本来属于学习的力气，要先用这么多来证明自己可以学习？

三、棚屋

1895年，她与皮埃尔结婚。他当时在巴黎市立工业物理化学学校任教，并不是索邦的教授。他们相互理解，也一起工作；合作没有让其中一个人变成另一个人的附属。

1897年，玛丽开始研究贝克勒尔发现的铀射线。那时，伦琴的X射线吸引了更多注意。她选择的是一个还没有被充分追问的问题，不是一种由她从无到有创造出来的现象。

她先在学校的一间简陋房间里测量。关键不只在“看见发光”，而在比较数字：为什么有些矿物发出的射线，比其中的铀所能解释的更强？那多出来的一截，不肯老老实实待在已有解释里。

她怀疑，矿物里还有未知的元素。皮埃尔加入研究。1898年，他们先后宣布发现钋和镭；镭的发现报告也有化学家贝蒙署名。钋的名字，把当时不在独立国家地图上的波兰，放进了元素的名单。

接下来却不是一声欢呼就结束。要拿出足够纯的物质，需要处理大量提取过铀的矿渣。在学校院内一座条件很差的旧棚屋里，溶解、搅拌、分离、结晶，反复进行。冷和热都很具体，沉重的容器也很具体。

经过数年工作，到1902年，她得到约十分之一克较纯的氯化镭，测定了镭的原子量。那还不是纯镭金属。1910年，她与德比埃尔才制得金属镭。小小的一点物质，后面是一长串不肯省略的步骤。

“放射性”这个词也是她提出的。她把研究从一种奇异射线，推进到关于元素本身性质的问题。她没有独自完成整个新领域，却实实在在改变了人们能问什么、怎样去问。

棚屋值得记住，不因为科学家就该在棚屋里吃苦，而因为条件如此糟，她仍做出了可靠的测量。后人的敬佩，最好包括不再让后来者只得到一间棚屋。

四、两个诺贝尔奖

1903年，玛丽、皮埃尔与贝克勒尔共同获得诺贝尔物理学奖。她是第一位获诺贝尔奖的女性。但奖章没有使生活从此免于失去。

1906年4月，皮埃尔在巴黎被一辆载货马车撞倒、碾过，四十六岁。她失去了丈夫，也失去了每天可以把一个问题接着讨论下去的人。她的哀伤日记还在。继续工作，并不说明那份哀伤已经被克服。

同年，她接下皮埃尔在索邦的课程与实验室职责；1908年正式获任教授。她是那里第一位授课的女性，后来成为第一位女教授。她继续的不是一场“证明丈夫不重要”的比赛，而是自己的研究，也是两人共同开启的事业。

1911年，诺贝尔化学奖授予她，表彰发现钋与镭、分离镭及研究其性质和化合物的工作。这次她独自获奖。两个奖落在物理与化学两个领域，不能把它们合成一张“天才吃苦终于得到报偿”的结算单。

同一年，她与已婚物理学家保罗·朗之万的关系成为报刊攻击的题材。她既被当作越界的女人，也被当作不受欢迎的外国人。私人关系当然可能涉及责任；但排外的辱骂与性别羞辱，并不因此成为合理的审判。

瑞典科学院成员阿伦尼乌斯写信劝她暂缓领奖。她拒绝接受把科学成果的评价交给这场舆论风暴的做法，仍然去了斯德哥尔摩。

我们不必说“如果是男人，绝不会有人过问”，也不必拿另一个科学家的私生活来替她辩护。只要把两件事分开：研究是否成立，要看证据；一个人如何生活，也不能交由起哄的人群随意处置。

她不是“女人也能得奖”的一件展品。她有自己的工作、亲密关系、判断和错误。看见一个完整的人，比赞美一个毫无瑕疵的榜样更难。

五、发现的另一面

她与皮埃尔曾在暗处看那些含镭制品发光。那份惊异不难理解：白天搬动、搅拌的东西，到了夜里，仿佛自己亮了起来。

可是眼睛看见的美，并没有把身体承受的风险一并告诉他们。早期研究者的防护远远不足。随后，皮肤灼伤等伤害逐渐显露，人们开始认识危险；并不是直到玛丽去世，所有人都还一无所知。

她后来也参与实验室防护，警惕不当医疗使用。把她写成明知会死、仍故意拒绝任何保护的圣徒，反而抹掉了她学习和判断的能力。人可以热爱一项工作，同时希望自己活得健康。

晚年的她经历白内障手术，健康不断恶化。1934年7月4日，她在法国去世，六十六岁，死因为再生障碍性贫血。长期辐射暴露通常被认为是重要原因，但不能把她一生中不同来源的暴露，写成已经逐次量清的一笔账。

她研究的是放射性，却也在战争中面对X射线。危险不按发现者的姓名划界，工作时没能看见的风险，可能很久以后才在身体上显露。

这个系列写过苏格拉底的死、贝多芬的耳聋、杜甫的困顿。比较能照亮处境，却不能把每个人的伤害都塞进同一个格子。玛丽的故事特别刺痛人的地方，是寻找知识的人，也会被尚未理解透的对象伤害。

打开一扇门，不等于已经知道门后的一切。发现没有保证发现者从此安全。这不是叫人放弃探究，而是提醒我们：照顾探究的人，也是探究的一部分。

六、她和这个系列

读到这里，很容易写出一句漂亮而残酷的话：身体毁了，事业留下来了。可如果一篇文章只肯这样记住玛丽，它也在把一个人当成可以烧掉的燃料。

普罗米修斯把火带给人间，自己承受惩罚。这个神话可以表达我们读她时的痛惜，不能证明科学必须索取一个人的健康。她的价值不需要由伤害来盖章，研究者也不欠世界一场献祭。

第一次世界大战期间，她把精力转向战地医疗。筹集设备，组织流动X光车，学习驾驶，与女儿伊雷娜一起工作，还培训女性操作人员。知识在这里不只是论文里的结果，还要变成一辆能到达医院的车、一位会使用仪器的人。

X射线是伦琴的发现，不是她从矿渣里提取出来的镭。她的贡献是把设备、技术和组织能力带到急需它们的地方，让医生更容易找到弹片、看清骨折。她不必先拥有这项发现，才有理由把它用于救人。

放射性物质的医疗用途是另一条相关的道路。辐射可以用于治疗，也可能损害健康；效果取决于对象、剂量、防护和使用方式，不是只要带着“科学进步”的名字就自动向善。

于是，这个故事留下的不只是“她没有停”。还有：什么时候该停下来检查设备，怎样保护工作人员，谁来承担后续照护。一个受伤的身体，已经足够要求我们认真。

把人放在事业之前，不是否定事业。恰恰是让事业值得继续。

七、她的笔记本

她的一些实验笔记，今天仍有放射性污染。法国国家图书馆说明，那些颗粒留在纸页与封面上，档案需要专门评估、保存与查阅程序。它们也可以在经过安全控制的展柜中展出。

翻开这些纸页，会看见字迹、计算，也会想到当年工作留下的痕迹。有些痕迹肉眼看不见，必须由保存的人认真对待。纸上承载的，并不只有我们能够读出的东西。

镭—226的半衰期约为一千六百年。“半衰期”不是一千六百年后危险准时归零，更不能据此替任何一本笔记本开出精确的安全日期。

有人选择保住卡夫卡的手稿，文献在秦朝焚书之后继续流传；玛丽的笔记本上，还发生着属于物质本身的变化。不同的延续有不同的机制，提醒我们：后果并不只有一种活下去的方式。

这里更贴近人的一层意思是：一项工作的后果，不会随着作者合上本子就全部结束。知识留下来了，污染也可能留下来。后来的人既接过发现，也接过保管、理解和纠错的责任。

一页纸，原来可以同时让人感激，又让人小心。

八、玛丽

在这个系列想象的桥头，现在站着玛丽。

她是这份名单上的第一位女性，不是历史上第一位有自己方向的女人。她身后还有许多我们尚未写出的名字，有些曾留下记录，有些连留下记录的机会都没有。

她可以与牛顿并肩，不是谁的附属，也不是桥梁的耗材。纪念一个人，不必再把她从人的位置上拿走。

让她作为一个人站在那里：从华沙来到巴黎，借助别人的帮助，也帮助别人；爱过，失去过，做出判断，继续追问。她的工作重要，她又不止是她的工作。

我们记住镭，也记住搬动容器、核对数字的那个人。记住她改变了什么，也记住她原本就有权学习、有权被认真听见，并不需要先拿出两枚奖章。

玛丽·斯克沃多夫斯卡—居里。把名字念完整，不是要替她选择姓氏。

是提醒自己，不要只记住一位夫人、一种元素，或一个牺牲的故事。

I. Her Name

We know her as Madame Curie. Say a little more: Marie Skłodowska Curie. Born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw in 1867, she used Marie in France.

Curie was a name she used herself, not an imposition we are entitled to erase on her behalf. The question is not which surname a woman should choose. It is what we hear when we say “Madame”: a scientist, or merely somebody's wife?

In 1903 the Nobel Prize in Physics might have recognized Becquerel and Pierre without her. The Swedish mathematician Gösta Mittag-Leffler alerted Pierre, who wrote back stressing Marie's contribution. All three ultimately shared the award. Her work existed before her husband defended it. Yet getting that work officially acknowledged could still depend on his letter.

In 1911 she received the chemistry prize alone, becoming the first person to win two Nobel Prizes. Pierre had been dead for five years. We need not ban the familiar name Madame Curie. We need to stop letting familiarity put her behind somebody else.

She is the first woman in this series. That tells us something about the limitations of our list, not that history had produced no memorable women before her. Access to education, appointments, publication and commemoration helps determine which names reach us.

Let us begin by seeing the person. The medals can wait.

II. Warsaw

When Maria was born, Poland had lost its independence and Warsaw was under Russian rule. Russification constrained schooling; Polish language and history needed spaces of their own. For a young woman, the University of Warsaw presented another barrier: she could not enroll.

But closed doors did not mean an empty intellectual life. She attended the clandestine Floating University and obtained some practical scientific training in Warsaw. Teachers, relatives and fellow students kept learning alive outside the official arrangements. She did not create herself from nothing.

She and her sister Bronisława made a plan. Maria would work to help Bronisława study medicine in Paris; when her sister could, she would help Maria in turn. Years of tutoring and governess work, years of studying on her own, money saved and sent away. Their ambition became something more durable than a wish: an arrangement two people undertook together.

In 1891 she reached Paris, aged twenty-four. At first she stayed with her sister, then moved to modest rooms nearer the university. Money was scarce, winters difficult; meals could disappear beneath the demands of study. Poverty was not a course a genius needed to pass. She needed food, time and a place where she could read in peace.

She came first in physics in 1893 and second in mathematics the following year. Family support, a scholarship and people willing to offer opportunities helped make that possible. The student in the garret is easily painted as a solitary hero. A life also contains hands extended at just the right moment.

There is a distant echo of Du Fu seeking a position in Chang'an: something one wants to do, with nowhere to do it. Yet his difficulties cannot simply be explained by corrupt examinations, nor are hers a repetition of his. She faced an explicit additional rule. Being a woman could disqualify her even from applying to take the same route.

Before celebrating her admission, ask how much energy that belonged to learning first had to be spent establishing her right to learn.

III. The Shed

She married Pierre in 1895. He taught at the Paris Municipal School of Industrial Physics and Chemistry, not yet at the Sorbonne. Their intellectual companionship became a working partnership. It did not turn either partner into an accessory to the other.

In 1897 she began studying the uranium rays Becquerel had discovered. Röntgen's X-rays were attracting more attention. She chose a question that had received too little sustained investigation, not a phenomenon she conjured into existence herself.

Her early measurements were made in rudimentary space at Pierre's school. The crucial thing was not simply seeing a mysterious glow. It was comparing results. Why did certain minerals produce more radiation than their uranium content could explain? A stubborn excess would not fit the account already available.

She suspected an unknown element. Pierre joined her research. In 1898 they announced polonium and then radium; the chemist Gustave Bémont also signed the radium report. Naming polonium placed Poland, absent as an independent state, on the list of elements.

An announcement did not finish the job. Obtaining sufficiently pure material meant treating large quantities of residues left after uranium extraction. In a badly equipped shed at the school, dissolving, stirring, separating and crystallizing had to be done again and again. Heat, cold and heavy vessels were not metaphors.

By 1902, after years of work, she had obtained roughly a tenth of a gram of purified radium chloride and determined radium's atomic weight. This was a salt, not pure radium metal. She and André Debierne produced the metal in 1910. Behind a small quantity lay a long sequence of steps that could not be wished away.

She also introduced the word radioactivity. Her work moved the inquiry beyond an unusual ray toward a question about the properties of elements themselves. She did not build the whole new field alone. She changed what people could ask, and how they could find out.

Remember the shed because dependable work was done despite its conditions, not because scientists ought to suffer in sheds. Admiration might usefully include giving the next researcher something better.

IV. Two Nobel Prizes

Marie, Pierre and Becquerel shared the 1903 physics prize. She was the first woman to receive a Nobel Prize. Recognition did not make her life safe from loss.

In April 1906 Pierre was struck and run over by a horse-drawn wagon in Paris. He was forty-six. She lost a husband and the person with whom a question could be picked up again the next morning. Her mourning diary survives. Returning to work did not mean she had finished grieving.

That year she took over his teaching and laboratory responsibilities at the Sorbonne; her appointment as a full professor followed in 1908. She was its first female lecturer and subsequently its first woman professor. She was not conducting a competition to prove that Pierre had never mattered. This was her own research, and a shared undertaking she was carrying forward.

The 1911 chemistry prize recognized the discoveries of polonium and radium, the isolation of radium, and study of its nature and compounds. This time the award was hers alone. Two prizes in two sciences are not a settlement in which genius is finally reimbursed for suffering.

That same year her relationship with the married physicist Paul Langevin became the subject of a press campaign. She was attacked both as a woman who had crossed a line and as an unwelcome foreigner. Intimate relationships can raise real questions of responsibility. Xenophobic abuse and sexual humiliation do not thereby become legitimate judgment.

Svante Arrhenius, a member of the Swedish Academy of Sciences, urged her to postpone accepting the award. She rejected the demand that a public scandal govern the assessment of scientific work. She went to Stockholm.

We do not need to claim that no man would ever have faced such scrutiny. Nor do we need another scientist's private failings as her defense. Keep the questions distinct: research must answer to evidence; a person's life is not the property of a shouting crowd.

She was not an exhibit proving that women could win prizes. She had work, attachments, judgments and mistakes of her own. Seeing a whole person is harder than admiring a flawless example.

V. The Other Side of Discovery

Marie and Pierre sometimes watched radium preparations glow in the dark. Their wonder is easy to understand. Material they had carried and stirred through the day seemed, at night, to make its own light.

Visible beauty did not announce the risks to their bodies. Protection in the early research was inadequate. Injuries, including skin burns, then made dangers increasingly apparent. It is not true that everyone remained wholly ignorant until Marie died.

She later took part in radiation protection and warned against unsuitable medical use. Turning her into a saint who knowingly refused every safeguard erases her ability to learn and judge. Someone can love her work and want to stay well.

In later life she underwent cataract operations and her health deteriorated. She died in France on 4 July 1934, aged sixty-six, of aplastic anemia. Long-term radiation exposure is generally considered an important cause. That does not give us a complete lifetime record, neatly separating every source and dose.

She studied radioactivity, but during the war she also worked with X-rays. Danger does not respect the boundaries drawn by a discoverer's name. A risk invisible at work may make itself felt in the body long afterward.

This series has visited Socrates' death, Beethoven's deafness and Du Fu's hardships. Comparisons can illuminate a predicament without forcing different injuries into one design. What hurts here is that a person seeking knowledge can be harmed by the very thing she does not yet fully understand.

Opening a door does not mean knowing everything behind it. Discovery offers no automatic protection to the discoverer. The response need not be to abandon inquiry. It can be to recognize that caring for the people who inquire belongs to the work too.

VI. Her Place in This Series

At this point a beautiful, cruel sentence almost writes itself: the body was destroyed, but the work survived. If that is all an essay can say about Marie, it too is treating a person as fuel.

Prometheus brought fire to humanity and suffered punishment. The myth can express our sorrow in reading her life. It cannot establish that science must exact somebody's health. Harm does not certify her worth. Researchers do not owe the world a sacrifice.

During the First World War she turned her energy to medical service. She found equipment, organized mobile X-ray units, learned to drive, worked alongside her daughter Irène and trained women to operate the apparatus. Knowledge had to become a vehicle that could reach a hospital and a person who knew how to use what it carried.

X-rays were Röntgen's discovery, not the radium she had extracted from mineral residues. Her contribution here was bringing equipment, expertise and organization to places that needed them, helping doctors locate shrapnel and fractures. A discovery did not have to belong to her before she could put it to work for other people.

Medical uses of radioactive substances formed a related but distinct path. Radiation can be used in treatment and can injure healthy tissue. Its effects depend on the target, dose, safeguards and method. The label “scientific progress” does not automatically make an application good.

The story leaves more than the injunction to keep going. When should work pause for an equipment check? How are staff protected? Who takes responsibility for later care? A wounded body already gives us reason to attend.

Putting people before an undertaking does not belittle it. It helps make the undertaking worth continuing.

VII. Her Notebooks

Some of her laboratory notebooks still bear radioactive contamination. The Bibliothèque nationale de France explains that particles remain in pages and covers; the archives require specialized assessment, preservation and consultation procedures. They can also be displayed under controlled, safe conditions.

On these pages are handwriting, calculations, and traces of work that cannot be seen. The words invite reading; the invisible traces require attention from those who preserve them. Paper carries more than what we can decipher.

Radium-226 has a half-life of about sixteen hundred years. That does not mean danger switches off after sixteen hundred years. Nor does it supply an exact date on which any particular notebook becomes safe.

People chose to preserve Kafka's manuscripts; texts continued to circulate after the Qin book burnings. In Marie's notebooks, the properties of matter also play their part. The mechanisms differ. They remind us that consequences continue in different ways.

The human point is closer to hand. The consequences of work do not all end when its author closes a notebook. Knowledge can remain; contamination can remain too. Those who come afterward inherit discovery, along with obligations to preserve, understand and correct.

A page can give us reasons for gratitude and for caution at the same time.

VIII. Marie

At this series' imagined bridgehead, Marie has arrived.

The first woman on this list is not the first woman in history to have a direction of her own. Behind her are names we have not yet written: some left records, and some were denied even that opportunity.

Let her stand beside Newton: nobody's accessory and no expendable material for building the bridge. Honoring a person need not mean taking away her place as a person once again.

Let her stand there as a person: someone who traveled from Warsaw to Paris, accepted help and helped others, loved, lost, judged and went on asking questions. Her work mattered. She was more than her work.

Remember radium, and the person who moved the vessels and checked the figures. Remember what she changed, and that she already had the right to learn and to be heard seriously. She did not first have to produce two medals.

Marie Skłodowska Curie. Saying the name more fully does not mean choosing her surname for her.

It means refusing to remember only a wife, an element, or a story of sacrifice.
