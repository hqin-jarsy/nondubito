苏轼，谁怕

Su Shi, Who's Afraid

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、乌台诗案

1079年，苏轼在湖州任知州。朝廷来人，把他带走了。

一些本来在朋友之间流传的诗，被放到审讯桌上，一句一句追问：这句讥刺什么，那句指向谁？对新法的批评、谢表里的措辞，都能被连成对朝廷不敬的证据。诗人写下的比喻，此时成了别人手里的绳索。

苏轼与王安石政见不合，对新法的内容及推行方式多有批评。但乌台诗案不是两位文人当面吵了一架。御史官员的弹劾、党争和皇权，都进入了这个人的命运。所谓乌台，是御史台的旧称。

他被拘押数月。在狱中写给弟弟苏辙的诗里，他已经想到死后把家人托付给谁。一个后来被奉为旷达典范的人，也曾认真地以为，自己走不出这道门。

审讯最后以处分收场。他活下来了。1080年，他到了黄州，挂着团练副使的名义，却不得参与实际政事，居住和行动受到约束。皇帝没有取走他的性命，不等于他从此可以像以前一样生活。

两年后，他在这里写下前后《赤壁赋》。作品后来成为名篇，不会使把人关进监牢的事变得值得。

二、不是一条向下的直线

他不是一直住在苦难里、从未见过别的生活。1057年，年轻的苏轼登进士第，文章受到欧阳修赏识。父亲、弟弟与他一起进入士人的视野，他也曾在中央和地方承担真实的政务。

黄州之后，他被重新起用，回到朝廷，做到翰林学士等要职。旧党要全面翻转新法时，他又提出异议：办法有利有弊，不能因为是谁制定的，就一口气全推倒。站回熟人一边，并不等于可以交出自己的判断。

后来他在杭州、颍州、扬州、定州等地任职。朝中冲突是背景，但这些外任不能一律写成流放。他仍有机会做事。杭州的疏浚、赈济与救治病人，也属于苏轼，不是诗歌之外无关紧要的履历。

1094年，他被贬惠州。1097年，再到海南儋州。距离越拉越长，药物、食物、居所的困难越来越具体。远离熟悉的生活，又渐渐老去，每一天都要面对实际的难处。

我们容易把人生画成一条线：年轻得意，逐步跌落，然后靠精神胜利翻身。苏轼的路比这复杂。他有被迫的离开，也有重新任职；有失望，也有认真处理别人难处的日子。

所以，先不要急着宣布他没有碎。这个词太容易把旁人变成失败者。先看看，一个会怕、会失望的人，怎样又把明天接了起来。

三、黄州的风和月

1080年至1084年，黄州。收入锐减，家口需要安顿，开销要一项项计算。朋友马正卿帮助他取得城东一片旧营地耕种。它并不是凭一个念头就变成田的：荒土要开，庄稼要等，天时也未必肯配合。

东坡。后来几乎比本名还响亮的名字，先是一处需要劳作的地方。“东坡居士”不是一个人失去全部关系后独自创造自己的证明。朋友的帮忙、家人的劳动、乡人的来往，都在这个名字背后。

1082年的《前赤壁赋》，先让客人的箫声把悲意带出来：“哀吾生之须臾，羡长江之无穷。”人很短，江很长。昔日英雄都不在了，我这一点生命又能留下什么？

文章没有让苏子立即喝止这种悲伤。他从水与月说起，让变化与不变成为两种观看的角度，然后把话带到江风和明月：“耳得之而为声，目遇之而成色。”最后不是一个人宣布胜利，而是“吾与子”可以共同享用。

风月不能替代饭，也不能撤销处分。它们打开的是另一扇窗：官位之外还有身体能感到的世界，还有可以坐在身边的人。失去一个位置，不必等于再没有任何生活。客人笑了，洗杯再饮，直到天亮。这个结尾里有陪伴。

同年的《念奴娇·赤壁怀古》，从“大江东去”写到英雄，也写到自己的早生华发。旷远不是站到时间外面，而是在时间里面，仍能抬头看得远一点。

还有《定风波》：“莫听穿林打叶声，何妨吟啸且徐行。竹杖芒鞋轻胜马，谁怕？一蓑烟雨任平生。”但词并没有在“谁怕”处结束。春风把酒吹醒，身体感到“微冷”，山头又有斜阳来迎。

到最后，他回首刚才的风雨。不是雨从未落下，而是雨、晴与回首，已经不再是同一刻。能走出这样的几步，很可贵；不必把它夸大成从此再没有怕过。

四、他并非只会笑

如果只摘“谁怕”，会错过同在黄州写下的《寒食雨》：“君门深九重，坟墓在万里。”接着是想哭也无路可走的痛。写风月的人，也写火炉、破屋、雨水和回不去的远方。

他没有先变成一个永不愤怒、永不绝望的人，才获得写诗的资格。我们也不必替他安排一场彻底治愈：早年怕过，后来悟了，从此无事。人的心不是这样整齐的。

惠州的荔枝进入诗里：“日啖荔枝三百颗，不辞长作岭南人。”这是带着夸张和兴致的诗句，不是每天食量的记录，也不是已经取消贬谪的声明。味觉还在，喜欢一件事的能力还在。

1100年北归渡海时，他写“九死南荒吾不恨，兹游奇绝冠平生”。“九死”与“奇绝”被放在同一联里。风险没有从字面上消失，诗人也没有只允许自己说风险。

更重要的是，他没有把应对都收回内心。做地方官时，他处理水患、饥荒、疾病。人需要堤岸、粮食和照护，他知道。能够欣赏一阵风，不是不要改善处境的理由。

苏轼动人的地方，不是别人再也伤不了他，而是别人造成的伤，未必能替他写完所有句子。写不出来、笑不出来的时候，也不表示受难者不够努力，更不能因此给伤害者减责。

五、他和杜甫

杜甫的诗写饥饿、征兵、流离。可是，诗不是苦难未经处理地漏到纸上。选择谁来说话、在哪一句停住、如何使一个屋顶连到天下的屋顶，都需要艺术，也需要判断。

《茅屋为秋风所破歌》从自家的漏雨，走向“大庇天下寒士俱欢颜”。这不是一个已经碎掉的人只剩记录。他在自己的困境里，还为没有见过的人提出要求：他们也该有地方住。

苏轼在黄州的雨里，有时走向另一种转折：这场雨已经让我如此狼狈，我是否还能重新看看自己与它的关系？一种写法把目光推向众人，一种写法把既定的感受松开一点。

这是阅读某些作品时可见的侧重，不是两个人的终身分工。杜甫也写喜悦和朋友，苏轼也关心民生与不公。一个不是只向外，另一个也不是只向内。

两个人都在语言里反复工作，把难以承受的经验，变成别人可以接近的东西。杜甫的诗有精密的组织，苏轼的旷达里也有尚未安顿的悲伤。读进去，会看见比两个性格标签更丰富的人。

读杜甫，不必急着劝他看开；读苏轼，也不必命令自己一定看开。诗可以让一个人暂时多出一种反应，而不是少掉原有反应的权利。

六、他和李白

李白写“安能摧眉折腰事权贵，使我不得开心颜”，句子里有转身离去的力量。但他并非从未遭受沉重打击。卷入永王事件后，他曾下狱，被判流放夜郎，途中遇赦。那不是想走便走的旅行。

两个人都尝过不能由自己决定道路的滋味。诗里飞得高的人，也可能被押着走；失去自由，并不总有一个潇洒转身的出口。

仍然可以作一个较小的比较。有时李白的句子把门推开，拒绝在权贵面前低头；有时苏轼的句子在暂时无法离开的地方，重新找到可以呼吸的角落。出走与安顿，都可能帮助人活下去。

但能离开不正当的拘束，仍是一项真实的自由。能住得安全、吃得饱、与亲人团聚，也不是心境可以替代的东西。没有谁因为在困境中写出好诗，就证明困境无关紧要。

“此心安处是吾乡”常被放到苏轼名下，像是他给这一比较写的结论。先停一下：在他的作品里，说出这句话的是柔奴。苏轼听见一个别人的回答，把它留下了。

自由不只在说“你们拿我没办法”的声音里。有时也在愿意听见：原来另一个人，对自己怎样活，有她自己的说法。

七、他和八大山人

八大山人朱耷经历明清易代。苏轼经历宋代党争与贬谪。两种政治处境不同，不能用同一把尺子量完。但我们尤其不该给一人判为碎片，再把另一人封成永不破碎的整块。

八大山人画过鱼、鸟、荷花，也留下书法和诗。传说里的沉默，需要放回具体的处境看；而作品一直在与后来的人相遇。画上有留白，笔下也有声音。

苏轼的信与诗留下笑，也留下愁苦；八大山人的画里则有凝视、距离与不肯被说尽的东西。一个人的表达，从来不只有音量这一种尺度。

留白可以让观看者停下来，诗句可以把一个转折慢慢说清。这是不同作品带给我们的可能，不是国破之后只许沉默、仕途失意尚可开口的等级。

也许读八大时，我们学着不要逼一个人解释所有东西；读苏轼时，学着听见笑声旁边尚未散去的疼。两种阅读并不互相胜过。

在桥头给苏轼留一个位置，不需要先把八大山人的位置搬走。可以一起在这里，谁都不必成为另一个人的失败例子。

八、此心安处

1100年，赦令使苏轼得以离开海南北归。1101年，他病逝于常州，六十四岁。走了那么远，最后停在这里。

最后这段路没有把他送回眉山。早年他曾返蜀守丧，故乡在他的生命里，并非一个抽象的远方。后来他也在别处建立关系。怀念故乡与活在此地，可以同时发生。

那句关于故乡的话，出自另一首《定风波》的小序和结尾。苏轼问随王巩南迁又归来的柔奴，岭南应当不太好吧？作品里，她回答：“此心安处，便是吾乡。”词的结尾写成“此心安处是吾乡”。

这里先发生的，是苏轼的预想被一个女人的回答轻轻挪开。她不只是用来证明大诗人旷达的材料。苏轼把话写下来，并不等于从此那声音只属于他。

可以由此想到慧能对执著的追问，但“空”不等于人不需要外部帮助，“心安”也不等于不用吃饭、不必回家。佛学与日常生活在这里有可对话的地方，不必宣布它们证明了同一个无条件自由的公式。

对苏轼而言，黄州有人帮他开地，海南有儿子相伴，也有当地人相助。心安不是从世界撤退后独自产生的胜利。它有时恰好长在一顿饭、一场谈话、一个愿意帮忙的人之间。

九、谁怕

苏轼留下诗、词、文章，也留下书画。他面对处境的姿态值得读，但不能把作品都收成一句成功学口号：只要你选择不碎，就没有人能伤你。

他没有给出这种保证。更接近诗句的，也许是一个小一些的动作：风雨已经来了，先试着再走几步。冷的时候承认冷，看见斜阳时，也不必拒绝那一点暖。

现在，让他来到这个系列想象中的桥头，手里有一杯酒。诗中的人暂时走进了一幅画。

杜甫在，李白在，八大山人也在。苏轼不必用一个笑，证明自己比他们更高明。他也许先听别人说一会儿。有人还不愿说，他就把旁边的位置留着。

桥上起风了。他喝一口，望向远处。也许笑，也许沉默。那个说过“谁怕”的人，并没有因此失去害怕的资格。

我们读他，不是为了学会责备自己为什么还会痛。只是当痛已经占了很大的地方，诗有时会提醒我们：这里还可以有一点别的。

谁怕？不妨仍让它是一个问句。然后，慢一点，再走几步。

注释

生平以苏辙墓志、《宋史》及苏轼作品相互对照。苏轼生于旧历景祐三年十二月，按公历纪年在1037年；本文去世年龄采用周岁六十四，旧传六十六为传统计龄。诗案的死刑风险不等于已经判死刑；曹太后、王安石营救的细节不在正文作确定引语。李白曾流放夜郎，朱耷留下书画与诗，均不作为苏轼的失败对照。“此心安处”保留作品中柔奴的发言归属。诗句引通行字形，外语诗意由本版自行转述。桥头为文学想象。

I. A Poem Becomes Evidence

In 1079 officials arrived in Huzhou and arrested its prefect, Su Shi.

Poems that had circulated among friends were laid on an interrogation table. What was this line mocking? Who was the target of that image? Criticism of the New Policies and phrases in an official memorial could be assembled into evidence of disrespect. The poet’s metaphors became ropes in someone else’s hands.

Su Shi disagreed with Wang Anshi over both policies and their implementation. But the Crow Terrace Poetry Trial was not an argument between two literary men. Accusing officials, factional politics and imperial power all entered the fate of one person. Crow Terrace was an old name for the Censorate.

He spent months in custody. In poems addressed to his brother Su Zhe, he imagined who would care for his family after his death. The man later held up as an example of serenity once seriously expected never to walk out of the prison.

The interrogation ended in punishment rather than execution. He lived. In 1080 he reached Huangzhou with a nominal military title but no permission to take part in government. His residence and movements were restricted. The emperor had spared his life, not restored the life he knew.

Two years later he wrote the Red Cliff rhapsodies there. Their subsequent greatness cannot make the imprisonment a worthwhile price.

II. Not a Straight Line Down

He had known other kinds of life. In 1057 the young Su Shi passed the metropolitan examinations, and Ouyang Xiu admired his writing. Together with his father and brother he became prominent among scholars. He also took on real responsibilities in central and provincial government.

After Huangzhou he returned to office and rose to important posts, including the Hanlin Academy. When the opponents of reform sought a sweeping reversal, he objected again. Policies had different advantages and costs; the identity of their author was no reason to undo everything at once. Returning among allies did not require surrendering judgment.

He later served in Hangzhou, Yingzhou, Yangzhou and Dingzhou. Court conflict mattered, but these appointments were not all exile. He could still act. Dredging waterways, famine relief and care for the sick in Hangzhou belong to Su Shi too, not to an irrelevant administrative résumé outside the poetry.

Banishment to Huizhou came in 1094, to Danzhou on Hainan in 1097. Distance increased; difficulties obtaining food, medicine and shelter became painfully concrete. Far from familiar life and growing older, he met practical difficulties every day.

We like a simple arc: youthful success, steady decline, spiritual triumph. His route included forced departures and restored office, disappointment and days spent addressing other people’s difficulties.

So let us not hurry to declare that he never broke. The phrase too easily turns everyone else into a failure. First look at how a man who could fear and despair found ways to meet another day.

III. Wind and Moonlight at Huangzhou

He lived in Huangzhou from 1080 to 1084. Income fell sharply; a household needed support and expenses needed counting. His friend Ma Zhengqing helped secure former military land east of town for cultivation. A thought did not turn it into a field. Ground needed clearing, crops needed time, and weather did not necessarily cooperate.

Eastern Slope: Dongpo, the name that would almost eclipse his birth name, began as a place requiring labour. His new identity was not proof of self-creation without other people. A friend’s help, family work and local relationships stood behind it.

The first Red Cliff Rhapsody of 1082 gives grief a voice through a guest and his flute. Our lives are brief, the guest laments; the river seems endless. If past heroes have vanished, what can this small life leave behind?

The host does not simply silence the sorrow. Water and moon become ways of looking through change and continuity. Then he turns to wind heard by the ear and moonlight met by the eye. The movement ends not with solitary victory but with something ‘you and I’ can enjoy together.

Wind and moonlight cannot replace food or cancel a sentence. They open another window: beyond office there is still a world the body can feel, and someone who can sit beside you. Losing a position need not mean losing every possibility of living. The guest laughs; cups are washed and filled again; morning comes. Companionship belongs to the ending.

In Remembering Red Cliff, also from 1082, the eastward river carries away heroes, while the poet notices his own greying hair. A widening view does not place him outside time. It lets a person inside time look a little farther.

Another lyric, to the tune Ding Feng Bo, begins in rain: why not sing and walk slowly? A bamboo staff, straw sandals, a rain cloak—who’s afraid? Yet the poem continues beyond that challenge. Spring wind sobers him; he feels a little cold. Slanting sunlight meets him from the hill.

At the end he turns towards the place where rain had fallen. Rain, clearing and the backward glance no longer occupy the same moment. Those few steps matter. They need not prove that he was never afraid again.

IV. He Could Also Grieve

Quoting only ‘who’s afraid?’ would hide the Cold Food Festival poems, written in Huangzhou too. There the imperial gates are impossibly deep, the family graves far away. The man who writes wind and moonlight also writes a stove, a poor dwelling, relentless rain and distances he cannot cross.

He did not have to become incapable of anger or despair before he earned the right to write. Nor must his life follow a complete recovery plot: once afraid, then enlightened, thereafter immune. Hearts do not move so neatly.

In Huizhou he jokes that three hundred lychees a day could persuade him to remain in Lingnan. This is exuberant poetic exaggeration, not a daily dietary record or a legal cancellation of banishment. Taste remains. The capacity to like something remains.

Crossing the sea northward in 1100, he puts near-death in the southern wilderness beside an experience more extraordinary than any other. Danger and wonder inhabit the same couplet. The danger is not erased; neither is it allowed the only word.

Nor did he place every response inside the mind. As an official he dealt with floods, hunger and illness. People needed embankments, food and care. He knew that. Enjoying a breeze is no argument against improving material conditions.

What moves us is not that others could no longer hurt him, but that the hurt they caused did not necessarily write every remaining sentence. Someone unable to write or smile has not failed a test of effort. The person causing harm receives no discount for a victim’s difficulty recovering.

V. Beside Du Fu

Du Fu writes hunger, conscription and displacement. But suffering does not leak unprocessed onto the page. Who will speak, where a line will stop, how one leaking roof opens onto the roofs of everyone else: these are artistic and ethical decisions.

His poem about a thatched roof wrecked by autumn wind moves from his own rain-soaked bed towards shelter for impoverished scholars everywhere. This is not a broken man left with mere recording. Within his trouble he makes a claim for strangers: they too ought to have somewhere to live.

Su Shi sometimes turns differently in the rain at Huangzhou. Given how wretched this storm has made me, can I reconsider my relation to it? One movement brings others into view; another loosens a response that seemed inevitable.

These are emphases in particular works, not lifelong job descriptions. Du Fu also writes joy and friendship. Su Shi also writes public suffering and injustice. One does not look only outward while the other looks only within.

Both worked repeatedly at language, making difficult experience available for someone else to approach. Du Fu’s poems are intricately made; Su Shi’s equanimity contains grief not yet settled. Reading closely reveals people richer than two contrasting labels.

We need not hurry to tell Du Fu to get over it, or command ourselves to get over it when reading Su Shi. A poem can add a possible response without taking away the right to the responses we already have.

VI. Beside Li Bai

Li Bai’s refusal to bow before the powerful carries the energy of walking out. But his life was not untouched by crushing force. After involvement with Prince Yong, he was imprisoned and sentenced to exile in Yelang, receiving a pardon on the journey. That was not travel undertaken at will.

Both knew what it meant to travel roads chosen for them. A poet who flies in verse may also be marched along under orders. Losing freedom does not always leave an elegant exit.

A smaller comparison remains possible. Some Li Bai lines throw a door open and refuse submission; some Su Shi lines discover breathing room where departure is temporarily impossible. Leaving and settling can both help a person live.

The ability to leave unjust confinement is still a real freedom. Safety, enough food and reunion with family are not things a change of mind can replace. Good poems written under constraint do not prove that constraint was irrelevant.

The line about home being where the heart is at peace often appears under Su Shi’s name, as though he supplied the perfect conclusion. Pause there: in his work, Rounu speaks it. He listens to somebody else’s answer and preserves it.

Freedom is not heard only in the declaration that nobody can defeat me. Sometimes it is heard in recognizing that another person has her own account of how to live.

VII. Beside Bada Shanren

Bada Shanren, Zhu Da, lived through the Ming–Qing dynastic transition. Su Shi lived through Song factional struggles and banishment. These political circumstances differ. We should not turn either man into a shattered fragment so that the other can become an unbreakable whole.

Bada painted fish, birds and lotus flowers, and left calligraphy and poems. The silences in stories about him need their particular circumstances. Meanwhile the works continue to meet later viewers. There is unpainted space on the paper, and there is a voice in the brush.

Su Shi’s letters and poems contain laughter and distress. Bada’s paintings offer looks, distances and things that resist being fully said. Volume is not the only measure of expression.

An unpainted space can hold a viewer still. A poem can unfold a change in feeling. These are possibilities offered by works, not a hierarchy in which dynastic catastrophe permits only silence while a damaged career still permits speech.

With Bada we may learn not to demand that a person explain everything. With Su Shi we may hear pain that has not entirely dispersed beside the laughter. Neither reading defeats the other.

Giving Su Shi a place at the bridgehead need not mean removing Bada’s. They can be there together without either becoming the cautionary failure in somebody else’s story.

VIII. Where a Heart Can Rest

An amnesty allowed Su Shi to leave Hainan in 1100. In 1101 he died at Changzhou, aged sixty-four. After so much travelling, this was where the journey stopped.

This final journey did not return him to Meishan. Earlier he had gone back to Sichuan for family mourning; home was not merely an abstract distance in his life. He later formed relationships elsewhere too. Missing home and living here can happen together.

The famous line belongs to the preface and ending of another Ding Feng Bo lyric. Su Shi asks Rounu, who had accompanied Wang Gong south and returned, whether Lingnan must have been unpleasant. In the text she replies that home is where her heart finds peace.

Something happens before this becomes a maxim: a woman’s answer gently moves the poet’s assumption aside. She is not simply material for demonstrating his serenity. Recording the words does not make her voice his property.

We might hear a connection with Huineng’s questions about attachment. But Buddhist emptiness does not mean nobody needs external help, and a peaceful heart does not remove the need for food or home. Buddhism and everyday experience can converse without proving a single formula of unconditional freedom.

At Huangzhou a friend helped him obtain land. On Hainan his son was with him, and local people helped. Peace of heart was not necessarily a solitary victory won after withdrawing from the world. Sometimes it grew between a meal, a conversation and someone willing to lend a hand.

IX. Who’s Afraid?

Su Shi left poems, lyrics, prose, calligraphy and painting. His way of meeting circumstances deserves attention. But the works cannot be compressed into a motivational guarantee: choose not to break and nobody can hurt you.

He offers no such guarantee. A smaller gesture is nearer the poems: the rain has come; try another few steps. Admit the cold when it is cold. When sunlight arrives, there is no need to refuse its warmth.

Now let him enter this series’ imagined bridgehead with a cup of wine. A figure from our reading walks into a picture.

Du Fu, Li Bai and Bada are there. Su Shi need not prove himself superior by smiling. Perhaps he listens first. If somebody is not ready to speak, he leaves the place beside him open.

Wind rises on the bridge. He drinks and looks into the distance. Perhaps he smiles; perhaps he remains quiet. Saying ‘who’s afraid?’ did not deprive him of the right to fear.

We do not read him to learn how to blame ourselves for still hurting. When pain occupies much of a life, a poem may remind us that there can also be something else here.

Who’s afraid? Let it remain a question. Then, a little more slowly, take another few steps.
