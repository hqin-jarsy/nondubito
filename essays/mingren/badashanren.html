八大山人，白眼朝天

Bada Shanren, Eyes Rolled Toward the Sky

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、那条鱼

先别急着读八大山人的生平。看一眼他笔下那种鱼：身体只用几笔，黑色的瞳仁却被推到眼眶上方，下面留出一片白。你很难从那只眼睛前面直接走过去。

它像在翻白眼。你觉得它不屑理你，再看，又好像只是游到了这里，偶然朝上看了一眼。鱼没有开口，先忙起来的是看画的人。

我们很容易给它配一句话：我看不起这个世界。再把画家的身世放进去，那句话就更像有了证据。可是，一只画出来的眼睛能让你想到什么，与我们已经知道画家心里是什么，并不是一回事。

白眼会使人想到阮籍，这个联想有很长的文化来路。但我们不必拿到一把解开全部画作的钥匙，才有资格站在画前。先看那一点墨怎样改变了整条鱼的神态，也看看自己的解释怎样跟着改变。

三百多年过去，纸上的鱼仍然让人拿不准：是嘲笑，是警觉，是不肯应酬，还是我们把自己的情绪放了进去？这些感觉可以同时靠近画面，不必立刻分出一个唯一的答案。

八大山人的厉害，有时就从这里开始。他画得很少，却让你不能只看一眼。

二、一个世界变了

我们通常称他朱耷，江西南昌人，出身明朝宗室，是宁王朱权的后裔。生年有不同记载，大致在1620年代。1644年北京陷落、崇祯皇帝自尽时，他还是一个约二十岁的年轻人。

明朝并不是在同一天、每个地方都消失的。南方还有延续的抵抗，新王朝的征服与秩序也要在战乱中逐步建立。对一个与旧皇室有血缘关系的人来说，原来表明身份的东西，忽然可能带来危险。

他后来进入佛门，长期以僧人的身份生活。这既让人想到避难，也不能只被看成一件用完就脱的伪装。几十年的修习、人与人的往来、书写与观看，都进入了他此后的生活。

传记中有过他失常、哭笑无定，以及以哑示人的记述。我们隔着这些文字，很难知道每一次行为发生了什么，更不能据此替他确诊，或者断言一切都是精心设计的表演。一个人在动荡中怎样保护自己，未必只有一种解释。

约1680年前后，他离开僧侣生活，转而靠书画维持生计；1684年开始使用“八大山人”这个后来最为人熟知的号。他没有从历史里消失，还在改换生活的方式，也在不断选择怎样署下自己的名字。

旧的世界变了，失去的东西不能轻易找回。但留下来的不是一块不会动的碎片。那个人还在过日子，还会学习、来往、落笔。

三、被改变的人，也在改变

这个系列写苏格拉底、马克思、伽利略、维特根斯坦时，很容易抓住一个鲜明动作：追问、分析、观察，或者回头修改自己的思想。到了八大山人这里，最先闯进来的却不是他的动作，而是历史对他做了什么。

这会使我们误以为，他只能被动承受。可是，决定哪一根线不画，把眼珠点在哪里，怎样让一只鸟站得像要失去平衡，这些都不是灾难自动替他完成的。那是他的工作。

他不因没有写出政治纲领，就变成没有方向的人。不走仕途，不等于离开所有人的世界；以书画谋生，也不等于把画中的自己卖给了买画的人。生计、交往与不肯完全迁就，可以同时存在。

与伽利略相比，我们看到的是不同的压力，不能据此给苦难排座次。一个人的审判文书，并不能说明另一个人的每一天；一场王朝更替，也不能替其中所有人决定同一种反应。

维特根斯坦关于不可说之物的提醒，可以陪我们来看画，但画家也有自己的语言。八大山人留下了诗、题跋和书法。顺着笔迹读下去，我们会发现，画中的沉默感与一个人的丰富表达，原来可以同时存在。

他没有把失去的王朝画回来。他做的是另外一件事：在已经改变的生活里，练习一种仍由自己决定的笔墨。

四、白纸也在画里面

看大都会艺术博物馆所藏、作于1699年的《鱼石图》。两块大石下面，七条小鱼游在一片没有涂墨的纸上。几笔鱼形，就让周围的空白忽然可以被看成水。

你不需要先看见每一道波纹，才相信它们正在游。石头的重量、鱼的轻小，以及中间拉开的距离，相互撑住了这个画面。留白不是背景消失了，而是另一种安排背景的办法。

再看故宫所藏《荷石水鸟图》，荷叶下一只水鸟立在石上，身体收拢，一足支撑。你可以从中感到冷意和孤独，也可以多停一下，看它怎样在那么少的支点上站住。那只鸟不只承受着我们的情绪，也有自己的姿态。

八大山人并不是从空无中发明了水墨。他看前人的画，学习书法，与沈周、徐渭、董其昌等传统发生关系。线条的轻重、墨的干湿、物象放在何处，背后有手的训练，也有长时间积累的判断。

他的画也有舒展开来的时候。1697年的《河上花图》长卷以荷花为主，长近十三米，卷后还有他自己的长诗。荷叶、山石与笔墨的展开，让观看需要时间。我们沿着画面往前走，遇到浓淡与疏密的变化，再遇到诗；这里的声音，比第一眼想的更多。

白纸能容纳身世，也能容纳水、空气、距离和观看者还没有想好的感觉。若我们把每一块白都翻译成同一句亡国之痛，反倒把画看窄了。

五、他和阮籍

阮籍生活在三世纪的魏晋之际。《晋书》记他以白眼对待某些拘于礼俗的来客，嵇喜来吊丧时就碰了冷脸；后来嵇康带着琴和酒来，他又欣然相待。所谓青眼，不是瞳孔变成了蓝色，而是换了一种看人的态度。

同一部传记还记他驾车出游，走到路尽处便痛哭而返。我们读到的，是后世保存的人物故事，不是每一个瞬间都有旁人在场的录像。即便如此，故事仍传来一种不愿把喜恶完全交给礼法的姿态。

阮籍并非在安全的高处蔑视世界。他也要在危险的政治关系中周旋，担任过官职，依靠过保护。名士的身份能给一些空间，不能让风险从此消失。

八大山人的白眼可以让我们与这段记忆相遇。但隔了一千多年，一个是关于诗人待客的叙述，一个是画家在纸上处理眼睛的方法。联想有力量，不等于每一条鱼都在复述阮籍的一句话。

更值得留下的，也许是这个问题：当别人希望你露出合适的表情，你还能不能保留自己的眼神？不热情，不解释，未必是在宣布没有人值得爱；有时只是暂时不肯按要求微笑。

我们不必让阮籍拥有全部立足之地、再把八大山人的脚下全部抽空。两个人都在缝隙里生活，也都没有把自己的样子完全交给别人决定。

六、他和慧能

说到留白，很容易想起慧能与禅。但一张没画满的纸，并不是佛学证明题。“本来无一物”也不能简单读成，一个人什么财物都没有，或者现实中的伤害根本不存在。

禅所问的执着，与战乱把家和依靠夺走，不是同一种空。自愿放下某种抓取，与被迫失去一种生活，不应因为用了同一个字，就被说成同样的自由。

八大山人长期在佛门生活，禅的修习可以进入理解他的视野。但我们不能因此把他的痛苦宣布为已经解脱，也不能把他的每一道笔墨都锁在创伤之中，仿佛他再也没有别的感受。

回到画上。留下空处，往往不是因为再也没有东西可画，而是因为这几笔已经使其他地方发生了变化。鱼旁边的白成了水，鸟周围的白让它的姿态更清楚。少，不等于被拿走。

这使慧能与他之间的比较，有了一个较谦逊的落点：我们能不能不把眼前的东西填满自己的定义？承认一个画家有过失去，也容许他继续长出技巧、幽默、亲近与新的兴趣。

他不需要先被剥夺一切，才获得画画的资格。灾难可以改变一个人，不应被后人的赞美写成天才必须付的学费。

七、留下来的不只是苦难

谈牛顿、马克思、伽利略、林肯，我们会分别想到计算的方法、分析社会的工具、可重复的观测和写进宪法的权利。它们不是四枚可以互换的勋章，却都能在一个人离开之后，继续被别人使用。

八大山人留下的是另一种可以继续相遇的东西。画仍在，我们的目光可以在一只鱼的眼睛前停住；书法仍在，一条线可以让人跟着感到提按、停顿和转弯。它们没有变成一条法令，但也不因此只是私人情绪。

画面看起来轻易，常使人忘记创作的劳动。一笔落下去，不能像说错的话那样随意收回。空处留下多大，浓墨压在哪里，都是需要拿捏的。流传下来的作品，还有题赠、应人所请的痕迹。朴素的表面，收纳着复杂的选择，也留着人与人的往来。

《河上花图》的题跋就提到受人请托而作，并记下跨越数月的作画过程。这里既有别人，也有时间。所谓个人风格，不是与任何人没有关系，而是在关系中仍能听出这个人的声音。

杜甫的诗没有因为来自苦难，就失去写作的技巧；八大山人的画也没有因为带着沉默的感觉，就失去主动创作的分量。两个人都把遭遇带进作品，却不只是遭遇的记录器。

我们珍惜这些画，不只因为它们证明他曾受苦。还因为他做出了值得继续看的东西，而那个人的生活，比我们从画里认出的苦难更宽。

八、他和米开朗基罗

本系列写米开朗基罗时，用过从石头中释放形体的比喻。它帮助我们靠近雕刻，是一个观看的入口，而不是说每尊完成的雕像，真的早已藏在石头里。

八大山人的纸，似乎在另一端。大理石有重量，需要凿去；白纸一开始就在，画家要决定把墨放到哪里。一个不断去掉，一个谨慎加上，两个人都在决定形体与周围空间怎样相处。

但八大山人并没有把一切都去掉。鱼的转向、鸟的重心、石头的棱角，都还在。水没有逐笔画出，并不说明鱼已经失去了水；有时，正是没有把水画满，鱼才有了可以游动的地方。

在《创造亚当》中，两根手指之间没有闭合的距离，使观看停在一个将触未触的瞬间。八大山人的大片空处，则使我们在墨迹之间来回移动。我们可以把它们并置着看，却不能把两个文化中的艺术都缩成同一个符号。

一个间隙可能让动作悬着，一片白可能让距离展开。它们留下的不一定是同一种情绪，也不是一种等待被填满的缺陷。作品没有把每一条关系都替观看者决定到底。

观众因此不是来领取标准答案的。你可以靠近，但不必把自己看到的一切，冒充为作者唯一想说的话。

九、哭之，笑之

1705年，八大山人去世，大约八十岁，康熙朝还没有结束。从明末走到清初，年轻人变成了老人。他经历动荡，也还俗、改号、作画、应人之请。一生里的年月，并不只有同一种颜色。

他的署名常把“八大山人”四字连写，后人从字形中读出“哭之”或“笑之”。这是耐人寻味的读法，不是一份能替他逐日记录心情的密码表。哭与笑靠近，也不意味着我们已经替他认出了最深的痛。

现在，让他来到本系列想象的桥头。维特根斯坦身旁放着两本书，伽利略的望远镜有人接着看，马克思仍在讨论。八大山人带着一支有墨的笔。他不必为了符合我们的印象，一直比所有人更安静。

他铺开纸，点下一只眼睛。旁边的人说像在生气，另一个人看着觉得好笑。他可以回答，也可以不回答。这一次，不用有人急着替他写好说明，更不用把他本人也画成一条终身翻白眼的鱼。

纸上还有很大的空处。不是因为这个人什么都没有了，而是因为他没有把所有地方都交给墨，也没有把每一次观看都提前规定好。

哭之，笑之。先让那只眼睛留在那里。让画家不止是身世，让看画的人有时间再看一眼。

桥头场景为文学想象。生年各馆资料有1624、1626等不同记载，正文采用约数；生平中的失常、哑与署名寓意均不作医学诊断或唯一心理解释。人物对照是散文阅读，并非画家意图的证明。

I. That Fish

Before reading Bada Shanren’s biography, stop at one of those fish. A few strokes make the body; the dark pupil sits high in the eye, leaving an expanse of white below. That little eye can be difficult to walk past.

It seems to roll at you. You suspect disdain. Look again, and perhaps the fish has simply arrived here and happened to glance upward. It says nothing. The busy one is the person looking.

We quickly supply a line: I despise this world. Add the painter’s history and the line seems to acquire evidence. But what a painted eye makes us think is not the same as knowing what was in the painter’s mind.

For a Chinese reader, the white eye can recall the poet Ruan Ji. That association has a long cultural history. Still, we do not need a key that unlocks every painting before we may stand before one. Notice how a dot of ink changes a fish—and how our interpretation changes with it.

More than three centuries later, the fish remains hard to settle. Mockery? Watchfulness? A refusal to make polite conversation? Or have we brought our own mood to the paper? These responses can approach the image without immediately competing for the one correct answer.

That is one place to begin with Bada. He puts down very little and makes a single glance insufficient.

II. A World Changes

We usually call him Zhu Da. He came from Nanchang in Jiangxi, a descendant of Zhu Quan, Prince of Ning, in the Ming imperial family. Records differ on his birth year, placing it in the 1620s. When Beijing fell and the Chongzhen emperor killed himself in 1644, he was a young man of about twenty.

The Ming did not disappear everywhere in one day. Resistance continued in the south; Qing conquest and the establishment of a new order unfolded through war. For someone descended from the old ruling family, what had once conferred standing could now bring danger.

He entered a Buddhist monastery and spent many years as a monk. Refuge belongs in that story, but a religious life is not necessarily a disguise worn only until it can be discarded. Years of practice, relationships, writing, and looking became part of the life that followed.

Biographical accounts describe disturbed behavior, sudden weeping and laughter, and periods of presenting himself as mute. Across the distance of those accounts, we cannot know every episode, diagnose him, or declare it all a calculated performance. Protecting oneself in upheaval need not have a single explanation.

Around 1680 he left monastic life and began supporting himself through painting and calligraphy. In 1684 he adopted the name Bada Shanren, by which he became best known. He had not vanished from history. He was changing how he lived and choosing how to sign his name.

The old world had changed, and what was lost could not simply be restored. But what remained was not an inert fragment. A person was still living, learning, meeting others, and putting brush to paper.

III. Not Only Someone Things Happened To

With Socrates, Marx, Galileo, or Wittgenstein, this series can begin from a conspicuous action: questioning, analyzing, observing, reconsidering one’s own thought. With Bada, the first thing to enter the room is often not what he did, but what history did to him.

That can tempt us to make him entirely passive. Yet deciding which line to omit, where a pupil should sit, or how a bird can seem almost unbalanced is not something disaster does on a painter’s behalf. It is work.

Not producing a political program does not mean having no direction. Remaining outside official service is not withdrawing from every human relationship. Selling paintings is not necessarily selling the self within them. Earning a living, knowing people, and declining to accommodate every expectation can coexist.

Galileo offers a comparison of pressures, not a ranking of suffering. One person’s trial records cannot explain another person’s days. A dynastic transition cannot dictate one identical response for everyone who lives through it.

Wittgenstein’s attention to what cannot be said may accompany us to the pictures, but the painter had languages of his own. Bada left poems, inscriptions, and calligraphy. Follow those marks and we discover that a sense of silence in an image can coexist with a life of varied expression.

He did not paint the lost dynasty back into existence. He did something else: within a changed life, he kept developing a way of handling ink that he could still make his own.

IV. The Paper Is in the Picture

Consider Fish and Rocks, dated 1699, at the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Beneath two large rock forms, seven small fish swim across unpainted paper. Those few fish marks allow the space around them to become water.

You need not see every ripple to believe they are swimming. Heavy rocks, tiny bodies, and the distances between them hold the image together. Blank space is not the disappearance of a setting. It can be another way of making one.

In Lotus, Rock, and Waterbird at the Palace Museum, a bird stands on a rock beneath lotus leaves, its body drawn in and supported by one leg. You may feel coldness or loneliness. Stay a little longer and consider how it remains standing on so small a support. The bird has a posture of its own, beyond the feelings we bring to it.

Bada did not invent ink painting out of nothing. He studied earlier painting and calligraphy, working in relation to traditions associated with Shen Zhou, Xu Wei, and Dong Qichang. The weight of a line, the wetness of ink, where an object is placed: trained hands and years of judgment stand behind them.

His work also opens out. Flowers on a River, from 1697, is a lotus-centered handscroll nearly thirteen meters long, followed by a substantial poem in his own hand. Leaves, rocks, and passages of ink take time to unfold. We move between densities and shades, then reach the poem. There are more voices here than a first glance suggested.

Bare paper can hold biography, but also water, air, distance, and a feeling the viewer has not yet named. Translate every blank into the same grief for a fallen dynasty, and we make the paintings smaller.

V. Ruan Ji and the White Eye

Ruan Ji lived through the third-century transition between Wei and Jin. The Book of Jin describes him showing the whites of his eyes to certain convention-bound visitors. Ji Xi received that cold welcome at a funeral visit; when Ji Kang later arrived with a zither and wine, Ruan was delighted. The contrasting “blue eye” is an idiom of favorable regard, not a report of a changing iris.

The same biography describes him riding out until the road ended, then returning in tears. These are remembered stories, not recordings of every moment. Still, they preserve a stance: refusing to hand one’s likes and dislikes wholly over to prescribed propriety.

Ruan was not looking down on the world from a place of perfect safety. He navigated dangerous politics, held office, and relied on protection. His standing as a celebrated scholar could open some room; it did not abolish risk.

Bada’s eyes can bring that memory into the gallery. But more than a millennium separates an account of a poet receiving guests from a painter’s way of placing pupils on paper. The association matters without making every fish a restatement of something Ruan said.

Perhaps the question worth keeping is this: when others expect an appropriate expression, can you retain a look of your own? Not being warm or forthcoming need not mean declaring everyone unworthy of love. Sometimes it means not smiling on demand.

We need not give Ruan all the ground in the world and pull every foothold from beneath Bada. Both lived in constrained spaces. Neither entirely surrendered the decision about how to appear.

VI. Bada and Huineng

Blank space readily suggests Huineng and Chan Buddhism. But an unfilled sheet is not a Buddhist proof. The familiar verse about nothing being there originally does not simply mean possessing no property, or that actual injury does not exist.

The attachment questioned in Chan is not the same as a home and its supports being taken away by war. Freely releasing a way of clinging and being forced out of a life must not become the same freedom merely because we call both emptiness.

Bada spent decades in Buddhist life, so religious practice belongs within our view. It does not entitle us to pronounce him cured of suffering, or to imprison every brushstroke in trauma, as though no other feeling could ever return.

Go back to the painting. Space may be left bare not because there is nothing left to paint, but because a few marks have already changed everything around them. White beside a fish becomes water; white around a bird makes its posture visible. Less does not necessarily mean something has been confiscated.

The comparison with Huineng can then make a more modest request. Can we refrain from filling what is before us with our definitions? Recognize the painter’s losses and also allow skills, humor, closeness, and new interests to keep growing.

He did not have to be stripped of everything to qualify as a painter. Catastrophe can change a person. Our admiration must not turn it into the tuition genius was required to pay.

VII. More Than Suffering Remains

Newton, Marx, Galileo, and Lincoln bring different inheritances to mind: ways of calculating, tools of social analysis, repeatable observations, rights written into a constitution. These are not interchangeable medals. Each nevertheless offers something others can use after the person is gone.

Bada left another kind of continuing encounter. A painting can still stop our gaze at a fish’s eye. Calligraphy can let us follow pressure, pause, and turning in a line. Neither becomes a decree. Neither is therefore merely a private mood.

Apparent ease can hide the work of making. A brushstroke is not easily recalled once it meets the paper. How much space remains open, where a dark mass bears down—these involve judgment. Surviving inscriptions and dedications also show works made for others. A spare surface holds complex choices and traces of human relationships.

The inscription on Flowers on a River records a request to make the scroll and a process extending across several months. There are other people here, and there is time. Personal style does not require being unrelated to anyone. It means that within those relations, this particular voice can still be heard.

Du Fu’s poetry did not lose its craft because it arose amid suffering. Bada’s paintings do not lose their deliberate making because they can feel silent. Both carried experience into art. Neither was merely a recording device for what happened to him.

We cherish these works for more than evidence that he suffered. He made something worth continuing to look at. And the life that made it was wider than the suffering we recognize in the images.

VIII. Beside Michelangelo

Writing about Michelangelo, this series used the image of releasing a form from stone. It brings us closer to carving: an imaginative way into looking, not a claim that every finished statue was already hidden inside its block.

Bada’s paper seems to begin at the other end. Marble has weight to be cut away; white paper is already there, awaiting a decision about where ink will go. One removes, the other adds carefully. Both decide how a form will live with the space around it.

Yet Bada did not remove everything. A fish’s turn, a bird’s balance, the edge of a rock remain. Water not drawn ripple by ripple is not water taken away from the fish. Sometimes leaving it unfilled is what gives the fish room to swim.

In The Creation of Adam, the interval between two fingers holds the gaze at an almost-touch. In Bada’s painting, a large unpainted area sets our eyes moving between marks. We can place the experiences beside each other without shrinking art from two cultures into one symbol.

A gap may suspend an action; bare paper may open distance. They need not carry the same feeling, and neither need be a defect waiting to be filled. A work has not decided every relationship to the very end on the viewer’s behalf.

The viewer is not there to collect the approved answer. We can come closer without passing off everything we see as the one thing the maker meant.

IX. Weeping, Laughing

Bada died in 1705, around eighty years old, before the Kangxi reign had ended. Between late Ming and early Qing, a young man had grown old. He lived through upheaval, left monastic life, changed his name, painted, and answered requests. The years of a life do not all have the same color.

He often joined the four characters of his artistic name in his signature. Later readers have seen shapes suggesting “weeping” or “laughing” in them. It is a compelling reading, not a codebook that records his daily moods. Tears and laughter approaching each other do not mean we have diagnosed his deepest pain.

Now let him arrive at this series’ imagined bridgehead. Wittgenstein’s two books lie nearby; someone is looking through Galileo’s telescope; Marx is still discussing. Bada brings a brush carrying ink. To fit our portrait, he need not remain quieter than everyone else forever.

He spreads paper and dots an eye. Someone thinks it looks angry; someone else finds it funny. He may answer or not. This time no one needs to hurry in with the definitive caption, or turn the man himself into a fish condemned to roll its eyes for life.

Much of the paper is still open. Not because this person has nothing left, but because he has not given every place over to ink or decided in advance how every encounter must go.

Weeping, laughing. Leave the eye there for a moment. Let the painter exceed his biography, and give the person looking time to look again.

The bridgehead is imagined. Museum records differ on a birth year of 1624 or 1626, so this essay uses approximate ages. Reports of disturbed behavior or muteness and readings of the signature are not treated as diagnoses or exclusive explanations. Comparisons are readings, not proof of the painter’s intention.
