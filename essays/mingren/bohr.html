玻尔，对面也是

Bohr, The Other Side Too

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、两种颜色的纹章

1947年，玻尔获授丹麦大象勋章。他为自己的纹章选了一个特别的中心图案：太极图。下面写着拉丁文，Contraria sunt complementa。对立即互补。

物理学家本来可以画原子、公式，或者一件仪器。他却放进黑与白，两边沿着弯曲的边界相接，没有哪一边涂满整个画面。看着它，目光会自然地从这边走向那边。

这幅图很容易让人想到生活。坐在桌子对面的人，也许看见了自己没看见的东西。一种说法可以有用，却不必包办全部。麻烦出在下一步：我们会不会顺势以为，所有冲突的背后都一定藏着和谐？

玻尔的互补性，原本是从一个更严格的问题里长出来的。在什么实验条件下，我们究竟能够知道什么？不同装置使不同的经典描述有了用处，但这些描述不能直接拼成一张熟悉的物体照片。

它不是说，在完全相同的条件下，两个互相排斥的断言都成立。恰恰相反，条件必须交代清楚。纹章上的格言很短，为它找到足够结实的根据，却需要漫长的工作。

跟着这份工作走，会慢慢遇到另一个问题：怎样给自己尚未看见的东西留位置，同时仍然分得清证据和愿望、不同意和欺骗，以及一个人的资格和他某句话是否正确？

二、没有散掉的原子

把一本书放到桌上，它还是一本书。这样普通的事实，在卢瑟福的原子核模型遇上经典电动力学时，忽然变得难以解释：绕核运动的带电电子应该不断辐射、失去能量。这个微型太阳系，照理说站不住。

玻尔1912年曾在曼彻斯特与卢瑟福工作，1913年发表原子论文。他提出稳定的定态，把辐射同允许能级之间的跃迁联系起来，而不是让电子沿每一条轨道持续漏掉能量。熟悉的图景留了一部分，基本规则却变了。

这个改动带来了具体的成绩。氢原子发出的光，并不是什么颜色都有；光谱仪里是一条条特定位置的线。玻尔用能量差解释它们的频率。仪器里安静的几道痕迹，成了想法必须回答的考题。

后来，量子理论会大幅改变对电子状态和运动的描述。我们今天画出的玻尔轨道，不是拍到了电子绕着跑的照片；跃迁也不必想成小人从三楼瞬移到一楼。那个模型的重要性，正在于它打开了下一步。

它不能解决所有原子的问题，多电子原子尤其难办。一个模型可以帮助我们认识世界，却未必已经把世界说尽了。知道它走到了哪里、又在哪里停下，本来就是理解这项工作的组成部分。

1922年，玻尔因原子结构和辐射研究获诺贝尔奖。桌上的书仍旧安静地放着。改变的是我们终于知道，那个仿佛不用解释的“还在这里”，背后藏着多少没有想明白的事。

三、装置在问什么

1927年，玻尔在科莫会议上提出互补性。我们通常从一个非要选边的问题走进去：光到底是波，还是粒子？日常的图像很好用，但此刻，它们向世界索要的东西可能太多了。

干涉装置呈现波动式的条纹，探测器记录下的却是一个个局部事件。这不是光在迎合观察者的信念，今天穿这套衣服，明天换那套。换一个人来，认真搭好同样的实验，结果依然可以核对。

更尖锐的冲突，是既要充分知道粒子走哪条路，又要保留完整的干涉。装置能留下什么记录，会影响可出现的图样。部分路径信息与部分干涉可见度，还存在精确的权衡；并非绝对不能同时出现两方面的任何特征。

玻尔坚持，说明一个现象时，要把取得结果的条件一起说出来。探测器怎样放，装置怎样连，回答的是什么问题，都不能在抄下结论以后顺手擦掉。正是这些条件，让那句话有明确的意思。

这里不需要人的意识把电子“看出来”。仪器可以先留下记录，过后才有人来读。测不准关系也不只是观察者手笨，把原本全都确定的东西碰乱了。数学上的限制和我们怎样理解它，要仔细区分。

奇怪之处不在于科学从此不要精确，而在于精确还要包括：这个实验究竟支持哪一句话。我们学会换一个问法，而不是强迫世界交出最初那个问法所预设的答案。

四、没有离开的对手

爱因斯坦一直回来追问。在索尔维会议上，也在后来的讨论中，思想实验把难题磨得更尖：一种理论预测得极好，是否就意味着，它已经交出了完整的物理描述？

流传最广的“上帝掷不掷骰子”，把两人的争论讲得像漂亮的对剑。但爱因斯坦的关切不只是讨厌概率，玻尔的回答也不是一句机锋便使对方哑口无言。他们争的是，描述世界究竟还欠世界什么。

1935年，爱因斯坦、波多尔斯基和罗森提出EPR论证。分开系统之间的关联，使人似乎可以通过对一个系统的测量，确定预测另一个的某些量。依照他们采用的实在判据和分离条件，量子描述还不完整。

玻尔质疑的是那个判据的运用。他强调，能够作出哪一种预测，与实验条件有关，并没有把答案写成一种机械扰动从一端传到另一端。纠缠也不能让我们随意发出一条超光速消息。

后来，贝尔把有关局域性的重要假定变成可以实验检验的限制。违背贝尔不等式的结果，排除了相应假定下的局域隐变量解释，不是排除一切可能的隐变量理论，更不是替玻尔写过的每句话补上一枚正确印章。

所以，争论留下的东西比胜负表大。爱因斯坦帮助人们把问题问得足够清楚，后来的人才有新的方法继续追。一个反对意见没有得到它希望的答案，也可能改变所有人此后必须解释的事情。

五、被占领城市里的一次谈话

哥本哈根研究所给年轻物理学家提供了争论的地方。玻尔与海森堡需要彼此的启发，也需要彼此的阻力。他们的理解并不完全相同，后来“哥本哈根诠释”这个整齐名称，也装着不那么整齐的分歧。

1927年，两人对测不准关系和测量的解释发生争执。年轻人的数学与年长者对实验描述的坚持，并不是两块一放就合的拼图。要理解，就得修改；关系再亲近，修改也未必舒服。

1941年9月，海森堡来到被德国占领的丹麦，探访玻尔。那时他参与德国的铀研究。占领不是这场见面的背景墙，它改变了来访者的话会怎样被听见，也改变了听者有理由害怕什么。

玻尔后来写而未寄的草稿，对那次谈话的记忆，与一些令人安心的战后自述差距很大。里面有他对核武器和来客立场的震惊。这些材料值得读，正因为那次见面不能简单定成一次已经证实的秘密反战使命。

然而草稿也是多年以后写下的回忆，不是谈话当时留下的录音。两人战后还有联系。我们不能因为信未寄出，就替玻尔宣判一辈子绝不原谅，更不能替海森堡把内心全部说完。

剩下的难处更像人会遇到的事：曾经帮你想明白许多问题的人，后来在一件大事上，你已经不敢信任他的判断。共同的过去不能消除眼前的危险，科学才能也不能免掉谁对自己行动的责任。

六、过海

1943年秋天，危险直接逼近玻尔。他的母亲出自犹太家庭，纳粹迫害威胁着他。他与玛格丽特逃离丹麦，抵达瑞典。边界忽然不再是地图上的抽象线，而是能否到达安全之地的区别。

丹麦犹太人的大规模营救，靠的是许多人：传递警讯的，提供藏身处的，安排船只的，驾船的，以及愿意接收难民的瑞典。七千多名犹太人抵达瑞典。著名科学家在这个共同历史之中，而不是站在它上面独自完成营救。

传记记下玻尔在瑞典为难民争取支持。他的名望让声音更容易被听见。但一段话替代不了一间藏身的屋子，一次接应，也替代不了把惊慌的乘客安全带走的人。这些事必须真正接起来。

海上的路，让“合作”有了另一种重量。讨论室里，另一个人可以帮你改进论证；这里，另一个人可以让你活到天亮。而这条路是否也对没有诺贝尔奖可出示的人开放，同样重要。

玻尔随后前往英国，再作为英国团队的一员到美国，参加战时原子能项目，参与了制造核武器的工作。他既是逃过迫害的人，也是参与造弹的科学家；前一种经历并没有免除后一种身份带来的责任。

一个亲身体会过逃离危险的人，又参与了将改变整个人类危险规模的事业。他后来的政治思考，长在这份纠缠的责任里面，不是从事件之外一个干净、安全的位置送来的智慧。

七、让世界仍然可以相通

爱因斯坦和玻尔的分歧很深，却不是“固执”与“开明”各自找了一个代表。爱因斯坦追问独立于测量选择的实在，要求一个令人满意的描述；玻尔追问，当实验条件被认真考虑以后，清楚的描述究竟能说到哪里。

“不看月亮，月亮就不在了”，不足以说明任何一方。玻尔不需要意识替世界打开电源，我们也不必把爱因斯坦写成拒绝证据的人，才能让他的对手显得勇敢。

他们之间可贵的，是那个继续要求回答的人没有被赶出去。尊重不意味着同意争议中的结论，而是承认：回答你很困难，不等于你这个提问者可以被取消。

1950年，玻尔致联合国公开信，把开放带入另一个场合。他回顾战时的参与，呼吁国际合作、信息交流和共同安全安排，希望在核竞争变成长期灾难之前，建立另一种国家关系。

这不是一句“大家互相信任就好了”。需要监督，需要制度，也需要相互承担的承诺。它同样不是把自己的战时工作一笔否定，而是尝试回答：自己参与制造的危险，现在该怎样共同面对？

实验室里的习惯，不会自动变成国家之间的办法。权力、暴力和处境的不平等，都得正面处理。但问题仍然值得问：对手能否给相互了解留一点空间，使各自的自保，不至于永远加深共同的危险？

八、与西田并坐

西田几多郎提供了另一条入口。他谈纯粹经验，让我们重新考虑那个仿佛早已摆好的分工：这边是认识的主体，那边是被认识的对象。在我们各就各位之前，经验里已经发生了什么？

玻尔从不同的方向追问：把实验对象与使结果有明确含义的条件分开，究竟还能怎样描述它？两种思考有一处相似，都让我们停下来看看，自己是不是把划分后的结果，当成划分之前便存在的起点。

但相似并不等于同一。西田的哲学概念，包括“绝对无的场所”，不能直接换成一幅太极图。玻尔的互补性没有证明西田的形而上学，西田也没有替量子理论补上数学推导。

再请蒯因加入，会多一个问题：那些我们依赖的界线，凭什么成立？他们不必成为替同一个真理作证的一组人。每个人有自己面对的难题，也要交出不同种类的回答。

不过，他们坐在一起，确实可能让读者更留心自己的语言。有用的描述，一旦忘记用途和边界，也会变成关人的围墙。看见这个风险，是邀请我们检查，不是科学已经宣布所有界线一概虚假。

从物理学走到怎样对待一个人，中间仍需要理由。难民应该得到安全，不是因为电子具有互补性。人先于我们喜欢的科学比喻，就已经值得认真对待。比喻可以提醒我们倾听，却不能独自承担为什么要倾听的全部论证。

九、没有寄出的信

1962年11月18日，玻尔在哥本哈根去世，七十七岁。他留下的文件里，有写给海森堡却未寄出的草稿。档案馆2002年将它们公开，读者由此得到更多证据，却不是得到了一扇能看穿所有动机的窗。

草稿保留着一个人怎样对待句子的过程。一个词被重新考虑，一项指责需要说得更准，一段解释遇到阻力。即使不知道为什么最终没有寄出，也能看出：这场谈话仍然向写信的人索要着什么。

现在请玻尔拿着其中一页，走到这个系列想象的桥上。这不是历史中的重逢。海森堡看到了他。他们站在说话能够听见的地方，暂时都等着，看对方是否会先开口。

休谟合起双陆棋盘，特斯拉把手里的鸽子食挪开，免得落到纸上。波伏瓦给两人都留出回答的位置。此刻没有谁必须立刻宣布理解了、原谅了，或者同意了。

玻尔把纸放到栏杆上。海森堡可以读，可以反驳某段记忆，但要回答纸上实际写出的东西。还可能有另一份叙述，并不意味着每一份叙述都有同样的证据。

他们可以回到标着日期的那一行，拿另一份文件来对照，追问一句话有什么根据。有些事实可以确立，有些说法可以否定，有些动机从来没有写下。不知道的部分留着，并不妨碍对已经知道的部分负责。

纹章的黑与白重新浮现。但在这座桥上，它们不保证自动和解。只有一个更小的可能：困难的工作还没有开始以前，先不让对面那个人消失。

信仍然在那里。这一次，在我们想象的场景里，它终于被放到了另一个人能够回答的位置。接下来发生什么，纹章没有预先替他们写好。

I. A Shield with Two Colours

When Niels Bohr received Denmark's Order of the Elephant in 1947, his coat of arms acquired an unusual centre: the Chinese yin-yang symbol. Beneath it stood the Latin words Contraria sunt complementa. Opposites are complementary.

A physicist could have chosen an atom, a formula, an instrument. Bohr chose an image in which black and white meet along a curved boundary. Neither colour covers the shield. Your eye keeps moving from one to the other.

The image speaks readily to us. Perhaps the person across the table can see something we cannot. Perhaps a description can be useful without containing everything. The difficulty begins when this reasonable modesty turns into a promise that every contradiction must conceal a harmony.

Bohr's complementarity had a more demanding origin. It concerned what could be learned under specified experimental conditions. Different arrangements could make different classical descriptions applicable; those descriptions could not simply be pasted into one familiar picture of an object.

This was not a licence to say two incompatible claims were true under identical conditions. The conditions were part of what had to be stated. His motto was short. The work needed to earn it was not.

Follow that work and another question gradually appears. How do you remain open to what your present account leaves out, while still distinguishing evidence from wish, disagreement from deception, and another person's dignity from the truth of what they say?

II. An Atom That Stays Together

Put a book on a table. It remains a book. This ordinary fact became surprisingly troublesome when Rutherford's nuclear atom was considered through classical electrodynamics: orbiting charged electrons should radiate energy. The miniature solar system should not remain comfortably intact.

Bohr had worked with Rutherford in Manchester in 1912. His atomic papers followed in 1913. He introduced stationary states, with radiation associated with transitions between permitted energy levels rather than continuous loss from every orbit. Something familiar was retained; something fundamental was suspended.

The success was concrete. Hydrogen did not emit every conceivable colour. Its spectral lines occurred at particular frequencies, and Bohr's energy differences gave a way to account for their pattern. A set of marks in an instrument became a demanding test of an idea.

The model was not a photograph of electrons travelling on tiny planetary tracks. Nor does a modern quantum transition require us to imagine a little object magically teleporting between floors. The later theory would change the account of states and motion considerably.

Bohr's model had limits, notably for more complicated atoms. Yet it opened a route where the old account failed. A model can be fruitful without being the final furniture of the universe. Recognizing its reach is part of understanding its achievement.

The 1922 Nobel Prize recognized his work on atomic structure and radiation. The book still lies on the table. What has changed is how much work we realize is hidden in that calm, apparently self-explanatory fact.

III. What the Apparatus Asks

In 1927, at Como, Bohr presented complementarity. We usually reach it through a question that seems to demand a single noun: is light a wave or a particle? Our ordinary pictures have begun to ask more of nature than they can reliably provide.

An interference arrangement makes a wave-like pattern visible. Individual detections arrive as localized events. These are not two capricious costumes chosen because someone happens to believe in one rather than the other. They are reproducible features of carefully arranged experiments.

The sharper conflict concerns obtaining full which-path information while retaining undiminished interference. Change what the apparatus can record and the available pattern changes. Partial information and partial visibility also admit precise trade-offs; the lesson is subtler than never seeing any features of both.

Bohr insisted that an account of the phenomenon include the conditions under which the result was obtained. The detector, the arrangement, and the question being answered do not disappear when we write down a finding. They make clear what the finding means.

No human mind has to conjure an electron into existence. An instrument can leave a record before anybody reads it. And uncertainty relations are not merely a clumsy observer shaking an otherwise perfectly known object; the mathematical limits and the interpretation must be distinguished.

The unfamiliar thing is not that science gives up precision. Precision now includes saying which claim the experiment supports. We have learned to ask a better question instead of forcing the world to fit the first question that occurred to us.

IV. The Opponent Who Stayed

Einstein kept returning to the difficulty. At the Solvay meetings and afterwards, thought experiments became a way of making it sharper. If quantum mechanics predicts so successfully, does that mean it provides everything a complete physical account should provide?

The popular exchange about God and dice gives their disagreement the neatness of a duel. But Einstein's concern was not simply a dislike of probability, and Bohr's response was not a clever sentence that ended the matter. They were arguing over what a physical description owes the world.

In 1935 Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen proposed their famous argument. Correlations between separated systems appeared to permit certain predictions about one through a measurement on the other. Given their criterion of reality and separation assumptions, they argued, the quantum description must be incomplete.

Bohr challenged the use of that criterion. His reply stressed the experimental conditions that define possible predictions, rather than a mechanical disturbance travelling from one distant particle to the other. Entanglement does not provide a freely controllable message faster than light.

Bell later made crucial assumptions about local accounts testable. Experiments violating Bell inequalities exclude the relevant local hidden-variable models under the assumptions of those tests. They do not abolish every possible hidden-variable theory or retrospectively certify every sentence Bohr ever wrote.

The enduring gift of the argument is therefore larger than a scoreboard. Einstein helped formulate a problem that later generations could investigate in new ways. An objection may fail to secure its preferred answer and still change what everyone must learn to explain.

V. A Conversation in an Occupied City

Copenhagen's institute gave young physicists somewhere to argue. Bohr and Heisenberg needed each other's resistance as well as each other's insight. Their accounts were never perfectly identical; even what later became known as the Copenhagen interpretation contains disagreements that a single school label conceals.

In 1927 they disputed the interpretation of uncertainty and the analysis of measurement. The younger man's mathematics and the older man's insistence on experimental description did not simply slot together. Understanding required revisions, and revision could be uncomfortable even among close collaborators.

Then came September 1941. Heisenberg visited Bohr in German-occupied Denmark while working within Germany's uranium research effort. The occupation was not background scenery. It changed what a visitor's words could mean and what the person hearing them had reason to fear.

Bohr's later unsent drafts remembered the conversation very differently from reassuring postwar accounts of Heisenberg's intentions. They record alarm over nuclear weapons and the visitor's position. The documents matter precisely because the encounter cannot be reduced to a confirmed mission by a secret saboteur.

Yet they are recollections, composed years afterwards, not a recording of the conversation. The two men did have postwar contact. We cannot read a lifetime of absolute non-forgiveness straight out of the silence of an envelope that was never sent.

Something more ordinary, and harder, remains: the person who once helped you think can become someone whose judgment you cannot trust. A shared past does not settle a present danger. Nor does scientific brilliance exempt either friend from answering for what he did.

VI. Across the Water

In the autumn of 1943, the danger reached Bohr directly. His mother came from a Jewish family; Nazi persecution threatened him. He and Margrethe escaped occupied Denmark for Sweden. Suddenly a border was not an abstraction but the difference between reaching safety and being caught.

The wider rescue of Danish Jews depended on many people: warnings, hiding places, boats, crews, and Sweden's willingness to receive refugees. More than seven thousand Jews reached Sweden. A celebrated scientist belonged inside that collective history, not above it as its sole author.

Biographical accounts describe Bohr advocating for refugees while in Sweden. The scale of his fame made his voice useful. But a speech could not replace the person finding a room, arranging passage, or keeping a frightened passenger hidden. Rescue needed those acts to meet.

The water gives the word cooperation a different weight. In a seminar, another person might improve an argument. Here another person might help you live until morning. It mattered whether the route remained available to someone who had no Nobel Prize to show.

Bohr went on to Britain and then to the United States as part of the British contribution to the wartime atomic project. He participated in the effort to build a nuclear weapon. His rescue does not make this later responsibility disappear into a story of pure innocence.

The same man who knew what it meant to escape danger was now involved in work that would transform danger for whole populations. His political thinking grew within that entanglement of responsibility. It was not wisdom delivered from a safe place outside events.

VII. Keeping a World Open

Einstein and Bohr disagreed deeply without becoming interchangeable representatives of stubbornness and enlightenment. Einstein wanted a satisfactory account of a reality independent of our choices of measurement. Bohr asked what an unambiguous account could say once the experimental conditions were taken seriously.

Neither position is adequately illustrated by claiming the moon vanishes when nobody looks. Bohr did not need consciousness to switch on the world. Nor must Einstein be made incapable of accepting evidence so that his opponent can appear courageous.

What is valuable in their relation is the continued demand for an answer. Respect did not mean conceding the disputed point. It meant recognizing that the questioner was not disposable merely because answering him had become difficult.

Bohr's 1950 Open Letter to the United Nations brought openness into a different arena. Reflecting on his wartime involvement, he urged international cooperation, information sharing, and arrangements for common security before nuclear rivalry hardened into permanent catastrophe.

This was not simply the advice that everyone should trust everyone. Inspection, institutions, and reciprocal commitments were needed. Nor was it a repudiation of every part of his wartime work. He was seeking a political response to dangers in whose creation he had participated.

A laboratory's habits do not automatically work between states. Power, violence, and unequal vulnerability have to be faced directly. Still, the question has force: can rivals make enough room for mutual knowledge that defending themselves does not endlessly increase the danger they share?

VIII. Beside Nishida

Nishida Kitarō offers another way into the question of our descriptions. His account of pure experience asks us to reconsider an immediately given division between a knowing subject and an object. Before we settle into those roles, what has already been happening?

Bohr, by another route, asks what it means to describe an experimental object apart from the conditions making a result intelligible. The resemblance can interrupt a habit: we often treat a distinction as though it had arrived before the work of drawing it.

The resemblance is not an identity. Nishida's philosophical vocabulary, including the place of absolute nothingness, cannot be replaced by a yin-yang diagram. Bohr's complementarity does not prove Nishida's metaphysics; Nishida does not supply the missing mathematical derivation of quantum theory.

Bring Quine into the conversation and a further question appears: what supports the distinctions we rely on? Again, these thinkers need not be made into witnesses for one universal discovery. Each works on different problems and owes different kinds of answers.

Their company may nevertheless make a reader more attentive. A useful description sometimes becomes an enclosure when we forget its purpose and limits. Noticing that danger is an invitation to examine our language, not a scientific licence to declare every boundary unreal.

The move from physics to how we treat a person requires its own reasons. A refugee is not owed safety because an electron is complementary. People matter before our favourite scientific image arrives. The image may remind us to listen; it cannot bear the entire argument for listening.

IX. The Unsent Letters

Bohr died in Copenhagen on November 18, 1962, aged seventy-seven. Among the papers he left were drafts addressed to Heisenberg and never sent. Released by the archive in 2002, they gave readers more evidence, not a final window into every motive.

A draft preserves work on a sentence. A word is reconsidered; an accusation is made more exact; an explanation meets resistance. We need not know why the letter remained unsent to see that the conversation still demanded something of its writer.

In the imaginary gathering on this series' bridge, let Bohr carry one of those pages. This is no historical reunion. Heisenberg notices him. For a moment they stand within speaking distance, each waiting to see whether the other will begin.

Hume closes his backgammon board. Tesla moves his handful of grain away from the paper. Beauvoir leaves room for an answer from each man. Nobody is obliged to decide immediately that they have understood, forgiven, or agreed.

Bohr puts the sheet on the railing. Heisenberg can read it. He can object to a recollection, but the objection must meet what the paper actually says. The possibility of another account does not give every account equal support.

They can return to a dated line, compare it with another document, ask what supports a claim. Some facts can be established; some assertions can be rejected; some motives were never written down. Leaving those motives open does not release either man from responsibility for what the record does show.

The black and white of the coat of arms return to mind. On this bridge they offer no automatic reconciliation. There is only the more modest possibility of not making the other person disappear before the difficult work begins.

The letter is still there. For once, in our imagined scene, it has been placed where another person can answer. What happens next is not already written into the emblem.
