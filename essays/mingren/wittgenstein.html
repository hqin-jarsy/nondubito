维特根斯坦，沉默之后

Wittgenstein, After the Silence

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、沉默

《逻辑哲学论》最后一句话，把门关得很轻，也很坚决：“对于不可说的东西，我们必须保持沉默。”

德文原作1921年发表，英文版次年出版。维特根斯坦在序言里说，他相信哲学问题在根本上已经得到解决。那时的他还很年轻，却觉得最要紧的工作已经做完了。不是再补几章，而是可以离开了。

他真的离开过学院生活。在奥地利乡村教小学，做过园丁，又参与设计姐姐在维也纳的住宅。房子由他与建筑师保罗·恩格尔曼共同设计。门、窗、比例，都是需要落到实处的问题。一个宣告哲学可以收工的人，仍然要过自己的日子。

但事情没有停在那里。1929年，他回到剑桥。后来那些讲课、讨论和反复修改的笔记，逐渐走向《哲学研究》。这本书1953年出版时，他已经去世两年。

一个年轻人说：到这里就够了。后来，还是这个人，重新坐到桌前。

维特根斯坦的故事值得从这里读起。不是因为他曾把全部问题解决，也不是因为他后来把全部答案推翻，而是因为，已经印成书、已经与自己名字连在一起的思想，仍然能被他重新打开。

二、第一本书

《逻辑哲学论》很薄。翻开来，是1、1.1、1.11这样的编号，句子一层层展开。它给人的第一印象，像一座已经量好尺寸的建筑：每一部分有位置，每个位置与其他部分相连。

我们不妨先用一句平常话靠近它：“猫在桌上。”你听懂这句话，不必先去看猫究竟在哪里。它描画了一种可能的情形；看见房间之后，你才知道它说得对不对。猫不在桌上，句子也不会因此失去意思。

年轻的维特根斯坦关心的，正是这样的可能性：语言怎样能够呈现一个事实，怎样让我们知道什么情况会使一句话为真、什么情况会使它为假。他用“图像”来说明命题与世界之间的关系。这里不是说，每个词都像照片一样贴在一件东西上；关键在于要素怎样组合，怎样表示一种可能的安排。

这给哲学带来一个不客气的问题：一句话听起来很深，是否真的说出了什么？词都认识，语法也像模像样，可如果我们始终不知道它究竟呈现了什么，它的庄严语气就不能替它作证。

苏格拉底让人重新检查“我知道”；维特根斯坦让人重新检查“我说出来了”。两个人的方法和处境不同，却都使我们停在最容易滑过去的地方。说得流利，不等于已经想清楚。

不过，这不是一张把所有话都送进实验室的通行证。图像理论不能简化为“当场验证不了，就是废话”，更不是后来科学哲学里那套证伪标准。书里最难的地方，恰恰在它画出界线之后。

三、沉默里面有什么

如果把世界上的事实一件件列出来，我们会得到什么？天气、疾病、工资、星体的位置。表可以不断延长。但一个人问“我该怎样活”，未必是在等表上再增加一行。

《逻辑哲学论》把伦理价值与事实的描述分开。它没有因此宣布，伦理不值得在意。相反，书的末尾留下强烈的张力：能够说清楚的，不一定就是我们生活中最要紧的；最要紧的，也未必能像说明桌子的尺寸那样陈述出来。

这里的沉默，更像一种克制。不要因为你渴望一个最终答案，就把它装扮成又一条已经查明的事实。描述一个人死于哪一年，与回答死亡对这一生意味着什么，不是同一种工作。

读到这里，也许会想起黑格尔：思想能否把分裂的部分重新理解在一个整体之中？两位哲学家的问题并不相同，不能用一场谁更诚实的比赛来判决。但对读者来说，两种姿态的距离很清楚：一种吸引我们继续寻找联系，另一种提醒我们留意，自己的话是不是已经越过了它能承担的范围。

而且，这本薄书没有把自己安放在界线之外，宣布永远免检。接近结尾时，维特根斯坦用了梯子的比喻：读者借它爬上去，最终要把梯子放下。如何理解这一步，至今仍有争论。至少，它让这本看起来极其坚硬的书，多了一道朝向自身的裂缝。

这不是“什么都不能说”。是说完之后，还得知道自己说到了哪里。

四、为什么回来

后来，维特根斯坦不再满足于早先的语言图景。这个转变经过许多讨论和修改，并不是某一天突然灵光一现，更不能只归因于别人误读了他。

《哲学研究》的序言提到拉姆齐和斯拉法给他的批评。思想改变的现场，并不只有一个天才独坐的房间。还有别人提出的问题，有自己一时回答不上的地方，也有一句话过了很久才显出的力量。

我们可以从饭桌上试着理解那个困难。有人说：“把盐递给我。”他通常不是在报告一项事实，而是在请求你做一件事。有人说：“我明天一定来。”这可以是预测，也可以是在作出承诺。要听懂，你得知道两个人正在做什么。

再想想问候、玩笑、道歉、喊痛。它们不必先改装成事实报告，才获得说出口的资格。一个孩子学会向人道谢，也不只是把某个名称与某个对象配对。

这些例子不是几招就能击倒一本复杂著作的武器。它们提醒我们的是：如果一幅关于语言的图景太迷人，我们可能先规定语言必须怎样工作，再把不合图景的生活挤到门外。

他回来的地方，不只是剑桥。也是那些曾经被一张整齐的图纸遮住的日常场景。

五、第二本书

《哲学研究》也有编号，但不再像前一本书那样，用层层嵌套的命题搭成一座体系。它的段落不断提出问题、换一个例子、让想象中的人反驳，甚至把刚走过的路再走一遍。读者很难站在远处，一眼把它看完。

“语言游戏”是其中一个重要说法。“游戏”不是说语言只是消遣，也不是说谁想怎样说就怎样说。它把说话放回活动里：请求、计数、讲故事、报告、表演，各有学会它的方式，也有别人能够理解和纠正你的条件。

比如“水”。火场上的呼喊，病床边的轻声请求，课堂里的讨论，都可能用到这个字。水并没有在每个场景里神秘地变成另一种物质；变化的是这句话在现场做的事。一个人要你接水管，另一个人希望你端起杯子。

所以，《哲学研究》第43节把词的意义引向它在语言中的使用。维特根斯坦特意留了限定：这是对很大一类情形的说明，不是包办所有情形的新公式。假如我们背下“意义就是使用”，从此拒绝再看具体例子，恰好又把一句提醒变成了新围墙。

使用也不是孤零零的个人随意决定的。我们一起学会说话，在共同的生活中辨认承诺、理解指令、争论一个词用得是否合适。语境重要，是因为里面有人、有惯例、有正在发生的事，不是因为任何解释都一样好。

早期的他追问语言凭什么能够描画世界；后来的他更愿意带我们去看语言实际怎样生活。这是深刻的转向，却不是从“只能说”倒向“只能做”。报告事实，本来也是我们用语言做的事情之一。

六、改自己的书

改别人的思想，有时比较容易。你的名字还在安全的一边。改自己的代表作，难处不只是重新写几句话，而是承认：大家通过这本书认识的那个我，并不拥有最后发言权。

这个系列里，苏格拉底追问雅典人的确信，马克思重新处理黑格尔的问题，伽利略用观测和论证挑战既有的天文学。维特根斯坦在这里的特别之处，是我们可以把两本署着同一个名字的书，并排放在桌上，看见作者怎样转过身来检查自己的起点。

他不是历史上唯一改变思想的人，也不是靠自己一人完成这一切。把他写成孤独地打败自己的英雄，会把那些批评、谈话和长期的犹豫全擦掉。真正值得看见的，是他没有因为书已经成功，就把继续思考当作背叛。

我们在日常生活里也遇得到缩小了的同类困难。你一直是家里“最理性的人”，现在却发现自己对亲人的判断不公平。承认错误，仿佛连过去那个身份也要交出去。于是你宁可把对方解释得更差一点，也不肯改自己的一句话。

维特根斯坦不能替我们解决这个困难。他自己的生活，也不是一份人人适用的温和指南。但两本书留下一种可以尝试的勇气：忠于自己，不必等于终身替自己曾经说过的话辩护。

也许，恰恰因为这个问题对我重要，我才不能让旧答案一直替我坐在那里。

七、两本书都还在

有趣的是，《哲学研究》没有让《逻辑哲学论》从书架上消失。前者成为后期哲学的重要著作，后者也继续被阅读、争论和重新解释。两本书都留下了很深的影响，影响又不只限于赞同它们的人。

这不证明两套冲突的主张能够同时全对。一本书值得读，也不要求它的每一个判断都获得最后胜利。有时，问题被问得足够准确，即使答案后来需要改变，那个问题仍然在起作用。

读第一本书，我们可以练习警惕空转的语言。有人告诉你，这个安排符合“历史必然”，所以你承受的一切都应该接受。先停一下：这里究竟说了什么？哪些事情被那个宏大的词遮住了？

读第二本书，我们又可以检查自己的筛子。一个朋友说“我撑不住了”，你若只问这句话对应哪项可测量的指标，很可能漏掉他正在向你求助。仔细听，不是放弃判断；是先弄清眼前的人在做什么，再决定怎样回应。

一边防止语言虚张声势，一边防止标准过窄，把活人的话削成一种形状。这不是维特根斯坦留给我们的速成方法，而是读者可以从两本书之间带走的一点练习。

他先前搭起的东西，仍然能帮助后来的人。后来改变的地方，也不用为了维护他的名声而藏起来。

八、画线的人

再往前看，会遇见康德。他追问：我们的经验和知识依赖什么条件？认识因而有了边界：“物自体”不能像经验中的对象那样被认识。

维特根斯坦早期追问的是表达的边界：怎样的句子真正说出了什么？两个人都使哲学回头检查自己的能力，但他们画的不是同一条线。康德谈经验的条件，不能简化成世界只是人的幻觉；维特根斯坦谈语言，也不是给康德的图纸换上几个新词。

如果在这个系列里把他们放在一起，吸引人的不是给两套哲学判输赢，而是画线本身的责任。我们经常以为，规定了边界，就再也不必说明边界从哪里来。

后来的维特根斯坦让我们再问一次：我用来整理经验的那幅图景，是否也需要检查？我以为所有问题都必须按一种方式回答，会不会已经把某些问题改坏了？

这里既没有宣布康德最后获胜，也没有宣布后来者全部失败。哲学史不是排出一个永远不动的积分榜。一个思想家的力量，有时就在于他让后人不得不继续问，而不是替后人免除了提问。

画线，可以是诚实。回头看看画线的手，也可以。

九、桥头的两本书

1951年4月29日，维特根斯坦在剑桥去世，刚过六十二岁生日。按照朋友诺曼·马尔科姆记录的转述，在贝文医生家里，他请贝文夫人替自己带一句话：“告诉他们，我度过了美好的一生。”

这句话很容易被写成一场戏剧性的反驳：年轻时不许说的，临终终于说了。但早期的他并未把生命的价值当作不值得在意的东西，日常说一句“美好的一生”，也不能不问语境，就判定为违反了哪一条哲学禁令。

我们只知道，这句话被留给了要来看他的朋友。它不是为证明第二本书比第一本书正确而说的。我们也无须替他列完苦难、成功和失败的账，才能允许他这样回望自己的一生。

现在，让他来到这个系列想象的桥头。伽利略的望远镜放在一旁，雕塑家的手上还像有石粉。维特根斯坦抱着两本书，一薄一厚。桥头的人并没有都停止思考；只是他带来的东西，让那个还没有结束的问题特别显眼。

他把两本书放下。我们不必在它们之间选一个永远不再改变的自己，也不必假装每一次改变都很轻松。还可以翻开某一页，指着一句曾经深信的话说：这里，我想再想一想。

“美好的一生。”书可以帮助我们听懂一个人，却不必替他签发这句话的许可证。那个说话的人，比我们为他整理出来的两本书，还多一点。

本文的桥头场景为文学想象；对两部著作的阅读是散文阐释，不把临终转述当作理论证明。

I. Silence

The final sentence of the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus closes a door: what we cannot speak about must be passed over in silence. The gesture is quiet. Its finality is not.

The German text appeared in 1921, followed by an English edition in 1922. In his preface, the young Wittgenstein expressed the conviction that the problems of philosophy had essentially been solved. He was not promising a sequel to fill the remaining gaps. He thought he could leave.

For a time, he did leave academic life. He taught children in rural Austria, worked as a gardener, and helped design his sister’s house in Vienna with the architect Paul Engelmann. Doors, windows, proportions: there were other kinds of exacting work to do. The man who thought philosophy could close its account still had a life to live.

In 1929 he returned to Cambridge. Years of teaching, conversation and revision would lead toward the Philosophical Investigations. That book appeared in 1953, two years after his death.

A young man announces that the work is done. Later, the same man sits down at the desk again.

This is where his story begins to matter to us. Not because he solved every problem, or because he subsequently withdrew every answer. Because a thought already printed, acclaimed and attached to his name could still be opened again.

II. The First Book

The Tractatus is a slim volume, built from numbered propositions: 1, 1.1, 1.11. Its successive levels can feel like a precisely measured building. Every part has a place, and every place belongs to an arrangement.

An ordinary sentence offers a way in: “The cat is on the table.” You can understand it before looking into the room. It presents a possible situation. Looking then tells you whether it is true. If the cat turns out to be under the chair, the sentence does not lose its meaning; it is simply false.

The young Wittgenstein wanted to understand how this was possible. How can language represent a fact? How can a proposition show what would make it true or false? His account treats a proposition as a picture. That does not mean every word is a little photograph stuck to a thing. What matters is an arrangement of elements capable of representing a possible arrangement in the world.

This brings an awkward question to philosophy. Has an impressive sentence actually said anything? Familiar words and respectable grammar may give us the feeling of understanding. But if we cannot tell what has been presented, solemn delivery cannot supply what is missing.

Socrates interrupts the confidence of “I know.” Wittgenstein interrupts the confidence of “I have said it.” They work in very different settings and ways, but each makes us pause where we are inclined to hurry past. Fluency is not yet clarity.

This is not a licence to send every utterance to a laboratory. The picture theory cannot be reduced to “anything we cannot immediately verify is rubbish,” nor is it the later philosophy-of-science criterion of falsifiability. The difficult part of the book begins when the boundary has been drawn.

III. Inside the Silence

Suppose we listed all the facts in the world: the weather, illnesses, wages, the positions of stars. The list could grow indefinitely. Yet someone asking how to live may not be waiting for one more entry.

The Tractatus distinguishes ethical value from the description of facts. It does not thereby announce that ethics is unimportant. Toward its close, an extraordinary tension appears: what we can state clearly need not be what matters most to us. What matters most may resist being stated as we would state the dimensions of a table.

Silence here can be heard as restraint. Wanting an ultimate answer does not entitle us to dress one up as another established fact. Giving the year of someone’s death and asking what that death means within a life are different undertakings.

Hegel offers a useful contrast for a reader: can thought understand divided things within a larger whole? His question is not Wittgenstein’s, and no contest in personal honesty could settle their differences. Still, the distance between the invitations is illuminating. One draws us toward further connections; the other asks whether our words are being made to carry more than they can bear.

Nor does the slim book simply place itself outside the boundary, permanently exempt from scrutiny. Near its end comes the image of a ladder to be discarded after the reader has climbed it. How to understand that instruction remains disputed. At the very least, the apparently unyielding building contains a question directed at itself.

This is not a demand to say nothing. It is a demand to notice how far what we have said can take us.

IV. Why Return?

Eventually Wittgenstein became dissatisfied with his earlier picture of language. The change developed through discussion and repeated revision. It was not a single flash of illumination, and other people’s misunderstandings of him cannot by themselves explain it.

In the preface to the Investigations, he acknowledges criticism from Frank Ramsey and Piero Sraffa. Intellectual change did not take place in a room containing only a solitary genius. Other people asked questions. There were objections he could not readily answer, and remarks whose force emerged only with time.

Consider a difficulty that becomes visible at the dinner table. “Pass the salt” is normally a request, not a report. “I will come tomorrow” might be a prediction, but it might also be a promise. To understand the words, we need to know what the people speaking them are doing together.

Think, too, of greeting, joking, apologising, crying out in pain. These activities need not be repackaged as factual reports before their words earn a place in language. A child learning to thank someone is not merely learning to match a name with an object.

Such examples are not a few easy punches that dispose of a difficult book. They expose a temptation: a picture of language can become so compelling that we first prescribe how language must work, then push inconvenient pieces of life outside the frame.

Cambridge was not the only place to which he returned. He also returned to everyday scenes that a beautiful diagram had made harder to see.

V. The Second Book

The Investigations has numbered remarks too. But they do not build the nested hierarchy of propositions familiar from the Tractatus. They ask questions, change examples, hear an imagined objection, and travel over ground already crossed. This is not a book easily surveyed from a safe distance.

One of its important expressions is “language-game.” A game here is not necessarily a diversion, nor does it mean we may use words however we please. It brings speaking back into activities: requesting, counting, telling a story, reporting, performing. These have ways of being learned, and conditions under which others can understand or correct us.

Take “Water!” At a fire, beside a sickbed, and in a classroom, that word may belong to very different exchanges. The substance need not mysteriously change from one setting to the next. What changes is the work the utterance is doing. One speaker may need a hose connected; another may want a cup lifted to their lips.

Section 43 directs attention from a word’s meaning to its use in language. Wittgenstein explicitly limits the observation to a large class of cases, not every possible case. If we memorise “meaning is use” and stop looking at examples, we have turned a reminder into another enclosing wall.

Nor is use a private decision made afresh by an isolated individual. We learn to speak with others. Within shared lives we recognise promises, follow instructions and dispute whether an expression fits. Context matters because it contains people, practices and events, not because every interpretation is equally good.

The early philosopher asks how language can picture the world; the later philosopher increasingly takes us to see how it lives. That is a profound change of direction, not a switch from “only saying” to “only doing.” Reporting a fact is itself one of the things we do with words.

VI. Revising Your Own Book

Criticising another person’s ideas can be easier. Your own name remains on the safe side of the table. Revising your defining work means more than altering sentences. It means admitting that the self everyone has learned to recognise through that book does not possess the final word.

In this series, Socrates questions Athenian certainties, Marx reworks questions inherited from Hegel, and Galileo challenges received astronomy through observation and argument. With Wittgenstein, we can put two books bearing the same name beside each other and watch an author turn to examine his own starting point.

He was not the only thinker ever to change his mind, and he did not accomplish the change alone. A heroic story about a solitary man defeating himself would erase the conversations, criticism and prolonged uncertainty. What deserves attention is his refusal to treat a successful book as a reason to stop thinking.

There are smaller versions of this difficulty in ordinary life. You have always been “the reasonable one” in your family. Now you begin to suspect that your judgment of someone close to you is unfair. Admitting it feels like surrendering an identity. You find another fault in the other person instead of changing one sentence of your own.

Wittgenstein cannot solve that difficulty for us. His own life is no universal handbook of gentleness. But the two books offer a courage we might try: being faithful to yourself need not mean defending everything you have ever said for the rest of your life.

Perhaps it is precisely because the question matters to me that I cannot let an old answer keep occupying my chair.

VII. Both Books Remain

The Investigations did not make the Tractatus disappear from the shelf. One became a major work of his later philosophy; the other continues to be read, disputed and reinterpreted. Both have mattered far beyond the circle of people who agree with them.

That does not establish that incompatible claims are all true at once. Nor must every claim in a book win a final victory before the book deserves reading. Sometimes a question has been posed so sharply that it keeps working even when an answer has to change.

From the first book, a reader can practise suspicion of language running on its own grandeur. Someone tells you an arrangement embodies “historical necessity,” and therefore you should accept whatever it costs you. Pause. What has actually been said? What has that imposing expression concealed?

From the second, we can learn to examine our own filter. A friend says, “I can’t keep going.” Ask only which measurable variable the sentence reports, and you may miss the request for help. Listening closely does not abandon judgment. It first asks what the person before us is doing, so that a response can meet them.

On one side, resistance to verbal inflation; on the other, resistance to a standard so narrow that living speech must be cut down to fit it. This is no quick method certified by Wittgenstein. It is a small practice a reader might carry away from the space between the books.

What he built earlier can still help people who come later. What he subsequently changed need not be hidden to protect his reputation.

VIII. Drawing a Boundary

Further back along this series stands Kant. He asks about the conditions that make our experience and knowledge possible. Knowledge has limits; the thing in itself cannot be known as one more object within experience.

The early Wittgenstein asks about the limits of expression: when does a sentence genuinely say something? Both philosophers turn inquiry back upon its own capacities, but they are not drawing the same line. Kant’s account of the conditions of experience does not reduce the world to an individual’s hallucination. Wittgenstein’s inquiry into language is not Kant’s plan with a few labels changed.

What makes the comparison fruitful here is not deciding who wins. It is the responsibility involved in drawing a boundary. We often behave as though establishing a limit excuses us from explaining where it came from.

The later Wittgenstein prompts a further question. Might the picture by which I organise experience itself require examination? If I assume every question must admit the same kind of answer, have I already distorted some of the questions?

None of this awards Kant a final victory or dismisses all his successors. Philosophical history is not a league table with positions fixed for ever. A thinker’s strength can lie in making later people continue to ask, not in releasing them from the need to do so.

Drawing a line can be an honest act. Looking back at the hand that drew it can be another.

IX. Two Books at the Bridge

Wittgenstein died in Cambridge on 29 April 1951, shortly after his sixty-second birthday. According to the account preserved by his friend Norman Malcolm, he asked Mrs Bevan, in whose home he was staying, to pass on a message: “Tell them I’ve had a wonderful life.”

It is tempting to make the words a dramatic refutation: at the end, he finally says what his younger self had forbidden. But his early philosophy did not dismiss the value of life as unimportant. Nor can an ordinary expression about a wonderful life be convicted of breaking a philosophical rule without attending to its setting.

The message was left for friends who were coming to see him. It was not delivered to prove that the second book had defeated the first. We do not need to finish an audit of suffering, achievement and failure before allowing him that backward look at his own life.

Let him now arrive at this series’ imagined bridgehead. Galileo’s telescope rests nearby; there seems to be stone dust on the sculptor’s hands. Wittgenstein carries two books, one thin and one thick. The others have not all stopped thinking. His particular baggage simply makes an unfinished question unusually visible.

He puts the books down. Between them, we need not choose a self that will never change again, or pretend that changing is easy. We can still turn to a page, point to a sentence we once believed, and say: here, I would like to think again.

A wonderful life. Books can help us hear a person; they need not issue a permit for that sentence. The person who spoke remains more than the two volumes we have arranged beside his name.

The bridgehead is an imagined scene. This essay offers a reading of the two books, not a philosophical proof extracted from a reported final remark.
