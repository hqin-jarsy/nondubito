费希特：认知者站在哪里

Fichte: Where Is the Knower Standing?

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、被当成康德的人

1792年。一本书匿名出版了：《试论一切启示之批判》。

不少读者以为是康德写的。书的来路确实经过柯尼斯堡：康德读过手稿，也帮助安排出版。只是到了读者手里，作者的名字不见了。

后来康德公开澄清：不是我写的。是一个叫费希特的年轻人写的。

忽然间，几乎没人认识的约翰·戈特利布·费希特，成了德语哲学界引人注目的名字。三十岁，织带工人的儿子，靠资助得到教育，做过家庭教师，也因为缺钱中断过学业。写了一本书，人们把它当成康德。

康德替他正了名。但这个正名也带着一个阴影：你从康德那里出发。你的名字第一次被看见，恰恰是因为它被错认成另一个名字。

你能走多远？

费希特走了很远。远到康德自己不认了。1799年，康德公开声明，拒绝把费希特的知识学当作自己的哲学。

七年前，是这个人说：书是他写的。七年后，也是这个人说：那不是我的道路。

费希特不能永远借着康德的名字走，也不能因为康德不再认同，就让自己的问题停下来。

他走向的那个地方，只有一个字：我。

二、我设定我自身

1794年。费希特到耶拿大学任教。他的讲课吸引了许多学生。荷尔德林听过；谢林、诺瓦利斯、黑格尔和早期浪漫派，也从各自的地方回应他。那些人没有全挤在同一间教室，却都要面对他推到眼前的问题。

他的课堂里有一种逼近感。不是只把一个结论交给你，而是要你自己做一遍那个思想动作。

我设定我自身。Das Ich setzt sich selbst。

什么意思？

孔德替知识铺地面。波普尔让猜想面对检验。狄拉克听方程说话。屈原把自己的声音留在诗里。沿着这几篇走过来，现在把目光转一下。

谁在观察？谁在猜想？谁在听方程？谁在写诗？

那个“谁”从哪里来？

你把一切放到面前，想找到“我”是其中哪一件。可是，把东西放到面前、辨认、比较的活动，已经在发生。你还没有给“我”找好位置，“我在判断”已经开始了。

费希特要抓住的，是这件事。自我意识不是一个做好的物件，后来又多添了“知道自己”这项功能。它就在与自身发生关系的活动中成立。

他把这叫作 Tathandlung，常译“事实—行动”。不是先有一块叫作“我”的石头，再由石头做一个动作。活动与自我意识的成立，不能这样拆成两件事。

也不是某一个早晨，约翰·费希特用意志把自己、桌子、树林和宇宙创造出来。他在追问认识的条件，不是在替宇宙编一份出生记录。

这句话绕口。但它抓住的问题很近：当你想从外面把认识解释完，那个正在解释的人，已经在里面。

三、缝隙站在你脚下

回到本乐章的弧线。

孔德灌缝。波普尔画线。狄拉克听到了方程的声音。屈原在水边，让美开口。

前几篇，我们的目光不断朝外：哪些说法站得住，怎样检验，还有什么没有被收进来。到费希特这里，目光落到了脚下。

你站在哪里？

你给观察立标准——好，是谁在立？你替理论划边界——好，划线的人，在那张图的什么地方？你说方程很美——好，是谁认出了那种美？

这些问题不是说前面几个人从没想过认知者。也不是拿“划界标准不能被证伪”当作一句妙语，就宣布波普尔输了。科学标准本来不必是一条物理假说。

费希特使我们的追问再转了一层：别只看图上有什么。看看画图这件事怎样发生。

你以为缝隙只在地板之间。现在发现，缝隙也在你脚下。

你能观察自己的身体，回忆自己的行为，反省自己为什么那样想。可是，当你把这些摆成一幅自己的画像，观看画像的人也还在。那幅画没有把正在看它的活动一起用完。

这里的“我”不是宣布自己不可研究。费希特恰恰要反思它。只是反思不能假装自己从一个没有人的地方开始。

你用来看世界的那只眼睛，也可以在镜子里看见自己。但镜中的眼睛，和此刻正在看，不是一回事。

认知者是认识容易略过的盲点。把镜头转过来，盲点并不会自动消失；至少，你不再以为画面就是全部。

这就是我在这里说的：你也站在缝隙上。不是你不存在，也不是你什么都不知道。是那张由你画出来的图，不能反过来装作自己不需要你。

四、被指控无神论的人

1798年。费希特在自己参与编辑的《哲学杂志》上发表文章，讨论人为什么相信神圣的世界秩序。

他不从世界需要一位制造者，推出一个在天上发号施令的人格神。他把宗教信念放到道德行动里：人相信自己的行动有意义，相信一个道德的世界秩序。

被指控无神论了。

同一期还有福贝格的文章。匿名小册子发难，萨克森等邦国施压，大学不能置身事外。费希特写辩护，公开还击，最后以辞职相威胁：如果受到申斥，他就走。

他把话说得很满。对方并没有因此退让。

1799年，他离开耶拿。三十七岁，妻子，年幼的儿子。从讲堂里引人注目的教授，变成一个要重新安排生计的人。

他去了柏林，靠私人讲学和写作继续工作。1800年的《人的使命》，从怀疑走到知识，再走到信念。他不满足于坐着看世界。知道之后，自己怎样行动，仍然要回答。

后来，普鲁士战败，柏林被法军占领。1807—1808年，他在这里发表《对德意志民族的演讲》，把复兴的希望放到教育里。

但不能只说后人利用了他。他自己也把德意志人的语言与使命抬到特殊的位置。一个讲自由的人，开始为一个民族设计共同的未来。那幅未来的图，会不会又把某些人留在外面？这个问题也该朝着他问。

1810年，柏林大学成立，他在这里任教，1811年当选校长，次年辞职。一个讲“我”的人，一直站在学校、出版、政治和别人的生活里。

1814年1月，他病逝，五十一岁。早期传记把那场疾病与妻子约翰娜的护理工作连在一起：她照顾伤病士兵，染病，逐渐好转；他随后也病了。我们不必凭这段记载补出一条已经查清的传染链。

留下来的，是很具体的两个人。她去照顾伤病者。他们自己也会生病，也需要别人照顾。

“我设定我自身”，没有把说这句话的人变成不受伤的东西。

但会受伤，也没有使他只剩下一条被外力推着走的管道。

五、他和康德

费希特和康德，是本系列里很重要的一组对话。

康德说，我们认识的是在人的感性与知性条件下呈现的对象，不能因此声称已经认识了物自体。费希特就沿着这个边界追问。

如果又把物自体当成一个在外面、因果地产生感觉的东西，我们是不是刚划完界，就越过去替界外的东西说话了？

提出这个问题的人，也得回来说明自己站在哪里。

康德并没有忘记“我”。他早已讨论“我思”如何伴随表象，怎样把它们统一为同一个意识的表象。费希特不满足于此。他要从主体的活动出发，更彻底地把认识与行动放到一个起点上。

不是在康德漏写的一页上补一个名字。是要重新安排整本书的开头。

于是，“我设定我自身”后面，还有“非我”。活动遇见不是自己随意决定的限制。费希特用 Anstoß 来说这种阻滞、触动。你往前走，却发现不是想怎样便能怎样。

这不是拿一个小小的个人意志，吞掉整个外部世界。有限的认识者，恰恰要在限制中解释自己的活动。世界没有因为“我”被放到哲学起点，就变成任我改写的纸。

康德1799年的公开声明很不客气。他拒绝费希特的知识学，也不认可从纯逻辑中推出真实对象的做法。

费希特认为自己在追问康德的前提。康德认为这条路已经走偏。谁也没有因为站在前辈或后辈的位置，就自动拿到最后一票。

费希特没有停。他继续讲，继续改写知识学。那个“第一原则”，不是写下以后就再也不用工作的护身符。

这场争论留给本系列的，不是一张谁超过谁的成绩单。是那个不能跳过去的问题：你不能只问什么算知识，而不问谁在提问；当你回答“我”的时候，别人也仍然可以问你，那是什么意思。

六、他和本轮其他人

把前面几个人再请回来。

孔德并非把认识者当成完全透明的窗户。他知道理论参与观察。可就算窗户擦得再亮，窗框是谁装的，为什么装在这里，仍然可以再问。

波普尔承认猜想由人提出。他把发现怎样发生，与提出之后怎样检验，分开研究。这不是把人逐出知识，却也使我们有理由继续朝检验之前看：提出猜想的那个“我”，并没有被检验程序解释完。

狄拉克让方程说话。可是谁在听？谁辨认出一种美，愿意顺着那个不熟悉的负号走下去？方程有不由他任意更改的后果，追随这些后果的人，却不是多余的。

屈原把“我”写得很早。《离骚》开头就说到自己的世系与父亲。那个声音有名字，有来处，有不肯放弃的东西。

费希特追问的，是这样一个声音能够说“我”的条件。诗里的自述和哲学里的论证，不必互相取代。一个让你听见谁在说话，一个让你停下来问，说“我”这件事意味着什么。

但费希特的“我”，也不能被我们写成永远独自站着的一个点。

1794年的《学者的使命》已经把学者放到社会中：他要向别人学习，也要把自己的知识交回去。到了1796—1797年的《自然法权基础》，另一个自由者进入了个体自我意识的条件。

他用 Aufforderung，说一种召唤：另一个人向我提出要求，却不是像推动物体一样，把我的回答也替我做完。我被当作能够自己行动的人来对待，才在这份关系里认出自己的自由。

这份关系有身体。我的行动占据空间，你的行动也需要空间。不能一边需要你来承认我，一边把你能站的地方全收走。

所以，后来的梅洛-庞蒂不是赶来给一个完全没有身体的费希特装上身体。薇依也不是把注意力说成“我”向外投出的光就算完成；她会问，能不能让自己的需要退一点，看见受苦的人。阿伦特会追问，不思考怎样与判断和责任的失守纠缠。

他们会从不同方向继续。费希特在这里留住的，是一个不能被跳过的位置。

认知者不是一个可有可无的括号。认知者就是认知的地基。而这个地基，不是一块没有身体、没有他者、没有历史的石头。

七、自由的第一个系统

费希特把自己的工作称作“自由的第一个系统”。

为什么？因为他不肯把“我”只写成结果。

基因、环境、教育、遭遇，都可以解释一个人的许多事情。但当解释变成“所以你不过是这些原因流过的一根管道”，那个会回应、会反省、会承担行动的人，就被删掉了。

承认因果关系，与宣布主体只是管道，不是一回事。费希特要守住的，是自由能够成为起点，而不是等所有外部说明都做完了，才看看还剩下多少。

他在1797年写道，一个人选择什么哲学，与他是什么样的人有关。理想主义还是独断论，不只是书架上两本书的区别，也牵涉你怎样理解自己的活动。

这句话很强，也带着他自己的偏向。不能拿它当作“论证无用”的口令，更不能把不同意自己的人都判成没有自由。费希特仍在论证，只是他不认为对自由的实际确信，可以像搬动物件一样从外面塞给一个人。

这让我想起本系列反复遇见的“不能不”。

你不能不说一句自己认为该说的话，不能不继续做一件别人觉得不值得的事。不是因为有人已经替你写好命令，也不是你忽然变成了没有因果来历的人。是走到这里，你认出了一个方向：如果把它交出去，外面的日子也许照样过，自己却知道放弃了什么。

你仍可能退缩、改口，也可能重新发现先前想错了。“不能不”不是一部替你取消判断的内部机器。它把判断和承担交回你手里。

这是我与费希特相遇的地方，不是说他的第一原则已经写出了同一套答案。把自己的方向认回来，不等于世界必须服从那个方向。

难处也在这里。一个相信自由的人，能不能允许另一个人的回答不是自己所期待的？

费希特写民族教育时，希望培养一种可以确信其道德方向的新人。可是，如果老师已经决定了学生必须成为怎样的人，教育还在召唤自由，还是在制造一个可靠的结果？对共同体太有把握，也可能使圈内的人失去自己开口的地方。

他的思想里已经有相互承认。我们正因此可以把这个问题拿回来问他。

“我”不能被抹掉。“你”也不能。我的起点要站得住，不能靠让你消失。

自由不是撤掉脚下所有的阻力。是面对阻力时，仍然不把自己仅仅交给它，也不把别人仅仅变成我的材料。

八、桥头

现在，让费希特走进这座想象的桥头。

他的步子很大，速度很快。他是一个急着行动的人。仿佛话刚说完，身体已经往前去了。

他上了桥，第一件事不是看别人。他看自己的脚。

桥面上有缝隙。孔德灌过的水泥。波普尔画过的线。狄拉克听到声音的那些裂纹。

费希特蹲下来，把手伸向缝隙。他不是在听里面有什么。像是在确认一件事：自己站的位置，也没有被桥面的图纸画完。

他站起来，对所有人说：

“你们讨论了这么久知识是什么，边界在哪里。你们有没有想过：你们自己在哪里？提出这些问题的那个你，在你的体系里有位置吗？”

孔德低头看日历。纸上有很多名字。正在看这张纸的自己，却没有因为名单齐全，就也被说清楚。

波普尔皱了皱眉。他还要问：你给这个位置作的说明，能不能被批评？

费希特没有退开。他的问题不是要求别人免于追问自己。恰恰是不要让追问的人从追问里消失。

屈原从水边抬起头。一个说“我”的人，看着另一个说“我”的人。他们没有说成同一句话。诗里的来处与哲学里的起点，在这里彼此看见。

康德在远处站着。费希特看了他很久。这个人曾经替他把名字说出来，也曾经对他的道路说不。

他没有再借康德的名字站着。康德也没有替他点头。

费希特找了一个位置。不站在一整块板的中央。他站在缝隙上，两只脚分别踩住两块板，身体正对着下面的黑暗。

他不害怕。他就是从那里来的。

I. The Man Mistaken for Kant

1792. A book appears anonymously: Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation.

Many readers take it for Kant’s work. Its road to publication has indeed passed through Königsberg: Kant read the manuscript and helped it find a publisher. But when it reaches readers, the author’s name is missing.

Kant subsequently makes the correction public. The book is not his. It was written by a young man called Fichte.

Suddenly, the almost unknown Johann Gottlieb Fichte has a name in German philosophy. Thirty years old. A ribbon weaver’s son, educated with a patron’s help, employed as a tutor, forced to interrupt his studies for lack of money. He writes a book, and people take him for Kant.

Kant gives him his name back. The correction also casts a shadow: you began from Kant. Your name was first noticed because it had been mistaken for somebody else’s.

How far can you go?

Fichte goes a long way. Far enough for Kant to reject the connection. In 1799 Kant publicly refuses to recognize Fichte’s Wissenschaftslehre as his own philosophy.

Seven years earlier, this man said: he wrote the book. Seven years later, the same man says: that is not my road.

Fichte cannot go on borrowing Kant’s name. Nor can Kant’s refusal be the end of his questions.

The place toward which he walks has one word: I.

II. The I Posits Itself

1794. Fichte begins teaching at Jena. His lectures attract a following. Hölderlin hears him; Schelling, Novalis, Hegel and the early Romantics respond from their different positions. They are not all crowded into one classroom. But the question he brings forward will confront them.

There is a pressure in his teaching. He does not merely hand you a conclusion. You must perform the movement of thought yourself.

The I posits itself. Das Ich setzt sich selbst.

What does that mean?

Comte lays a floor for knowledge. Popper exposes conjectures to tests. Dirac listens to the equation. Qu Yuan leaves his voice in a poem. We have followed them this far. Now turn your eyes a little.

Who observes? Who conjectures? Who listens to the equation? Who writes the poem?

Where does that who come from?

You put everything before you and try to find which item is the I. Yet the activity of placing, recognizing, comparing is already happening. Before you have found a place for the I, “I am judging” has already begun.

Fichte wants to grasp that activity. Self-consciousness is not a finished object to which knowing itself is subsequently added as another feature. It exists in the activity of relating to itself.

He calls this Tathandlung, often translated as fact-act. Not first a stone called I, then an action performed by the stone. The activity and the being of self-consciousness cannot be separated in that way.

Nor does Johann Fichte wake one morning and create himself, the table, the trees and the universe by willing them into existence. He is asking about the conditions of knowing, not issuing the universe a birth certificate.

The sentence is difficult. The question is close at hand: when you try to explain cognition entirely from outside, the person doing the explaining is already inside.

III. The Crack Is Under Your Feet

Back to this movement’s arc.

Comte grouts the cracks. Popper draws a line. Dirac hears the equation. Qu Yuan, by the water, lets beauty speak.

Our eyes have kept turning outward: which claims stand up, how to test them, what remains unaccounted for. With Fichte, our gaze drops to our feet.

Where are you standing?

You establish standards for observation. Who establishes them? You draw a boundary for theories. Where, in that drawing, is the person holding the pencil? You say the equation is beautiful. Who recognized the beauty?

This does not mean the preceding thinkers never considered the knower. Nor can we dispose of Popper with the clever objection that his criterion cannot itself be falsified. A criterion for science need not be a physical hypothesis.

Fichte turns our question one level further: do not look only at what is in the drawing. Look at how drawing happens.

You thought the cracks were only between the floorboards. Now you find one under your own feet.

You can observe your body, recall your conduct, reflect on why you thought as you did. But once you have assembled these into a portrait of yourself, the person looking at the portrait remains. The picture has not used up the activity of looking.

The I is not declaring itself beyond investigation. Fichte wants precisely to reflect upon it. Reflection simply cannot pretend to begin from a place where nobody stands.

The eye with which you see the world can see itself in a mirror. The reflected eye and the seeing now underway are not the same thing.

The knower is a blind spot that knowing readily overlooks. Turning the camera around does not automatically remove it. At least you no longer mistake the picture for everything.

That is what I mean here by standing on the crack. Not that you do not exist, or that you know nothing. A map you have drawn cannot turn around and pretend it never needed you.

IV. Accused of Atheism

1798. In the Philosophical Journal, which he co-edits, Fichte publishes an essay about the grounds of belief in a divine world-order.

He does not infer a personal ruler in heaven from the world’s supposed need for a maker. He locates religious conviction in moral action: trust that our actions have significance within a moral order.

He is accused of atheism.

Forberg has an essay in the same issue. An anonymous pamphlet attacks them. Saxony and other states exert pressure. The university cannot remain outside it. Fichte writes in his defence, attacks publicly, and finally threatens resignation if he is censured.

He leaves himself little room to retreat. The authorities do not retreat instead.

In 1799 he leaves Jena. Thirty-seven, with a wife and a young son. From a celebrated lecturer to someone who must find a livelihood again.

He goes to Berlin and continues through private lectures and writing. The Vocation of Man, published in 1800, moves through doubt and knowledge to faith. He will not be satisfied with watching the world. After knowing, the question of how to act remains.

Prussia is subsequently defeated; French troops occupy Berlin. Here, in 1807–08, he delivers the Addresses to the German Nation, placing his hope of renewal in education.

It will not do to say only that later readers misused him. He himself gives German language and vocation a privileged position. A thinker of freedom begins designing a people’s common future. Whom might that picture leave outside? The question must face him too.

The University of Berlin opens in 1810. He teaches there, becomes its elected rector in 1811, resigns the following year. A man who speaks of the I keeps standing inside schools, publishing, politics and other people’s lives.

He dies in January 1814, aged fifty-one. An early biography connects his final illness with his wife Johanna’s nursing: she cares for sick and wounded soldiers, falls ill, begins to recover; he subsequently falls ill too. That account does not give us a medically established chain of transmission.

What remains is two particular people. She goes to care for the wounded. They, too, can fall ill and need somebody’s care.

“The I posits itself” has not made its speaker invulnerable.

But vulnerability has not reduced him to a pipe through which outside forces run.

V. Fichte and Kant

Fichte and Kant form one of this series’ important conversations.

Kant says we know objects under the conditions of human sensibility and understanding; we cannot thereby claim knowledge of things in themselves. Fichte follows that boundary with a question.

If we then treat the thing-in-itself as something outside us that causally produces sensations, have we crossed the boundary just drawn to make a claim about what lies beyond it?

The person asking must also return to explain where he stands.

Kant had not forgotten the I. He had already examined the I think that can accompany representations and their unity within one consciousness. Fichte wants to start more radically from subjective activity, grounding knowing and acting together.

He is not adding a name to a page Kant overlooked. He wants to rearrange the beginning of the book.

After the self-positing I comes the not-I. Activity encounters a limitation it cannot arbitrarily choose. Fichte calls this check or impetus Anstoß. You move, and discover that things do not simply happen as you please.

No small personal will has swallowed the external world. The finite knower must account for its activity in limitation. Placing the I at philosophy’s beginning does not turn the world into paper I may rewrite at will.

Kant’s public declaration of 1799 is unsparing. He rejects the Wissenschaftslehre and the attempt to extract real objects from pure logic.

Fichte thinks he is pursuing Kant’s premises. Kant thinks that pursuit has gone astray. Neither being the predecessor nor being the successor automatically confers the final vote.

Fichte does not stop. He continues lecturing and reworking the Wissenschaftslehre. A first principle is no talisman that ends the work once written down.

What this exchange leaves our series is not a scorecard showing who surpassed whom. It is the question that cannot be skipped: you cannot ask only what counts as knowledge without asking who asks. And when you answer I, others may still ask what you mean.

VI. Fichte and the Others in This Round

Bring the preceding figures back.

Comte did not treat the knower as a perfectly transparent window. He knew theory participates in observation. But however clean the window, we can still ask who fitted its frame, and why it was fitted here.

Popper acknowledged that people propose conjectures. He distinguished their discovery from the testing that follows. This does not expel people from knowledge. It does give us reason to keep looking toward what precedes the test: its procedure has not fully explained the I who proposes the conjecture.

Dirac lets the equation speak. Who listens? Who recognizes a beauty and follows that unfamiliar minus sign? The equation has consequences he cannot arbitrarily change. The person pursuing them is not superfluous.

Qu Yuan brings the I in early. The Li Sao opens with his ancestry and father. This voice has a name, a history, things it will not surrender.

Fichte asks about the conditions under which a voice can say I. A poem’s self-account and a philosopher’s argument need not replace one another. One lets you hear who speaks. The other makes you pause over what saying I involves.

But we must not make Fichte’s I an eternally solitary point either.

The Scholar’s Vocation of 1794 already places the scholar in society: learning from others and returning knowledge to them. In the Foundations of Natural Right of 1796–97, another free being enters the conditions of individual self-consciousness.

Fichte speaks of a summons, Aufforderung. Another person addresses me without completing my response for me, as though pushing an object. Addressed as someone capable of acting for myself, I recognize my freedom within that relationship.

Bodies belong to it. My actions occupy space; yours need space too. I cannot require you to recognize me while appropriating every place where you could stand.

Merleau-Ponty therefore does not arrive later to install a body in an entirely bodiless Fichte. Nor will Weil finish with attention by calling it light cast outward by the I: she asks whether our own needs can recede enough for a suffering person to be seen. Arendt will ask how thoughtlessness becomes entangled with failures of judgment and responsibility.

They will continue in different directions. What Fichte keeps before us here is a position that cannot be skipped.

The knower is not an optional parenthesis. The knower is the foundation of knowing. And that foundation is no stone without body, others or history.

VII. The First System of Freedom

Fichte called his work the first system of freedom.

Why? Because he refuses to make the I only a result.

Genes, surroundings, education, encounters can explain much about a person. But when explanation becomes “therefore you are only a pipe through which these causes run,” the person who responds, reflects and takes responsibility has been deleted.

Acknowledging causality and declaring the subject nothing but a pipe are different moves. Fichte wants freedom to be a starting point, not whatever might be left after every external explanation is finished.

In 1797 he writes that a person’s choice of philosophy depends on the kind of person they are. Idealism or dogmatism: not merely two books on a shelf, but different ways of understanding your activity.

It is a forceful claim, carrying his own partisanship. It is no password for dismissing argument, still less for declaring everyone who disagrees unfree. Fichte continues to argue. But he does not think practical conviction of freedom can be inserted from outside as one moves an object.

This brings me back to the cannot-not that keeps appearing in this series.

You cannot not say something you believe needs saying. You cannot not continue work that others find pointless. Not because someone has already written your orders, or because your life has suddenly lost its causal history. You have recognized a direction. Surrender it and outward life might continue much as before, but you would know what you had relinquished.

You may still falter, withdraw your words, or discover you were mistaken. Cannot-not is no internal machine that removes judgment. It returns judgment and responsibility to your hands.

That is where I meet Fichte, not evidence that his first principle already contains the same answers. Recovering your direction does not mean the world must obey it.

There is the difficulty. Can someone committed to freedom allow another person’s answer to differ from the one he wants?

In his national education, Fichte seeks to form people whose moral direction can be relied upon. But if the teacher has already decided what the pupil must become, is education still summoning freedom, or manufacturing a dependable result? Too much certainty about a community can silence its own members as well as exclude outsiders.

Reciprocal recognition is already in his thought. That is precisely why we can bring this question back to him.

The I cannot be erased. Neither can you. My starting point cannot be secured by making you disappear.

Freedom is not the removal of every resistance beneath our feet. It is facing resistance without surrendering ourselves entirely to it, or making others merely our material.

VIII. The Bridgehead

Now let Fichte enter this series’ imagined bridgehead.

He arrives with long strides, moving fast. A man impatient to act. As though his body has already gone forward before the sentence is finished.

He steps onto the bridge. The first thing he looks at is not the others. He looks at his own feet.

The bridge has cracks. Grout Comte poured. Lines Popper drew. Fractures where Dirac heard sounds.

Fichte crouches and reaches toward a crack. Not to listen to what is inside. As if confirming something: the place where he stands has not been exhausted by the bridge’s plan either.

He rises and addresses them all:

“You have spent so long debating what knowledge is, where its boundaries lie. Have you asked where you are? Does the you who poses these questions have a place in your own system?”

Comte looks down at the calendar. So many names. A complete list has not completed an account of the person reading it.

Popper frowns. He still wants to ask whether the account offered of this position can be criticized.

Fichte does not withdraw. He is not asking to be exempt from questions. He is asking that the questioner not disappear from the question.

Qu Yuan looks up from the water’s edge. A person who says I looks at another who says I. They have not said the same thing. The poem’s origins and philosophy’s starting point see one another here.

Kant stands at a distance. Fichte watches him for a long time. This man once spoke his name for him. This man also said no to his road.

He no longer stands on the strength of Kant’s name. Kant does not nod his approval for him.

Fichte finds a place. Not in the middle of a single board. On the crack, one foot on each side, his body facing the darkness below.

He is not afraid. He came from there.

Self-positing and Tathandlung concern the activity of self-consciousness, not a person manufacturing the world through will at a moment in time. Anstoß belongs to the account of finite activity’s limitation; embodiment, Aufforderung and recognition are developed in the Foundations of Natural Right. Kant did address the I think. These arguments are not identical to this essay’s cannot-not.

Distinguish the anonymous publication of 1792 from Kant’s public rejection in 1799. The Addresses combine an educational ideal with a privileged national mission and an ambition to shape pupils’ wills. The nursing and illnesses follow the early Memoir of Johann Gottlieb Fichte. The Berlin university archive gives 29 January 1814 for his death; the memoir gives the night of the 27th. The essay gives the month only, without inventing a mechanism of transmission or a deathbed speech.

The movement’s sequence is the author’s reading itinerary, not a single historical succession or ranking. Eyes, maps, foundations and cracks are interpretive images, not substitutes for argument. The bridge meeting, gestures and dialogue in Section VIII are literary imagination.
