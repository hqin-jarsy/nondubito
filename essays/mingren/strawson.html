斯特劳森：重新铺地板

Strawson: Re-Laying the Floor

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、描述，不改造

1959年。牛津。彼得·弗雷德里克·斯特劳森出版了一本书：《个体》。副标题是"描述性形而上学导论"。

“描述性形而上学”——不是他的全部哲学，却是进入这本书的一把钥匙。

他区分两种工作。一种是修正性的：不满足于我们现有的思想结构，希望换一副更好的框架。笛卡尔和莱布尼茨是他举出的重要例子。修正不只是说“眼睛骗了你”，而是重新安排什么最基本、什么需要由别的东西来解释。

另一种是描述性的：先弄清我们已经在用的那些最基本的概念。你已经在用“物体”“人”“空间”“时间”来思考、说话、生活。他暂不急着换掉它们，而问：它们怎样彼此支撑，使我们能够认出同一件东西，谈论同一个人？这不是给每一种日常偏见盖章。

这听起来很温和。温和到了无聊的地步。前面十五篇的人物各个都在做大事——灌缝，画线，听方程，撕棉絮，坐在空里，把认知扩展到群体。斯特劳森上来说：我不做大事。我只是看看地板长什么样。

但这恰恰是他在本轮弧线上的位置。

经过了十五篇——孔德追索规律，波普尔划出科学的界线，狄拉克听方程，屈原走向水，费希特追问“我”，麦克林托克靠近玉米，薇依练习注意，撒切尔关门，龙树拆除自性的地板，伍尔夫写出意识的时间，汤川从另一张地图走来，阿伦特追问不思考的危险，默多克学习看见，赫勒敦写群体的凝聚与耗损，维果茨基把他人带进发展——经过这一切之后，读者需要重新看看自己站在哪里。

斯特劳森就是那个铺地板的人。

这里的地板是本系列的比喻，不是说斯特劳森读过龙树以后决定替他补洞。我们把不同思想放在同一座桥上，才看出这个位置：怀疑过地板，不等于从此不需要站立；认识过框架的边界，也不等于只能活在框架之外。

二、"人"是原初的

《个体》最重要的论点之一："人"这个概念是原初的（primitive）。

什么意思？

心身问题常被摆成一道拼装题：先给你身体，再给你心灵，最后问它们怎样接在一起。一个会疼、会思考、会走路的人，好像必须等两个零件接通以后才能出现。

斯特劳森问：是不是第一步就拆错了？

“人”是原初概念，意思不是人没有生物学来源，而是：在我们的概念结构中，身体属性和意识状态可以归于同一个主体。“他受伤了”和“他害怕”说的是同一个人，不必先在两个世界里各找半个他，再设法拼合。

这给本系列一个有用的提醒。费希特的“我”、麦克林托克的身体经验、伍尔夫的时间、维果茨基的共同活动，不必被读成四个零件。它们可以照向同一个活人的不同面向。这里是我们的对读，不是四家已经共享了斯特劳森的理论。

所以，这里的“基岩”是概念分析的起点，不是禁止科学继续研究，更不是把人的资格交给旁观者：即使别人认不出、拒绝承认，一个人也不因此失去成为目的的地位。

三、反应态度

1962年。斯特劳森发表了另一篇改变了整个道德哲学的论文：《自由与怨恨》。

道德哲学里有一个老问题：如果世界是决定论的（每一件事都由之前的原因决定），那我们还能对别人的行为做道德判断吗？如果一个人杀了人是因为他的基因加上他的环境加上一连串因果链，那他"应该"被谴责吗？他有"选择"吗？

有人认为责任需要一种不受决定论约束的自由，有人否认这种自由，也有人认为责任与决定论可以相容。争论没有因为一篇论文就结束。

斯特劳森换了一个入口：先看我们实际怎样相待。

你不需要先解决"世界是不是决定论的"才能做道德判断。你已经在做了。你每天都在做。

有人踩了你的脚。你可能生气，但如果发现他是被人推过来的，或者为了扶住你才踩到，怨恨就可能消退。痛一样，意思却不同。斯特劳森原文举的是踩到手的例子；关键不是“痛了，所以我有理”，而是我们在意别人对自己表现出的善意、恶意或漠视。怨恨、感激、宽恕等反应态度，生长在这种人与人的关系里。

这跟认知论的关系是什么？

如果把理论当作进入生活的许可证，仿佛没解决决定论就不能感激或原谅，斯特劳森会让我们看看次序是不是倒了。但日常反应也可以偏狭，需要澄清、修正；描述它们的地位，不是宣布每次愤怒都正当。论文还区分具体行为的借口与改变我们看待行动者方式的豁免，不能用“感觉在先”一笔抹平。

这与麦克林托克、薇依有一个可供对读的地方：已经在实践的东西，未必等理论写完才开始。麦克林托克的经验没有取代遗传学证据，薇依的注意也不是情绪冲动；三人并不共同主张“感觉永远正确”。

人与人的生活，早于我们对它的完整说明。地板在理论写完之前，已经承受过脚步。

四、他和康德

斯特劳森是康德的传人——但是是一种很特殊的传人。

1966年的《感觉的边界》（The Bounds of Sense），是他对康德《纯粹理性批判》的重要重读。他想保留其中关于经验何以可能的洞见：我们怎样把经验理解为关于一个客观世界的经验，而不只是互不相干的感觉。

但他反对康德的先验唯心论，试图把关于经验结构的论证与那套他不接受的形而上学分开。这种拆分有争议；不能把它缩成“康德偷看框架外面，斯特劳森说外面不存在”。康德本人也严格限制我们对物自体能知道什么。

对普通读者来说，可以先抓住这一点：讨论我们怎样认识世界，不必先编出一个可以让我们脱离一切经验、从外面观看世界的位置。

用本系列的语言说，框架不是一堵只负责把真实隔在外面的墙。它也使我们能够辨认、争论、改正错误。斯特劳森描述这种结构，并不等于说世界由我们任意制造，更不是说“能用就是真的”。

五、知道缝隙在那里的地板

这就是斯特劳森在本轮弧线上独特的位置：他铺的是一种知道缝隙在那里的地板。

前面的文章不断问：地板是不是把别的东西压在下面了？斯特劳森这篇把另一个问题放回来：当我们辨认一个物体、向一个人道歉，脚下究竟已经用了什么？

但他说：你还是需要一个地板。

我们需要从悬置与怀疑回到生活。要走路，要说话，要认出旁边那个会疼的人；要生气，也要在误会时收回怨恨。龙树的空不取消日常生活，薇依的注意也不让人永久悬空。把他们带到这里，是为了看见这些问题能够并存。

斯特劳森铺的地板是日常经验的地板。不是理论的地板。不是孔德的"知识的唯一标准"。是你每天用来活着的那些概念——物体，人，空间，时间。这些概念不是终极真理。但它们是你跟世界打交道的必要工具。

可以借一个锤子来想：它依赖材料、手艺和用途，并非自足，却仍能敲钉子。这是我们的比喻，不是斯特劳森对龙树的回答。工具不是绝对的，与工具完全无效，是两回事。

六、他和本轮其他人

斯特劳森在本轮的位置是恢复性的——在一系列的否定，拆解，超越和扩展之后，他把讨论拉回到了日常经验的地面上。

龙树追问自性，斯特劳森分析日常概念。放在桥上，好像一个拆除了“必须绝对牢固”的要求，一个提醒我们仍在共同走路。他们没有因此变成同一学派。

薇依让自我的占有退开，斯特劳森让完整的人留在视野里。去我不是消灭人，原初概念也不是为自我中心辩护。这两种提醒，可以同时带着。

费希特追问“我”的能动性，斯特劳森追问“人”这个概念怎样运作。他不是替费希特结案，而是换了一种提问尺度：先看看我们已经怎样把身体与意识归于同一个人。

默多克说，重新看见可能改变道德生活。斯特劳森提醒，感激与怨恨本来就在其中。不是一个比另一个低级：注意可以修正反应，反应也暴露我们尚未看清的地方。

维果茨基让我们看共同活动中的成长，斯特劳森让我们看归于自己与他人的心灵属性怎样相关。这不等于两套理论相同，但它们都提醒读者：一个封闭在自己里面、随后才勉强猜出别人存在的“我”，未必是最好的起点。

七、温和的勇气

在本系列那些强烈的身影之间，斯特劳森显得温和、安静。他的入口不是一声宣布：从今天起，一切推倒重来。

他只是说：让我们看看我们已经有的东西。

这需要一种特殊的勇气。在逻辑实证主义者质疑形而上学、蒯因挑战分析与综合的区分、维特根斯坦改变哲学提问方式之后，重新提出“我们还可以做形而上学”，并不是简单地退回旧书架。

他要做的，不是再造一个感官世界背后的王国，而是耐心说明我们已经在用的概念结构。这种说明也有论证负担，可以被批评，不是把常识抄一遍就算完成。

这种温和的勇气，在一个充满激进否定的世纪里，本身就是一种认知行为。有时候，最重要的认知不是发现新东西。是重新看清楚你已经有的东西。

八、桥头

现在让他走上本系列那座虚构的桥。斯特劳森步子很稳。

不快。不慢。不戏剧性。一个牛津教授的步子——有教养的，不慌不忙的，每一步都踩在地板上。

他上了桥。看了看桥面。

他注意到了一件事：桥面上到处是痕迹。孔德灌过的水泥。波普尔画过的线。龙树拆过的空洞。维果茨基带孩子走过的脚印。薇依站过的位置（什么也没有，只有一种空的干净）。撒切尔插在栏杆上的TINA纸条。

这不是一块干净的地板。这是一块被十五个人踩过，灌过，画过，拆过，补过的地板。

斯特劳森蹲下来。他摸了摸地板。不是在检查质量。是在确认：地板还在。

它不完美。它有缝隙。有些地方是空的。有些地方被灌了不该灌的水泥。有些地方被拆得露出了下面的黑暗。

但它在。

他站起来。他不修地板。不灌缝。不拆板。不画新线。

他只是站在那里。用自己的重量确认：这是一块还可以共同站立的地板。不完美，需要检查，也需要修补。但你能在上面走，能向旁边的人开口，能指出哪一块松了。

这不是最后的答案。是继续提问的起点。

他看见龙树坐在那里。关于空的追问没有消失。他看看自己的脚：一块需要条件才能成立的地板，仍然承受着脚步。这里的空，不是脚下什么都没有。

这不是无视龙树。这是在龙树之后还能站着。

他找了一个位置。不是特别的位置。不在缝隙上。不在边缘。就在地板上。一个普通的位置。

一个人站在一块不完美的地板上。不是描述性形而上学的全部，却是我们可以带走的一个画面。[1][2][3]

I. Describe, Don't Revise

1959. Oxford. Peter Frederick Strawson publishes Individuals: An Essay in Descriptive Metaphysics.

Descriptive metaphysics: not his entire philosophy, but a key to this book.

He distinguishes two kinds of work. Revisionary metaphysics wants to replace our existing structure of thought with a better one. Descartes and Leibniz are important examples for him. Revision is not simply a claim that our eyes deceive us; it rearranges what counts as basic and what must be explained through something else.

Descriptive metaphysics begins with the concepts already at work: objects, persons, space, time. You think, speak and live through them. Before replacing them, he asks how they support one another, allowing us to identify the same thing or speak about the same person. This does not endorse every prejudice embedded in everyday life.

This sounds mild. Mild to the point of dull. The preceding fifteen figures all did dramatic things — grouting cracks, drawing lines, hearing equations, tearing cotton wool, sitting in emptiness, extending cognition to groups. Strawson arrives and says: I won't do anything dramatic. I'll just look at what the floor looks like.

But this is precisely his position in this round's arc.

Fifteen essays have brought us here: Comte seeking laws, Popper drawing science’s boundary, Dirac listening to equations, Qu Yuan moving toward water, Fichte questioning the I, McClintock attending to maize, Weil practicing attention, Thatcher closing a door, Nāgārjuna dismantling a self-grounded floor, Woolf writing the time of consciousness, Yukawa approaching by another map, Arendt questioning thoughtlessness, Murdoch learning to see, Khaldun tracing solidarity and its erosion, Vygotsky bringing others into development. After all this, the reader needs to look again at what supports their feet.

Strawson is that person.

The floor belongs to this series’ imagery. It is not a claim that Strawson read Nāgārjuna and decided to repair his damage. Placing different thinkers on one imagined bridge reveals a position: questioning the floor does not remove our need to stand; recognizing a framework’s limits does not require living outside every framework.

II. "Person" Is Primitive

One of Individuals' most important arguments: the concept of "person" is primitive.

What does this mean?

The mind–body problem is often posed as an assembly problem. First take a body, then a mind; now explain their connection. A person who hurts, thinks and walks seems to appear only after two separately defined components have been joined.

Strawson asks whether the initial division has already led us astray.

Calling person a primitive concept does not deny biological origins. It means that, within our conceptual scheme, bodily properties and states of consciousness belong to one subject. “He is injured” and “he is afraid” concern the same person. We need not find half of him in each of two worlds and then assemble the halves.

For this series, that is a useful reminder. Fichte’s I, McClintock’s bodily experience, Woolf’s time and Vygotsky’s shared activity need not be four detachable parts. They can illuminate different aspects of one living person. That is our comparative reading, not a claim that all four already shared Strawson’s theory.

Bedrock here names a starting point for conceptual analysis, not a ban on further scientific inquiry. Nor does it put a person’s standing at the mercy of observers. Failure or refusal to recognize someone does not strip that person of their status as an end.

III. Reactive Attitudes

1962. Strawson publishes another enormously influential work: Freedom and Resentment.

An old problem in moral philosophy: if the world is deterministic (every event caused by prior events), can we still make moral judgments about people's actions? If someone killed because of their genes plus environment plus a causal chain, "should" they be condemned? Did they have a "choice"?

Some think responsibility requires freedom incompatible with determinism; some deny that freedom; others regard responsibility and determinism as compatible. One paper did not end the dispute.

Strawson chooses another entrance: how do we actually respond to one another?

You don't need to first solve "is the world deterministic?" before making moral judgments. You're already making them. Every day.

Someone steps on your foot. You may resent it—until you realize they were pushed, or stepped there while trying to help you. The pain may be the same; its meaning changes. Strawson’s own example concerns a hand. The point is not “I hurt, therefore I am right,” but our concern with another person’s goodwill, ill will or disregard. Resentment, gratitude and forgiveness belong to this interpersonal life.

What does this have to do with epistemology?

If theory becomes a permit to enter life, as though we could not thank or forgive anyone before settling determinism, Strawson asks whether we have reversed the order. Yet ordinary reactions can be narrow or mistaken. Describing their place does not justify every angry response. His distinction between excuses for particular actions and exemptions affecting our stance toward an agent also resists a simple slogan about feeling coming first.

There is a point of comparison with McClintock and Weil: a practice need not wait for its complete theory before it begins. McClintock’s experience did not replace genetic evidence; Weil’s attention was not an emotional impulse. None of this amounts to a shared doctrine that feeling is always right.

Life with other people precedes our complete account of it. The floor has already carried footsteps before its theory is finished.

IV. Strawson and Kant

Strawson is Kant's heir — but a particular kind.

The Bounds of Sense, published in 1966, is his major rereading of Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason. He wants to preserve insights into what makes experience possible: how experience can be of an objective world rather than an unrelated succession of sensations.

He rejects transcendental idealism and tries to separate arguments about experience from the metaphysics he finds unacceptable. That separation remains disputed. It is not simply “Kant looked behind the framework; Strawson declared there was nothing there.” Kant himself sharply limits what we can know of things in themselves.

For a general reader, a useful starting point is this: asking how we know the world need not require inventing a position outside all experience from which to inspect it.

In this series’ language, a framework is not merely a wall keeping reality out. It also allows identification, disagreement and correction. Describing that structure does not mean that we manufacture the world at will, or that whatever works must be true.

V. A Floor That Knows About Its Cracks

This is Strawson's unique position in this round's arc: the floor he lays knows about its cracks.

Earlier essays kept asking what the floor might have pressed underneath it. This one restores another question: what are we already relying on when we identify an object or apologize to a person?

But he says: you still need a floor.

We have to return from doubt and suspension to living: walking, speaking, recognizing the person beside us who can hurt; becoming angry, and withdrawing resentment when we were mistaken. Nāgārjuna’s emptiness does not abolish everyday life, nor does Weil’s attention leave people suspended forever. Bringing them here lets these questions coexist.

The floor Strawson lays is the floor of everyday experience. Not theoretical floor. Not Comte's "sole standard of knowledge." The concepts you use every day to live — objects, persons, space, time. These concepts are not ultimate truths. But they are necessary tools for engaging with the world.

Think of a hammer. It depends on materials, craft and use; it is not self-sufficient, yet it can drive a nail. This is our image, not Strawson’s reply to Nāgārjuna. A tool’s not being absolute is different from its being useless.

VI. Strawson and the Others in This Round

Strawson's position in this round is restorative — after a series of negations, dismantlings, transcendences, and expansions, he pulls the discussion back to the ground of everyday experience.

Nāgārjuna questions intrinsic nature; Strawson analyzes everyday concepts. On the bridge, one seems to remove the demand for absolute foundations while the other reminds us that we still walk together. They do not thereby become members of one school.

Weil asks possessive selfhood to step back; Strawson keeps the whole person in view. Unselfing does not destroy the person, and a primitive concept does not defend egocentrism. We can carry both reminders.

Fichte asks about the I’s activity; Strawson asks how the concept of a person works. He does not close Fichte’s inquiry. He changes its scale, examining how bodily and conscious states already belong to one person in our discourse.

Murdoch shows how seeing anew can change moral life. Strawson reminds us that gratitude and resentment are already part of it. Neither is simply more primitive: attention can correct a reaction, while a reaction can reveal what we have not yet seen clearly.

Vygotsky examines development in shared activity; Strawson examines the connection between attributing mental states to oneself and to others. These are not identical theories, but both unsettle the image of an I sealed inside itself, only later guessing that other people exist.

VII. The Courage of the Mild

Among this series’ forceful figures, Strawson appears mild and quiet. His point of entry is not a proclamation that everything must be demolished and begun again.

He just says: let's look at what we already have.

This takes a particular courage. After logical positivists challenged metaphysics, Quine questioned the analytic–synthetic distinction, and Wittgenstein transformed philosophical questioning, proposing metaphysics again was not a simple retreat to an old bookshelf.

He is not building another kingdom behind the sensible world. He patiently describes the conceptual structure already in use. That description has to earn its claims and remains open to criticism; copying common sense is not enough.

This mild courage, in a century full of radical negation, is itself a cognitive act. Sometimes the most important cognition isn't discovering something new. It's clearly re-seeing what you already have.

VIII. The Bridgehead

Now let him enter this series’ invented bridge scene. Strawson approaches steadily.

Not fast. Not slow. Not dramatic. The gait of an Oxford professor — measured, unhurried, each step landing squarely on the floor.

He steps onto the bridge. Looks at the surface.

He notices something: the bridge surface is covered in traces. Comte's grout. Popper's lines. Nāgārjuna's empty patches. Vygotsky's child-sized footprints. The spot where Weil stood (nothing there, just a clean emptiness). Thatcher's TINA note on the railing.

This is not a clean floor. It's a floor that fifteen people have walked on, grouted, drawn on, dismantled, and patched.

Strawson crouches. He touches the floor. Not checking quality. Confirming: the floor is still here.

It's imperfect. It has cracks. Some parts are empty. Some parts are grouted with material that shouldn't be there. Some parts are torn open, revealing darkness below.

But it's here.

He stands. He doesn't fix the floor. Doesn't grout. Doesn't tear up boards. Doesn't draw new lines.

He simply stands there, letting his weight confirm that this is still a floor people can share. Imperfect, needing inspection and repair. Yet you can walk on it, address your neighbor, point out the loose board.

This is not the final answer. It is a place from which questioning can continue.

He sees Nāgārjuna sitting there. The question of emptiness has not disappeared. He looks at his feet: a floor that depends on conditions still carries footsteps. Empty here does not mean that there is nothing beneath them.

This isn't ignoring Nāgārjuna. This is being able to stand after Nāgārjuna.

He finds a spot. Not a special one. Not on a crack. Not at the edge. Just on the floor. An ordinary spot.

A person standing on an imperfect floor. Not the whole of descriptive metaphysics, but an image we can take with us.[1][2][3]
