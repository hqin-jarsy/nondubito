阿伦特：谁在替你判断

Arendt: Who Is Judging in Your Place?

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、她去看了

1961年。耶路撒冷。阿道夫·艾希曼坐在防弹玻璃后面受审。

汉娜·阿伦特为《纽约客》前来报道。她不只想读一份罪行清单。她还要听这个具体的人怎样谈论自己做过的事。

面对这样的罪行，人很容易期待看见一个一眼就能认出的怪物。

玻璃后面却有一张可以出现在普通办公室里的脸。

在阿伦特的描述中，他喜欢套话，反复谈职责、命令、服从与仕途。但这不等于他没有反犹信念，更不等于他不邪恶。后来的研究，尤其对他在阿根廷的言谈与材料的研究，凸显了他的意识形态投入和自我包装。被告说自己只是执行者，不能就照单全收。

他组织驱逐，将人送往死亡。纳粹及其帮凶杀害了六百万犹太人，艾希曼是这场种族灭绝的重要组织者之一。一个看似普通的外表，没有减轻他的责任；恰恰让我们不能靠长相辨认罪恶。

1963年，她的报道发表，并结集为《艾希曼在耶路撒冷：关于恶的平庸性的报道》。

“恶的平庸性”很快成为一个著名短语。太著名，以至于人们有时只记住了它，没有再问它指什么。

它不是说罪行不严重，也不是说做出罪行的人无辜。阿伦特注意的是，骇人的行动可以和空洞的语言、对判断的逃避同时存在。但她对艾希曼的具体刻画仍须接受史料检验；一个有力量的概念，不会让它的作者从此免于被纠正。

二、不思考

这里的“不思考”，不是不会算数、不会安排工作，也不是偶尔发呆。艾希曼能够计划、周旋、争取权力。问题在于，这些能力会不会被用来替他挡住另一类问题。

我正在做什么？我把别人当成了什么？命令为什么可以替我决定这些？

时刻表可以精确，运输可以高效，报表可以毫无差错。可是，当活着的人被写成一个个待处理的数量，准确本身并不保证正当。把事情办得好，与这件事应不应该办，是两道不同的问题。

读阿伦特时值得警惕的，是残酷不一定总以张牙舞爪的样子出现。它可以有仇恨与信念，也可以借助职责和惯例让人拒绝追问。不能据此倒过来说，所有恶都没有意图，或者只要走神就已在作恶。

这时会想起薇依。她让我们把注意力交给受苦的人，阿伦特让我们追问自己参与了什么。一个问“你听见他了吗”，一个问“这件事你能向自己交代吗”。

她们不是在讲同一套理论。但放在一起，两个问题会互相照亮：别让一套熟练的说法，把眼前的人遮住，也把做事的自己藏起来。

三、两种关门

前面读撒切尔，我们谈过“没有替代方案”怎样可能把讨论关掉。这里可以借用关门的比喻，却不能把一位民主政治家的政策争论与种族灭绝并列成同一种罪。

我们比较的只是两种值得留意的说话方式，不是在宣判两个人的内心构造。

一种说：我已经想过了，不必再谈。

这种话可能出自认真作过的决定，也可能成为拒绝新证据的盾牌。作出判断是必要的；把某一次判断变成永远不准追问，又是另一回事。

另一种说：不是我决定的，我只负责执行。

这句话把理由送到别处，仿佛签字的人、安排的人、动手的人可以从事情里退场。可是在有选择和行动的地方，责任并不会跟着语法消失。

不能因此说艾希曼里面“没有人”。如果他只是一个空壳，反而容易替他抹去应当承担的责任。要追问的是一个具体的人怎样支持、推进、辩护自己的行动，而不是宣布他从来没有一个“我”。

费希特的自我设定也不能被拿来作罪犯的心理诊断。借本系列的线索问一句已经够了：当你说“这与我无关”时，真的能够把你做过的事一并拿走吗？

四、她也必须接受追问

书出版以后，阿伦特自己也站到了争论的中心。

最尖锐的争议之一，是她对犹太委员会的处理。纳粹强迫设立或利用这些机构，有些领导人在威胁之下执行了登记、征发、驱逐等要求，也有人拖延、拒绝、援助抵抗，并为拒绝付出生命。地区、时间、处境不能抹成一张表。

所以，不能用一句“她只是说出了大家不愿听的事实”，就替这场争论结案。有人质疑她的语气，也有人质疑她的概括、证据和对受迫者选择空间的判断。迫害者的责任不能转到受害者身上；讨论具体行动，也不能省略恐怖统治下的胁迫。

批评中有不公正的攻击，有友谊的破裂。但并不是整个犹太社群只有一种声音，阿伦特也并非完全没有支持者。

可以在这里想起屈原：一个人说出不受欢迎的话，怎样继续站住。但这个呼应不替阿伦特的每句话担保。勇于独立，不等于永远正确。

也可以想起麦克林托克，不过不再把她写成整整三十年无人理会的孤独天才。科学争论、公共争论都需要看材料和理由，不能因为有人遭到反对，就断定他说得“太真了”。

阿伦特要求别人不要把判断交给权威。读她，也不该把判断交给“阿伦特”这个名字。她可以成为我们认真争论的人，不必成为不能被追问的人。

五、心灵的生命

审判之后，思考与判断成为她持续追问的问题。一个人怎样才能不只是聪明地完成任务，还能停下来问任务本身？

她晚年的《心灵的生命》计划讨论思考、意志、判断。1975年，她去世，终年六十九岁，“判断”部分未能写成。据编辑玛丽·麦卡锡记述，打字机里留下的纸页有“判断”的标题和两则题辞，不是只有标题的一张白纸。

那张纸让人难忘。但不能据此想象她死在打字机前，也不能说她从来没开始思考判断。她已经有康德讲稿和其他讨论；缺的是那部计划中的完成稿，不是此前所有工作。

思考、意志、判断，在她那里是有区别的活动，不能被压成一条自动流水线：思考启动，善就产出了。

沿着苏格拉底的线索，她写一个人与自己的无声对话。你做了某件事，回到独处时，还能不能与做过它的自己相处？这里的“一中之二”是思考经验，不是脑子里真的住着两个人。

这场对话可能打断一句太顺口的“大家都这样”。它未必立刻给出规则，却让一条规则不再只因被重复就显得无须解释。判断还要回到具体事情，听见不同位置的人，而不只是在心里把自己说服。

思考不是道德保险单，更不是做人的资格考试。休息、疲惫、疾病或认知能力受损，不会使人失去价值。我们要守住的，不是每秒都在反省，而是不把“我没想过”当作永远有效的免责条款。

六、她和前面的人

费希特帮助我们看见主体的活动，阿伦特逼我们看见行动中的责任。两条线可以对读，不必说后者从反面“证明”了前者，更不能靠宣布作恶者没有自我来解释恶。

薇依让自己的急切后退，听受苦者说话；阿伦特让人停下来，与自己和他人的位置相遇。后退不等于消失，思考也不等于占满舞台。两人都能提醒读者，别用自己的解释替别人过完一生。

撒切尔那扇门提醒我们，决定以后仍要容许追问。这里的命令则提醒我们，执行不能自动代替负责。不同处境不可等量齐观，一个比喻能提出问题，不能代替历史判断。

刚读过的汤川让古书与物理学相遇。他的直觉不是“不思考”，更不是庄子直接制造了介子理论。汤川让尚未出现的答案有机会被提出，阿伦特让已经习惯的答案重新成为问题。开放的方向不同，却都不能少了对现实的回应。

七、诞生性

阿伦特并不只写恶。她还写开始，写“诞生性”。

一个新人来到世界，并不只是多出一个旧模式的复制品。有人将以此前没有过的方式说话、行动，与他人相遇。这个可能性，扎根于每个人都是新来者这一事实。

这条思想在1958年的《人的境况》中已很重要，不是看完艾希曼审判才发明的善恶对称表。新的行动也不自动是善的；意外可以带来希望，也可以带来破坏。

但过去并没有替我们写完未来的每一句话。人能开始一些事，同时也活在权力、危险和条件之中。不能把“你总可以重新开始”变成对无路可退者的责备。

艾希曼的责任要从他的选择与行动来谈，不能说他丢掉了某种诞生性资格。从别人身上拿走人的资格，并不是阿伦特思想最值得我们继承的路。

新的开始有时很小：一句拒绝，一次公开说话，几个人决定不再照原样继续。它不只发生在一个人的头脑里，还需要有人回应，有可以相遇和行动的空间。

思考可能帮助我们不再自动重复，诞生性让政治不只剩下重复。两者不是开关和灯泡的关系。世界能不能容下新来者，也要问我们共同建立了怎样的生活。

八、桥头

在这座想象中的桥上，阿伦特走来，手里夹着一根烟。

烟是读者熟悉的形象，不是思想的发动机。我们让她停下来，看一会儿桥上的人。

她不检查谁才配拥有一个“我”。每个人已经在这里。她也许想问的是：你刚才说的那句话，你愿意再想一遍吗？

孔德在翻日历。分类帮助人安排世界。她看一眼那些格子，也看一眼格子旁边的人。日历上的名字，没有替他们说完所有的话。

撒切尔站得笔直。两人不必在这里达成一致。也不必通过艾希曼来给她打分。桥上可以留着争论的位置：一个决定怎样才算有力，又怎样才不把后来的人堵在门外？

薇依在听。阿伦特看她，也被她提醒：问自己是否能够心安，不等于已经听清别人所受的苦。内心的对话要有窗，而不只是一面镜子。

阿伦特找个位置坐下，拿出笔记本。

她在写什么？

也许是我们放到她笔下的一句问话：当命令、惯例和漂亮的解释都已准备好，我还怎样判断眼前这件事？

我们想起打字机里那张有标题、有题辞，却没有完成正文的纸。不是一整片空白，也不是我们可以擅自替她填好的最后答案。

她坐在桥上，继续写。

也许写完一页，也许划掉一行。旁边有人说话，她抬起头。问题不只在纸上。

不是一刻不能停，是不能把判断永远交出去。[1][2][3]

I. She Went to Look

Jerusalem, 1961. Adolf Eichmann sat on trial behind bulletproof glass.

Hannah Arendt had come to report for The New Yorker. A list of crimes was not all she wanted to read. She wanted to hear this particular man account for what he had done.

Faced with crimes on that scale, we may expect a monster we can recognize at a glance.

Behind the glass was a face that would not have looked out of place in an ordinary office.

In Arendt’s account, Eichmann took refuge in stock phrases about duty, orders, obedience and his career. That does not establish that he lacked antisemitic convictions, much less that he was not evil. Later research, especially into his words and activities in Argentina, has brought his ideological commitment and calculated self-presentation into sharper view. A defendant’s claim to have been merely an executor is not evidence enough to believe him.

He organized deportations that sent people to their deaths. The Nazis and their accomplices murdered six million Jews; Eichmann was a major organizer of that genocide. His unremarkable appearance diminished none of his responsibility. It should, instead, unsettle our confidence that evil will announce itself in a face.

Arendt’s reports appeared in 1963 and became Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil.

“The banality of evil” soon became famous. Famous enough, sometimes, to be remembered without anyone asking what it meant.

It does not mean that the crimes were trivial or the perpetrators innocent. Arendt drew attention to the coexistence of appalling actions, hollow language and an evasion of judgment. But her portrait of Eichmann remains answerable to historical evidence. A powerful concept does not place its author beyond correction.

II. What It Means Not to Think

Thoughtlessness here is not an inability to calculate or organize, nor a moment spent staring out of a window. Eichmann could plan, maneuver and pursue power. The question is whether those abilities can also be used to keep a different kind of question at bay.

What am I doing? What am I making of these other people? Why should an order settle either question for me?

A timetable can be precise, transport efficient, a report free of errors. Yet once living people have become quantities to be processed, accuracy offers no guarantee of rightness. Doing a job well and having any business doing it are different questions.

One warning worth taking from Arendt is that cruelty need not always bare its teeth. Hatred and conviction can work alongside routines and duties that shut down inquiry. This does not establish the reverse proposition: that evil is always unintentional, or that a wandering mind is already committing a crime.

Here we might remember Simone Weil. She asks us to attend to the person who suffers; Arendt makes us ask what we are taking part in. One question is, Have you heard this person? The other: Can you account to yourself for what you are doing?

These are not interchangeable theories. Put beside each other, however, the questions throw light in both directions. A practiced explanation can hide the person in front of us—and the person doing the explaining.

III. Two Ways of Closing a Door

Earlier, in the Thatcher essay, we considered how “there is no alternative” can close a discussion. We can borrow the image of a door without treating a democratic politician’s policy disputes and genocide as equivalent wrongs.

The comparison concerns two habits of speech, not a verdict on the inner constitution of two people.

One says: I have already thought about it. There is nothing more to discuss.

That may follow a carefully considered decision. It may also become a shield against new evidence. We have to make judgments; making one judgment immune to further questions is something else.

The other says: I did not decide. I only carried it out.

The sentence sends the reasons elsewhere, as if the signer, the organizer and the person who acts could withdraw from the event. Where there is choice and action, responsibility does not disappear because the grammar has changed.

This is no reason to say there was “nobody” inside Eichmann. Calling him an empty shell risks erasing the very responsibility he must bear. The question concerns how an actual person supported, advanced and defended his actions, not whether he ever possessed an “I.”

Nor can Fichte’s self-positing supply a psychological diagnosis of a criminal. The thread of this series gives us a plainer question: when you say this has nothing to do with you, can you take everything you did out of the story as well?

IV. She, Too, Must Answer Questions

After the book appeared, Arendt herself stood at the center of a bitter dispute.

One of its sharpest points concerned her treatment of the Jewish councils. The Nazis imposed or exploited these bodies. Under threat, some leaders carried out demands for registration, requisition and deportation; others delayed, refused or helped resistance, sometimes dying for their refusal. Different places, moments and constraints cannot be flattened into a single table.

“She simply told a truth nobody wanted to hear” cannot settle this dispute. Some critics challenged her tone; others challenged her generalizations, evidence and assessment of the choices available under coercion. The persecutors’ responsibility cannot be transferred to their victims. Examining particular actions still requires us to face the terror in which they occurred.

There were unjust attacks. Friendships broke. But Jewish communities did not speak with one voice, and Arendt was not without supporters.

We might think of Qu Yuan, the Chinese poet encountered earlier in this series: how does someone remain standing after saying something unwelcome? The echo cannot vouch for every sentence Arendt wrote. Intellectual independence is not infallibility.

Barbara McClintock also comes to mind—but not as a genius whom nobody noticed for thirty uninterrupted years. Scientific and public disagreements require attention to evidence and reasons. Being opposed does not prove that someone has been “too truthful.”

Arendt asked people not to surrender judgment to authority. Reading her, we should not surrender it to the name Hannah Arendt. She can be someone with whom we argue seriously, not someone whom we are forbidden to question.

V. The Life of the Mind

After the trial, thinking and judging remained pressing questions for her. How can a person do more than complete a task intelligently—and stop to question the task itself?

Her late work The Life of the Mind was to address Thinking, Willing and Judging. She died in 1975, aged sixty-nine, before the planned volume on judging was written. Her editor Mary McCarthy reported a sheet in the typewriter bearing the heading “Judging” and two epigraphs: not an otherwise blank page with a title alone.

It is a haunting image. It does not mean she died at the typewriter, or that she had never begun to consider judgment. There were already her Kant lectures and other discussions. What is missing is the completed projected volume, not every prior piece of work.

Thinking, willing and judging are distinct activities in her account. They cannot be compressed into an automatic production line: switch on thinking, and goodness rolls out.

Following Socrates, she explores a person’s silent conversation with oneself. After doing something, can you live in solitude with the person who did it? The “two-in-one” names an experience of thinking, not two literal occupants inside the skull.

Such a conversation may interrupt an easy “everybody does it.” It need not instantly produce a rule. It can make a repeated rule stop looking self-explanatory. Judgment must also return to particular situations and consider other standpoints, rather than merely winning an argument inside one’s own head.

Thinking is neither moral insurance nor an entrance examination for humanity. Rest, exhaustion, illness and cognitive impairment do not deprive a person of worth. What matters is not uninterrupted self-scrutiny but refusing to make “I didn’t think about it” a permanent exemption from responsibility.

VI. In the Company of Earlier Lives

Fichte helps us see the activity of a subject; Arendt presses us to see responsibility in action. The two can be read together without treating the latter as a negative “proof” of the former. Evil cannot be explained away by declaring that its agent has no self.

Weil asks her own urgency to step back so that the suffering person can speak. Arendt asks us to stop and encounter ourselves and other perspectives. Stepping back is not vanishing; thinking is not occupying the whole stage. Both can remind us not to let our explanations live another person’s life for them.

Thatcher’s door reminds us to permit questions even after a decision. The language of orders reminds us that carrying something out does not automatically discharge responsibility. These situations are not morally interchangeable. A metaphor can pose a question; it cannot replace historical judgment.

Yukawa, whom we have just read, brought old books into contact with physics. His intuition was not thoughtlessness, and Zhuangzi did not directly produce meson theory. Yukawa made room for an answer not yet available; Arendt made a familiar answer become a question again. Their openness moves in different directions, but neither can dispense with an answer from the world.

VII. Natality

Arendt wrote not only about evil. She also wrote about beginning: natality.

A newcomer to the world is more than another copy of an existing pattern. Someone will speak, act and encounter others in a way that has not happened before. That possibility is rooted in the fact that each of us arrives as someone new.

The idea was already central to The Human Condition in 1958. It was not invented after the Eichmann trial as the good half of a symmetrical chart. Nor is every new action good: the unexpected can bring destruction as well as hope.

Yet the past has not written every sentence of the future. People can begin things while also living within power, danger and material constraints. “You can always start again” must not become an accusation against someone with no safe way out.

Eichmann’s responsibility rests on his choices and actions, not on having lost some qualification for natality. Taking someone’s standing as a human being away is not the most valuable path to carry forward from Arendt.

A beginning may be small: a refusal, an act of public speech, several people deciding not to continue as before. It happens not only in a solitary mind. It needs a response from others and somewhere to meet and act.

Thinking may interrupt automatic repetition; natality keeps politics from becoming nothing but repetition. They are not related like a switch and a light bulb. Whether a world has room for newcomers also depends on the life we build together.

VIII. At the Bridge

On this imagined bridge, Arendt approaches with a cigarette in her hand.

It is a familiar image, not the engine of her thought. Let her stop for a moment and look at the people gathered here.

She does not inspect them to discover who deserves an “I.” They are already here. Perhaps she wants to ask: that sentence you just spoke—would you be willing to think about it once more?

Comte is turning the pages of a calendar. Classifications help people organize a world. She looks at the compartments and then at the people beside them. The names on the calendar have not said everything there is to say about them.

Thatcher stands very straight. The two do not need to agree here, nor does Eichmann need to become a scale on which to weigh her. The bridge can leave room for an argument: how can a decision be firm without barring the way to those who arrive later?

Weil is listening. Looking at her, Arendt is reminded of something too: asking whether I can live with myself is not the same as having heard another person’s suffering. The inner conversation needs a window, not just a mirror.

Arendt finds somewhere to sit and takes out a notebook.

What is she writing?

Perhaps a question we have placed beneath her pen: when orders, routines and elegant explanations are all ready to hand, how do I judge this thing in front of me?

We remember the page in the typewriter, with its heading and epigraphs but no completed text. It was not an absolute blank. Nor is it a final answer that we are entitled to finish for her.

She sits on the bridge and keeps writing.

Perhaps she finishes a page; perhaps she crosses out a line. Someone nearby speaks, and she looks up. The question is not only on the paper.

Not that we must never stop. That we must not give our judgment away for good.[1][2][3]
