华盛顿，不穿龙袍的人

Washington, the Man Who Would Not Wear the Crown

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、他可以

1783年。独立战争已走向胜利，和平把另一个问题摆到了眼前。

乔治·华盛顿是大陆军总司令。他有军中的威望，也有远远超出军营的声望。战争的胜利不是他一个人的功劳，但这个名字，已经有了制度之外的重量。

军队会不会成为他自己的权力？

这不是凭空想出来的诱惑。1782年，军官刘易斯·尼古拉在信中讨论君主制，暗示华盛顿可以担当新的统治者。华盛顿严厉拒绝了。那不是全国捧来一顶王冠，而是一条确实有人提出、他不肯走的路。

1783年春，纽堡的军官因欠饷和养老金问题激愤。华盛顿又出面劝阻以军队向文官政府施压的危险。他承认士兵受了亏待，却不愿让这份亏待变成军人接管国家的许可证。

从军事胜利走向个人统治，历史上有足够多的身影：凯撒在内战后成为终身独裁官，拿破仑从将军走向皇帝；秦始皇、项羽、刘邦，也各自把战胜与新的权位连在一起。路径并不相同，更不是所有胜利者的必然结局。可是，那条路一直存在。

华盛顿选择让自己的路在这里转弯。

1783年12月23日。他走进马里兰州安纳波利斯国会开会的厅堂，交还军事委任状。他把受托的职权交回授予它的机构。然后告别，回弗农山庄去了。

有一个经画家本杰明·韦斯特转述的故事：乔治三世听说华盛顿打算归田，表示如果真如此，他将是世上最伟大的人之一。话语的流传有版本差异，我们不必把它当作当天的宫廷速记。

一个国王的赞叹，围绕着一个人不把胜利据为私产的动作。这个故事之所以留下来，是因为那动作确实值得看。

二、他又可以

六年后。1789年。

《邦联条例》下的中央权力薄弱，财政、债务与各州之间的协调困难重重。1787年，费城制宪会议提出新宪法；经过各州批准，新政府开始运转。

这个新职位需要第一任总统。许多目光又落到华盛顿身上。

他在书信中反复表达退居乡间的愿望。弗农山庄有农业实验、小麦、牲畜，也有他牵挂的经营事务。那不是一个没有劳动与权力关系的桃花源，却是他想回去的地方。

1789年，他得到所有参与投票的选举人的支持。1792年又是如此。他是以这种方式两次全票当选的总统。

他接受了职务。他的声望有助于新制度获得信任；责任感、他人的劝说和个人判断，都在这个决定里。没有必要替他宣布内心只有一个动机。

四年。再四年。两届。

到第二届后期，他仍有很高的威望，也已承受激烈的党争和攻击。继续参选没有法律障碍，却不是“所有人都请他留下”，更不是无需选举便能终身在位。

第三次参选的路仍然开着。

他没有走。

1796年9月，他刊出告别文告，宣布不再参选；1797年完成交接，回到弗农山庄。不是被一道两届限令赶走。那道宪法限制，要等到1951年。

三、他知道

为什么不把军队变成自己的国家？为什么不一直坐在总统的位置上？

一种回答是共和信念：职权来自授权，不来自占有。古罗马辛辛纳图斯放下独裁官职、回到田间的故事，也早已成为人们理解华盛顿的镜子。

政治思想和榜样很重要。但读过一个故事，并不等于能够照着做。

知道与做到之间，有时隔着一整支军队，有时隔着一张还可以继续坐下去的椅子。

华盛顿两次跨过了这段距离。第一次交还军职，第二次让总统职位离开自己，落到下一任手里。

我愿意这样读他的动作：如果一个职位只能由我占据，它就还没有真正成为公共的职位。

独立战争的诉求有多重来源。《独立宣言》把政府的正当权力系于被统治者的同意，现实中的“人民”却远没有包括所有人。即便在这个狭窄的起点上，把胜利再铸成个人的世袭权利，也会伤到共和国的理由。

离开于是有了建造的意义。那个人走出了门，职位却留了下来；后来的人，可以进来，也应当能够离开。

在本系列“凿”的语言里，放下不是建造以后的空白。放下，是最后一刀。

四、他和秦始皇

上一篇写了秦始皇：把辽阔的土地纳入同一套秩序，也让这套秩序承受了巨大的压力。秦帝国在统一后约十五年覆亡，许多制度却并没有随之消失。

现在，我们把两个人放在一起。

一个加强君主的最高权位，让帝国围绕不可替换的中心运转。另一个在一场集体的共和实验中，把自己的军职与总统任期交还出去。这不是两个人各自创造全部历史，却是两个可以相照的动作。

本文给它们一个形象：穿龙袍，与不穿龙袍。不是要替秦代裁定后世服饰。

称呼也会暴露期待。1789年，美国国会为总统是否应有尊贵头衔争论，参议院一度支持“殿下”式称呼。众议院不赞成，最后采用简朴的总统称谓。这不是华盛顿独自挑中一个词；新共和国在争论自己该以什么姿态说话。

秦始皇的中心像一座向顶端收拢的塔。美国宪法试图把权力分配到不同职位，让掌权者也受规则约束。规则能否高过人，是一项工作，不是一张已经兑现的保证。

秦代焚书等压制措施，与共和制度中为批评保留的位置，可以形成对照。但第一修正案来自国会、各州和长期的政治争取，不是华盛顿私人的恩赐。

一边追求更整齐的命令，一边必须容忍不能全由自己控制的声音。这样读，两个姿势才显出距离。

秦的短命与美国共和国跨越两个多世纪的延续，不能只用一个人的性格解释。战争、社会、制度与后来的选择，都在其中；更长久，也不自动等于更正义。

庄子写七窍凿成、混沌死去。这里借来的，是给生命留出呼吸处的提醒，不是一个寓言已经得到政治实验的证明。

五、他的构

但华盛顿不是不构。他没有只站在追问的空地上，也没有说完一句话便从政治中消失。

他确实参与建造了许多东西。

他主持1787年的制宪会议。他不是宪法唯一的作者，但他的在场为会议带来分量。任总统后，他与国会、官员一道，把纸上的制度变成日常行政。

财政部长汉密尔顿，国务卿杰斐逊：两个人对国家的未来争得激烈。华盛顿在他们之间作判断、作取舍。内阁怎样商议，总统怎样行使宪法已经授予的否决权，外交事务怎样处理，许多先例在具体事务里长出来。

他参与构成了一个能运转的政府。

这个构，不需要所有分歧都先消失，才有资格继续。

1791年批准的第一修正案，为言论、出版等自由提供保障。批评政府不应仅因使执政者不快而被取消。权利的边界与实施，后来仍经过无数冲突；这不是“任何话在任何情况下都不受限制”的许可证。

分权与制衡让决定不能总由一只手完成。它们可能拖慢事情，也可能阻止伤害。效率不是罪，阻滞也不天然高贵；要问的是，谁有权说不，谁能纠正错误。

两届后退出，则给继任者留下一个醒目的先例。在1940年罗斯福第三次当选以前，没有人第三次赢得总统选举。这个习惯不是一开始就有的法律，也不是从未有人想挑战它。

1951年，第二十二修正案才把当选次数的限制写进宪法。个人的退出与后来的法律，不是同一个时间点，却可以接成一条线。

这是一座有争吵、有裂缝的构。它需要修正，需要有人不断把纸上的承诺带进现实。

能呼吸，不是不会受伤。是伤口不能只由掌权的人决定是否存在。

六、他的缺口

华盛顿不是完美的人。我们不需要借一个数学定理，才能认真看待一个人的成就与伤害。

最不能绕开的，是他作为奴隶主的生活。

1799年，弗农山庄有317名被奴役者。其中123人在法律上归华盛顿所有，153人属于卡斯蒂斯遗产，由玛莎享有终身权益，其余41人从别的奴隶主那里租来。法律的账本分成几栏，劳动与不自由却在同一个庄园里发生。

他的遗嘱规定威廉·李在他死后立即获得自由；其他由他拥有的人，原定在玛莎死后释放。玛莎后来提前执行，于1801年1月让他们获得自由。卡斯蒂斯遗产中的人，不在这道释放条款之内。

这样的安排确实改变了一些人的命运，却不能把他们活过的奴役倒写成自由。华盛顿在私人信件里表达过废奴愿望，任内却没有领导公共废奴运动，还追索过逃离华盛顿家的人。

一个把公共军权交还的人，没有把同样的退出权交给庄园里的所有人。

这里不是宪法忘记涂到的一块空白。原宪法关于代表名额、奴隶贸易和逃亡者的妥协，把奴隶制的利益嵌进了新秩序。被奴役者不是还没被看见的抽象“余项”；他们一直在那里，有家人、有意志，也有自己的抵抗。

自由的语言与奴役的生活，并排存在于这个人的世界里。

后来有内战，有1865年的废奴修正案，有重建的希望与挫折，有1964年的民权法案。但抗争并不是到那些年份才开始。被奴役者、自由黑人和废奴者，早就在逼问这个国家的承诺。

共和国的历史不是一张自动变好的时间表。已经得到的权利也会被侵蚀，没被承认的人不会因为别人称制度开放，就自动获得位置。

哥德尔的定理不能证明一部宪法的伦理缺陷。这里的“余项”是本文用来追问排除的词，不是把人装进公式。一个人不能只是另一个人的工具；华盛顿的奴隶制实践，正与这一立场冲突。

我们可以承认他的交权具有意义，同时拒绝让这份意义替他发出无罪证明。

真正要保留的，不只是伟人改正自己的机会。还有被他占有的人说“不”的资格。

七、种地

1799年12月14日，华盛顿在弗农山庄去世，六十七岁。临终记录写到严重的喉部症状、呼吸困难，以及医生反复放血。他是在一场急病中离开的，不是一幅无痛的退隐图。

他死在家里，在自己的床上。这个庄园是他经营的地方，也是许多被奴役者被迫生活和劳动的地方。

回看这个系列：苏格拉底被雅典判死，耶稣遭罗马处刑；秦始皇死于巡途，身后继承陷入权力操弄；尼采晚年重病，哥德尔死于营养不良，杜甫在漂泊晚年去世。不同的死亡，不能排成一张选择正确与否的奖惩表。

华盛顿能够回家，值得看见。不能由此说被杀的人给了别人杀他的理由，或者患病的人没有学会放下。

他回去经营农庄。

小麦、骡子、土壤、账目：政治以外，生活还有这些具体的事。它们并不纯洁无争，却提醒人，一个职位不该吞掉一个人全部的时间。

借孔子的“述而不作”作一个有限的对照：华盛顿没有发明共和制，他接受前人的思想，也在实践中改变了继承之物。他留下的姿势，不是空手，而是拿过以后仍肯放下。

康德长期生活在柯尼斯堡，华盛顿回到弗农山庄。我们可以把书桌与田地并置，却不必把康德写成从未离城、没有具体生活的人。

这里有泥土、种子、四季。人从公共职位退出，还可以重新安排日子。这样的返回，让权力不再冒充生命唯一的高度。

但问一句：这个“回家”的自由，是否也属于替他种地的人？如果这句不问，泥土的画面就太容易把脚印抹平。

八、不穿龙袍的人

这些人物面对权力的方式，各不相同。下面是本系列的并置，不是替他们各写一行足以结案的生平。

秦始皇强化帝国的中心；下一篇的亚历山大把征服推得越来越远，三十二岁去世。康德在书桌前追问自由；苏格拉底留在雅典发问；孔子曾任职，也在未被采纳的理想中继续教学。老子在传说里离去；佛陀的教导让修行者依靠自己与法，不把老师的个人权威当作永久拐杖。

华盛顿的姿势是：接过权力。交还。回家。

这里的放下，不需要我们替他断言没有欲望，也不需要虚构他曾经暗想称王。我们能看见的，是他在可以继续掌权的关口，为自己划了界。

自我约束，是让职位有一天不再属于我，而制度不因此停转。

康德要求人不能只被当作手段。华盛顿没有把军队和总统职位永远据为己有，这一动作可以与那条要求相照；他的奴隶制实践又迫使我们看见，他并没有把它落实到所有人。

他不是圣人。两次交权，不会抹去庄园中的不自由。庄园中的不自由，也不要求我们装作两次交权没有发生。

在本文想象的桥头，又多了一个人。他不穿龙袍，穿着返乡劳作的衣服，手上有泥。他站在秦始皇旁边。一个人的衣服指向最高的位置，另一个人的衣服指向可以离开那个位置的生活。

脚下是泥土。但这个画面里，也要给曾在同一片土地上被迫劳作的人留下位置。他们不是伟人归田时安静的布景。

泥土也会裂。可它能翻动，能重新播种。活着，不是没有裂缝；是仍能修补，而动手的人不只一个。

注释

临终部分依据同时代记述及弗农山庄整理；“急性喉炎”“会厌炎”等现代回溯诊断有不同意见，正文不作确定诊断或对放血与死亡的单一因果裁决。蒙太奇保留各篇已说明的史料限度，不以疾病、处决或死亡奖惩思想。康德并非从未离开柯尼斯堡；杜甫确切死地仍有争议。

I. He Could Have

1783. The War of Independence was reaching its victorious end. Peace placed another question on the table.

George Washington commanded the Continental Army. His standing extended far beyond its camps. Victory belonged to many people, but this name now carried a weight greater than the office alone.

Would the army become his personal power?

The temptation was not invented afterward. In 1782 an officer, Lewis Nicola, discussed monarchy in a letter and pointed toward Washington as a possible ruler. Washington rejected the proposal sharply. This was not a whole nation presenting a crown, but a road someone had actually suggested and he refused to take.

In the spring of 1783, officers at Newburgh were angry about unpaid wages and pensions. Washington again intervened against the danger of using the army to pressure civilian government. He acknowledged the soldiers' grievances without making those grievances a license for military rule.

History supplies enough figures who carried military victory into personal sovereignty: Caesar became dictator for life after civil war; Napoleon moved from general to emperor; Qin Shi Huang, Xiang Yu and Liu Bang each connected conquest with a new position of power. Their paths differed. They were not the inevitable fate of every victor. But that road was there.

Washington chose a turn away from it.

On December 23, 1783, he entered the chamber where Congress was meeting in Annapolis, Maryland, and surrendered his military commission. Entrusted authority went back to the body that had granted it. He said farewell and returned to Mount Vernon.

A story transmitted through the painter Benjamin West has George III responding to Washington's intention to retire by saying that such an act would make him one of the greatest men in the world. Versions and occasions differ; we need not mistake the story for a court transcript made that day.

A king's admiration circles around a man refusing to make victory his private property. The story endured because the action deserves our attention.

II. He Could Have Again

Six years later. 1789.

Under the Articles of Confederation, central authority was weak. Finance, debt and coordination among the states posed serious problems. In 1787 the Philadelphia convention proposed a new Constitution; after state ratification, the new government began operating.

The new office needed its first president. Many eyes returned to Washington.

His letters repeatedly express the wish to retire to the countryside. Mount Vernon meant agricultural experiments, wheat, livestock and the business of an estate he cared about. It was no refuge outside labor and power relations, but it was where he wanted to return.

In 1789 every elector who voted supported him. In 1792 they did so again. He was twice elected unanimously by the participating Electoral College.

He accepted the office. His reputation helped the new institutions win confidence. Duty, persuasion and personal judgment all belonged to the decision; we need not claim access to a single motive in his mind.

Four years. Four more. Two terms.

Late in his second term he retained great prestige while enduring bitter partisan conflict and attacks. No law prevented another candidacy. That does not mean everyone wanted him to stay, or that he could remain for life without further elections.

The road to a third candidacy was still open.

He did not take it.

In September 1796 he published his Farewell Address and announced that he would not stand again. In 1797 he completed the transfer and returned to Mount Vernon. No two-term constitutional prohibition had driven him out. That would come in 1951.

III. He Knew

Why not turn the army into one's own state? Why not keep occupying the presidential chair?

One answer is republican conviction: office comes from authorization, not possession. The Roman story of Cincinnatus surrendering his dictatorship and returning to his fields had also become a mirror in which people saw Washington.

Ideas and examples matter. Knowing a story does not mean being able to live it.

Between knowing and doing there may stand an entire army, or simply a chair in which you could remain seated.

Washington crossed that distance twice: first returning military command, then allowing the presidency to leave him and pass to a successor.

I read the gesture this way: if an office can be occupied only by me, it has not truly become a public office.

The struggle for independence had many sources. The Declaration grounded legitimate government in the consent of the governed, while the actual political people excluded many. Even at that narrow beginning, recasting victory as a personal hereditary entitlement would injure the republic's reason for being.

Leaving acquired a constructive meaning. The person walked out; the office remained. Others could enter, and they too should be able to leave.

In this series' language of carving, letting go is not an empty interval after the building. It is the final cut.

IV. Washington and Qin Shi Huang

The preceding essay followed Qin Shi Huang: a vast territory brought within one order, an order also subjected to enormous strain. The Qin empire fell about fifteen years after unification, while many of its institutions outlived it.

Now place these two figures beside one another.

One strengthened the sovereign's supreme position, organizing an empire around an irreplaceable center. The other, within a collective republican experiment, relinquished military office and eventually the presidency. Neither man made the whole of history himself. Their gestures can still illuminate one another.

This essay gives them an image: wearing the imperial robe, refusing it. That is not a claim about the clothing actually worn in the Qin period.

Titles reveal expectations too. In 1789 Congress debated an exalted form of address for the president. The Senate briefly favored a title beginning “His Highness.” The House resisted, and a simpler presidential designation prevailed. Washington did not single-handedly choose a word; the new republic was arguing over how to speak about itself.

Qin's center resembles a tower narrowing toward its summit. The American Constitution sought to distribute authority among offices and bind officeholders to rules. Making the rules stand above a person remains work to be done, not a guarantee already redeemed.

Qin's suppression, including book burning, can be set against the place reserved for criticism in republican arrangements. But the First Amendment came through Congress, the states and sustained political demands. It was not Washington's personal gift.

One movement seeks a more uniform command; the other must tolerate voices it cannot entirely control. Read this way, the gestures stand apart.

The Qin empire's short life and the American republic's survival across more than two centuries cannot be explained by one man's character. War, society, institutions and subsequent choices all enter the story. Lasting longer does not by itself mean being more just.

Zhuangzi writes that Hundun died when seven openings had been bored into him. We borrow a reminder to leave life room to breathe, not a claim that politics has experimentally proved a fable.

V. His Construction

But Washington was not against construction. He did not remain only in a clearing of questions, or speak once and disappear from politics.

He helped build a great deal.

He presided over the Constitutional Convention in 1787. He was not the Constitution's sole author, but his presence gave the proceedings weight. As president, he worked with Congress and officials to turn a written arrangement into everyday administration.

Hamilton at the Treasury, Jefferson at State: the two disputed the country's future fiercely. Washington judged between them and made choices. How a cabinet deliberates, how the president uses the veto already granted by the Constitution, how foreign affairs are conducted: precedents grew out of particular business.

He helped construct a government that could function.

It did not require every disagreement to disappear before it could go on.

Ratified in 1791, the First Amendment protected speech, publication and other liberties. Criticism of government should not be erased merely because it displeases those governing. Boundaries and enforcement would remain contested; this was no permission to say anything under any circumstances without restriction.

Separated powers and checks mean that decisions cannot always be completed by a single hand. They may delay action; they may prevent injury. Efficiency is not a sin, nor obstruction inherently noble. The questions are who can say no, and who can correct a mistake.

Retiring after two terms left successors a conspicuous precedent. Before Roosevelt's third election in 1940, no one won a presidential election for a third time. The practice was not originally law, nor did it go wholly unchallenged.

Only in 1951 did the Twenty-Second Amendment place a limit on the number of elections in the Constitution. A person's departure and a later legal rule are different moments, though a line connects them.

This construction has disputes and cracks. It needs amendment, and people repeatedly bringing promises on paper into actual life.

Being able to breathe is not being unable to suffer injury. It means those in power cannot be the only ones allowed to say that a wound exists.

VI. His Gap

Washington was not a perfect person. We need no mathematical theorem in order to take both a person's achievements and the harm he caused seriously.

The unavoidable fact is his life as an enslaver.

In 1799 Mount Vernon held 317 enslaved people. Washington legally owned 123; 153 belonged to the Custis estate, in which Martha held a life interest; another 41 were hired from other enslavers. The legal ledger had different columns. Labor and unfreedom occurred on the same estate.

His will gave William Lee freedom immediately upon Washington's death. The others whom Washington owned were to be freed after Martha died; she brought that date forward to January 1801. Those belonging to the Custis estate were outside the provision.

These arrangements changed some lives. They could not turn the years already lived in bondage into freedom. Washington expressed wishes for abolition in private letters, but did not lead a public abolition movement while president; he also pursued people who escaped his household.

A man who returned public military authority did not give everyone on his estate the same right to leave.

This was not an empty patch the Constitution had forgotten to color in. Its compromises over representation, the slave trade and fugitives embedded slaveholding interests in the new order. Enslaved people were not an abstract remainder waiting to be noticed. They were already there, with families, purposes and resistance of their own.

The language of liberty and the practice of enslavement stood together in this man's world.

Civil war would follow, then the abolition amendment of 1865, Reconstruction's hopes and defeats, and the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Struggle did not begin on those dates. Enslaved people, free Black people and abolitionists had long been pressing the country's promises against its practices.

The republic's history is no timetable of automatic improvement. Rights already gained can be eroded. Calling a system open does not by itself secure a place for someone it excludes.

Gödel's theorem cannot establish a constitution's ethical failures. Remainder is this essay's word for questioning exclusion, not a formula into which people disappear. No person can be merely another person's instrument. Washington's practice of slavery conflicts directly with that position.

We can recognize the importance of his relinquishing office and refuse to let that importance issue a certificate of innocence.

What needs protecting is not only a great man's chance to correct himself. It is also the standing of those he possessed to say no.

VII. Farming

Washington died at Mount Vernon on December 14, 1799, aged sixty-seven. The accounts describe severe throat symptoms, difficulty breathing and repeated bloodletting by his physicians. He died during an acute illness, not inside a picture of painless retirement.

He died at home, in his own bed. The estate was a place he managed, and a place where many enslaved people were compelled to live and work.

Look back through this series. Athens condemned Socrates; Rome executed Jesus. Qin Shi Huang died while traveling, and the succession became entangled in manipulation. Nietzsche suffered grave illness in his final years; Gödel died of malnutrition; Du Fu died late in a wandering life. Different deaths do not form a table of rewards and punishments for making the right choice.

Washington could return home. That matters. It does not mean the murdered gave others a reason to kill them, or that the ill had failed to learn how to let go.

He returned to managing a farm.

Wheat, mules, soil, accounts: outside politics, life contains these particular things. They are not pure or free of conflict. They remind us that an office should not consume all of a person's time.

Borrow Confucius' idea of transmitting for a limited comparison. Washington did not invent republican government. He inherited ideas and changed what he inherited through practice. His posture was not empty-handedness, but a willingness to release what his hands had held.

Kant spent much of his life in Königsberg; Washington returned to Mount Vernon. We can place a desk beside a field without turning Kant into someone who never left his city or had no concrete life.

Here are soil, seeds and seasons. Someone can leave public office and arrange the days anew. Such a return prevents power from pretending to be life's only height.

But ask: did the people farming for him share that freedom to go home? Without the question, the lovely picture of earth too easily smooths away their footprints.

VIII. The Man Who Would Not Wear the Crown

These figures encountered power differently. What follows is this series' juxtaposition, not a one-line verdict on each life.

Qin Shi Huang strengthened the imperial center. Alexander, the subject of the next essay, extended conquest ever farther and died at thirty-two. Kant questioned freedom at his desk; Socrates stayed in Athens asking questions; Confucius held office and continued teaching when his hopes went unfulfilled. Laozi departs in the legend. The Buddha's teachings direct practitioners toward themselves and the teaching, rather than making a teacher's personal authority a permanent crutch.

Washington's gesture is to accept power, return it, and go home.

We need not declare him free of desire, or invent a secret wish to become king. What we can see is a boundary he set himself when continuing to hold power remained possible.

Self-restraint allows an office eventually to cease being mine without the institution ceasing to function.

Kant insists that people must not be treated merely as means. Washington's refusal to make the army and presidency permanently his own can be read beside that demand. His enslavement of people forces us to see that he did not extend it to everyone.

He was not a saint. Two relinquishments do not erase the estate's unfreedom. That unfreedom does not require us to pretend the two relinquishments never happened.

At this essay's imagined bridgehead, another person arrives. He wears no imperial robe, but the clothes of someone returned to work at home, earth on his hands. He stands beside Qin Shi Huang. One man's clothing points toward the highest position; the other's toward a life able to leave that position.

There is soil underfoot. But the image must also leave room for those forced to labor on the same land. They are not a silent backdrop to a great man's homecoming.

Soil can crack too. It can also be turned and sown again. Being alive is not having no fissures. It is remaining open to repair, with more than one pair of hands at work.
