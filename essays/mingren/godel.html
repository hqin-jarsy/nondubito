哥德尔，构不可闭合

Gödel, The Limits of Closure

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、维也纳

1906年。库尔特·哥德尔出生在奥匈帝国的布尔诺。

他小时候被家人叫作“为什么先生”——什么都要问为什么。一个回答给下来，后面还有一个问题。

这个孩子后来把追问带进了数学。

他在维也纳大学转向数学和逻辑。1920年代的维也纳聚集着许多重问知识基础的人。维也纳学圈想用经验与逻辑分析澄清科学：关于世界的断言要接受经验检验，逻辑和数学则被他们理解为分析性的。那些无法落实的宏大话语，究竟有没有说出什么？

哥德尔参加过这些讨论，却没有接受学圈的全部立场。我们可以先记住这个年轻的听者：身处对澄清充满信心的年代，仍然追问澄清所用的工具。

1931年，他发表了不完备性论文。那一年他满二十五岁。他找到的不是逻辑的失败，而是某一类形式理论的边界。

二、希尔伯特的梦

要知道哥德尔做了什么，先要知道他凿的是什么。

大卫·希尔伯特，当时最有影响力的数学家之一。1900年的二十三个问题，为此后数学研究指出许多方向。他相信数学不应停在“我们永远不会知道”面前：问题摆在那里，就要寻找解答，或者证明所求不可能。

后来所谓希尔伯特计划，一个核心目标是把数学形式化，再用可信的有限性方法证明它不会推出矛盾。与它相连的，还有完备性与机械判定的雄心：一套规则能不能把领域里的问题全部处理好？这些目标相互关联，却不是同一个要求。

让我们把那份雄心放到一张桌上：公理摆好，规则写清，每一步都可检查。若所有算术真理都能沿这些规则抵达，而规则又绝不相互冲突，桌上就像有了一个没有遗漏的世界。

这是一个极其壮阔的构造之梦。这里的“构”，先指我们搭起的公理、规则和证明秩序。从公理走到结论，路径透明，脚下有据。

康德在哲学里划出物自体的界限；在这幅数学之梦里，人却忍不住问：纯形式是我们亲手规定的，难道还会有什么逃出掌握？这是本文摆出的对照，不是两人的问题可以直接互换。

希尔伯特的信心影响深远，数学基础的道路却一直有人争论。不是全世界一起做同一个梦。

哥德尔的两个定理，切中了这份雄心最坚硬的地方。基础研究没有结束；一种把一切一次封口的希望，必须改变。

三、第一个不完备性定理

1931年，哥德尔发表《论〈数学原理〉及相关系统的形式不可判定命题Ⅰ》。一篇年轻人的论文，改变了“证明”所能承诺的范围。

今天通常把第一个定理连同罗瑟后来的加强一起说：

如果一个形式理论的公理能够由程序有效列举，包含足够的初等算术，而且是一致的——不同时证明一个句子及其否定——那么总有算术句子，在这个理论里既不能被证明，也不能被反驳。这叫不完备。

把理论固定下来，这道边界就不是“数学家还不够聪明”。在自然数的通常解释中，真与可证不是同一回事：可以有真的算术句子，找不到这个理论内部的证明。

不是今天没找到、明天加班就能找到。只要公理和规则不变，那份证明就不存在。换用更强的理论，有些原先的问题可以解决；若新理论仍满足那些条件，又会有它自己的未决句子。

哥德尔怎样做到的？他把公式和证明编码成自然数，让算术能够谈论证明。然后构造出一个算术句子，大意是：“在这个理论里，没有我的证明。”这不是一句玩弄词语的悖论，而是一套可以逐步检查的数学构造。

若这个句子有理论内的证明，编码后的证明关系就会与句子所说的不可证明性冲突。因此，一致性排除了这样的证明。

从理论之外看，在一致性的前提下，确实没有那份证明；这个哥德尔句子因而在通常的自然数中为真。至于它的否定也不可证明，哥德尔原来的论证用了更强的条件；罗瑟后来换了构造，把要求降到通常的一致性。

于是，在有效公理化、足够算术能力这些条件下，一致性与完备性不能兼得。条件不可悄悄删掉：较弱的理论可以完备；把所有真的算术句子都收作公理，也能得到一致而完备的理论，却失去了有效列举公理的办法。

希尔伯特式的雄心在这里遇到的，不是数学从此无话可说，而是任何这样一套固定规则，都不能把算术的全部话说完。

四、第二个不完备性定理

第二个定理，把问题推回证明者脚下。

对有效公理化、具有足够算术能力并满足通常证明条件的理论，如果它一致，就不能在自身内部证明那个按标准方式表达“本理论一致”的算术句子。

像一个人想踩着自己的肩膀，把自己举起来。比喻很短，定理的条件却不能省。

希尔伯特希望给数学一个可靠的一致性证明。哥德尔的结果给这种希望划了界：若所用的有限性推理已能在待证明的理论中表达，就不能这样完成任务。我们仍可以在更强的框架里寻求相对的一致性证明，只是新框架的根据又需要说明。数学没有因此停止，也没有被判成全无根据。

它说的是一个特定的自我保证不能由某些内部工具完成。把这句话带向人生时，我们已经从证明走进了比喻。

人们常用“知道自己不知道”概括苏格拉底。他在追问中拆穿自以为知道的姿态；那是一个人如何面对知识的问题。

哥德尔处理的是另一个严格得多、也窄得多的问题。但两者并读，会让人对一种姿态迟疑：只要把自己的说法整理得足够漂亮，就已经替自己办好了全部担保。

庄子的寓言写，七窍凿成，混沌死了。好意的塑造也可能毁掉它所面对的生命。这种代价，不能换算成一个逻辑矛盾。

在哥德尔那里，只要还要有效规则、足够的算术和一致性，就必须放下“这套规则可以决定一切”的要求。这是精确的边界，不是说任何完整的东西都必然崩塌。

寓言中的混沌死了，问题却留给了后来的人：我们的整理遗漏了什么？本文从定理旁边借来一道光，照向这个问题；“构不可闭合”在这里是阅读的线索，不是包办一切的定理。

五、散步

1940年，哥德尔与妻子阿黛尔离开纳粹统治下的欧洲，来到美国，在普林斯顿定居。他此前已经访问过高等研究院。

在那里，他与爱因斯坦的友谊日渐深厚。

两个人常一起走在研究院与住处之间。一位划出了算术形式化的界限，一位改变了物理学理解空间、时间和引力的方式。道路上走着两种不同的追问。

1905年，爱因斯坦的狭义相对论改变了牛顿式绝对时间与空间的图景；1915年，广义相对论把引力描述为时空几何的效应，而不再只是平直背景上的力。他是二十世纪最深刻的开凿者之一。

但面对量子理论，他仍在寻找一个不把概率当作最后答案的描述。

量子力学给出测量结果的概率，并对位置和动量能够同时具有的确定性作出限制。怎样理解测量之前的物理实在，却涉及不同的解释。不能把“测量前根本没有答案”说成所有物理学家的共同结论。

“不掷骰子”表达了爱因斯坦的不满。他与波多尔斯基、罗森在1935年的论文中，质疑波函数是否完整描述物理实在。他追问的不只是随机性，还有相隔遥远的系统怎样各自拥有实在；晚年的统一场探索也不只是寻找一个漏掉的变量。

这里的“完整描述”与哥德尔所说的形式理论完备性，问的不是同一件事。一个数学定理不能替两人裁决量子理论的争论。

于是，那条路上的两个人，不必被排成“已经接受边界的人”和“至死不肯接受的人”。他们都还在追问。哥德尔本人也继续做数学与哲学，而不是在发现不完备之后放下了所有问题。

这个画面仍然温柔：两位思想家一起走路，没有必要先把世界观合并成一个答案，才能成为朋友。我们不必替那些散步编写一场从未记录的辩论。

有一则广为转述的回忆说，爱因斯坦晚年到研究院去，是为了享受与哥德尔一起走回家的时光。宏大的理论之外，还有这一段路。

六、饿死

哥德尔长期受身心疾病困扰，晚年的被害恐惧尤其严重。

他担心食物被下毒。阿黛尔长期帮助他进食，甚至为他试食。她是他的妻子和重要的依靠，不是一件维持天才运转的设备。

1977年，阿黛尔自己也因重病住院，无法继续原有的照护。

哥德尔的进食和健康状况进一步恶化。朋友和医护曾尝试帮助他；故事的残酷，不在于世界上真的只剩一个人会做饭，而在于疾病使他难以接受送到身边的帮助。

1978年1月14日，哥德尔在普林斯顿去世，死因为营养不良与衰竭。道森的传记记载，他去世时体重只有六十五磅，约二十九点五公斤。

一个能把证明看得如此清楚的人，也可能无法靠思考摆脱疾病。理解一个定理，不会自动给人一副可以承受一切的身体。

若仍借“构”的语言来想，这里最值得看见的是生活对关系和照护的依赖。疾病侵蚀了他接纳帮助的能力；它没有取消他作为目的的地位，也不是一种应当责备的哲学选择。

所以我们停在病床前，不把他的死亡写成“不完备性定理的肉身版本”。定理说明形式证明的限度，照护面对一个具体的人。两者之间，没有可以替代医学和生平研究的因果捷径。

苏格拉底的毒芹、耶稣的十字架、哥德尔的饥饿，可以在这个系列里彼此照见，却不能排成一张“信任谁，所以怎样死”的表。审判、处决与重病，各有不能抹掉的处境。

信任与恐惧，构造与余项，确实会进入人的生活。到了这里，思想应当帮助我们多看见这个人，而不是让他只剩下一则证明。

七、余项守恒

现在可以把哥德尔放进这个系列了。

苏格拉底凿到空地——凿掉自以为知道。 孔子凿到仁——凿到每个人自己的地上。 老子说到语言的边界，在传说中离去。 庄子回望混沌——发现开凿的代价。 康德凿了以后构——为认识划界，留下物自体。 尼采凿到底——上帝死了。 王阳明换方向——向内凿到良知。 释迦牟尼用筏渡河——到了彼岸，不再背着筏。 耶稣在十字架上——传下赦免的声音。

哥德尔给这个系列带来了一种不同的工作：他不是用数学替前面九个人盖章，而是在一个明确的领域里，把某种限度证明出来。

苏格拉底不把自以为知道当作知道；哥德尔区分一个理论能够证明什么。这两种追问让我们警惕自满，但苏格拉底的生活没有被化约成一个定理。

庄子的七窍与混沌，让人看见善意塑形的代价；哥德尔的结果则要求我们承认，某些形式化目标不能同时实现。并读的力量在于照见各自的边界，不在于把寓言翻译成算式。

康德追问认识的条件，哥德尔限制某些理论的内部一致性证明。脚下的根据未必能由同一套工具全部担保——我们可以听见这样的呼应，同时保留物自体与形式证明之间的距离。

老子追问语言怎样遮蔽它所指向的东西；哥德尔让真与可证分开。那个不可证明的句子恰恰能够写出来：无法在一套规则中证明，不是无法言说。语言的边界与证明的边界，在这里相邻而不重合。

释迦牟尼教人不执著于渡河的筏；哥德尔让我们看清固定规则的限度。这能促使读者放下对工具的迷信，却没有把无常变成数学结论。

在这个系列的画面里，他们像是在不同地方摸到墙面。墙是作者连缀他们的比喻；哥德尔真正留下的，是可以逐步核查、有明确前提的证明。

“余项守恒”是本文对那种未被收尽之物的称呼，不是哥德尔证明过的守恒定律。数学在这里提供了一个严格的例子：固定理论内部的未决是结构性的，而不是搜索得还不够久。它没有说答案在任何地方都不存在，更没有说真理不存在。

构不可闭合——若把它读作不让眼前的构造冒充全部世界，它是这篇文章愿意承担的提醒。

这未必是坏消息。它可以是一个重新开始的理由。

设想有人递来一套声称解释了所有人的完美系统，没有余项，没有死角。孔子、苏格拉底、耶稣、庄子，都只能被安排成注脚。这个系列抗拒的，正是那种取消别人继续说话资格的封口。

我们愿意保留每一个人的开凿，也保留每一个真实的缺口。不是因为定理替所有思想保证了正确，而是因为没有一个人的生命，应当只被当作另一个体系的注脚。

桥头要站满人。不是为了凑出人多，而是让不同的人继续相遇、追问、修正彼此。每一个人都是目的，不只是手段。这是本文的伦理立场，不是从算术里演绎出来的结论。

哥德尔证明了形式理论的某些限度。后来，他在疾病与饥饿中死去。他的生命不只是定理的附注。

他的证明还活着。

I. Vienna

1906. Kurt Gödel was born in Brünn, in the Austro-Hungarian Empire.

As a child, his family called him “Mr. Why.” Everything invited a question. An answer arrived, and another question followed.

He would carry that habit into mathematics.

At the University of Vienna, he turned to mathematics and logic. In the Vienna of the 1920s, many people were reopening the question of what knowledge rests on. The Vienna Circle sought to clarify science through experience and logical analysis: claims about the world should answer to experience, while logic and mathematics were understood as analytic. Did grand statements that could not be brought down to such ground actually say anything?

Gödel attended some of these discussions without accepting all the Circle's positions. Let us first remember the young listener: in an age confident of its power to clarify, he kept asking about the tools of clarification.

In 1931, he published his incompleteness paper. He turned twenty-five that year. What he had found was not a failure of logic, but a boundary for a particular class of formal theories.

II. Hilbert's Dream

To understand what Gödel did, first understand what he was carving into.

David Hilbert was one of the most influential mathematicians of his time. His twenty-three problems of 1900 set many directions for the mathematics that followed. He believed mathematics should not stop at “we shall never know”: confront the problem, find a solution, or prove that what it asks for is impossible.

A central aim of what became known as Hilbert's Program was to formalize mathematics and then establish its freedom from contradiction by trustworthy finitary methods. Connected to it were ambitions for completeness and mechanical decision: could a body of rules settle every question in its domain? These aims belonged together, but they were not the same requirement.

Put that ambition on a table. Lay out the axioms, write down the rules, make every step checkable. If every arithmetical truth could be reached by those rules, and the rules never conflicted, the table would seem to hold a world without omissions.

It was an extraordinary dream of construction. Here, “construction” first means the arrangement of axioms, rules, and proofs that we put in place. From assumptions to conclusions, the route is visible and the footing secure.

Kant had drawn the boundary of the thing-in-itself in philosophy. Within this mathematical dream, a question becomes irresistible: if we ourselves stipulate the pure forms, how could anything escape our grasp? This is the essay's juxtaposition, not an interchangeability between their problems.

Hilbert's confidence had enormous influence, but the foundations of mathematics were always contested. The whole world was not sharing one dream.

Gödel's two theorems cut into its hardest point. Foundational research did not end. A certain hope of settling everything once and for all had to change.

III. The First Incompleteness Theorem

In 1931, Gödel published “On Formally Undecidable Propositions of Principia Mathematica and Related Systems I.” A young man's paper changed what a proof could promise.

Today, the first theorem is commonly stated with Rosser's later strengthening:

If a formal theory has axioms that can be effectively enumerated by a program, contains enough elementary arithmetic, and is consistent—never proving both a sentence and its negation—then some arithmetical sentences can be neither proved nor refuted in that theory. This is incompleteness.

Once the theory is fixed, this boundary is not a matter of mathematicians being insufficiently clever. Under the usual interpretation of the natural numbers, truth and provability are different: there can be true arithmetical sentences with no proof inside that theory.

Not merely undiscovered today, awaiting another night's work tomorrow. As long as the axioms and rules remain unchanged, that proof does not exist. A stronger theory may settle some previously open questions; if it still meets the conditions, it will have undecided sentences of its own.

How did Gödel do it? He encoded formulas and proofs as natural numbers, allowing arithmetic to talk about proof. He then constructed an arithmetical sentence whose rough meaning is: “There is no proof of me in this theory.” This is not a verbal paradox. It is a mathematical construction whose steps can be checked.

If that sentence had a proof in the theory, the encoded proof relation would conflict with the sentence's claim of unprovability. Consistency therefore rules out such a proof.

From outside the theory, assuming consistency, there really is no such proof; the Gödel sentence is consequently true of the usual natural numbers. To rule out a proof of its negation as well, Gödel's original argument used a stronger condition. Rosser later changed the construction and reduced the requirement to ordinary consistency.

Thus, under effective axiomatization and sufficient arithmetical strength, consistency and completeness cannot both be had. The conditions matter: weaker theories can be complete. Taking all true arithmetical sentences as axioms also gives a consistent, complete theory, but no effective procedure can enumerate those axioms.

The Hilbertian ambition met a limit here. Mathematics had not run out of things to say. No fixed body of rules of this kind could say everything arithmetic has to say.

IV. The Second Incompleteness Theorem

The second theorem turns the question back toward the ground beneath the prover's feet.

An effectively axiomatized theory with sufficient arithmetical strength and the usual conditions on proof cannot, if consistent, prove within itself the arithmetical sentence that expresses its consistency in the standard way.

It is like trying to lift yourself by standing on your own shoulders. The image is brief; the theorem's conditions still matter.

Hilbert wanted a reliable consistency proof for mathematics. Gödel's result placed a boundary on that hope: if the finitary reasoning employed can already be represented within the theory under examination, it cannot finish this task. We can still seek a relative consistency proof in a stronger framework, but the grounds of that framework then need explaining. Mathematics neither stopped nor became groundless.

A particular kind of self-guarantee cannot be supplied by certain internal tools. When we carry that thought into a life, we have moved from proof to metaphor.

People often summarize Socrates as “knowing that he does not know.” His questions expose the posture of presumed knowledge. His concern is how a person faces what they know.

Gödel's question is different: more tightly defined, and much narrower. Read together, however, they make one posture less comfortable—the idea that arranging our own account beautifully is enough to provide every guarantee it needs.

In Zhuangzi's fable, seven openings are bored and Hundun dies. Even well-intended shaping can destroy the life before it. That cost cannot be converted into a logical contradiction.

For Gödel, if we retain effective rules, sufficient arithmetic, and consistency, we must surrender the demand that these rules decide everything. This is a precise boundary, not a claim that anything complete must collapse.

Hundun dies in the fable, while a question survives for its readers: what has our ordering left out? This essay borrows a light from beside the theorem to illuminate that question. “The limits of closure” is a thread for reading here, not a theorem that settles everything.

V. The Walk

In 1940, Gödel and his wife Adele left Nazi-controlled Europe for the United States and settled in Princeton. He had already visited the Institute for Advanced Study.

There, his friendship with Einstein deepened.

They often walked together between the Institute and their homes. One had established limits to the formalization of arithmetic. The other had changed how physics understood space, time, and gravity. Two different inquiries shared the road.

In 1905, Einstein's special relativity transformed the Newtonian picture of absolute time and space. In 1915, general relativity described gravitation through spacetime geometry, rather than merely as a force acting against a flat background. He was one of the twentieth century's most profound carvers.

Yet, facing quantum theory, he continued to seek a description that did not make probability the final answer.

Quantum mechanics supplies probabilities for measurement outcomes and restricts how definite position and momentum can simultaneously be. What physical reality means before measurement remains a question on which interpretations differ. “There is simply no answer before measurement” is not a conclusion shared by all physicists.

The image of a God who does not play dice expressed Einstein's dissatisfaction. In their 1935 paper, he, Podolsky, and Rosen questioned whether the wave function completely describes physical reality. His concern was not only randomness, but how widely separated systems could possess realities of their own. His later search for unified field theories was not merely a search for one missing variable.

“Complete description” here and completeness of a formal theory in Gödel's sense ask different questions. A mathematical theorem cannot settle the quantum debate on behalf of the two men.

The walkers need not be cast as one man who had accepted limits and another who refused them until death. Both were still asking questions. Gödel himself continued working in mathematics and philosophy; incompleteness had not ended his inquiry.

The image remains tender: two thinkers walking together, without first having to merge their worldviews into one answer in order to be friends. We need not supply an unrecorded debate for those walks.

A widely repeated recollection has Einstein saying that, in his later years, he went to the Institute for the pleasure of walking home with Gödel. Beyond the great theories, there was this stretch of road.

VI. Starvation

Gödel struggled for many years with physical and mental illness. In his later life, fears of persecution became especially severe.

He feared that his food was poisoned. Adele helped him eat over many years, even tasting food for him. She was his wife and an important source of support, not a device for keeping a genius running.

In 1977, Adele herself became gravely ill and was hospitalized, unable to continue the care she had provided.

Gödel's eating and health deteriorated further. Friends and medical professionals tried to help. The cruelty of the story is not that only one person in the world knew how to cook. Illness made it difficult for him to accept help brought to his door.

On January 14, 1978, Gödel died in Princeton of malnutrition and inanition. Dawson's biography records that he weighed only sixty-five pounds, about twenty-nine and a half kilograms.

Someone who sees a proof with extraordinary clarity may still be unable to think his way out of illness. Understanding a theorem does not give a person a body capable of bearing everything.

If we continue to borrow the language of “construction,” what matters here is how a life depends on relationships and care. Illness impaired his ability to accept help. It neither cancelled his standing as an end nor amounted to a philosophical choice for which he should be blamed.

So we stop at the bedside without turning his death into “the incompleteness theorem in flesh.” The theorem concerns limits to formal proof; care attends to a particular person. No causal shortcut connects the two in place of medicine and biographical inquiry.

Socrates' hemlock, Jesus' cross, and Gödel's starvation may illuminate one another within this series. They cannot be reduced to a table of whom each man trusted and therefore how he died. Trial, execution, and grave illness each have circumstances that must remain visible.

Trust and fear, construction and remainder, do enter human lives. Here, thought should help us see more of the person, not leave him as nothing but an illustration of a proof.

VII. The Conservation of Remainder

Now Gödel can take his place in this series.

Socrates carved toward a clearing—away from presumed knowledge. Confucius carved toward ren—toward each person's own ground. Laozi reached the edge of language and departed in the legend. Zhuangzi looked back toward Hundun—and the cost of carving. Kant carved and then constructed—setting limits to knowledge, leaving the thing-in-itself. Nietzsche carved to the bottom—God is dead. Wang Yangming reversed direction—carving inward toward innate knowing. Shakyamuni crossed with a raft—and did not carry it after reaching the farther shore. Jesus on the cross—leaving a voice of forgiveness.

Gödel brings a different kind of work into this company. He does not certify the preceding nine lives with mathematics. In a clearly specified domain, he proves a certain limit.

Socrates refuses to mistake presumed knowledge for knowledge; Gödel distinguishes what a theory can prove. Both inquiries can unsettle complacency, without reducing Socrates' life to a theorem.

Zhuangzi's openings and Hundun reveal the cost of well-intended shaping. Gödel's result requires us to recognize that certain formal goals cannot all be met at once. Reading them together illuminates their respective boundaries; it does not translate a fable into an equation.

Kant asks after the conditions of knowledge; Gödel limits certain theories' internal consistency proofs. The same tools may not supply every guarantee for the ground beneath them. We can hear that resonance while keeping the distance between the thing-in-itself and formal proof.

Laozi asks how language obscures what it points toward; Gödel separates truth from provability. The unprovable sentence can, precisely, be written down. Inability to prove it under one set of rules is not inability to say it. The boundaries of language and proof stand near one another here, without coinciding.

Shakyamuni teaches nonattachment to the raft used for crossing; Gödel makes the limits of fixed rules visible. This may encourage a reader to relinquish the worship of tools, but it does not turn impermanence into a mathematical result.

In the image this series assembles, these people seem to touch a wall at different places. The wall is the author's metaphor connecting them. What Gödel actually leaves is a proof with definite premises, open to step-by-step checking.

“The conservation of remainder” is this essay's name for what has not been gathered in, not a conservation law proved by Gödel. Mathematics supplies a rigorous example: undecidability within a fixed theory is structural, not a consequence of an insufficiently long search. It does not say that no answer exists anywhere, still less that truth does not exist.

The limits of closure—understood as a refusal to let the construction before us impersonate the whole world—is the reminder this essay chooses to carry.

That need not be bad news. It can be a reason to begin again.

Imagine being handed a perfect system claiming to explain every person, with no remainder and no blind spots. Confucius, Socrates, Jesus, Zhuangzi: all could only be assigned footnotes. What this series resists is precisely that act of sealing off another person's right to go on speaking.

We want to preserve each person's carving, and each real opening. Not because a theorem guarantees that every thought is correct, but because no person's life should be treated merely as a footnote to somebody else's system.

The bridgehead must be filled with people. Not to make up a crowd, but so that different people can keep meeting, questioning, and correcting one another. Each person is an end, not merely a means. That is this essay's ethical commitment, not a conclusion deduced from arithmetic.

Gödel proved certain limits of formal theories. Later, he died amid illness and starvation. His life is not merely an appendix to a theorem.

His proof is still alive.

Notes

This essay uses the modern Gödel–Rosser formulation of the first incompleteness theorem: effective axiomatization, consistency, and sufficient elementary arithmetic. Gödel's original 1931 argument used ω-consistency to exclude a refutation of the Gödel sentence; Rosser's different construction in 1936 strengthened the result to require ordinary consistency alone. ‘True’ here refers to the standard natural numbers, not to untestable belief in general.

The second theorem also depends on the usual formalization of provability and appropriate derivability conditions; its consistency sentence is the standard arithmetized statement. Gödel's earlier completeness theorem for first-order logic says that every logically valid formula has a proof, a different matter from whether particular arithmetic axioms decide every sentence. ‘Construction’ and ‘conservation of remainder’ are this essay's philosophical vocabulary, not universal laws of psychology, physics, or ethics derived from the theorems.

Biographical details were checked chiefly against the Institute for Advanced Study, the University of Vienna, and Kleene's National Academy of Sciences memoir. The paper appeared in 1931, the year Gödel turned twenty-five. The childhood nickname comes through family recollection. Documents and photographs support the walks; Einstein's remark is retained as a reported recollection, without inventing their conversations.

Late-life care, Adele's hospitalization in 1977, and the recorded weight follow chapter XII of Dawson's archive-based Logical Dilemmas, especially pages 251–253, alongside the IAS biography. Illness and death are not treated as consequences of incompleteness. This essay offers neither a retrospective clinical diagnosis nor blame directed at his wife.

The completeness of a physical description in the 1935 Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paper must be distinguished from deductive completeness of a formal theory. The nine-person montage and comparisons belong to this series' reading framework, not to a unified proof of its traditions.
