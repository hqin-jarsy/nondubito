梅洛-庞蒂：身体先于概念知道

Merleau-Ponty: The Body Knows Before Concepts Do

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、你的手已经知道了

你伸手去拿一个杯子。

你不必先想“现在启动右臂的哪块肌肉，再调整手指角度，使它与杯子的直径匹配”。熟悉的动作往往已经开始。你还没把它说成一句指令，手就伸过去了。

你走路。你没有计算每一步的角度和力量。你的腿已经知道了。

你骑自行车。波兰尼下一篇会用这个例子。但梅洛-庞蒂先到了。你骑自行车的时候，你的身体在做一件你的意识说不出来的事。你说不出来你的身体到底在做什么——哪块肌肉在什么时候用多大的力，重心怎么移动。但你的身体知道。

身体不只是你的工具，也是你认识世界的方式。说“我就是我的身体”，不是把人缩成一堆器官，而是拒绝把自己想成一位住在肉身驾驶舱里的乘客。你不是先在别处成为一个我，随后才领到身体。

二、知觉现象学

1945年。梅洛-庞蒂出版了他最重要的书：《知觉现象学》。

这本书做了一件事：把身体放到了哲学的中心。

我们很容易把认识想成：里面有一个意识，外面有一个世界，身体负责传递消息。这种简化的笛卡尔式图景，把“我思”放到起点。不过笛卡尔也讨论身心结合，不能把整个西方哲学写成没有人认真看过身体。

梅洛-庞蒂把目光移到“我能”：在逐条思考之前，身体已经会朝向东西、绕开障碍、形成习惯。“我能故我在”可以当作入门时的意译，不能冒充他的逐字名言，更不能读成“有某种能力才配存在”。

婴儿还不会讲一套关于世界的理论，却已经用嘴、手、姿势与周围相处。这不是说婴儿不会思考，而是说：认识不必等到能够把认识说明白才开始。失去一种能力的人，也并不因此失去自己与世界的关系。

三、幻肢

梅洛-庞蒂有一个著名的分析：幻肢。

一个人的手臂被截肢了。但他还是"感觉到"那只手臂在那里。他感觉到疼痛。他感觉到手指在动。一只不存在的手臂，产生了真实的感觉。

怎么解释？

不能因为肢体已失去，就说疼痛不真实；也不能说“神经断了，所以生理机制不可能参与”。神经系统仍在，生理与心理研究都有其位置。梅洛-庞蒂关心的是：只把身体当零件清单，或只把经验当头脑里的想象，都不足以描述这个人的生活。

过去能够抓、提、碰的生活，并不会在手术后一笔清零。世界还可能向这个人呈现出旧的行动可能：杯子仍像是伸手可取的。身体图式（body schema）让我们理解这种习惯性的朝向，而不是拿“投射”替代医学解释。

身体图式不是一张需要随时翻看的解剖图。你未必知道胳膊精确多长，却熟悉哪些东西够得到；未必量过腿，却知道通常能跨多远。它涉及姿势、感觉和行动彼此配合的整体，也会在受伤、学习或使用工具时改变。

身体改变了，与世界相处的习惯却不必立刻跟上。这个错位，不能用“硬件没了、软件还在”就讲完。

四、他和麦克林托克

前面写过麦克林托克。她对玉米的熟悉，不只是记住一张资料表，而是在多年观察、实验和照料中长出来的辨别力。所谓“对有机体的感觉”，不是隔着身体直接感应基因移动。

梅洛-庞蒂给了我们一种读她的语言，不是说她的发现依赖他的哲学。

看显微镜、授粉、分辨一个异常，都是需要学习的动作。眼睛和手不是等待大脑下发一切细节的末端；训练过的整个身体，参与了“这次不太一样”的判断。

这种感觉不在大脑的对立面，也不是六十年过去后才忽然发生的神秘压缩。她在长期工作中形成经验，又靠遗传分析与细胞学证据提出发现。经验让一些细节先显出分量，证据则要求那份感觉接受检验。

有时候，手已经换了姿势，眼睛已经停住，说明还在后面追。

把费希特放在旁边，会多出一个问题：能动的“我”怎样在具体身体中生活？这不是把费希特翻译掉，也不是说他没有身体理论，而是从设定与能动性，转向姿势、习惯和处境。三个人的路在这里相遇，并不合成一条。

五、世界不是我想的，是我活的

“世界不是我所思的，而是我所生活的。”可以用这句意译记住《知觉现象学》的一个入口：在把世界当对象讨论之前，我们已经在其中。

你不是从一个没有位置的地方观看。你身体在这里，目光朝那里，别人的脚步从身后过来。到晚期，他用交织（chiasm）来进一步追问感知者与被感知者的关系。

左手碰右手时，右手既能被触摸，也能转过来触摸。看世界的我，本身也在可见世界里面。但这不是两种位置终于完全重合，更不是桌子也有一双眼睛在看我。交织保留着差异：能互相转换，不等于成为同一件事。

这可以与龙树的主客反思相互照看，但不能说两个人走到了同一个结论。中观的无自性与现象学的交织，各有自己的问题和论证。

梅洛-庞蒂用“肉”（la chair）来摸索一种存在方式。它不只是肌肉，也不是藏在一切事物后面的巨大身体，而是感知者与可感者相互牵连、又不能互相吞没的关系。

空与肉，可以在阅读中相遇。让它们彼此照亮，比急着宣布它们相同更有意思。

六、他和本轮其他人

孔德重视观察，梅洛-庞蒂让我们再问：观察怎样发生？显微镜前不是一块无处安放的纯意识，而是会疲劳、会学习、占着位置的身体。这是补问，不是断言孔德从没想过观察者。

费希特问能动性，梅洛-庞蒂把镜头推近：这个我怎样伸手、转身、走进房间？哲学里的第一人称，落到了一个有习惯、有姿态的生活里。

薇依让占有性的自我安静下来，这不要求身体消失。注意别人时，你仍会呼吸，会疲倦，会靠近或后退。身体习惯可以改变，也可能丧失；重要的是，注意从来不是无身体的观看。

斯特劳森从概念上不把人拼成身心两个零件，梅洛-庞蒂从经验中追问身体怎样生活。两种入口相邻，不必替他们虚构一句“我完全同意你”。

维果茨基把共同活动带进发展。牵着孩子学走路，是一种看得见的身体合作；说话、手势与工具同样可以支持学习。后来所谓“脚手架”，不只是一套抽象说明，也不只限于身体牵引。

七、死在笛卡尔面前

1961年5月3日，梅洛-庞蒂猝然去世，五十三岁。关于最后工作情景，常见的传记叙述把一部打开的笛卡尔著作放在他的书桌上；这里保留这个有意味的画面，不把病因或“明天那堂课”的细节写死。

桌上摊着的那本书是什么？

通常被说成是笛卡尔的《光学》。

一个终身与笛卡尔争辩的人，最后仍在读笛卡尔。这个画面动人，不因为死亡证明了哪一方正确，而因为思想上的分歧，并不妨碍一场对话持续一生。

克洛德·勒福尔整理的遗稿《可见的与不可见的》于1964年出版，连同工作笔记，把没有完成的探索交给读者。阿伦特后来也会留下未完成的“判断”。我们可以并读这两处中断，但不能替未写成的书断言：最重要的思想一定还没到来。

有些没写完的书，不给人封口的答案，却让一个问题继续有地方生长。

八、桥头

在这座虚构的桥上，梅洛-庞蒂走过来。你首先注意到的不是他在想什么，是他怎么走。

他走路的样子很自然。不刻意。每一步都踩得很实——不是在"想"踩，是身体在踩。他的重心微微前倾。他的手臂自然摆动。他不看脚下——他的身体知道地板在那里。

他上了桥。

他看到了斯特劳森刚铺好的地板。斯特劳森说"人是原初的"——不能拆成身体和心灵。梅洛-庞蒂走过去，用脚感受了一下那块地板。

不是用眼睛看。用脚感受。

他的脚感到质地、硬度、微微的弹性。它们不必先被写成判断句，才进入他的经验。

他看到了麦克林托克蹲在那里看玉米。他认出了她——不是因为他认识她，是因为他认出了那种跟对象之间的身体关系。她弯在显微镜前的姿势——那不是"一个观察者在看一个对象"。那是一个身体跟另一个生命之间的交织。

他看到了费希特站在缝隙上。费希特说"我设定我自身"。梅洛-庞蒂走过去站在他旁边。他没说话。他只是站在那里。他的身体在缝隙旁边。他的身体知道缝隙在那里——不是因为看到了，是因为脚底感觉到了。

他在桥头找了一个位置。不是特别的位置。他站在那里。他的身体在那里。

世界不是他想的。世界是他活的。

他能感觉到风。风不是一个概念。风是皮肤上的触感。他能感觉到桥面的微微震动——其他人走路的震动通过地板传到他的脚底。他能感觉到温度在变——太阳要落了。

这些已经进入了他的生活，不必逐件在心里点名。

他站在那里。世界在他身上。他在世界里面。

不是没有边界。是边界并不把我们隔在世界之外。[1][2][3]

I. Your Hand Already Knows

You reach for a cup.

You need not issue instructions to individual muscles or calculate the angle of every finger. A familiar movement is already underway. Before you have put it into a sentence, your hand is reaching.

You walk. You don't calculate the angle and force of each step. Your legs already know.

You ride a bicycle. Polanyi will use this example in the next essay. But Merleau-Ponty arrived first. When you ride, your body does something your consciousness cannot articulate. You can't say what your body is doing — which muscles fire when, how your center of gravity shifts. But your body knows.

The body is not merely your tool; it is your way of meeting the world. To say “I am my body” is not to reduce a person to organs. It refuses the picture of a passenger housed in a bodily cockpit. You do not become a self somewhere else and receive a body afterward.

II. Phenomenology of Perception

1945. Merleau-Ponty publishes Phenomenology of Perception.

The book does one thing: it places the body at the center of philosophy.

It is easy to picture knowing this way: consciousness inside, a world outside, and the body carrying messages between them. This simplified Cartesian picture puts “I think” first. Descartes also addressed mind–body union; it would be false to say that no earlier Western philosopher took embodiment seriously.

Merleau-Ponty turns toward “I can”: before step-by-step reflection, the body reaches toward things, negotiates obstacles and forms habits. “I can, therefore I am” may serve as an introductory paraphrase, not an exact quotation—and never as a claim that existence must be earned through a particular ability.

A baby cannot yet state a theory of the world but already meets it through mouth, hands and posture. This does not mean babies cannot think. Knowing need not wait until it can explain itself. Nor does losing an ability erase a person’s relation to the world.

III. The Phantom Limb

Merleau-Ponty has a famous analysis: the phantom limb.

A person's arm is amputated. But they still "feel" the arm. They feel pain. They feel the fingers moving. A nonexistent arm producing real sensation.

How to explain this?

A missing limb does not make the pain unreal. Nor does amputation establish that physiological mechanisms cannot be involved. The nervous system remains; physiological and psychological inquiry both have their place. Merleau-Ponty asks what is missed when we treat a lived body as a list of parts, or experience as a picture confined to the mind.

A life of grasping, lifting and touching is not erased at surgery. The world may still present old possibilities: the cup still solicits a reach. Body schema helps describe this habitual orientation. It does not substitute a philosophical “projection” for medical explanation.

A body schema is not an anatomical chart consulted before each movement. You may not know your arm’s exact length yet know what is within reach; you may never measure your leg yet know the gap you can usually cross. Posture, sensation and action work together in a whole that can change through injury, learning or tool use.

The body has changed; its habitual relation to the world need not change at the same instant. “The hardware is gone, the software remains” would merely repeat the division we are trying to understand.

IV. Merleau-Ponty and McClintock

Earlier we met McClintock. Her familiarity with maize was not a memorized table of facts, but discrimination cultivated through years of observation, experiment and care. A feeling for the organism is not an extrasensory detection of moving genes.

Merleau-Ponty gives us a language for reading that practice, not a claim that her discovery depended on his philosophy.

Using a microscope, pollinating a plant, spotting an anomaly: each is learned. Eyes and hands are not merely endpoints waiting for every detail to be commanded. A trained, embodied person participates in the judgment that this case is different.

Such discernment is not opposed to the brain, nor a mysterious compression occurring only after sixty years. Long practice mattered, as did genetic analysis and cytological evidence. Experience made certain details stand out; evidence required the hunch to answer for itself.

Sometimes the hand has changed position and the eye has paused while the explanation is still catching up.

Beside Fichte, another question appears: how does an active I live in a particular body? This neither translates Fichte away nor denies his work on embodiment. It shifts attention from positing and agency toward posture, habit and situation. Three paths meet here without becoming one.

V. The World Is Not What I Think, but What I Live

Before making the world an object of discussion, we are already living in it. That is one entrance to Phenomenology of Perception: the world is not merely something thought about, but the setting of a life already underway.

You do not look from nowhere. Your body is here, your gaze turns there, someone’s footsteps pass behind you. In his later work, intertwining—the chiasm—further explores the relation between perceiving and being perceived.

When the left hand touches the right, the right can be touched and can in turn touch. The one who sees belongs to the visible world. Yet these positions never settle into complete coincidence, and the table need not possess eyes of its own. Intertwining preserves difference: reversibility is not identity.

This can illuminate a comparison with Nāgārjuna without making their conclusions identical. Madhyamaka’s critique of intrinsic nature and phenomenology’s intertwining have different questions and arguments.

His word flesh—la chair—explores a manner of being. It is neither muscle alone nor a gigantic body hidden behind everything. The perceiving and the sensible belong together without one swallowing the other.

Emptiness and flesh can meet in a reading. Letting them illuminate one another is more interesting than hastily declaring them the same.

VI. Merleau-Ponty and the Others in This Round

Comte values observation; Merleau-Ponty prompts a further question: how does observing happen? At the microscope stands no placeless consciousness, but a situated body that learns and tires. This adds a question rather than asserting that Comte never considered the observer.

Fichte asks about agency; Merleau-Ponty moves the camera closer. How does this I reach, turn, enter a room? Philosophy’s first person acquires the habits and posture of a life.

Weil asks the possessive self to quieten, not the body to disappear. Attending to someone, you still breathe, tire, approach or retreat. Habits can change or be lost. The point is that attention is never a view without a body.

Strawson resists conceptually assembling a person from two parts; Merleau-Ponty examines embodied experience. These entrances are neighbors. We need not invent their complete agreement.

Vygotsky brings shared activity into development. Holding a child’s hand while they learn to walk is visible bodily cooperation; speech, gesture and tools can also support learning. What later writers call scaffolding is neither abstract explanation alone nor only bodily pulling.

VII. Dying in Front of Descartes

On May 3, 1961, Merleau-Ponty died suddenly, aged fifty-three. Familiar biographical accounts place an open Descartes volume on his desk during his last work. We can retain that suggestive image without making the cause of death or tomorrow’s lecture into established details.

The book on the desk?

The volume is usually identified as Descartes’ Optics.

A thinker who argued with Descartes remained a reader of Descartes. The image moves us not because a death proves one philosophy right, but because disagreement can sustain a conversation for a lifetime.

Claude Lefort edited the unfinished manuscript and working notes published in 1964 as The Visible and the Invisible. Arendt would later leave Judging unwritten. We may read these interruptions together without claiming to know that their most important thought necessarily lay in the work they could not complete.

Some unfinished books offer no closing answer. They leave a question room to grow.

VIII. The Bridgehead

On our invented bridge, Merleau-Ponty approaches. The first thing you notice is not what he is thinking, but how he walks.

His walk is natural. Unforced. Each step lands firmly — not "thought" into place, but bodied into place. His center of gravity tilts slightly forward. His arms swing easily. He doesn't look at his feet — his body knows the floor is there.

He steps onto the bridge.

He sees the floor Strawson has just laid. Strawson said "person is primitive" — can't be split into body and mind. Merleau-Ponty walks over and feels the floor with his foot.

Not with his eyes. With his foot.

His foot feels texture, hardness, a slight give. These need not first become propositions in order to enter his experience.

He sees McClintock crouching, studying corn. He recognizes her — not because he knows her, but because he recognizes the bodily relationship with the object. Her posture bent over the microscope — that is not "an observer looking at an object." That is one body intertwining with another life.

He sees Fichte standing on the crack. Fichte says "the I posits itself." Merleau-Ponty walks over and stands beside him. He says nothing. He just stands there. His body beside the crack. His body knows the crack is there — not because it sees it, but because the sole of his foot feels it.

He finds a spot on the bridgehead. Not a special one. He stands there. His body is there.

The world is not what he thinks. The world is what he lives.

He can feel the wind. Wind is not a concept. Wind is a sensation on skin. He can feel the bridge's faint vibration — other people's footsteps transmitted through the surface to his soles. He can feel the temperature changing — the sun is going down.

These things have already entered his life without needing to be individually named.

He stands there. The world is on him. He is inside the world.

Not an absence of boundaries. Boundaries that do not exile us from the world.[1][2][3]
