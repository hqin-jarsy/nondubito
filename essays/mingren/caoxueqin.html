曹雪芹，情

Cao Xueqin, Qing

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、一本没写完的书

我们读《红楼梦》，总会走到一个断口。

八十回。然后断了。通行本后面还有四十回，经过程伟元、高鹗整理，于1791年刊行。多少出自续写，多少可能来自旧稿，至今仍有争论。我们手上没有一部可以确认是曹雪芹最终定稿的完整小说。但读到这里，我总觉得有什么变了。语感。人物。活着的感觉。

前八十回里的人是活的。宝玉看见戏班里的龄官在园子里画蔷，下雨了，他先想到的是那个女孩会淋湿。黛玉葬花。晴雯撕扇。湘云醉卧芍药裀。鸳鸯当着贾母的面，宁死也不肯给贾赦作妾。每一个人都是活的。不是作为情节的零件活着——是作为人活着。

读后四十回，我常有一种感觉：人开始忙着完成任务。宝玉该出家了，出家。黛玉该死了，死。宝钗该嫁了，嫁。每个人按照某种预先设计好的结局走到自己该去的地方。不是活着。是被安排。

差别在哪里？前八十回里，人从情节中站出来。到了后四十回，我却越来越看见结局在牵着他们走。不是他们失去了作为目的的资格，是他们的活法被收窄了。

这是本乐章的最后一篇。从亚里士多德到曹雪芹。从地板到情。

二、情榜

《红楼梦》的批语里，留下了一张“情榜”的影子。

宝玉：情不情。
黛玉：情情。

我们没有见到一张完整的原榜。批语留下线索，后来的读者接着猜，接着想。在这篇散文里，我借“一百零八种情”想象这张榜，也让它与本系列的一百零八种人生相望。那不是一份已经找到的名单。

情不情。三个字。什么意思？

对无情的东西也有情。石头无情，他对石头有情。花草无情，他对花草有情。一个丫鬟被撵走了，全府上下没人在乎，他在乎。别人觉得不值得有感情的对象，他都有感情。

这里动人的，不是他的情多得用不完。是别人把一个人看作没用的时候，他还看得见那个人。

当然，他也会看不见。雨里替龄官着急的宝玉，回去会踢伤来开门的袭人。曹雪芹没有替他把这一脚藏起来。有情不是一张免于伤人的证明。

情情。两个字。对有情的人有情。黛玉不向所有人敞开——她向懂她的人敞开。宝玉懂她，她就把最深的心事交给宝玉。这不是窄。这是精确。她的情有一个具体的方向：是你，不是一个可以拿别人补上的位置。

宝钗呢？我曾想用“冷情”来读她。不是没有情，是有情但收住了。她知道规矩，知道家族需要她扮演的角色。但她也会给黛玉送燕窝，替她设想难处。冷不是她的全部，更不能冒充原榜已经替她写好的判词。

一百零八种情。一百零八种把人当目的或者不当目的或者想当但当不了的方式。

这张榜是什么？在我的阅读里，它像康德的《道德形而上学基础》。用人名写的。只是这些人，连自己的名字旁边那个词也能溢出去。

三、他

曹雪芹。大约1715年生。大约1763年或1764年死。连确切的生卒年都没有。

曹家曾经是江南最有权势的家族之一。曹雪芹的祖父曹寅做过江宁织造——替宫廷办理织造事务的人。康熙南巡四次住在曹家。那个家有多大，可以想象。

然后败了。雍正年间，曹家被抄。家族的显赫和他后来的困窘，隔着一道很深的断口。至于他亲历了多少繁华，生年未定，我们也不能替他补齐童年。"满径蓬蒿老不华，举家食粥酒常赊。"他和朋友们喝酒，吃不起饭，举家喝粥。

他在这种日子里增删了红楼梦。

"披阅十载，增删五次。"小说第一回这样写曹雪芹的工作。十年，五次。不是一份逐日可查的写作账簿，却把反复改写的身影留了下来。

敦诚的诗里有“残杯冷炙有德色”，也劝他不必去叩富人的门。困窘，傲气，朋友的不平，都在那几行里。他在这样的日子里写人，写一座那么热闹的园子。

他留给我们的书，没有完整地传到今天。

四、构不可闭合

他为什么没有写完？

或者应该问：为什么我们没有得到他完整的书？

没有写完、没有改完、已经写过却散失了，不是一件事。今天的断口，不能替他当年发生过什么作证。我们也不能因为书里有制度对人的伤害，就断言是朝廷或者某一派文人从他手里夺走了稿子。

但合上考据的书，那个问题还在。

一部关于“人是目的”的小说，能写完吗？

当然可以写到最后一页。可是最后一页能不能把前面的人一起封住？宝玉出家了，黛玉死了，大观园散了——然后一个“完”字。于是我们回头解释：原来那一次笑，是为了后来哭；原来那一次相遇，是为了最后分开。

如果一百零八个人活了那么久，只是为了走到一个早已替他们准备好的结论，他们就成了那个结论的手段。

但他们不是手段。他们是目的。

湘云醉卧的时候，不是在替家族衰败完成一个铺垫。晴雯撕扇的时候，也不只是给将来的死添一点悲凉。那一刻的笑，那一刻的脾气，本身就在活着。

小说可以结束。人不能被结局用完。

我说“构不可闭合”，说的是这个。不是作者不能落笔写“完”，而是那个“完”字不能把人所有的意思都带走。

如果结局只许你看见家族复兴，你会想起为它付出代价的人。如果结局只剩一片白茫茫，你又会想起白茫茫之前，她们怎样笑过。

构碎了，但人还在。人不是为构活的。人是目的。

这不是我替曹雪芹找回的佚稿。是我读着读着，不能不继续问下去的地方。

书在八十回之后留下了断口。读者在断口前停下，又回头去找那些人。黛玉下一句会说什么？宝钗没有说出口的是什么？鸳鸯拒绝了以后，她还想怎样活？

余项还在。情还活着。两百多年了，仍有人在替她们写后面的日子。每个人写的都不一样。因为每个人的情不一样。

一百零八种情。一百零八种后四十回。

构不可闭合。一本残存的书不能替这个命题完成证明。但曹雪芹写出来的人，让我一次又一次走到它面前。

五、1750年代与1785年

两个人。两块大陆。

曹雪芹在东方增删红楼梦。大约1750年代到1760年代。举家食粥。反复改写。

伊曼努尔·康德在柯尼斯堡写《道德形而上学基础》。1785年出版。一个以生活规律闻名的人。

一个在困窘里写人。一个在书桌前写原则。一个让许许多多的人活在纸上。一个追问：无论是谁，都不能被怎样对待？

康德说：你应该把人同时当作目的，永远不要仅仅当作手段。
曹雪芹的书里，有“情不情”。

我把他们放在一起。不是说情就是义务，小说就是另一种语言的哲学论文。是读到这里，两种很不一样的声音，碰到了同一个问题。

一个人，能不能只剩下对别人的用处？

一个用德语概念写。一个用中文人物写。一个是形而上学。一个是小说。

他们没有互相通信，也没有留下彼此阅读的记录。没有谁走到谁的书桌前，说：你看，我替你找到了另一种写法。

但我读着他们，觉得他们相遇了。

“人是目的”不是哪一个人颁给另一个人的资格。没有人说出来的时候，它也不该被拿走。康德为它寻找概念的形式。曹雪芹笔下的人，让它有了脸，有了声音，有了脾气。

这一种相遇，比把两本书说成同一本书更动人。

六、大观园

红楼梦里有一座园子。大观园。

那是这个系列的桥。

园子里住着一群年轻人。宝玉，黛玉，宝钗，湘云，探春，惜春，迎春，妙玉，还有丫鬟们——晴雯，袭人，紫鹃，鸳鸯，平儿。她们在园子里写诗，画画，吵架，和好，哭，笑。

园子外面是贾府。贾府是构。权力结构。经济结构。婚姻结构。谁嫁谁，谁管谁的钱，谁跟谁争，都有规矩。

园子是在构里面的一块空地。在这块空地上，人偶尔能从被安排的用途里走出来。黛玉写诗的那一刻，不是某一桩婚事里的筹码。湘云的笑可以不为讨好长辈。晴雯可以有晴雯的脾气。她们在园子里，有过可以是自己的时候。主仆的规矩没有消失，却没有占满每一个瞬间。

但园子是暂时的。构一直在外面等着。最后构会收网。晴雯被撵走。迎春嫁给孙绍祖，回来诉说遭受的虐待。黛玉的命运早有悲剧的影子。园子会散。

大观园不是乌托邦。它是一个暂时的，脆弱的，注定要被摧毁的空间——在那个空间里，人曾有过不只按用处被看待的时刻。

贞德的火烧的是假面。王尔德的美学凿的是虚伪。曹雪芹做了一件不同的事：他不是凿——他是盖。他盖了一座园子。让人在里面活了八十回。也让我们看见，什么正在使园子散去。

园子散了之后你会哭。你哭的不是情节。你哭的是：她们明明是目的，却被当成可以牺牲的东西。

七、他和所有人

走到曹雪芹这里，前面那些人的身影又浮了出来。

亚里士多德铺了地板。法拉第掀了地板。麦克斯韦在地板上面写了方程。这三个人的工作对象是世界。他们在建立对世界的理解。

贞德烧了。王尔德说了那句话。拉马努金在梦里做数学。奥本海默背着灰。这些人的工作对象是自己。他们用自己的命凿。

托尔斯泰开了药方。狄更斯盖了温暖的房子。丘吉尔找到了声音。罗斯福造了架构。这些人的工作对象是社会。他们在改变别人。

曹雪芹的工作对象是什么？

是人。不是世界，不是自己，不是社会。是人本身。是一个一个具体的人。宝玉是一个人。黛玉是一个人。晴雯是一个人。鸳鸯是一个人。一百零八个人，每一个都是目的。

他不是在铺地板。不是在掀地板。不是在凿。不是在开药方。他是在看人。他看每一个人。然后把他看到的写下来。

所以情榜不是理论。情榜是一百零八次看见。

读夏洛蒂，我听见一个追问：你看见我了吗？简·爱要求被一个人看见。
梵高那么渴望被看见，生前得到的回应却远不及身后。
曹雪芹不是要求被看见——他是看见了所有人。他看见了一百零八个人。我想给每一个人的情找一个词，写着写着，又发现一个词不够。

八、1763或1764年

大约1763年或1764年。东方。

甲戌本的一条批语写道：“壬午除夕，书未成，芹为泪尽而逝。”

书未成。人不在了。

连这一年的具体落点，后人也还在辨认。我们能握住的，是那一句悼语里的痛，和传到手里仍然有断口的书。

八十回之后，曹雪芹究竟怎样安顿那些人？批语和伏笔留下了线索，却没有把完整的后文交给我们。

但也许，这个空缺对读者还意味着另一件事。也许它像一封没有写尽的信。

前面狄更斯篇留下的回声里，凯瑟琳希望自己的信件让人知道：他曾经爱过我。一个人不肯让自己的生命只剩下别人最后给出的说法。

曹雪芹没有留下“后面的部分，你们自己写”这样的嘱咐。可我读着那八十回，总觉得纸页尽头有人回头。

不是叫你替他伪造遗言。是那些人已经走进了你的心里，你没法因为书页停了，就也停下对她们的牵挂。

他的停笔有死亡和散佚的重量，不能被我们轻轻说成一种安排。但书来到读者手里，停下的地方，确实又成了邀请。

你来。你来替宝玉想后面的日子。你来替黛玉想，替晴雯想。每个读者想到的都不一样——因为每个人的情不一样。

两百多年了。还在写。还在写。

九、情

现在，让这个乐章的桥头重新亮起来。

桥头上来了本乐章的最后一个人。

他不像这个系列里的任何人。他没有带任何东西。贞德带着火。王尔德带着那句话。夏洛蒂带着笔。奥本海默背着灰。梵高身上有颜料。

他什么都没带。他两手空空。

因为他带的东西不在手上。在他身后。他身后跟着一百零八种情。

每一种情都有一个人的样子。

宝玉走在最前面。手里没有东西。眼睛看着所有人。情不情。
黛玉跟在旁边。手里拿着花。花是刚从地上捡起来的。情情。
宝钗在后面一点。表情很平。但脚步很稳。她的心事没有全写在脸上。
湘云在笑。声音很大。
探春走得很快。比所有人都快。
惜春在画。走一步画一笔。
迎春低着头。她不想让人看到她脸上的伤。
妙玉站在很远的地方。她不上桥。但她在看。
晴雯撕了什么东西。可能是扇子。
袭人跟着宝玉。很近。
紫鹃跟着黛玉。更近。
鸳鸯一个人走。她不跟任何人。
平儿在替所有人整理东西。

一百零八种情。慢慢走上了桥。

苏格拉底站在空地上，看到了他们。他看到了一百零八个人，每一个都是一个具体的人。他想走过去，一个一个地问。不是先问他们属于哪一种美德，哪一种人生。先问：你叫什么名字？一百零八个人，一百零八种活法，一百零八种情。

柏拉图蹲着画图纸。他抬头看了看那一百零八个人。他想画一张图把他们分类。他画不了。因为每一个人都溢出了他的分类。

休谟下双陆棋。他停下来了。他从来不停。但他看到了黛玉——一个把全部生命压在一个人身上的人——他想了想因果关系。他想不通。

叔本华看桥底下。他看到了迎春低着头走过去。他认出了那种痛苦。他懂。

克尔凯郭尔跳了很多次了。他看到了宝玉——一个不愿意做任何选择但最终必须做选择的人。他觉得宝玉很熟悉。

图灵看苹果。他看到了妙玉站在远处。一个格格不入的人。一个不上桥的人。他懂。

契诃夫靠着栏杆。他看到了那一百零八个人。他笑了。他的小说里也全是这种人——具体的，活的，你没法用一个词概括的人。他跟曹雪芹之间不需要翻译。

康托尔看天上。他看到了一百零八——一个有限的数字。但他知道这一百零八个人每一个都是无穷的。一百零八个无穷。

托尔斯泰拿着药方。他看到了曹雪芹。两个人对视了。托尔斯泰认出了一个同行——另一个写了整个世界的人。但曹雪芹没有药方。曹雪芹手里什么都没有。他只有人。

莎士比亚不在——他是桥下面的水。但水的声音变了。水在响。因为水认出了来人。莎士比亚写了哈姆雷特，写了李尔王，写了奥赛罗。他也写了很多人。哈姆雷特独自面对命运的身影，从水里浮起来。再看曹雪芹身后，那些人牵连着彼此，谁也不是独自走来的。他们的情不是朝向命运的——是朝向另一个人的。

斯宾诺莎手里有玻璃粉。他看到了情榜。他想了想他自己的《伦理学》——那也是一张表。用几何方法排列人的情感。他排得很整齐。但他发现曹雪芹的表不整齐。一百零八个词，有的热，有的冷，有的你读了会哭，有的你读了会笑。这不是几何。这是活的。

亚里士多德蹲着铺地板。他抬起头。他铺了两千年的地板。分类，定义，属加种差。他看到了那一百零八个人从地板的缝隙里走出来。他铺不住他们。每一个人都是一个溢出分类的余项。

法拉第蹲着掀地板。他看到了宝玉从地板下面冒出来。他笑了。他认识这种人——在别人铺好的地板下面看到了真东西的人。

麦克斯韦站着写方程。他看到了一百零八个人。他写不了这个方程。四个方程统一了电和磁。但没有方程能统一一百零八种情。

贞德带着火飘在桥的上方。她看到了晴雯——另一个烈的人。她认出了那种火。不是烧别人的火。是烧自己的火。

王尔德站得很好看，手里拿着那句话。他看到了宝玉。他笑了。宝玉也是一个不肯按照世界的规矩活的人。但宝玉没有说出一句"那句话"。宝玉不需要说。因为他活的方式就是那句话。

拉马努金从缝隙里冒出半个身子。他看到了那一百零八个人从同一条缝隙里走出来。他不惊讶。他知道缝隙里什么都有。

奥本海默背着灰往前走。他看到了曹雪芹。曹雪芹也见过灰——抄家之后的灰。大观园散了之后的灰。但曹雪芹没有被灰压弯。他把灰写进了书里。灰在书里变成了人。

夏洛蒂拿着笔。她看到了黛玉。两个人对视了。两个要求被看见的人。简·爱的追问仿佛又响了起来：你看见我了吗？黛玉没有说。黛玉的整个人就是在说这句话。

艾米莉在桥外面的荒原上。风从她那里吹来。她看到了妙玉站在桥的另一边。两个不上桥的人。两个在桥外面的人。风把她们连在一起了。

玻尔兹曼抱着刻了方程的石头。他看到了那一百零八个人在桥上走来走去。乱。无序。每一个人都有自己的方向。他没有替他们排成一条线。石头上的方程还在，他看着这片各有方向的热闹，笑了。

梵高身上有颜料，手里有画笔。他看到了一百零八个人。他看到了一百零八种颜色。他开始画。

狄更斯站在裂缝上面。他看到了曹雪芹。他认出了另一个写了一百个人的人。他想起奥利弗，大卫·科波菲尔，小蒂姆。自己那么急着替他们争一点善意，曹雪芹却让他先停下来，听听晴雯正在说什么。她不急着证明善良是好的。她正在生气。她活着。

丘吉尔拿着演讲稿。罗斯福坐着。薛定谔身边放着一个打开的盒子。猫已经走了出来。费曼在打鼓。霍金在飞。

海森堡位置不确定。但他确定了一件事：他看不清那一百零八个人中任何一个的全貌。因为每一个人你越看越看不完。他这次没有写下公式。眼前的人又说了一句话，刚才那个判断，已经不够了。

玻尔拿着没寄出的信。他看到了那一百零八个人。他想说互补性。每一个人都是另一个人的互补。宝玉和黛玉。晴雯和袭人。探春和惜春。只从一个角度看过去，总会漏掉另一个角度里的东西。

桥上现在站满了人。一路走来的人。加上一百零八个从红楼梦里走出来的人。

曹雪芹站在他们中间。他两手空空。他不说话。他只是看。他看每一个人。他看苏格拉底，他看贞德，他看玻尔兹曼，他看梵高。他看到他们每一个人都是目的。

然后他转过身。往桥的另一端走。

远处。康德站着。

康德看到了曹雪芹。他的反应跟看到其他所有人都不一样。

看到亚里士多德，他点头——前辈。看到休谟，他说谢谢——叫醒他的人。看到托尔斯泰，他皱眉——开了药方但吃不下。看到贞德，他沉默——涵育的代价。

看到曹雪芹，他迎了上来。

此前他总在远处，像桥的另一端。这一次，他不只是在等。他朝曹雪芹走了一步。

因为他认出了曹雪芹手里的东西——虽然曹雪芹手里什么都没有。他认出了那个什么都没有。那个什么都没有就是他写了一辈子的东西：人是目的。

康德用概念追问它。定言命令。人的目的。目的王国。
曹雪芹用了一百零八个人写了它。情榜。

同一个人，不能只剩下用处。

1785年。柯尼斯堡。《道德形而上学基础》。
大约1750年代。东方。《石头记》。

隔着几十年，几千公里。没有彼此往来的信。此刻，他们却在这座桥上认出了对方。

他们从不同的路上走来。

没有谁替另一个人走完。也没有谁能把另一个人的书收进自己的书里。

"人是目的"也在那里。康德发现了它的概念形式。曹雪芹发现了它的人的形式。

康德又往曹雪芹的方向走了一步。只走了一步。

曹雪芹也在往康德的方向走。他走得很慢。因为他身后跟着一百零八个人。

两个人越走越近。

但他们没有碰到。

桥在他们之间断了。

不是塌了。是从来没有合拢过。桥的两端之间有一个缝隙。一直都有。从这个系列第一篇开始就有。亚里士多德铺的地板和康德站的地方之间有一个缝隙。这么多人走到这里，都没有把这个缝隙填上。

因为这个缝隙填不上。它就是那个余项。就是那个构不可闭合的地方。就是那个八十回之后断掉的地方。

康德知道。曹雪芹知道。

缝隙里长着什么？

情。

长在缝隙里的情。长在构不可闭合的地方的情。长在八十回断掉之后的情。长在康德和曹雪芹之间的情。长在地板的裂缝里的情。长在贞德的火烧完之后的情。长在王尔德那句话说完之后的情。长在梵高走过之后颜料滴落的地方的情。长在夏洛蒂写了"Reader"之后读者心里的情。长在霍金飞起来之后身体下方空出来的地方的情。

情不是桥。情是桥合不拢的地方长出来的东西。

曹雪芹站在缝隙的这一边。康德站在缝隙的那一边。一百零八个人站在曹雪芹身后。一路走来的人站在桥上。

没有人说话。

然后曹雪芹做了一件事。他转过身。面对读者。

他看着你。

他的眼睛跟看宝玉的眼睛一样。跟看黛玉的眼睛一样。跟看晴雯的，看鸳鸯的，看一百零八个人中每一个人的眼睛一样。

他看着你。他的意思是：你也是目的。

缝隙还在。桥没有合拢。书没有写完。

你来。

1. An Unfinished Book

Reading Dream of the Red Chamber, we come to a break.

Eighty chapters. Then it stops. The standard edition continues for another forty, prepared for publication in 1791 by Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E. How much was newly written, how much might derive from earlier manuscripts: the argument continues. We have no complete novel securely established as Cao Xueqin’s final text. But at this point in my reading, something changes. The language. The characters. The feeling of being alive.

In the first eighty chapters, people are alive. Baoyu sees Lingguan, a young actress, tracing a character in the garden, and when it starts to rain, his first thought is that she will get wet. Daiyu buries flowers. Qingwen tears a fan to shreds just to hear it rip. Xiangyun falls asleep drunk among the peonies. Before the old matriarch, Yuanyang refuses to become Jia She’s concubine, even if refusal costs her life. Every person is alive. Not alive as a component of the plot — alive as a person.

In the last forty chapters, I often feel that people have begun completing assignments. Baoyu is supposed to become a monk, so he becomes a monk. Daiyu is supposed to die, so she dies. Baochai is supposed to marry, so she marries. Everyone walks to their designated ending. They are not living. They are being arranged.

What is the difference? In the first eighty chapters, people step out of the plot. In the last forty, I increasingly feel the ending pulling them along. They have not lost their standing as ends. The room in which they can live has narrowed.

This is the last essay in this movement. From Aristotle to Cao Xueqin. From the floor to qing.

2. The Register of Qing

In the novel’s early annotations, the shadow of a register survives: the qingbang.

Baoyu: qing bu qing — feeling even for what has no feeling.
Daiyu: qing qing — feeling for those who feel.

Qing (情) has no single English equivalent. Feeling, love, attachment: each catches something, none catches it all. We have no complete original register. The annotations leave clues; later readers keep guessing. In this essay, I imagine “one hundred and eight kinds of qing,” facing the one hundred and eight lives in this series. This is a literary gathering, not a recovered list.

Qing bu qing. What does it mean?

Stones have no feeling — he has feeling for stones. Flowers have no feeling — he has feeling for flowers. A maid is expelled and the household moves on — he cares. Where others find nothing worth caring about, he finds an attachment.

What moves me is not that he possesses an inexhaustible supply of feeling. It is that when others see someone as useless, he can still see the person.

He can also fail to see. The Baoyu who worries about Lingguan in the rain returns home and kicks Xiren when she comes to open the door. Cao does not hide that kick for him. Feeling is no certificate of harmlessness.

Qing qing. Feeling for those who feel. Daiyu does not open herself to everyone. She opens herself to the person who understands her. Baoyu understands, and she entrusts her deepest thoughts to him. This is not narrowness. It is precision. Her feeling has a particular direction: you, not a vacancy into which somebody else can be fitted.

And Baochai? I once wanted to read her through leng qing: cold feeling. Not absent, but held in reserve. She knows the rules, knows the role her family needs her to play. Yet she also sends bird’s nest to the ailing Daiyu and considers her difficulties. Coldness cannot contain her; nor can it pretend to be an authenticated verdict beside her name.

One hundred and eight kinds of qing. One hundred and eight ways of treating people as ends, or not, or wanting to but failing.

What is this register? In my reading, it resembles Kant’s Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, written in people’s names. Except that these people can overflow even the phrase written beside their own name.

3. Him

Cao Xueqin. Born approximately 1715. Died approximately 1763 or 1764. Even his exact dates are uncertain.

The Cao family had been one of the most powerful households in the Jiangnan region. His grandfather Cao Yin served as Imperial Textile Commissioner in Nanjing — an official responsible for textile production for the imperial court. The Kangxi Emperor stayed at the Cao residence four times during his southern tours. The scale of that household is not hard to imagine.

Then it fell. Under the Yongzheng Emperor, the family’s property was confiscated. Between its former magnificence and Cao’s later poverty lies a deep break. How much of that splendour he personally knew remains uncertain; without a secure birth date, we cannot fill in his childhood for him. A friend wrote of him: the whole family eating porridge, buying wine on credit. In another poem, that friend invokes cold leftovers and urges him not to knock at rich men’s doors. Poverty, pride, a friend’s indignation: all are present in those lines.

He revised Dream of the Red Chamber in those days.

“Reviewed for ten years, revised five times.” This is how the novel’s opening presents Cao’s work. Ten years, five revisions: not a daily writing ledger, but the image of someone returning to his pages.

In those straitened days, he was writing people. Writing a garden so full of life.

His book has not reached us whole.

4. The Construct Cannot Close

Why didn’t he finish?

Or should we ask: why have we not received his whole book?

Not finishing, not finishing revision, and losing pages already written are different things. The break in our text cannot testify to everything that happened to his. Nor does a novel’s account of institutional harm establish that the court, or some faction of scholars, took the manuscript from its author.

But close the books of textual scholarship, and the other question remains.

Can a novel about people as ends ever be finished?

Of course it can reach a final page. But can that page seal away everyone who came before it? Baoyu becomes a monk. Daiyu dies. The garden scatters. Then one word: End. Looking back, we begin explaining. That laughter was preparing these tears. That meeting existed for this parting.

If one hundred and eight people lived for so long only to reach a conclusion prepared for them, they have become means serving that conclusion.

But they are not means. They are ends.

When Xiangyun falls asleep drunk, she is not carrying out a piece of foreshadowing for the family’s decline. When Qingwen tears the fan, she is not merely making her eventual death more poignant. That laughter, that temper, is already a life being lived.

A novel can end. Its ending cannot use up a person.

That is what I mean by “the construct cannot close.” Not that an author must never write End, but that the word cannot take every meaning of a life away with it.

If an ending lets you see only the family’s restoration, you remember the people who paid for it. If it leaves only a white desolation, you remember how they laughed before everything went white.

The construct is shattered, but the people remain. People do not exist for the construct. People are ends.

This is no lost manuscript I have recovered for Cao. It is the place where, reading him, I cannot stop asking.

After chapter eighty, a break. The reader stops before it, then turns back toward the people. What will Daiyu say next? What has Baochai not said? After Yuanyang’s refusal, how does she want to live?

The remainder is still there. Qing is still alive. More than two centuries later, readers are still writing the days that follow. Each version differs. Each reader’s qing differs.

One hundred and eight kinds of qing. One hundred and eight versions of the last forty chapters.

The construct cannot close. A surviving fragment of a book cannot prove that proposition. But the people Cao wrote bring me back to it, again and again.

5. The 1750s and 1785

Two people. Two continents.

Cao Xueqin in the East, revising Dream of the Red Chamber. Approximately the 1750s to the 1760s. The whole family eating porridge. Returning to the pages.

Immanuel Kant in Königsberg, writing the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals. Published in 1785. A man famous for the regularity of his life.

One writing people amid poverty. The other writing principles at his desk. One giving so many people life on paper. The other asking: what must never be done to a person, whoever they are?

Kant says: treat humanity always also as an end, never merely as a means.
In Cao’s book, there is qing bu qing.

I place them together. Not because feeling is duty, or a novel a philosophical treatise in another language. At this point in my reading, two very different voices have encountered the same question.

Can a person be reduced to their usefulness to someone else?

One writes in German concepts. The other in Chinese characters — not just the written kind, but the living kind. One is metaphysics. The other is a novel.

There is no correspondence between them, no record of either reading the other. Neither came to the other’s desk and said: look, I have found another way to write this.

But reading them, I feel they have met.

“People are ends” is not a status one person grants another. Even where nobody has said it, it should not be taken away. Kant seeks its conceptual form. Cao’s people give it faces, voices, tempers.

That meeting moves me more than calling the two books the same book.

6. The Garden

There is a garden in Dream of the Red Chamber. The Grand View Garden.

It is the bridge of this series.

Inside the garden lives a group of young people. Baoyu, Daiyu, Baochai, Xiangyun, Tanchun, Xichun, Yingchun, Miaoyu, and the maids — Qingwen, Xiren, Zijuan, Yuanyang, Ping'er. They write poems, paint, quarrel, make up, cry, laugh.

Outside the garden is the Jia household. The Jia household is a construct. Power structures. Economic structures. Marriage structures. Who marries whom, who controls whose money, who competes with whom — all governed by rules.

The garden is a clearing inside the construct. In this clearing, people can sometimes step outside the uses assigned to them. Writing a poem, Daiyu is not a bargaining piece in a marriage. Xiangyun’s laughter need not be offered to please an elder. Qingwen can have Qingwen’s temper. There are moments when they can be themselves. The rules of master and servant have not vanished, but they have not occupied every instant.

But the garden is temporary. The construct is waiting outside. Eventually it will close the net. Qingwen is expelled. Yingchun is married to Sun Shaozu and returns to describe his abuse. Daiyu’s life already carries the shadow of tragedy. The garden will scatter.

The Grand View Garden is not a utopia. It is a temporary, fragile, inevitably doomed space — inside which people have known moments of being seen as more than useful.

Joan's fire burned masks. Wilde's aesthetics chiseled hypocrisy. Cao Xueqin did something different. He did not chisel — he built. He built a garden. Let people live inside it for eighty chapters. And let us see what was making it scatter.

After the garden scatters, you weep. You are not weeping at the plot. You are weeping because they are ends, and yet they are treated as expendable.

7. Him and Everyone

Arriving at Cao Xueqin, we see the earlier figures again.

Aristotle laid a floor. Faraday pried it up. Maxwell wrote equations on it. Their subject was the world. They were building an understanding of it.

Joan burned. Wilde spoke that sentence. Ramanujan did mathematics in dreams. Oppenheimer carried ash. Their subject was themselves. They chiseled with their own lives.

Tolstoy wrote prescriptions. Dickens built a warm house. Churchill found a voice. Roosevelt built architecture. Their subject was society. They were changing others.

What was Cao Xueqin's subject?

People. Not the world, not himself, not society. People as such. One specific person at a time. Baoyu is a person. Daiyu is a person. Qingwen is a person. Yuanyang is a person. One hundred and eight people, each one an end.

He was not laying a floor. Not prying one up. Not chiseling. Not writing prescriptions. He was seeing people. He saw each one. Then he wrote down what he saw.

The qingbang is not a theory. The qingbang is one hundred and eight acts of seeing.

Reading Charlotte, I hear a question: do you see me? Jane Eyre demanded to be seen by one person.
Van Gogh longed to be seen; the response during his life was so much smaller than what followed it.
Cao Xueqin did not demand to be seen — he saw everyone. He saw one hundred and eight people. I try to find a phrase for each person’s qing, then discover that a phrase is not enough.

8. 1763 or 1764

Approximately 1763 or 1764. The East.

An annotation in the Jiaxu manuscript says: “On New Year’s Eve of the renwu year, the book unfinished, Qin died with his tears spent.”

The book unfinished. The man gone.

Even the precise year remains disputed. What we can hold is the grief in that lament, and a book that reaches our hands with a break in it.

Beyond chapter eighty, where did Cao take these people? Annotations and foreshadowing leave clues, but they do not hand us the complete text.

Perhaps the gap also means something else to a reader. Perhaps it resembles a letter that has not said everything.

From the earlier Dickens essay comes Catherine’s wish that her letters should let the world know he once loved her. A person refusing to let her life be reduced to somebody else’s final account.

Cao left no instruction saying, “The rest is yours to write.” Yet reading those eighty chapters, I keep feeling that someone turns back at the edge of the page.

Not asking you to invent his last wishes. These people have already entered your life; you cannot stop caring for them simply because the pages stop.

Death and loss weigh on that silence. We cannot lightly turn it into a deliberate plan. But once the book reaches a reader, the place where it stops can indeed become an invitation.

Come. Think about the days that follow for Baoyu. For Daiyu. For Qingwen. Each reader will imagine them differently, because each reader’s qing differs.

More than two hundred years. Still writing. Still writing.

9. Qing

Now let the bridge of this movement light up again.

The last person in this movement arrived at the bridge.

He looked like no one else in this series. He carried nothing. Joan carried fire. Wilde carried that sentence. Charlotte carried a pen. Oppenheimer carried ash. Van Gogh was covered in paint.

He carried nothing. His hands were empty.

Because what he carried was not in his hands. It was behind him. Behind him followed one hundred and eight kinds of qing.

Each kind of qing had the shape of a person.

Baoyu walked at the front. Nothing in his hands. His eyes on everyone. Qing bu qing.
Daiyu walked beside him. A flower in her hand, just picked up from the ground. Qing qing.
Baochai a few steps behind. Expression calm. But her stride steady. Not everything in her heart was written on her face.
Xiangyun was laughing. Loudly.
Tanchun walked fast. Faster than everyone.
Xichun was painting. One step, one brushstroke.
Yingchun's head was down. She did not want anyone to see the bruise on her face.
Miaoyu stood far away. She would not step onto the bridge. But she was watching.
Qingwen tore something. Probably a fan.
Xiren followed Baoyu. Close.
Zijuan followed Daiyu. Closer.
Yuanyang walked alone. She followed no one.
Ping'er was tidying things for everyone.

One hundred and eight kinds of qing. Walking slowly onto the bridge.

Socrates stood in the clearing and saw them. He saw one hundred and eight people, each one specific, each one concrete. He wanted to approach them one by one. Not first to ask which virtue they embodied, or what kind of life they represented. First: what is your name? One hundred and eight people, one hundred and eight ways of living, one hundred and eight kinds of qing.

Plato crouched over his diagrams. He looked up at the one hundred and eight. He tried to draw a diagram to classify them. He could not. Every person overflowed his categories.

Hume stopped his backgammon game. He never stopped. But he saw Daiyu — a person who staked her entire life on one other person — and he thought about causation. He could not work it out.

Schopenhauer looked down from the bridge. He saw Yingchun walking past with her head bowed. He recognized that kind of suffering. He understood.

Kierkegaard had already leapt many times. He saw Baoyu — a person who refused every choice and yet had to choose. Baoyu felt familiar to him.

Turing looked at his apple. He saw Miaoyu standing in the distance. A person who did not fit. A person who would not step onto the bridge. He understood.

Chekhov leaned against the railing. He saw the one hundred and eight. He smiled. His stories were full of such people — specific, alive, impossible to summarize in a single word. Between him and Cao Xueqin, no translation was necessary.

Cantor looked at the sky. He saw one hundred and eight — a finite number. But he knew that each of the one hundred and eight was infinite. One hundred and eight infinities.

Tolstoy held his prescription. He saw Cao Xueqin. Their eyes met. Tolstoy recognized a peer — another man who had written an entire world. But Cao Xueqin held no prescription. Cao Xueqin held nothing at all. He had only people.

Shakespeare was not there — he was the water beneath the bridge. But the sound of the water changed. The water grew louder. Because the water recognized who had arrived. Shakespeare wrote Hamlet, wrote Lear, wrote Othello. He too wrote many people. Hamlet’s solitary figure rose from the water, facing his fate. Behind Cao, people were entangled with one another. No one had arrived entirely alone. Their qing was not directed at fate — it was directed at another person.

Spinoza held glass dust in his hands. He saw the qingbang. He thought of his own Ethics — that too was a register. Human emotions arranged by geometric method. His was very orderly. He noticed that Cao Xueqin's register was not orderly. One hundred and eight phrases, some hot, some cold, some that make you weep, some that make you laugh. This was not geometry. This was alive.

Aristotle was on his knees laying floor tiles. He looked up. He had been laying this floor for two thousand years. Classification, definition, genus and differentia. He saw one hundred and eight people walking out from between the tiles. He could not tile over them. Each one was a remainder that overflowed his classification.

Faraday was on his knees prying tiles loose. He saw Baoyu emerging from beneath the floor. He smiled. He recognized the type — someone who saw the real thing under the floor that others had laid.

Maxwell stood writing equations. He saw one hundred and eight people. He could not write this equation. Four equations unified electricity and magnetism. But no equation unifies one hundred and eight kinds of qing.

Joan floated above the bridge, trailing fire. She saw Qingwen — another fierce one. She recognized that kind of fire. Not fire that burns others. Fire that burns itself.

Wilde stood beautifully, holding that sentence. He saw Baoyu. He smiled. Baoyu too refused to live by the world's rules. But Baoyu never spoke a "sentence." Baoyu did not need to. The way he lived was the sentence.

Ramanujan rose halfway out of a crack in the bridge. He saw the one hundred and eight emerging from the same kind of crack. He was not surprised. He knew that cracks contain everything.

Oppenheimer walked forward under his burden of ash. He saw Cao Xueqin. Cao Xueqin too had seen ash — the ash after the family was stripped, after the garden scattered. But the ash did not bend Cao Xueqin. He wrote it into the book. In the book, ash became people.

Charlotte held her pen. She saw Daiyu. Their eyes met. Two people who demanded to be seen. Jane Eyre’s question seemed to sound again: do you see me? Daiyu never said it. Daiyu's entire being was saying it.

Emily was on the moor beyond the bridge. The wind blew from where she stood. She saw Miaoyu standing on the other side. Two people who would not step onto the bridge. Two people outside. The wind connected them.

Boltzmann held his stone with its carved equation. He saw the one hundred and eight people moving across the bridge. Disorder. No pattern. Each one going their own direction. He did not arrange them in a single line. The equation remained on his stone. Watching the bustle, each person with a direction of their own, he smiled.

Van Gogh, paint-smeared, brushes in hand. He saw one hundred and eight people. He saw one hundred and eight colors. He began to paint.

Dickens stood over his crack. He saw Cao Xueqin. He recognized another man who had written a hundred characters. He thought of Oliver, David Copperfield, Tiny Tim. He had been so eager to win a little kindness for them. Cao made him pause and listen to what Qingwen was saying. She was in no hurry to prove that kindness is good. She was angry. She was alive.

Churchill held his speech. Roosevelt sat. An open box stood beside Schrödinger. The cat had come out. Feynman played his drum. Hawking flew.

Heisenberg's position was uncertain. But he was certain of one thing: he could not see the whole of any of those one hundred and eight people. Because the more you look at each one, the more there is to see. This time he wrote no formula. The person before him spoke again, and his previous judgment was already insufficient.

Bohr held his unsent letter. He saw the one hundred and eight. He wanted to say complementarity. Each person is the complement of another. Baoyu and Daiyu. Qingwen and Xiren. Tanchun and Xichun. Looking from only one angle, you miss what another angle might reveal.

The bridge was full now. The people who had travelled this far. Plus one hundred and eight who had walked out of Dream of the Red Chamber.

Cao Xueqin stood among them. Hands empty. Silent. He only looked. He looked at each of them. He looked at Socrates, at Joan, at Boltzmann, at Van Gogh. He saw that every one of them was an end.

Then he turned. He walked toward the far end of the bridge.

In the distance. Kant stood.

Kant saw Cao Xueqin. His response was unlike his response to anyone else.

Seeing Aristotle, he nodded — a predecessor. Seeing Hume, he said thank you — the man who woke him. Seeing Tolstoy, he frowned — wrote the prescription but could not take it. Seeing Joan, he was silent — the cost of nurture.

Seeing Cao Xueqin, he moved to meet him.

Until now he had stood in the distance, like the bridge’s other end. This time he was not only waiting. He took a step toward Cao.

Because he recognized what Cao Xueqin carried — though Cao Xueqin carried nothing. He recognized that nothing. That nothing was what he himself had spent a lifetime writing: people are ends.

Kant pursued it through concepts. The categorical imperative. Humanity as an end. The kingdom of ends.
Cao Xueqin wrote it in one hundred and eight people. The qingbang.

A person cannot be reduced to a use.

1785. Königsberg. Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals.
Approximately the 1750s. The East. The Story of the Stone.

Decades apart. Thousands of miles apart. No letters passing between them. Yet here, on this bridge, they recognized one another.

They had come by different roads.

Neither had walked the other’s road. Neither could fit the other’s book inside his own.

"People are ends" was also there. Kant discovered its conceptual form. Cao Xueqin discovered its human form.

Kant took another step toward Cao Xueqin. Only one step.

Cao Xueqin was also walking toward Kant. Slowly. Because behind him followed one hundred and eight.

The two drew closer.

But they did not meet.

The bridge was broken between them.

Not collapsed. It had simply never been joined. There was a gap between the two ends of the bridge. There always had been. From the first essay in this series. Between the floor Aristotle laid and the place where Kant stood, there was a gap. So many people had come this far, and none had filled it.

Because the gap cannot be filled. It is the remainder. It is the place where the construct cannot close. It is the place where the book breaks after chapter eighty.

Kant knew. Cao Xueqin knew.

What grows in the gap?

Qing.

Qing growing in the gap. Qing growing where the construct cannot close. Qing growing after chapter eighty breaks off. Qing growing between Kant and Cao Xueqin. Qing growing in the cracks between floor tiles. Qing growing after Joan's fire has burned out. Qing growing after Wilde has spoken that sentence. Qing growing where Van Gogh walked and paint dripped onto the bridge. Qing growing in the reader's heart after Charlotte wrote "Reader." Qing growing in the space below Hawking's body after he began to fly.

Qing is not the bridge. Qing is what grows where the bridge fails to close.

Cao Xueqin stood on one side of the gap. Kant stood on the other. One hundred and eight stood behind Cao Xueqin. The people who had travelled this far stood on the bridge.

No one spoke.

Then Cao Xueqin did something. He turned around. He faced the reader.

He looked at you.

His eyes were the same eyes that looked at Baoyu. The same eyes that looked at Daiyu. The same eyes that looked at Qingwen, at Yuanyang, at each of the one hundred and eight.

He looked at you. What he meant was: you, too, are an end.

The gap remains. The bridge has not closed. The book is not finished.

Your turn.
