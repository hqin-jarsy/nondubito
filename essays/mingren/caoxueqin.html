曹雪芹，情

Cao Xueqin, Qing

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、一本没写完的书

我们习惯说，《红楼梦》没有写完。

这句话里，其实叠着几件不同的事：作者有没有写到结尾，有没有完成最后的修改，写成的部分有没有传下来。它们不能互相代替。我们手里没有一部能够确认为曹雪芹最终定稿的完整长篇；早期抄本与后来的一百二十回印本，也不能当成同一条没有断口的河。

1791年，程伟元、高鹗整理的一百二十回本刊行。他们在序言及翌年的引言里说明搜集、拼合、修订的经过。那是编者关于自身工作的陈述，不是一张能替后四十回每一页确认作者身份的证明。续写、整理、旧稿的可能成分，至今仍是版本研究的问题。

可是，读到前八十回，我们已经遇见了一群不能轻易交还给概括的人。

第三十回，一个女孩蹲在花阴下，用簪子在地上反复画“蔷”字。她是戏班里的龄官，心事牵着贾蔷。宝玉看着她，猜她心里正在怎样熬煎。雨下来了。他提醒她衣裳湿了，直到她反问，才发现自己也淋得透湿。

不是一项功绩。没有因此挽救家族，也没有完成什么宏大的使命。

一个人看见另一个人，把自己的湿衣服忘了一会儿。

还有黛玉葬花，晴雯撕扇，湘云醉卧芍药裀，鸳鸯不肯给贾赦作妾。葬花的不是一幅“多愁善感”的插图，撕扇的不是一条“任性”的注解。她们各有话，各有脾气，也各有不能替换的位置。

但不能只选温柔的一面。同一回里，宝玉回到怡红院，因迟迟没人开门，抬脚踢伤了来开门的袭人。刚才替陌生女孩担心的人，现在伤了身边的人。

这本书没有替他把两件事剪开。

这些人物常常从我们给他们准备好的解释里走出来，连“有情”这样好听的解释，也不能替他们结案。读到温柔的地方，还得记得那一脚。

书的传承有断口。

人也没有被我们一句话说完。

那是两种不同的未完。

二、情榜

脂批提到过“情榜”。

第十九回的庚辰本夹批留下两条评语：“宝玉情不情”，“黛玉情情”。但是，一张包含全部名字与评语的原始情榜，并没有完整地摆在我们面前。三十六、六十、一百零八，都是相关讨论里出现过的规模；不能选中一个数字，便说找到了曹雪芹亲笔盖章的总名册。

“情不情”，可以读成把情意给到不能回应情意之处。落花不会感谢，燕子不会答礼，雨中的一个陌生人也没有义务以同样的关心回报你。

“情情”，可以读成对有情者的深切回应。但黛玉不只是一双对着宝玉流泪的眼睛。她会作诗，会开玩笑，会误会别人，也会承认自己曾经误会。第四十五回，她向宝钗敞开了寄人篱下的难处；宝钗听了，提出送燕窝，免得她再去惊动贾府里的人。

这样的宝钗，一个“冷”字装不下。她听见黛玉难以开口的地方，也替她想到下一次请求之前的为难。

懂得规矩的人，也可能在规矩之间替另一个人留一点方便。接受照顾的人，也可能重新认识照顾她的人。不是一个“冷”字翻成“暖”字便算读懂；是两个字都不够。

宝玉尤其不能被“情不情”免检。

第十九回，他希望袭人的漂亮亲戚也属于自家。袭人问他，难道自己做奴才，亲戚也该做奴才？他喜欢众人相聚，却还要学习：不愿分离，不等于有权留下别人。

情可能使人走近，也可能使人攥紧。

情榜若有用，不该是把活人钉进格子的榜。它应该让我们回到那个人面前，多看一次。

三、他

曹雪芹自己的轮廓，反而比许多人物模糊。

生年有1715、1724等不同说法；卒年常在1763、1764之间讨论。曹家曾与江宁织造的职务、宫廷的供应和南方的繁华紧密相连，后来遭到抄没。1728年前后的家族败落，是生平重建中的重要节点；不能据此精确补出他当时的年龄、看见的每个场面。

小说不是家谱的逐页复印件。

但一个家为什么曾经那么热闹，又为什么会忽然冷下来，是读这本书时无法绕开的经验。

朋友敦诚在《赠曹芹圃》中写他：“满径蓬蒿老不华，举家食粥酒常赊。”另一首《寄怀曹雪芹（霑）》里，有“残杯冷炙有德色，不如著书黄叶村”。

前一句把困窘留在诗里。后一句的“德色”，不是夸他吃剩饭也保持美德，而是写施舍者把一点残羹当作恩惠的脸色。朋友劝他，不必总去叩那些门。

第一回说曹雪芹“披阅十载，增删五次”。那句话在石头、道人、传抄与题名层层交错的叙述里，并不是一份可以逐日对账的工时记录。

不过，修改这个动作留了下来。

一次不够，再看一次。

写宝钗，不能只写宝玉怎样看她。写袭人，不能只写她怎样照顾宝玉。写一大家子人的衰败，也不能让每个人都只是“衰败”这两个字的证人。

贫穷没有替他完成这些选择。苦难不自动生成好小说，更不因为生成了好小说而值得感谢。

在失去之中，他仍然做了这份工作。

四、结局之后

翻到最后一页，我们往往想把前面的一切重新理顺：原来那次相逢是为了分别，那场笑是为了后来的哭。知道了结局，仿佛也就知道了每一天的用途。

《红楼梦》很早就让结局的影子出现。第五回的《飞鸟各投林》已经唱到“落了片白茫茫大地真干净”。读者带着这句曲词，再回到那些还不知道明天的人中间。

曹雪芹会构造。场面彼此照应，名字牵着诗，笑声背后提前响着哭声。可人物活在其中，不只是为了替预言一一应验。

龄官在雨里写字的那一刻，有她自己的时间。

最后一页不能反过来宣布，一个人此前全部的生活，不过是为这个结局服务的零件。

婚姻不能概括一个女人。失败不能概括一种才华。家族的终局也不能把花阴下一次没有听众的出神，改写成可有可无的过场。

一部小说可以写完。读者对其中一个人的认识，还可以改。

传本残缺并不证明这条原则。即使将来找到了可靠的完整稿，这个问题仍然存在：我们会不会因为终于知道结局，就以为终于拥有了那个人？

五、1750年代与1785年

把曹雪芹与康德并置，是这组文章的读法，不是十八世纪两位作者共同署名的宣言。

一边，是十八世纪中叶已经进入写作、修改与传抄过程的小说。另一边，是1785年出版的《道德形而上学奠基》。康德要求：无论对自己还是他人，都要把人性同时当作目的，绝不仅仅当作手段。

“仅仅”不能删。

人可以合作，可以彼此帮助，可以请另一个人为一件事出力。问题不在于他对你有没有用，而在于有用之外，他自己的目的是否还算数。

园中的饭要有人做，衣裳要有人缝。把劳动写进来，不是取消目的；把做饭、缝衣的人只剩下用处，才是我们要追问的事。

康德寻求可以普遍要求的原则。小说则让我们进入特殊的情境，遇见偏爱、嫉妒、体谅、误会和欲望。情不是义务的同义词，动情也不保证判断正确。宝玉踢伤袭人的那一脚，不能被他对龄官的怜惜抵销。

这两条路不能直接画成等号。

但它们可以在这里彼此发问：你承认的是抽象的“人”，还是眼前这个不肯按你的期待生活的人？你说你爱她，是否也容得下她不属于你？

是让原则走到一张具体的脸前，让动人的故事也经受原则的追问。

六、大观园

大观园不是从地上凭空长出来的自由。

它为元妃省亲而建，后来成为宝玉与姊妹们生活、作诗、读书的一处所在。墙里面的时间，靠墙外也靠墙里面的收入、劳动、服侍与等级维持。

有人在花下睡着，另有人守着什么时候该送饭。

所以它不是一个已经实现的目的王国。主子和丫鬟的区别一直存在，长辈的命令也并没有在园门前失效。连最可爱的相聚，也可能把不愿留下的人算进“永远不要散”的愿望。

可是，这不等于那些相聚都是假的。

一个人可以在尚不自由的生活里，度过一刻没有被用处完全占据的时间。诗社没有废除等级，但写下的一首诗不必只供家族增光；朋友来看病，也不必只是完成一项人情手续。

园子的可贵，正在这样的片刻里。它的不可靠，也一直在。

抄检之后，晴雯被逐；迎春的婚姻把她带进另一种无法自己作主的处境。第八十回已经写到她回娘家诉说丈夫的凌虐，不必借后来的死亡情节，才知道伤害已经发生。

被赶出去的人，并没有从目的变成东西。

是她原本应得到的承认和保护，被人拿走了。

因此，不能只为园子唱挽歌。还要问：园子里谁有钥匙，谁只能等人开门，谁一旦不被喜欢，便失去留下的资格？

有情的地方，也仍然需要自我约束。

七、他和所有人

写到这里，可以回头看看桥上那些人。

这是本系列的桥，不是历史上的一场聚会。石头、火、信、画布、鼓、轮椅，是前面人物画中留下的道具。它们帮助相认，不负责把一生缩成一件物品。

我们曾沿着科学家的工作追问世界怎样成立，沿着作家与思想者的工作追问自己怎样生活。现在，曹雪芹把一群人带了过来。

契诃夫写过那么多人，狄更斯也写过那么多人。莎士比亚的人物活在爱、误会与争执之间。桥上已经有许多声音，新来的人又带来自己的。

他的不同，在这次相遇的姿势里。

夏洛蒂·勃朗特那支笔，可以在这里被听成一个问题：你看见我了吗？梵高的画布可以把问题继续留下：你看见的，是价格，是传说，还是画前这一次不能被人代看的颜色？

曹雪芹身后的人，不只等作者来回答。黛玉会回看宝钗，晴雯会顶撞宝玉，袭人会提醒他，亲近不是所有权。

作者给人物声音，不是从此替他们说所有的话。

读者也一样。

喜欢一个人，不必删掉她难相处的地方。理解一个人，不等于批准他做过的一切。承认一个人是目的，尤其不是等他符合了我们的善良标准，才颁发给他的一张奖状。

八、1763或1764年

连他离开的年份，也没有完全停止争论。

甲戌本第一回的一条眉批说：“壬午除夕，书未成，芹为泪尽而逝。”这首先是批者的悼语，不是曹雪芹的遗言。按纪年，壬午岁末落在公历1763年；另一条常用的生平路线把去世放在1764年。不能把两条路线揉成一个貌似精确的日子。

我们不知道他最后放下笔时，心里是不是还有下一句。

不知道，便让这句话停在那里。

遗憾可以就是遗憾。

但在留下来的书里，许多生活仍然发生。每次有人重新读到那场雨，龄官的簪子又在地上动；每次读到宝钗探病，黛玉就还有一次改变看法的机会。

前篇里凯瑟琳留下的信，也让一个现成的故事重新打开。读到她自己的声音，关于她的一切就不能只由别人来讲。

今天的我们可以续写，可以争论，可以提出一种新的结局。但应当把自己的笔迹留下：这是我们的想象，不是刚刚找回的曹雪芹。

没有原稿，不等于没有约束。

有了原稿，也不等于不再需要判断。

九、情

现在让传记停下，让桥上的寓言继续。以下不是《红楼梦》佚稿，也不是人物死后的历史。

曹雪芹来了。

宝玉先走近，回头看大家有没有跟上。黛玉没有立刻催他，只看了他一眼。宝钗带着一点预备送人的东西，并不急着解释。湘云笑得比脚步还快。探春先看桥怎样搭的，惜春停下来，想把那道桥影画进纸里。

迎春走得慢。没有人拿她的受伤要求她证明坚强。

妙玉站远一点。这里不以走近人群作为合格的条件。晴雯的一句话已经到了人前；袭人听完，另有她自己的话。紫鹃陪着黛玉，却不必永远只剩一个“陪”字。鸳鸯不必再说明为什么拒绝。平儿先放下手里的忙碌，站一会儿。

康德从桥边向前一步，给他们让出地方。

他们不必先经过一场考试，才可以来。

苏格拉底从空地上看过来。这次不用问出一个大家都同意的答案，便能先为迎春挪开脚边的石头。柏拉图把图纸展开，给惜春看；她不必把自己的园子画成他的。休谟把双陆棋的一颗棋子掉在脚边，湘云笑着替他拾起来。

叔本华本来望着桥下，现在望见走得很慢的人。痛苦不是供他验证的一句话。克尔凯郭尔看见宝玉停下来，没有替他喊“跳”；那个选择还应由他自己做。

图灵把苹果放在一旁，给妙玉留了一段不被围观的距离。契诃夫扶着栏杆，看晴雯与袭人说话，没有急着把谁分到可爱的一边、可责的一边。她们不是一道只准勾选一个答案的题。

康托尔数着来的人，听见一个名字，就抬头看一张脸。托尔斯泰把手里的处方折起来，先听曹雪芹说。莎士比亚仍是桥下面的水；新来的声音落下去，水声也变了。

斯宾诺莎袖口还沾着磨镜片的细尘。宝钗看见他，他也看见宝钗；几何式的写法，并没有使相遇只能发生在图形中。亚里士多德整理着地板，腾出一块可以落脚的地方，不要求大家先归进同一个类别。

法拉第从地板下托住一处接缝，让走得慢的人也能过去。麦克斯韦在旁边留出图纸的空边：方程处理它能处理的问题，不替园中的人编排谁该爱谁。

贞德身旁仍有前篇留下的火影。没有人说，是火造就了她值得被看见的资格。王尔德说了一句话，晴雯先笑了；她并不因此变成他的漂亮句子的例证。

拉马努金从缝隙另一边露出身影。他带来的仍是数学问题，不是替所有心事揭晓谜底的钥匙。奥本海默看见曹雪芹袖上的灰；在这幅画里，两种失去可以相望，不必被说成同一场灾难。

夏洛蒂把笔递到黛玉能够接到的地方。黛玉未必要写别人希望读到的爱情。艾米莉·勃朗特从荒原上走来，见妙玉还没有进来，便也不催她。

玻尔兹曼把刻着公式的石头挪开一点，让人坐。梵高给桥影留了一笔颜色，惜春看着，拿出了自己的笔。狄更斯在一旁听那些姓名，又把目光移到说话的人脸上。

丘吉尔收拢讲稿，给正要开口的人让出声音。罗斯福坐在桥的一侧，平儿在旁边歇脚。薛定谔抱着猫，猫朝人群看了一眼，又把头收回他的臂弯。

费曼的鼓响了一下。湘云转过头，笑声接了过去。霍金从上空看着他们。他身旁那些托住、照料他的手，也还在画面里。

海森堡看着人们一边说话一边让出位置。玻尔把没有寄出的信留在手边，听了一会儿，还有一句话想问。

康德没有在最后给曹雪芹盖一个“证明了我”的印章。

曹雪芹也没有说，自己已经把在场的人都写完。

宝玉还可能犯错。宝钗还有没有被说出的难处。妙玉可以继续站远一点。平儿这次可以先休息。

情不是把所有人拥进自己的怀里。

是伸出去的手，遇见另一只手时，也承认对方可以不握。

这个字不替一切作保证。它仍然可能偏爱，可能误认，可能伤人。正因此，看见别人不是一次成功之后便可终身免修的本领。

桥要有人继续修。栏杆不能因为我们赞美缝隙而省去。

但修桥不是把两岸的人浇成同一块石头。

书可以有最后一页。

人不必有最后一种解释。

你也不是为了给这个系列作结，才来到这里。

你也是目的。

缝隙还在。桥没有合拢。书页在这里停下。

你来。

I. An unfinished book

We are used to saying that Dream of the Red Chamber was never finished.

Several different uncertainties shelter inside that sentence. Did the author reach the ending? Did he finish revising it? Did everything he wrote survive? One question cannot answer the others. We do not possess a complete text securely established as Cao Xueqin’s final manuscript. The early manuscript tradition and the later, printed novel of 120 chapters are not one uninterrupted river.

In 1791, Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E published a 120-chapter edition. Their prefaces, and a further statement the following year, describe collecting, assembling and editing material. These are the editors’ accounts of their work, not certificates establishing the authorship of every page. The respective contributions of continuation, revision and earlier material remain questions for textual scholarship.

Yet within the first eighty chapters we have already met people whom a summary cannot easily take back.

In Chapter 30, a girl sits beneath flowering branches, tracing the same character in the earth with a hairpin: qiang, “rose.” She is Lingguan, a young performer; Jia Qiang is the person on her mind. Baoyu, the young heir of the Jia household, watches, wondering what could be hurting her so much. Rain begins. He warns her that she is getting wet. Only when she asks about his shelter does he notice that he, too, is soaked.

No achievement is recorded. No family has been saved. No great mission advances.

Someone sees someone else, and for a moment forgets his own wet clothes.

There is Daiyu burying fallen flowers; Qingwen tearing fans; Xiangyun asleep among fallen peony petals; Yuanyang refusing to become Jia She’s concubine. These are not illustrations labelled “sensitivity,” “temper” or “defiance.” Each woman has a voice, a disposition, a place no other person can occupy for her.

But we cannot keep only the tender scene. In that same chapter, Baoyu returns home, grows angry at being kept outside, and kicks Xiren when she opens the door. The boy who worried about a stranger has hurt someone close to him.

The novel does not cut the two scenes apart to protect him.

These characters keep stepping outside the explanations we prepare for them. Even qing, that generous-sounding word, cannot close their cases. Reading the tender scene, we still have to remember the kick.

There is a break in the transmission of the book.

There is also something in a person that our account has not exhausted.

Those are two different kinds of incompletion.

II. The register of qing

Early annotations mention a qingbang: a register of qing.

The word qing can carry feeling, affection, attachment and responsiveness. “Love” catches part of it, but not the whole. A comment preserved in the Gengchen manuscript tradition, attached to Chapter 19, gives two assessments: Baoyu is qing bu qing; Daiyu is qing qing. We do not, however, have the complete original register with all its names and descriptions. Reconstructions have proposed thirty-six, sixty or 108 figures. Choosing one number does not turn it into an authenticated list in Cao’s hand.

Qing bu qing: feeling extended even toward what does not return feeling. A flower cannot thank you. A swallow cannot repay your courtesy. A stranger in the rain owes you no equal measure of concern.

Qing qing: a deep response to another being who feels. But Daiyu is not simply a pair of eyes weeping toward Baoyu. She writes poems, makes jokes, misjudges people and sometimes admits that she has misjudged them. In Chapter 45 she tells Baochai how vulnerable she feels as a dependent in the Jia household. Baochai listens and offers to send edible bird’s nest, sparing her another request to the household.

“Cold” cannot hold this Baochai. She hears what Daiyu finds hard to say, and anticipates the difficulty of having to ask again.

A person who understands the rules may also find a way between them to make someone’s life easier. A person receiving care may discover that she has misunderstood the person offering it. Changing the label from “cold” to “warm” is not enough. Neither word is large enough.

Nor does qing bu qing exempt Baoyu from scrutiny.

In Chapter 19 he wishes that Xiren’s attractive relative belonged to his household too. Xiren asks whether, because she herself is a servant, her relatives must be servants as well. He wants everyone to remain together. He still has something to learn: not wanting to part is not the same as having the right to keep someone.

Feeling can bring a person closer. It can also tighten a grip.

A register worth reading would not pin living people into compartments. It would send us back to look at them again.

III. The man

Cao Xueqin’s own outline is less distinct than those of many of his characters.

1715 and 1724 are among the proposed years of his birth; 1763 and 1764 figure prominently in discussions of his death. His family had been closely involved in the imperial textile administration at Jiangning, present-day Nanjing. Its position connected it to court provision and the prosperity of the south. Confiscation and the family’s collapse around 1728 are important points in reconstructions of his life. They do not tell us his exact age then, or furnish a record of every scene he witnessed.

The novel is not a page-for-page copy of a family archive.

But how a house can become so full of life, and then so suddenly fall quiet, is an experience the reader cannot avoid.

His friend Duncheng wrote, in a poem addressed to Cao under the name Qinpu, of weeds crowding the path, a household living on gruel and wine bought on credit. In another poem, To Cao Xueqin, he contrasts the condescending charity of cold leftovers with writing in the village of yellow leaves.

The first preserves an image of poverty. The second does not praise Cao for eating scraps virtuously. Its point is the benefactor’s air of having bestowed a great favour. A friend is telling him that he need not keep knocking at those doors.

Chapter 1 says Cao read and revised the work over ten years, making five rounds of additions and deletions. But the statement belongs to a layered narrative of a stone, a wandering reader, copying and changing titles. It is not a timesheet we can reconcile day by day.

What remains visible is the act of revision.

Once was not enough. Look again.

Writing Baochai cannot mean writing only what Baoyu thinks of her. Writing Xiren cannot mean describing only how she cares for him. Writing a household’s decline cannot make every person merely a witness called to prove “decline.”

Poverty did not make those choices for him. Suffering does not automatically produce a good novel, and producing one would not make the suffering deserving of our gratitude.

Amid loss, he continued this work.

IV. After the ending

At the last page, we often want to put everything in order. The meeting was preparing the separation; the laughter was preparing the tears. Knowing the ending, we seem to know what every earlier day was for.

The novel lets the shadow of an ending appear early. Chapter 5’s closing song already imagines a vast white earth left utterly clean. Carrying that line, the reader returns to people who do not yet know tomorrow.

Cao knew how to construct. Scenes answer one another, names catch in poems, crying sounds behind laughter. Yet the people living there do more than fulfil predictions.

Lingguan tracing a character in the rain has a time of her own.

The final page cannot turn everything a person lived into a component serving the ending.

A marriage cannot summarize a woman. Failure cannot summarize a talent. A family’s fate cannot make a private reverie beneath flowering branches a disposable interval.

The writing of a novel may end. Our understanding of a person within it may still change.

The missing manuscript does not prove this principle. Even a securely authenticated complete text would leave the question standing: once we finally knew the ending, would we imagine that we finally owned the person?

V. The 1750s and 1785

Placing Cao beside Kant is a reading proposed by this series, not a declaration jointly issued by two eighteenth-century authors.

On one side, a novel being written, revised and circulated in manuscript in the middle of the century. On the other, the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, published in 1785. Kant asks that humanity, in oneself and in every other person, always be treated at the same time as an end, never merely as a means.

“Merely” matters.

People can cooperate, assist one another and ask one another to do things. The question is not whether someone is useful to you. It is whether, beyond that usefulness, the purposes of their own life still count.

Someone must cook the garden’s meals. Someone must sew its clothes. To acknowledge labour does not cancel the idea of ends. To leave the workers nothing but their usefulness is what calls for examination.

Kant seeks principles that can make universal demands. The novel enters particular situations: preference, jealousy, consideration, misrecognition, desire. Feeling is not another name for duty; being moved does not guarantee a sound judgment. Baoyu’s tenderness toward Lingguan cannot offset his kick to Xiren’s ribs.

These two paths cannot simply be joined by an equals sign.

They can question each other here. Do you acknowledge an abstract “human being,” or this particular person who refuses to live as you expect? When you say you love her, is there room for her not to belong to you?

It is a principle brought before a particular face, and a moving story made answerable to a principle.

VI. The garden

The Garden of Grand Prospect did not grow out of the earth as freedom fully formed.

Built for the imperial consort Yuanchun’s visit to her family, it later became a place where Baoyu and the young women lived, wrote poems and read. Its sheltered hours depended on income, service, labour and hierarchy, both inside and outside its walls.

Someone sleeps among flowers. Someone else keeps track of when the food must arrive.

This is not a kingdom of ends already achieved. The distinction between masters and servants remains. The commands of elders do not cease to operate at the gate. Even the most lovable gathering may include unwilling people in its wish never to break up.

That does not make every gathering false.

Within a life that is not free, a person can still have a moment not wholly occupied by usefulness. The poetry club does not abolish hierarchy; a poem need not therefore exist only to honour the family. A visit to someone ill need not be the fulfilment of a social obligation and nothing more.

The garden’s value lives in these moments. Its insecurity never leaves.

After the search of the garden, Qingwen is expelled. Yingchun’s marriage carries her into another situation she cannot govern. By Chapter 80, she has already returned to her family and described her husband’s abuse. We do not need the later account of her death to know that harm has occurred.

The person expelled has not ceased to be an end and become a thing.

The recognition and protection she should have received have been withdrawn.

So an elegy for the garden is not enough. Who has the keys? Who must wait for a door to open? Who loses the right to remain as soon as affection is withdrawn?

A place with feeling still needs restraint.

VII. He and all the others

We can turn now toward the people on the bridge.

It is this series’ bridge, not the site of a historical gathering. Stone, fire, letters, canvases, a drum, a wheelchair: these are objects retained from earlier portraits. They help people recognize one another. They are not authorized to reduce a life to a single possession.

Through scientists’ work we have asked how the world holds together; through writers and thinkers, how a person might live. Now Cao arrives with a company.

Chekhov wrote so many lives; so did Dickens. Shakespeare’s people lived amid love, misunderstanding and quarrels. The bridge already holds many voices. The new arrivals bring their own.

His difference lies in the manner of this arrival.

Charlotte Brontë’s pen may be heard here as a question: Have you seen me? Van Gogh’s canvas leaves a further question: what are you seeing—a price, a legend, or the encounter with colour that nobody can have on your behalf?

The people behind Cao do not simply wait for their author to answer. Daiyu can look back at Baochai. Qingwen can contradict Baoyu. Xiren can remind him that intimacy is not ownership.

Giving a character a voice does not entitle the author to say everything for her thereafter.

Nor does reading entitle us to do so.

Liking someone need not erase what makes her difficult. Understanding someone does not approve everything he has done. Recognition as an end is not a prize handed out after a person has met our standards of goodness.

VIII. 1763 or 1764

Even the year of his departure has not fallen silent.

A marginal annotation associated with the Jiaxu manuscript says that, on the last night of the renwu year, the book remained unfinished and Cao died with his tears exhausted. It is first of all an annotator’s lament, not Cao’s last words. The end of that lunar year falls in the Gregorian year 1763. Another frequently adopted chronology places his death in 1764. We cannot merge the two into one apparently exact date.

We do not know whether he had another sentence in mind when he last put down his pen.

Let that uncertainty stand.

A loss can remain a loss.

Yet many lives still take place in the surviving book. Whenever someone reads the rain scene, Lingguan’s hairpin moves again. Whenever someone reads Baochai’s visit, Daiyu has another opportunity to change her mind.

Catherine’s letters in the earlier portrait reopened a story that had seemed settled. Once her own voice is heard, others can no longer do all the telling.

We may write continuations, argue, propose different endings. But we should leave our signatures visible. This is our imagining, not Cao newly recovered.

The absence of an original does not abolish responsibility.

The discovery of one would not abolish judgment.

IX. Qing

Let biography stop here, and the bridge’s fable continue. What follows is neither a recovered chapter nor a history of the dead.

Cao Xueqin arrives.

Baoyu approaches first, looking back to see who is coming. Daiyu does not hurry him; she only glances at him. Baochai carries something she has thought to bring for another person and feels no urgency to explain it. Xiangyun’s laughter travels ahead of her. Tanchun examines the bridge’s construction; Xichun pauses, wanting to draw its shadow.

Yingchun walks slowly. Nobody uses her injuries to demand a demonstration of strength.

Miaoyu remains a little apart. Joining the crowd is not an entrance requirement. Qingwen’s words arrive before she does; Xiren has something of her own to say in reply. Zijuan accompanies Daiyu without having to be reduced forever to “the one who accompanies.” Yuanyang need not explain her refusal again. Ping’er puts down her work and stands still for a while.

Kant takes a step forward from the side of the bridge and makes room.

There is no examination to pass before they can come.

Socrates looks over from the clearing. He can move a stone out of Yingchun’s path before everyone has agreed on a definition. Plato unfolds a drawing for Xichun; she need not redraw her garden as his. Hume drops a backgammon piece at his feet. Xiangyun laughs and picks it up for him.

Schopenhauer has been looking below the bridge. Now he sees the person walking slowly. Her pain is not a sentence supplied to confirm him. Kierkegaard sees Baoyu hesitate and does not call out “Jump.” The choice should remain Baoyu’s.

Turing sets his apple aside, leaving Miaoyu some space in which she will not be stared at. Chekhov rests a hand on the rail and listens to Qingwen and Xiren. He does not rush to assign one to the lovable side and one to the blameworthy side. They are not a question permitting only one box to be ticked.

Cantor counts the arrivals, looking up at a face with each new name. Tolstoy folds his prescription and listens to Cao. Shakespeare is still the water beneath the bridge; the new voices reach it, and its sound changes.

There is lens-grinding dust at Spinoza’s cuff. Baochai sees him, and he sees Baochai; geometrical exposition has not restricted encounters to geometrical figures. Aristotle tidies the floor and clears a place to stand, without first requiring everyone to enter the same category.

From below, Faraday supports a joint so that those who move slowly can cross too. Maxwell leaves a margin on the plans. Equations can address their proper questions without assigning whom the people from the garden must love.

A trace of fire from her earlier portrait remains beside Joan of Arc. No one claims that the fire created her right to be seen. Wilde says something, and Qingwen laughs first; she does not thereby become an example belonging to his elegant sentence.

Ramanujan appears from the far side of a gap. He still brings mathematical questions, not a key to every private sorrow. Oppenheimer notices ash on Cao’s sleeve. In this picture, two kinds of loss can face one another without becoming the same catastrophe.

Charlotte puts her pen within Daiyu’s reach. Daiyu need not write the love story somebody else hopes to read. Emily Brontë comes from the moor, sees that Miaoyu has not joined them and does not press her.

Boltzmann shifts the stone bearing his formula to make room for someone to sit. Van Gogh adds color to the bridge’s shadow; Xichun watches, then takes out her brush. Dickens listens to the names, then looks at the people speaking them.

Churchill gathers his speech notes, leaving room for the next voice. Roosevelt sits to one side, and Ping’er rests nearby. Schrödinger holds the cat; it glances at the company, then settles its head into his arm.

Feynman’s drum sounds once. Xiangyun turns, her laughter answering it. Hawking looks down from above. The hands that support and care for him remain in the picture.

Heisenberg watches people making room as they talk. Bohr keeps the unsent letter beside him. He listens for a while; there is still something he wants to ask.

Kant does not finish by stamping Cao’s book “Proof of my philosophy.”

Cao does not announce that he has finished writing everyone present.

Baoyu may still do wrong. Baochai still has difficulties no one has heard. Miaoyu may remain at a distance. This time, Ping’er may rest first.

Qing is not gathering everyone into your own embrace.

It is an outstretched hand acknowledging, on meeting another hand, that the other may choose not to take it.

The word guarantees nothing by itself. Feeling may favour, misread or injure. That is why seeing another person is not an accomplishment that excuses us from trying again.

Someone must keep repairing the bridge. Admiration for its gaps is no reason to dispense with railings.

But building a bridge does not mean casting the people on both banks into the same stone.

A book may have a final page.

A person need not have a final explanation.

You have not come here merely to give this series an ending.

You, too, are an end.

The gap remains. The bridge has not closed. The pages stop here.

Come.
