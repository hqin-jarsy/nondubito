鲁米，被凿成歌的人

Rumi, Carved into Song

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、学者

沙姆斯来之前，鲁米首先是一位学者。后来让世人记住他的那片诗海，还没有展开。

他生于1207年，名叫贾拉勒丁·穆罕默德。家族与巴尔赫相连；他的出生地究竟是巴尔赫还是瓦赫什，研究中仍有不同说法。“鲁米”则指向他后来生活的鲁姆——安纳托利亚，那片曾属于东罗马的土地。

父亲巴哈丁·瓦拉德是一位讲道者和宗教学者。家族经过多次迁居向西，最后落脚科尼亚，鲁姆塞尔柱王朝的重要城市。政治与宗教的紧张、辗转的居所，以及蒙古扩张的阴影，都在这段漫长旅途的背景里。

父亲去世后，鲁米承接了教导者的位置，又继续求学和苏菲修习。他熟悉经文、教法与神学，有学生，有家庭，有需要照料的日常。

如果借本系列的语言说，他有构：知识、职位、传承，以及别人走近时已经知道该怎样称呼他的名字。

但那不是一块等待被砸碎的死物。多年读书和修习，后来仍会进入他的诗。沙姆斯改变的，不是让一个有学问的人突然变得一无所知。

他让这个已经有位置的人，重新不知道自己将走向哪里。

二、沙姆斯

1244年，三十七岁的鲁米在科尼亚遇见沙姆斯·大不里兹。

沙姆斯是四处行旅的苏菲。他的谈话由追随者记下，传为《言谈录》（Maqalat）；其中有经文、争辩，也有对同伴的强烈要求。

后来的传记喜欢把相遇写成一道闪电：沙姆斯拦住坐骑上的鲁米，问一个关于先知与神秘家的问题；学者震动，甚至跌落或昏厥。不同版本给出了不同动作。那是传记传统为转折画出的形状，不是一份现场速记。

可以确定的是，这段交往深刻改变了鲁米的生活。两人长时间相处，原有的讲学节奏受到冲击，周围也出现了嫉妒与敌意。两人之间的热度，传到了他们所在的房间之外。

沙姆斯究竟碰到了什么？也许是知识与经验之间，那个不能只靠多读一本书跨过去的距离。

用本文的比喻说：可以把火的性质讲得很清楚，手掌仍然不知道它的热。这是我们靠近那次相遇的一种方式。

“凿”发生在这里：不是凿掉《古兰经》，不是凿掉理性，而是使“我已经知道”的外壳出现开口。书仍在，世界却不再只按书架的次序走进来。

三、消失

沙姆斯第一次离开科尼亚，去了大马士革。鲁米后来派儿子苏丹·瓦拉德去迎接，他才回来。

重逢没有让裂隙消失。大约在1247年前后，沙姆斯再次离去，从鲁米的生活中消失了。

后世圣传里有谋杀的故事，甚至牵涉鲁米的另一个儿子。证据不足以把这些叙述变成判决。一个人的不知所终，不能靠故事需要一个凶手来填满。

鲁米后来到叙利亚寻找，也没有把沙姆斯带回来。路程可以走到尽头，想念却不会因此照着日历结束。

诗让相遇以另一种方式继续。一个名字反复被呼喊，眼前不在的人进入声音。在这层意义上，可以说他在自己的心里寻找沙姆斯；这不是宣布那位真实的他者从此只是鲁米的一部分。

也不能说诗直到最后一次失踪才突然诞生。相遇、相伴、第一次分别、再相聚与最后的失落，都属于诗歌展开的时间。

失去没有替鲁米完成作品。留下来的人仍要生活，结交新的同伴，听、说、组织语言。我们听到的是他怎样与缺席相处，而不是缺席怎样自动制造了天才。

四、芦笛

晚年的《玛斯纳维》在胡萨姆丁的鼓励、记录和协助下逐渐成篇。那是持续的合作与劳动，不只是失去沙姆斯后的一个瞬间。开头先让芦笛说话：

听这芦笛怎样哀诉，
怎样讲起一次次分离。

自从我被割离芦苇之地，
男女都随我的长声悲吟。

我寻一颗因离别而片片裂开的心，
好向它说尽渴望的疼痛。

谁远离了自己的根源，
谁便寻找再度相聚的时光。

四联诗先要求的不是解释，而是听。芦笛不是向世界宣告自己毫发无伤；它让分离进入了声音。

顺着意象想下去：芦苇离开苇丛，经过制作者的手，才成为可以吹奏的笛子。但诗不是一张造笛图纸，更不是让人互相伤害的命令。

杜甫、贝多芬、司马迁也在这里回响。经历过的事进入诗、四重奏和《史记》，却没有一条公式能把痛苦兑换成作品。有人受伤后只能先活下来，那也不欠世界一首歌。

芦笛还多说了一件事：我想回去。它不只表达疼痛，还朝向重逢。声音从一处断裂出发，却不愿永远以断裂为家。

在这幅文学对照里，杜甫朝向人间的仁，贝多芬朝向抗争，司马迁朝向历史的记录，鲁米朝向与神圣本源的重逢。每个人都远比这一笔丰富。

同样是声音，方向却不同。听见共同的震动，不必抹平它们各自想去的地方。

五、Ishq

鲁米诗里反复燃起的一个词是ishq，通常译作热烈的爱。它经过阿拉伯语与波斯语的漫长使用，不能被一个中文定义装完。

在鲁米的许多诗句里，爱不只是“我喜欢你”。它动摇那个只会计算自己所得的我，使人被一个不能占有的对象牵引，甚至改变看世界的尺度。

火是他的诗性语言。若说自我被烧掉，烧掉的可以是自满与占有的要求；不必把人格、判断或尊严也一起扔进火中。

苏菲传统有多种爱的学说，基督教的agape也有复杂的历史。不同表达带着各自的问题走来，不必排成一列爱的强弱次序。

鲁米与沙姆斯的关系也不必被我们替他们选定一个排他的现代标签。师友、爱与精神追寻在诗里交织，而诗的亲密不能当作私人生活的完整档案。

从一位同伴走向更多生命，是我们可以沿着这些诗读出的开放；不是“爱沙姆斯、爱上帝、爱所有人”三个按钮依次按下。失落之后的世界，仍有需要学习如何相处的人。

苏格拉底在问答中使别人的确信动摇；鲁米让自己被相遇改变，再把变化化成声音递出去。一个像从外面追问，一个像从里面涌出——这是本系列的两笔肖像，不是说苏格拉底从未受人影响。

若爱使世界变大，那里面应当听得见更多声音，不只是一遍遍返回的自己。

六、旋转

人们想到鲁米，常会看见白衣旋转的身影。但今天熟悉的梅夫拉维Sema仪式，是在他身后由传承者逐渐形成的，不能把整套礼仪写成他的单人发明。

音乐、吟诵与转动，让记念不只发生在头脑里。展开的右掌向上、左掌向下，常被解释为接受恩典，再向世界传递；动作有它的传统，也有训练与节律。

如果把它放进本系列的画面，身体也能参与“凿”：不是消灭身体，而是暂时松开“世界必须以我的朝向为中心”的固执。

每转一圈，刚才在身后的东西来到眼前。衣摆展开，脚仍然接触地面。想象可以随旋转走远，却不能把眩晕当作灵性成就，也不能保证转到某个时刻自我就会消失。

苏菲语汇里的fana，常谈自我中心的消退与面向上帝的转化。自满的我可以退后，生活中的那个人却没有因此被删除。

庄子的逍遥与坐忘，可以在这里成为另一种回声：一个人怎样不再被习惯的界限钉住？但道家并不只静坐，苏菲也不只旋转；不能把两种传统画成静与动的两条路，再宣布终点相同。

井筒俊彦的比较研究提醒我们可以跨传统阅读，却不能代替具体差异的辨认。这里并置的是两幅阅读图景，不是一张证明所有宗教同一的地图。

身体仍在，别人也仍在。一次转身以后，值得问的也许不是“我消失了没有”，而是“我是否更能看见不由我决定的世界”。

七、他写的不是他的

鲁米的抒情诗集通常称为《大诗集》，也以《沙姆斯·大不里兹诗集》（Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi）著称。鲁米的诗，为什么响着另一个人的名字？

许多诗在本应出现诗人署名的位置呼唤沙姆斯。那名字可以是被爱者，可以朝向神圣的临在，也参与诗中说话者身份的流动。不能简单断言鲁米亲自给后来的整部编集定名，或者沙姆斯才是实际作者。

但读者确实会听见一种让位：这声音不愿只把自己围在“我的作品”里。相遇进入了语言，来源不再像一块可以独占的财产。

巴赫写下S.D.G.——荣耀唯归上帝——时，也把作品指向比个人名声更大的东西。我们可以让两个姿态彼此照亮，不能因此说基督教与鲁米的神学完全一样。

也不能把巴赫写成从未受凿、天生完整的管道。他有丧失、摩擦、修改和日复一日的工作。鲁米同样没有因为灵感而免去学习、记诵与作诗的工夫。

若仍保留“管道”的比喻，它也必须有手的痕迹。恩典不是把劳动从纸上擦去，相遇也不是把作者从作品中删除。

一个在声部之间安排回应，一个在呼喊另一个名字时改变自己的声音。两人都做了什么，才让读者或听者感到：这里有不只属于一人的东西经过。

八、他的葬礼

1273年12月17日，鲁米在科尼亚去世，六十六岁。

后来的圣传叙述他的葬礼时，把穆斯林、基督徒与犹太人写在同一条送葬路上。不同信仰的人都为他哀悼，并在故事里说起他怎样帮助自己理解信仰。

这是一个值得停留的画面。也要知道，我们是在读阿夫拉基等人的传承叙事，不是在听现场每一句话的录音。画面的力量，在于一个群体愿意怎样记住他。

鲁米的诗里有《古兰经》、先知穆罕默德和伊斯兰思想。让别的信仰者也受到触动，并不要求先把这些根系从诗中拔掉。

也许可以这样说：他从自己的传统深处发声，而声音越过了熟悉的院墙。越过院墙，不等于院墙两边从此没有差异，更不等于所有人都已经同意。

他的墓仍在科尼亚，成为梅夫拉纳博物馆建筑群的一部分。我们不必替来到墓前的人决定为什么来；有人祈祷，有人阅读，也有人只是停下来看看。

诗也这样等待。它可以被迎接，被误读，被质问。一个能容纳更多人的声音，不必先替每一位听者写好回答。

九、被凿成歌的人

这个系列写过许多遭遇改变的人。借那套“凿”与“构”的语言说，鲁米被凿成歌。

杜甫让苦难进入诗与仁的关切；贝多芬在失聪后继续寻找音乐；司马迁在极辱后仍写历史；达尔文让观察不断修改解释。这些人生不能归结为“受苦，所以伟大”。

鲁米留下的也不只是一片知识烧尽后的空白。他的诗有经文，有故事，有推理，有多年形成的语言。歌不是学问的反面；有时是学问终于带着呼吸说话。

苏格拉底的追问会让人不安。杜甫的颠沛、贝多芬的失聪、司马迁的酷刑，都不是应当向往的课程。再美的作品，也不能倒过来证明伤害值得发生。

但鲁米的歌确实可能使我们愿意松开一点：不是请人来伤我，而是允许一次相遇改变那些我以为永远不会变的答案。

愿意被改变，与愿意被毁掉，是两件事。我们可以拒绝，可以退出；对方也可以。另一个人不是凿开我的工具，我也不是供他完成的材料。

再回到本系列想象的桥头。多了一个白衣的身影，转动着。右掌向上，左掌向下。接受，传递。

杜甫的破袖子在风里，贝多芬的手慢慢松开，司马迁站在自己的文字旁边。桥上的人不必学同一种姿势，才能听见彼此。

芦笛响起来。你可以走近，也可以在原地听。那声音不说“先被切开，你才配歌唱”。

它像在问：如果你愿意，可否让一个不由你安排的声音进来？然后，用你自己的呼吸，回答。

阅读说明与来源

生平与迁徙参照《伊朗百科全书》巴哈丁·瓦拉德条、史密森学会科尼亚专题及圣安德鲁斯大学《沙姆斯言谈录》手稿目录。巴尔赫是家族归属，瓦赫什为研究提出的出生地；迁居的年代与原因不宜简化。幼年会见阿塔尔、伊本·阿拉比等故事不作确证行程。

1244年相遇的坐骑、发问与昏厥属传记传说；第二节火与手的对照是本文比喻，非沙姆斯逐字引语。第一次离去后的迎回者为苏丹·瓦拉德。最后失踪的具体日期与原因有异说，杀害及涉子之说不作事实判决。诗歌的发展不能压缩为最后失踪后的一刻。

芦笛四联对照《玛斯纳维》第一卷第1—4联波斯文，为本篇重新译写，不搬用现代出版译文；第一联另有字序传本差异。胡萨姆丁的记录和编辑工作参照《伊朗百科全书》专条。《大诗集》的名称、署沙姆斯及写作时序参照玛斯纳维之家介绍，区分诗的署名、精神解释与实际作者。

ishq、fana及人与神的关系有丰富的神学与诗学背景，本文不提供单一定义；参照贾维德·穆贾迪迪的鲁米教导专文，亦不把鲁米的神秘主义化约为现代世俗伦理。与agape、庄子、巴赫及其他人物的对照是本文阅读。井筒俊彦仅作比较研究的提示，不转引本轮未直接核对的具体结论。Sema发展与墓地参照史密森学会专题；旋转不是医疗或自我消除操作指引。

葬礼跨宗教叙事参照摩根图书馆与博物馆所藏《圣者事迹星集》葬礼插图说明；该16世纪手稿承自阿夫拉基14世纪圣传，非现场记录。未采用游客数字及对访客心理的推测。桥头、凿、构与管道均为文学意象，伤害不因作品而正当，人可拒绝或离开。

I. The Scholar

Before Shams, Rumi was known above all as a scholar. The ocean of poetry by which we now recognize him had not yet opened.

Born in 1207, Jalal al-Din Muhammad belonged to a family associated with Balkh; whether his birthplace was Balkh or Vakhsh remains disputed. The name Rumi points elsewhere, toward Rum, the Anatolian lands formerly ruled by Eastern Rome, where he spent his adult life.

His father, Baha al-Din Walad, was a preacher and religious scholar. A westward journey through several homes eventually brought the family to Konya. Political and religious tensions, changing homes, and the shadow of Mongol expansion belong to the background of that long journey.

Rumi succeeded his father as a teacher and continued his own studies and Sufi training. Scripture, law, and theology were not merely furniture around him. They were years of attention. He also had pupils, a household, and ordinary obligations.

In this series' vocabulary, he had a construction: learning, office, inherited authority, a name others knew how to address.

It was not a lifeless structure waiting to be smashed. Its learning would remain audible in the poems. Shams did not turn an educated man into someone who knew nothing.

He made it possible for a man with an established place to become uncertain again about where his life might go.

II. Shams

In 1244, at thirty-seven, Rumi met Shams of Tabriz in Konya.

Shams was an itinerant Sufi. Followers recorded his conversations in the Maqalat. They preserve scriptural discussion, argument, and the demanding voice of a companion.

Later biographies give the meeting the shape of lightning. Shams stops the mounted scholar and asks about the Prophet and a mystic; Rumi is overwhelmed, perhaps falls or faints. Versions differ. These are ways a tradition pictured a turning point, not transcripts made in the street.

Their companionship did change Rumi's life profoundly. Long periods together disrupted familiar patterns of teaching; jealousy and hostility gathered around them. The heat between two people reached beyond the room they occupied.

What had been touched? Perhaps the distance between knowing about something and undergoing it, a distance another volume cannot by itself close.

Here is this essay's image: one may explain fire perfectly while one's hand knows nothing of its warmth.

The carving is an opening in the shell of “I already know.” It need not destroy scripture or reason. The books remain; the world no longer enters only in the order of the shelves.

III. Disappearance

Shams's first departure took him to Damascus. Rumi sent his son Sultan Walad to bring him back.

Reunion did not settle the tensions. Around 1247, Shams left again and disappeared from Rumi's life.

Later hagiography tells of murder, in some versions implicating another of Rumi's sons. The evidence does not warrant a verdict. A missing person cannot be supplied with a murderer simply because a story wants an ending.

Rumi subsequently searched in Syria without recovering his companion. A road can end; longing does not therefore obey the calendar.

Poetry continued the encounter differently. A name was called again and again; someone absent entered a voice. To say that Rumi found Shams within himself can describe this inward presence. It must not turn the other man into nothing but an extension of Rumi.

Nor did poetry begin only after the final disappearance. Meeting, companionship, earlier separation, return, and loss all belong to its unfolding.

Loss did not do the writing. The survivor still had to live, find other companions, listen, speak, and make language. What reaches us is a person's work with absence, not absence automatically manufacturing a genius.

IV. The Reed

The Masnavi took shape in Rumi's later years with Husamuddin's encouragement and work as recorder and collaborator. Long attention, not a single bereaved instant, made the book. At its entrance a reed speaks:

Hear this reed lament,
recounting separation after separation.

Since they severed me from the reed-bed,
men and women have mourned within my cry.

I seek a heart torn piece by piece by parting,
to tell it all the ache of longing.

Whoever remains far from their source
seeks the time of union once again.

The four couplets ask first for listening. The reed does not announce that it is undamaged. It brings separation into sound.

Following the image, we imagine a reed taken from its bed and worked by a maker's hands into an instrument. But the poem is neither a workshop diagram nor an instruction to wound another person.

Du Fu, Beethoven, and Sima Qian sound here too. Experience enters poems, quartets, and the Records of the Grand Historian; no formula converts suffering into art. Someone who can only survive after injury owes the world no masterpiece.

The reed also says: I want to return. Its cry reaches beyond pain toward reunion. It begins at a separation without wishing to make separation its permanent home.

In this literary grouping, Du Fu turns toward human care, Beethoven toward resistance, Sima Qian toward historical witness, Rumi toward reunion with the divine source. Each life exceeds its outline.

Their voices resonate without acquiring one destination. Listening for kinship need not silence what makes them different.

V. Ishq

One word keeps catching fire in Rumi's poetry: ishq, often rendered as passionate love. Its long Arabic and Persian life cannot fit inside a single English definition.

In many of his poems, love is more than liking. It unsettles the self that calculates only its own returns. Something one cannot possess begins to alter the scale on which the world is seen.

Fire belongs to the poetry. What it burns may be self-satisfaction and the demand to own; personality, judgment, and dignity need not be thrown in after them.

Sufi traditions offer different accounts of love, and Christian agape has its own varied history. These are expressions carrying different questions, not entries in a ranking of stronger and weaker love.

Rumi and Shams likewise need not be assigned one exclusive modern label. Friendship, discipleship, love, and spiritual search intertwine. The intimacy of a poem is not a complete archive of private life.

We may read an opening from one companion toward a wider world. That is not a sequence of switches marked Shams, God, and everybody. After loss, there are still actual people with whom one must learn to live.

Socrates unsettles another's certainty through questions; Rumi lets encounter change him and gives that change a voice. One portrait moves inward from an external question, the other outward from an inward disturbance. These are emphases, not proof that Socrates was never changed by anyone.

A world enlarged by love should have more voices in it, not merely one's own returning again.

VI. Whirling

Rumi's name often brings a white-robed turning figure to mind. Yet the familiar Mevlevi Sema ceremony developed through his successors. He did not invent its finished form single-handed.

Music, recitation, and movement give remembrance a bodily life. The right palm turned upward and the left downward are commonly understood as receiving grace and passing it on. The gesture belongs to a disciplined tradition, with rhythm and training.

In this series' imagery, a body too can participate in carving: not by being destroyed, but by loosening the insistence that the world must face in one's own direction.

With each turn, what was behind comes into view. Cloth opens; feet still meet the ground. Imagination may follow, but dizziness is not a spiritual credential, and no number of turns guarantees the disappearance of self.

Fana, in Sufi vocabularies, concerns the passing away of self-centeredness and transformation toward God. The self-satisfied I may step back; the person who must live after the ceremony remains.

Zhuangzi's free wandering and sitting in forgetfulness offer another question about habitual limits. Daoism, however, is not all stillness, nor Sufism all movement. Two traditions cannot be reduced to stationary and revolving routes toward an identical destination.

Toshihiko Izutsu's comparative work invites reading across traditions, not ignoring their differences. Here we are placing two images beside each other, not drawing a map that proves every faith the same.

The body remains, and so do other people. After turning, the fruitful question may be not “Have I vanished?” but “Can I see more of a world that does not take its orders from me?”

VII. What He Wrote Was Not His

Rumi's lyric collection is known as the Great Divan and as the Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi. Why does another person's name resound through Rumi's poems?

Many lyrics invoke Shams where a poet's signature might appear. The name can address the beloved, evoke divine presence, and unsettle the speaker's identity. This does not establish that Rumi personally named the entire later collection, still less that Shams literally composed it.

Yet there is a yielding in the voice: it will not remain enclosed within “my work.” An encounter has entered language. Its source no longer resembles exclusively owned property.

Bach's S.D.G., “Glory to God alone,” also points beyond individual fame. These gestures can illuminate each other without making Christian and Sufi theology interchangeable.

Bach was not an untouched conduit, born complete. There were losses, conflict, revisions, and daily work. Inspiration did not exempt Rumi either from learning, memory, or the labor of verse.

If we retain the conduit as an image, it must carry the marks of hands. Grace does not erase work from the page; encounter does not delete authorship.

One composes responses between musical voices; the other changes his voice while calling another name. Both did something that lets us feel something not exclusively theirs passing through.

VIII. His Funeral

Rumi died in Konya on December 17, 1273, aged sixty-six.

Later hagiographical accounts place Muslims, Christians, and Jews together in his funeral procession. In these stories, people of different faiths mourn him and say that he helped them understand their own beliefs.

It is a moving picture. It is also a picture transmitted by Aflaki and others, not a recording of each mourner's words. It tells us something about how a community wished to remember him.

The Quran, Muhammad, and Islamic thought inhabit Rumi's poetry. Its power to move readers of other faiths does not require those roots to be pulled out.

Perhaps a voice speaking from deep within its own tradition carried beyond the courtyard wall. Crossing that wall does not abolish the differences on either side or mean that everyone has agreed.

His tomb remains in Konya, within the Mevlana Museum complex. We need not decide why every visitor comes. Some pray, some read, some simply pause to look.

Poetry waits in a similar way. It can be welcomed, misread, or questioned. A hospitable voice need not write every listener's answer in advance.

IX. Carved into Song

This series has followed many lives changed by what they encountered. In its vocabulary of carving and construction, Rumi was carved into song.

Du Fu brought suffering into poetry and human concern; Beethoven kept seeking music after deafness; Sima Qian wrote history after extreme humiliation; Darwin let observations revise explanations. None of these lives means “suffering, therefore greatness.”

Rumi did not leave only a vacancy where learning had burned. Scripture, stories, reasoning, and cultivated language live in his poems. Song is not scholarship's opposite. Sometimes it is learning that has found a breath.

Socrates' questions can discomfort us. Du Fu's displacement, Beethoven's deafness, and Sima Qian's torture are not desirable courses of instruction. No beautiful work retrospectively makes its maker's injury right.

Rumi's song may nevertheless make us willing to loosen something: not to invite harm, but to let an encounter change answers we thought could never change.

Consent to change is not consent to destruction. We may refuse or leave; so may the other. Another person is not the tool for opening me, and I am not raw material for their completion.

Return to this series' imagined bridge. A white-robed figure turns, one palm upward, one downward. Receiving, passing on.

Du Fu's worn sleeve moves in the wind. Beethoven's hand slowly opens. Sima Qian stands beside his words. They need not adopt one posture to hear one another.

A reed sounds. You may approach or listen where you are. It does not say that you must first be cut open to deserve a song.

Perhaps it asks: if you are willing, could a voice you did not arrange enter? Then answer with your own breath.

Reading Notes and Sources

Life and migration were checked against Encyclopaedia Iranica on Baha al-Din Walad, the Smithsonian's Konya exhibition, and the University of St Andrews manuscript record for the Maqalat. The family's connection with Balkh is distinguished from the proposed birthplace of Vakhsh. Migration dates and motives are not reduced to one event. Childhood meetings with Attar or Ibn Arabi are not treated as established itinerary.

The mounted encounter, question, and fainting in 1244 belong to biographical tradition. Fire and the hand are this essay's image, not Shams's verbatim speech. Sultan Walad brought Shams back after the first departure. The final disappearance has disputed dating and circumstances; murder and a son's involvement are not treated as a verdict. The development of the poems is not confined to the aftermath of that disappearance.

The reed's four couplets are newly rendered here from the Persian opening of Masnavi I, 1–4, not reproduced from a modern published translation; the first couplet also has transmitted word-order variants. Husamuddin's recording and editing are checked against Iranica. Names of the Divan, the invocation of Shams, and its chronology follow Dar al-Masnavi's introduction; poetic signature, spiritual interpretation, and actual authorship are distinguished.

Ishq and fana have diverse theological and poetic contexts. Jawid Mojaddedi's account of Rumi's teachings is a guide, not grounds for a single definition or for reducing his mysticism to modern secular ethics. Agape, Zhuangzi, Bach, and the other comparisons are this essay's reading. Izutsu indicates a comparative field, not a particular conclusion directly verified in this review. Sema's development and the tomb follow the Smithsonian account. Whirling is not offered as a therapeutic or self-erasure procedure.

The multifaith funeral follows the Morgan Library & Museum's account of its funeral miniature. That sixteenth-century manuscript descends from Aflaki's fourteenth-century hagiography, not an on-the-spot record. No visitor figures or guesses about visitors' inner motives are used. Bridge, carving, construction, and conduit are literary images. Art does not justify injury, and a person may refuse or leave.
