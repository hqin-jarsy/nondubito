卡夫卡，早上醒来你已经是虫子了

Kafka, You Wake Up and You Are Already a Bug

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、一句话

一天早晨，格里高尔·萨姆沙从不安的梦中醒来，发现自己在床上变成了一只骇人的虫子。

《变形记》这样开始。没有药水，没有诅咒，没有一个负责解释变故的人。卡夫卡甚至不给我们先认识格里高尔的机会：认识他的时候，事情已经发生了。

原文的Ungeziefer带着令人厌恶的害物之意，并没有确定是哪一种虫。还没看清他的样子，我们已经听见了排斥他的词。

贝多芬第五交响曲的开头，常被听作命运敲门。敲门至少留下一个瞬间：有什么要来了。卡夫卡连这个瞬间也省掉了。闹钟还在，窗外有雨，房间仍是自己的房间。只有自己不再是熟悉的样子。

在《罪与罚》里，拉斯科尔尼科夫的痛苦当然不只来自杀人，但那场罪行给读者一个可以追问的起点。我们会问，他为什么杀人，又怎样面对自己做过的事。

格里高尔没有这样一份起诉书。小说没有安排一桩罪行，让变形显得像惩罚。他是个旅行推销员，一直负担着家里的生活。然后，一个普通早晨不再普通。

我们习惯向故事索取一个因为。因为他傲慢，因为他犯错，因为他忽略了征兆。只要有因为，灾难似乎就和我们隔着一点距离：我不那样做，也许就不会遇上。

卡夫卡把那一点距离拿走了。先别忙着寻找寓意。床上有人醒着，他还不知道怎样把今天过下去。

二、虫子之后

格里高尔也惊讶，也害怕。但他很快开始盘算火车时刻，担心迟到，担心老板怀疑他偷懒。身体出了这么大的事，工作却仍在向他催账。

这比一句抽象的“人被工作异化了”更扎人。他试着翻身，那些细腿各动各的。他需要帮助，脑子里却先出现了迟到后的解释。

公司的经理已经来到家里。门外还有母亲、父亲、妹妹。格里高尔努力说话，我们能读见他的意思，门外的人却听不懂。终于，他用陌生的身体打开了门。

经理后退，母亲受惊，父亲把他赶回房间。此后，一扇本来普通的卧室门，开始区分谁能待在一家人的生活里，谁只能躲在里面。

妹妹格蕾特起初为他送食物，试着猜他现在爱吃什么。家人也逐渐出去工作。他们疲倦、缺钱，不是没有自己的难处。照料却一天天缩减，他的房间后来堆进了杂物。

父亲曾向他扔苹果，其中一只嵌进背部，留下长期的伤。变形为什么发生，我们不知道；这一下是谁扔的，小说并没有含糊。

有一晚，妹妹为房客拉小提琴。格里高尔被音乐吸引，爬出房间。他想起自己曾打算送妹妹去音乐学院，还想让她留在自己身边。那愿望里有爱，也有孤独和占有，并不是一张毫无褶皱的圣人画像。

但读到这里，我们很难把他当作一团没有内心的麻烦。他听见的，比房客听见的多。那个不会说人话的身体里，仍有一整个尚未被别人听见的生活。

妹妹终于要求家人摆脱“它”，并拒绝再把眼前的生物认作哥哥。可怕的不是作者宣布格里高尔不再有资格成为人，而是家里的人逐渐用一种称呼，替自己结束了对他的承认。

格里高尔回到房间，在黎明前后死去。清洁女工用扫帚碰动他的身体，后来告诉家人，里面的东西已经不用他们操心了。叙述没有详写她怎样处理尸体。

一家人乘电车出城，谈工作、住处和女儿的未来。春天到了。

卡夫卡没有站出来责骂他们。阳光就这样照在故事结尾，也照在读者的不舒服上：一个家的轻松，竟然可以从少掉一个人开始。

三、审判

换一间房，另一个早晨。银行职员约瑟夫·K被告知，他被捕了。

这是《审判》的开头。卡夫卡在1914至1915年间写下这部未完成的小说；1925年，他去世后，马克斯·布罗德整理出版。

K问自己犯了什么罪。来人不能给出他能够核实的指控。更怪的是，他还可以照常去银行工作。被捕没有立刻把他关进牢房，却逐渐占据了他的生活。

法庭在普通住宅楼里，开庭的房间挤满人，楼上还有闷热的办公室。K在这样的地方找程序、找依据、找一个能把话说清的人。得到的却总是更多关系、暗示和规矩。

他找律师，找与法官有往来的画家。在教堂里，一个神父向他讲起《在法的门前》：乡下人来到入口，守门人说，现在不能进去。

门开着。守门人又警告，里面还有更强大的守门人。乡下人坐下来等，恳求，送出自己带来的东西，直到一生快要结束。他才得知，这个入口原是专为他设的，现在要关了。

读者很容易喊：为什么不直接进去？但那个带着威胁的“不行”，不是空气。把漫长等待全归咎于他的软弱，会让守门人的权力悄悄退场。

神父和K还在争论：谁受了骗，谁被自己的职责困住，谁真正知道法？寓言没有发给我们一把标准答案的钥匙。

最后，两个人把K带到采石场。他看见远处一扇窗打开，似乎有人伸出了双臂。帮助还可能来吗？他仍在看。刀却已经落下。他说：“像一条狗！”羞耻仿佛还要在他死后继续。

我们并没有得到足以裁定他一生清白无瑕的全知材料。可是，一个人不必先是圣人，才有权知道自己被指控什么，才不该被这样处置。

四、没有为什么

苏格拉底追问我们以为自己懂的正义，牛顿追问运动，达尔文追问生命的变化。弗洛伊德在诊室里听那些尚未说清的痛苦。他们得到的答案不一样，问题也没有从此停止。

卡夫卡把我们留在另一个时刻：你已经遭遇了一件事，而能使它变得可理解的解释迟迟不来。

为什么变成虫子？为什么被捕？我们和人物一样，想把手伸到故事背后，摸到那台机器。手伸过去，什么也没有抓住。

这并不证明事情没有原因。小说拒绝交出的，是能让读者安心合上书的总说明。家庭、劳动、欲望、官僚权力，都能照亮其中一角，却没有一角可以替整本书说话。

哥德尔会让我们想到证明的边界。但数学定理有严格条件，不能拿来证明人世荒诞。拉康的实在界也可以成为阅读的回声：解释已经说了很多，眼前的遭遇仍没有被消化。

这些比较最好停在门口。不要刚说完卡夫卡没有答案，就替他挂上一块“答案是虚无”的牌子。

尤其不能拿“世界不欠你解释”替现实里的办事窗口说话。一个制度不给理由，并不因此获得了不给理由的权利。

小说让人难受的地方，恰恰是人物还在问。K没有收到解释，他想要解释的那一部分，也没有随着公文一起被盖掉。

五、他自己

1883年，卡夫卡出生在布拉格的犹太家庭。他以德语写作，也会捷克语，后来学习希伯来语。几种语言和归属在这座城市交错，不能把他的生活排成三个严丝合缝的孤岛。

父亲赫尔曼经营生意，有一种让儿子觉得自己格外弱小的力量。1919年的《致父亲的信》里，卡夫卡写下自己的恐惧、羞耻和受挫。信没有交到父亲手中。

那不是一份让法官听完双方证词的庭审记录，而是一个儿子终于试着把话说完。它的重要性，不需要靠把父亲解释成一切作品的唯一病因来成立。

卡夫卡取得法律博士学位，后来在工伤保险机构工作。他嫌公事侵占写作，却也认真处理它。档案里的工人身体、事故、赔偿，不只是通向小说的布景；那也是别人真实的生活。

夜里，他把时间争回来写作。想象那张桌子：第二天还要去上班，句子却还没有写到可以停下的地方。疲倦和专注坐在一起。

他曾两次与菲利斯·鲍尔订婚，又与尤莉·沃赫里策克订婚，最后都没有结婚。米莱娜·耶森斯卡和朵拉·迪亚曼特，则属于另外两段重要的关系。

在信中，他反复拉扯亲近与独处、共同生活与写作的时间。我们能看见这种困难，却不能替几位女性把各自的一生缩成作家的障碍或救赎。

1923年，他与朵拉到柏林生活。这个行动很小，也很具体：那个常被画成永远孤独的人，曾认真尝试与另一个人一起过日子。

布罗德还记下他朗读作品的热情。一个会旅行、交朋友、被文学激动的人，和那些恐怖的房间，是同一个人的不同侧面。

陀思妥耶夫斯基不能只用刑场解释，卡夫卡也不能被收进一份“整个存在都是创伤”的判词。格里高尔与K来自他的笔，却不等于他的身份证。

写作对他极其重要。但他不是因为留下了伟大的小说，才值得作为一个具体的人被看见。

六、烧掉

1924年6月3日，卡夫卡在基尔灵去世，四十岁。结核病累及喉部，最后进食、吞咽都十分艰难。这里先是一个需要照护的病人，不是语言为文学献上的象征。

他给布罗德留下过两份处理遗稿的嘱托，要求烧毁日记、书信、草稿等材料。其中一份又区分了少数已经出版、他尚可容忍保留的作品。

布罗德没有照办。他保存、整理并出版了《审判》《城堡》《失踪者》（早期以《美国》之名出版），还有日记和书信。这决定深刻改变了卡夫卡被阅读的方式。

但不能说，没有布罗德，世上就从未有过卡夫卡。《变形记》等作品在他生前已经发表。我们会失去极重要的一部分，而不是让所有痕迹凭空归零。

布罗德后来解释，他早已当面告诉朋友，自己不会执行焚稿的要求。这份回忆值得读，却不能自动替卡夫卡签署另一份相反的同意书。

一边是朋友留下的明确文字，一边是布罗德相信不能让它们消失的作品。我们是这个决定的受益者，正因此，更难假装自己站在毫无利害的位置上。

想到秦始皇焚书，会看见纸张多么脆弱；但统治者毁掉别人的话，与作者想处置自己的私人文字，并不是同一种权力。

书稿活了下来，是因为有人带走、收藏、编辑、保存了它们，不是因为存在一条保证纸张不灭的定律。也确实有文字失去，再也无法补回。

余项在这里，可以指那场仍不能被一句赞美平息的争论：我们读到的，超出了作者准许我们读到的范围。这份幸运里，带着一个没有消失的问题。

七、城堡

《城堡》里，K来到一个积雪的村庄，自称受聘的土地测量员。他想把自己的身份弄清，想见到有权作出确认的人。

城堡的消息却互相抵牾：电话、信件、传话人与官员，每一处似乎都能给他一点依据，又让依据变得可疑。不是完全没有人理他；是有人答话，却没人让事情落定。

他沿着村路走。城堡在上面，路却不肯干脆把他送过去。一次会面变成等待，一种关系牵出另一种关系。距离不仅在山坡上，也在别人的手续与眼神里。

城堡可以被读作权威，也可以牵动宗教、归属和意义的问题。但对K来说，困境还有很朴素的一面：我能住在这里吗？我被当作谁？明天怎么办？

人为什么会把生活的重量，都押在那一声来自上面的确认上？又是什么，使他很难不这样做？小说的路在这两个问题之间绕行。

把它与康德并读，会想到认知的界限。不过，物自体不是山顶的办公楼，康德也没有保证人可以在现象界过得舒舒服服。城堡是一个故事中的权力与距离，不是哲学概念的插图。

K最终如何，手稿没有给出结局。卡夫卡在1922年停下写作，后来没有完成它；不是死亡当天把一个本来已经计划妥当的答案从我们手里夺走。

阅读停了下来，等候的感觉却没有。我们离开书页时，仿佛仍有人在那条路上，试着听懂刚收到的消息。

八、空的地下室

沿用这个系列的地下室比喻，弗洛伊德让我们寻找还在起作用的冲突，陀思妥耶夫斯基让我们听见互相撕扯的声音。到了卡夫卡这里，灯照向深处，却未必照出我们等着找到的那件东西。

不是人物心里空无一物。格里高尔有愿望、有委屈；K有骄傲、恐惧，也会伤害别人。空下来的，是那个本以为最后一定会放着解释的位置。

我们原来盼着找到一封判决书，或一段把所有线索收拢的往事。找到以后，就能说：原来如此。卡夫卡迟迟不让那句话落地。

治疗也不是翻出一件东西就从此和解，忏悔也不是按下开关便能重生。三位作者没有各自发给人一张可以保证离开地下室的车票。

庄子的混沌故事提醒我们，按照自己的图纸给他者凿出七窍，可能毁掉原来活着的存在。它不是一份证明深处空无一物的报告。读卡夫卡时，这个提醒也许适用于我们手里的解释。

还没有弄懂的人，不要先填成虚无。

现在，让卡夫卡走到想象的桥头。他很瘦，站在稍远的地方。弗洛伊德望着桥下，陀思妥耶夫斯基带着地下室里尚未平息的争辩，老子的背影在另一端渐远。

卡夫卡仿佛更难看清。他不像在宣布离去，也没有替自己摆好一个能被记住的姿势。我们的目光几乎从他身上滑过去。

是他正在消失，还是我们只认得那些已经得到名字、用途和解释的人？

桥上没有人拿出一张批准他存在的公文。他也没有忽然找到使所有事情好起来的答案。

他仍站在那里。先别替他关门。

注释

《变形记》《审判》的场景据德文原文校读，开场句为本文转述。Ungeziefer不确定具体虫种。苹果、小提琴与妹妹最后的要求见小说；女工以扫帚推移遗体，随后宣称已处理，原文没有详写搬走尸体的过程。

《在法的门前》中，门卫明确提出威胁，不能把等待完全归咎于乡下人的意志不足。神父与K的讨论保留多种解释。《审判》没有提供可核实的罪状，也没有授予读者裁定K全部人生的全知位置。

贝多芬、哥德尔、拉康、康德与庄子的并置是笔者的文学比较，不是跨领域证明。“命运敲门”是广为流传的贝多芬接受史形象，不在此认定为作曲者确凿自述。不得以文学中的解释缺席替现实机构免除责任。

生平区分写作语言、所会语言、工作与感情经历。婚约为与菲利斯两次、尤莉一次，不与米莱娜或朵拉混排。《致父亲的信》表达儿子的经验，不是一份涵盖双方的完整家庭诊断。

两份遗稿处置文字及布罗德的辩解，可在1925年《审判》的布罗德后记中查阅。他自述生前已拒绝焚稿，并不能自动证明卡夫卡默许反向执行。保留作品的收益没有消除作者意愿问题；所谓余项不是纸张不灭的定律。

《城堡》核对了开头两章及S. Fischer出版社作品说明：1922年写作、未完、1926年首次出版。卡夫卡博物馆英文年表在去世月及出版年上有误植，本文以其生平页等所记1924年6月3日为准，不照搬错误。桥头为笔者想像。

I. One Sentence

A man wakes from troubled dreams and discovers that, in his own bed, he has become something monstrous: a creature the household will come to regard as vermin. His name is Gregor Samsa.

The Metamorphosis begins with the event already accomplished. No potion, curse, or helpful explanation precedes it. We do not even get to know Gregor first. By the time we meet him, it has happened.

Kafka's Ungeziefer does not identify a species. It carries disgust: something unwanted, something to get rid of. Before we can see this body clearly, language has begun to turn against it.

People often hear the opening of Beethoven's Fifth as fate knocking. A knock at least offers warning. Kafka removes even that interval. The alarm clock is there, rain is falling outside, the room remains his room. Only its occupant is no longer familiar to himself.

Raskolnikov's suffering in Crime and Punishment begins before his murders and exceeds them. Nevertheless, the crime gives the reader a place to start asking questions. Why did he do it? How will he live with what he has done?

Gregor receives no such account. Kafka supplies no offense for which metamorphosis might be the punishment. Here is a traveling salesman who has been supporting his family. Then an ordinary morning ceases to be ordinary.

We like a because. Because he was arrogant, because he made a mistake, because he ignored the warning. An explanation can hold disaster at a reassuring distance: perhaps, if I behave differently, it will pass me by.

Kafka takes away that distance. Before reaching for the allegory, stay with the bed. Someone is awake there. He does not yet know how to get through today.

II. After the Transformation

Gregor is startled and frightened. Yet almost immediately he is calculating train times and worrying that his employer will suspect idleness. His body has become unrecognizable, and work is still demanding an explanation for his lateness.

No lecture on alienation is needed. He tries to roll over; the little legs refuse to cooperate. He needs help. Instead, he rehearses what he will have to say about missing the train.

The firm's chief clerk arrives. His mother, father, and sister are outside his room. Gregor struggles to speak. We can follow his thoughts, but the people beyond the door cannot understand his voice. Eventually he manages to open it.

The clerk retreats, his mother is horrified, and his father drives him back. An ordinary bedroom door starts doing a new job: separating those allowed to participate in family life from the one who must remain concealed.

At first his sister Grete brings food and tries to discover what he can eat. The others gradually take jobs. They have exhaustion and money troubles of their own. But care contracts, day by day, and Gregor's room becomes a place to dump unwanted things.

His father throws apples at him. One lodges in his back and leaves a lasting wound. We do not know what caused the transformation. We do know who threw the apple.

One evening Grete plays the violin for the lodgers. Gregor crawls toward the music. He remembers intending to send her to the conservatory and imagines keeping her close to him. Love is entangled with loneliness and possessiveness; he is no spotless saint.

Nor is he an empty inconvenience. He hears something the lodgers do not. Inside a body whose speech has become unintelligible, a whole life continues beyond the reach of the household's understanding.

Grete finally insists that they get rid of it, refusing to recognize her brother in the creature. The horror is not that Kafka has revoked Gregor's claim to personhood. It is that a family learns to withdraw recognition by changing the name it uses.

Gregor returns to his room and dies toward dawn. The charwoman prods his body with her broom. Later she announces that the family need no longer worry about what was inside. The narrative does not describe exactly how she disposes of it.

They take a tram into the country, discussing jobs, a new home, and their daughter's future. It is spring.

Kafka does not step forward to rebuke them. Sunlight falls on the ending and on our discomfort. A household's relief has become possible through the disappearance of one of its members.

III. The Trial

Another room, another morning. A bank officer, Josef K., is informed that he is under arrest.

The Trial was written in 1914–15 and remained unfinished. Max Brod prepared it for publication in 1925, the year after Kafka's death.

K. asks what he has done. No intelligible charge is supplied for him to examine. Stranger still, he can continue going to the bank. Arrest does not immediately put him in a cell. It gradually takes possession of the rest of his life.

The court occupies an ordinary tenement, with a crowded hearing room and suffocating offices upstairs. K. searches for a procedure, a basis for accusation, someone who can give a straight answer. Instead he acquires more contacts, hints, and obscure rules.

He consults a lawyer and a painter with connections to the judges. In the cathedral a priest tells him the story we know as Before the Law. A man from the country asks to enter. The doorkeeper says: not now.

The door is open. But the doorkeeper warns of more powerful guards within. The man waits, pleads, spends what he brought with him, and grows old. Only at the end does he learn that this entrance was meant for him alone. It is now being closed.

Why didn't he simply walk through? The reader wants to shout it. Yet the threatening prohibition was not nothing. Blaming the whole wasted life on the man's timidity quietly removes the doorkeeper's power from the picture.

K. and the priest dispute the story. Who was deceived? Who was trapped in a duty? Who actually knew the Law? The parable does not hand over an official key to its meaning.

At the end, two men lead K. to a quarry. A distant window opens; a figure seems to stretch out its arms. Could help still come? He is looking when the knife enters. He says, 'Like a dog!' It is as though the shame will outlive him.

We have not been given an omniscient certificate of K.'s moral innocence. We do not need one. A person need not be a saint to deserve knowledge of the charge against him, or protection from such a death.

IV. Without a Because

Socrates questions what we think we know about justice; Newton asks about motion; Darwin asks about the changing forms of life. Freud listens to suffering not yet adequately understood. Their answers differ, and none ends the business of questioning.

Kafka holds us at another moment: something has happened to you, but the explanation that would make it comprehensible does not arrive.

Why this body? Why this arrest? With his characters we reach behind the story, hoping to touch the machinery. Our hands find no handle.

That is not proof that events have no causes. What the fiction withholds is the comprehensive account that would let us shut the book in peace. Family, labor, desire, bureaucracy: each illuminates a corner. No corner can speak for the whole room.

Gödel may come to mind, with the limits of formal proof. But his theorems have precise conditions; they do not establish the absurdity of existence. Lacan's Real can offer another echo: after all our explanations, the encounter has still not been absorbed into them.

Let these comparisons pause at the threshold. It would be a peculiar way of honoring Kafka's uncertainty to nail up a sign announcing that the definitive answer is Nothingness.

And 'the world owes you no explanation' must not become a slogan on an actual office counter. An institution's refusal to give reasons does not establish its right to refuse them.

The question has not stopped mattering to the person inside the story. K. receives no adequate explanation. The part of him that wants one has not been stamped out along with the paperwork.

V. The Man Who Wrote It

Kafka was born in Prague in 1883, into a Jewish family. He wrote in German, also spoke Czech, and later studied Hebrew. Languages and affiliations crossed in his city; his life cannot be sorted into three perfectly sealed islands of exclusion.

His father Hermann was a businessman whose forcefulness could make his son feel very small. In the 1919 Letter to His Father, Kafka set down fear, shame, and repeated frustration. The letter never reached its addressee.

It is not a judicial record in which both parties have testified. It is a son's sustained attempt to finish saying something. Its force does not depend on turning the father into the single cause of every story the son wrote.

After qualifying in law, Kafka worked in accident insurance. He resented the time office work took from writing, but also performed it conscientiously. Injured workers, claims, and compensation were not merely scenery for a future novelist. They concerned other people's actual lives.

At night he fought time back for the page. Imagine the desk: work again tomorrow, but the sentence has not reached a place where he can leave it. Exhaustion sits beside concentration.

He was twice engaged to Felice Bauer, and later to Julie Wohryzek; none of those engagements led to marriage. Milena Jesenská and Dora Diamant belong to two other important relationships.

The letters repeatedly pull between intimacy and solitude, a shared home and time for writing. That conflict is visible. The women's independent lives should not disappear into roles as obstacles to, or rescuers of, an author's vocation.

In 1923 he moved to Berlin with Dora. This is a small, concrete fact worth keeping beside the legend of absolute solitude: he tried to make a life with another person.

Brod also remembered his passionate readings aloud. The traveler, friend, and writer excited by his own prose belongs to the same life as the terrifying rooms. Neither image cancels the other.

Dostoevsky cannot be contained in the memory of a firing squad. Nor should Kafka receive the lifelong sentence that his entire existence was trauma. Gregor and K. came from his pen. They are not his identity papers.

Writing mattered enormously to him. It was not the condition he had to fulfill before he could matter as a person.

VI. Burn It

Kafka died in Kierling on 3 June 1924, aged forty. Tuberculosis had affected his larynx, making swallowing and eating agonizingly difficult. Before this becomes a symbol of language silenced, it is a sick person needing care.

He left Brod two sets of instructions asking for the destruction of diaries, correspondence, drafts, and other papers. One also distinguished a small group of published works whose survival he could tolerate.

Brod did not comply. He preserved, edited, and published The Trial, The Castle, and The Man Who Disappeared, first issued as Amerika, as well as diaries and letters. His decision profoundly shaped the Kafka later readers would encounter.

It did not conjure an entirely unknown writer out of nothing. The Metamorphosis and other works had appeared during Kafka's lifetime. Obedience would have cost us an immense part of the work, not made every trace disappear.

Brod later said that he had already told Kafka he would refuse to burn the papers. That recollection belongs in the account. It cannot automatically sign a contrary permission on the dead author's behalf.

On one side stands the friend's written request; on the other, Brod's conviction that these works must not be lost. We benefit from his decision. That makes it harder, not easier, to pretend our judgment is disinterested.

Qin Shi Huang's burning of books reminds us how vulnerable paper can be. But a ruler destroying other people's words and a writer deciding what should happen to his own private papers exercise very different kinds of power.

The manuscripts survived because people carried, collected, edited, and preserved them. No law of conservation guaranteed their safety. Other writing really was lost and cannot be restored by a reassuring metaphor.

The remainder here may be the argument our gratitude cannot settle: we are reading beyond the permission the author gave. Our good fortune carries a question it has not extinguished.

VII. The Castle

In The Castle, K. arrives in a snowbound village claiming to be an appointed land surveyor. He wants his status clarified and an encounter with someone authorized to confirm it.

Messages from the Castle conflict. Telephone calls, letters, messengers, officials: each seems to offer a little evidence and then makes that evidence doubtful. It is not absolute silence. People answer him without allowing anything to be settled.

He follows the village road. The Castle is above him, but the road will not simply take him there. An appointment becomes waiting; one relationship leads to another. Distance resides in procedures and other people's judgments as much as in the slope.

We can read authority into the Castle, or questions about religion, belonging, and meaning. K.'s predicament also has a plain everyday edge. Can I stay here? Who do they take me to be? What am I supposed to do tomorrow?

Why does a person place so much of his life in an acknowledgment from above? What has made it so difficult for him not to? The novel winds between those questions.

Read beside Kant, it can evoke the boundaries of knowledge. But the thing-in-itself is not an office on a hill, and Kant never guaranteed comfort in the phenomenal world. This Castle gives narrative form to power and distance; it is not a diagram of his philosophy.

The manuscript does not tell us K.'s ultimate fate. Kafka stopped writing the novel in 1922 and did not complete it. Death did not suddenly snatch a fully prepared solution from the last page.

Our reading stops; the sensation of waiting does not. Somewhere beyond the closed book, someone seems still to be on that road, trying to understand the message he has just received.

VIII. The Empty Basement

Return to this series' image of the basement. Freud asks us to investigate conflicts still at work; Dostoevsky lets us hear voices tearing against one another. With Kafka, the lamp goes down but may not illuminate the thing we expected to find.

The characters are not empty. Gregor has wishes and grievances. K. has pride and fear, and can mistreat other people. What stands empty is the place where we were sure the final explanation would be waiting.

We expected a verdict, or a piece of history tying all the threads together. Then we could say: so that was it. Kafka keeps that sentence from settling.

Therapy is not simply the retrieval of an object followed by peace; confession is not a switch that guarantees rebirth. None of these three writers distributes a ticket ensuring escape from the basement.

Zhuangzi's story of Hundun warns that boring openings according to our own design can destroy the being we intend to improve. It is not a report proving that the inside is empty. Reading Kafka, we might turn the warning toward our eagerness to explain him.

Do not fill a person we have not understood with nothingness.

Let Kafka join our imaginary gathering at the bridge. Thin, a little farther from the others. Freud looks underneath; Dostoevsky brings arguments that have not fallen quiet. At the other end, Laozi's departing outline grows faint.

Kafka is harder to make out. He is not announcing his departure or striking a pose for memory. Our gaze almost slips past him.

Is he disappearing? Or have we learned to recognize only people already supplied with a name, a use, and an explanation?

No one on the bridge produces a document authorizing his existence. He does not suddenly discover an answer that makes everything well.

He is still standing there. Do not close the door for him.
