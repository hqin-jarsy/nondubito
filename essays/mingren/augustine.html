奥古斯丁，但不要现在

Augustine, But Not Yet

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、花园里的声音

386年夏天，米兰。按奥古斯丁多年后的回忆，他在一座花园的无花果树下哭了。

他三十一岁。他教过修辞学，做过摩尼教的听众，又读过柏拉图派的著作。如今，许多过去挡住他的思想障碍已经松动，可他仍然无法迈出那一步。他并不只是问基督教是否可信，也在问：如果真的相信，我是否愿意换一种生活？

母亲莫尼卡盼望他的归信。但母亲的盼望不能替他作决定，论证也不能替他收拾生活。旧日的习惯、身体的欲望、对前程的打算，并不因为一个道理讲通了，就自动退场。

他记得，附近传来一个孩子般的声音，反复唱着“Tolle, lege”——拿起来读。他把这声音理解为召唤，拿起保罗书信，读到《罗马书》关于不再沉溺宴乐与欲望、披戴基督的劝告。按照他的叙述，疑惑的黑暗在那一刻散开了。

这是后来写进《忏悔录》的皈依场景，不是一份现场录音。它之所以动人，也不只因为某个声音改变了一生。更因为在声音出现以前，一个很会说话的人，已经走到再也不能靠漂亮话哄过自己的地方。

花园的故事可以在这里收尾。但奥古斯丁的人生没有。那一刻改变了方向，并没有让此后的问题全部消失。

二、把自己写进去

《忏悔录》大约写于四世纪末，共十三卷。前九卷回望童年、求学、欲望、归信以及母亲的去世；第十卷转向写作当下的自己，追问记忆和仍在发生的诱惑；第十一至十三卷展开《创世记》、创造与时间的问题。

所以这不是一本讲到“我终于变好了”便结束的书。读者已经跟着他走出花园，他却又把读者领进记忆深处：现在这个会写忏悔录的人，难道就完全认识自己了吗？

古代已有自我检视的传统，例如《论语》中曾子的每日反省。奥古斯丁的特色，是把漫长的私人经历写成持续的追问，让回忆的那个人也留在问题里面。这种写法对后来的自传文学产生了格外深的影响。

他写少年时的偷窃，写与一位女子相伴多年、育有一子的往事，写求取声名的野心，也写自己从前怎样祈求贞洁与节制，心里却偷偷加上：但不要现在。

这半句话把人拦住了。我愿意承认道理，愿意赞成改变，甚至愿意为未来更好的自己感动。只是不要今天就把眼前的好处拿走。承诺成为延期的办法，连诚恳也能替拖延效劳。

他向上帝说话，也明确向可能读到这本书的人说话。这里的忏悔既有认罪，也有赞美，不是为了把消息告诉一个原本不知情的上帝。与维特根斯坦重新打开自己的书相比，奥古斯丁重新打开的是自己曾经讲得通的生活。两个人都让某个已经站稳的“我”，再次接受追问。

三、梨并不重要

《忏悔录》第二卷里，有一棵梨树。少年奥古斯丁和伙伴们趁夜去偷梨，摘下许多，自己只尝了一点，大部分扔给猪。他并不缺吃的，家里的果子甚至更好。

多年以后，他还在问：那一夜，我到底想要什么？如果答案只是“想吃梨”，事情反而好解释。可梨并不重要，重要的似乎是做了不被允许的事，又没有当场受到阻拦。

他没有停在“我就是爱做坏事”这个响亮的结论上。写着写着，伙伴们又回到画面里。他说，如果只有自己一个人，他不会去偷。一起胡闹的快感，害怕显得胆小，怕不够坏反而被笑话，都挤进了那个决定。

这让故事离今天很近。几个人围着一个不在场的人开玩笑，你未必真的讨厌那个人，但也跟着笑了。事后说“我没有恶意”，或许是真的，却没有回答你为什么愿意替那一场羞辱添一点声音。

奥古斯丁把这些放在罪与爱的失序中理解。他不是在写现代心理学，我们也不必把梨树直接搬进弗洛伊德的诊室。值得跟随的是他的迟疑：第一个解释不够，第二个也不能轻易结案。一次小小的偷窃，容得下比一个动机更复杂的人。

四、原罪不是一句骂人的话

原罪是奥古斯丁思想里影响深远、也争议极大的部分。他继承了更早的基督教传统，又在后来的争论中强调：人的困境不只来自一次次自选的坏行为，也与亚当的堕落及整个人类的处境相连。

这是一个神学主张，不是观察几个人以后便能宣布成立的实验结果。《忏悔录》提到婴儿的嫉妒，也不意味着一个婴儿的表情就替整套教义完成了证明。读者可以理解他怎样思考，不必因此接受他每一步的结论。

更不能把这套复杂思想缩成“人生来就是脏的”。奥古斯丁认为受造的本性是善的，恶不是与善并列的一种实体；受损、失序与需要医治，不等于一个人从根本上没有价值。

如果把慧能请到旁边，也不能安排成一场“人本来干净还是肮脏”的口号比赛。慧能传统里的觉悟与不执著，不是把人身上一切未被说尽的东西清除掉；奥古斯丁的原罪，也不是给那些未被说尽的东西统统起名为恶。两个传统问的问题和期待的解脱，都不一样。

我们可以带走一个比较窄、却很切身的问题：为什么已经知道的道理，仍然不能使我照着去做？奥古斯丁的答案是神学性的。问题本身，却可以留给不接受这个答案的人。承认自己的限制，不必变成对自己这个人的憎恶。

五、不是靠更用力

“那就再下决心。”这句话我们很熟悉。戒掉一个习惯，停止一段伤人的关系，承认一次自己不愿承认的错。失败以后，最容易追加的处方还是：你不够努力。

奥古斯丁不满足于这个处方。如果下决心的那个我，本身就被几种相反的愿望拉扯，再大声地命令自己，未必能把分裂缝好。他描述的不是意志完全消失，而是意志不能仅凭自足的力量完成救赎。

他把希望放在恩典上：上帝的给予不是一张凭成绩换来的奖券，也不是人先把自己修到合格，才有资格领取的帮助。在他的信仰里，连转向善、愿意接受帮助的能力，也离不开恩典。

他的恩典论在长期争辩中发展。自由意志怎样与恩典相容，人在其中怎样行动，后来的基督教传统也继续争论。奥古斯丁仍然劝诫、祈祷、要求行动；依赖恩典，不是宣布人从此不必回应。

与维特根斯坦相对照，也不必编出一个全靠自己、一个完全没有自己的二选一。维特根斯坦的改变离不开别人的批评；奥古斯丁所说的上帝，更不只是心理上的外援。比较有用的距离在这里：一个人改变自己时，是否能把所有力量都记在自己名下？

未信教的读者不必把恩典改名为友情或治疗，才能读到这一节。保留它原本的宗教含义，同时承认一个朴素的经验就够了：求助未必是自主的反面。有时，我们正是在不再假装独自能办到一切之后，才开始真正行动。

六、唱完一首歌

到了第十一卷，问题忽然变成了时间。我们每天都说早了、晚了、等一会儿，可真要解释时间是什么，熟悉的词反而不好用了。

已经过去的，此刻不在；尚未来到的，此刻也不在。现在一说出口，就往过去滑去。那么，我们怎样知道一段时间很长，另一段很短？我们到底在量什么？

奥古斯丁请读者想想背诵或歌唱。一首熟悉的歌还没开始，整首在期待中；唱到一半，有些已进入记忆，有些尚待唱出，注意力把将来不断带向过去。歌并不是一次交到手里的整块东西，却确实被我们经历了。

记忆、注意和期待，于是成为理解时间经验的入口。他用心灵的延展来描述这种张力。这不是一句“外面的时间不存在”，也不是提前替康德或胡塞尔写好了答案。相似的问题可以相互照亮，却不能因此抹去思想之间的距离。

这和花园里的那个人并不遥远。说“但不要现在”的时候，他正把希望放在未来，同时让习惯替现在作主。人不只是在时间里挪动，也在记忆和期待之间理解自己，给自己许诺，或给自己延期。

在他的信仰中，上帝的永恒不是一条无限延长的时间线。我们可以不赞成这个答案，却不必假装一句反驳就已经处理了整个问题。至少，唱完那首歌之后我们知道：解释一生，与把一生同时握在手里，并不是一回事。

七、同一个疑问，几种走法

读到那些自我辩护和互相打架的愿望，很容易想起弗洛伊德，也想起陀思妥耶夫斯基笔下的人。一个人越郑重地解释自己，有时越让我们听出解释底下还有别的声音。

这个相遇值得保留。但奥古斯丁不是穿着古装的精神分析师。“我想改变，可我又不想”不一定是意识与无意识的一一对战；《忏悔录》里的许多欲望，他恰恰知道得十分清楚。

梨的故事里，越界和结伴本身就有快乐。弗洛伊德后来还追问，人为什么会重复那些痛苦的经历；《超越快乐原则》由重复强迫等困难展开的问题，已经不是“要果子还是要偷”能够概括的了。

奥古斯丁关心意志、爱、罪与恩典；弗洛伊德提出另一套关于冲突和心理过程的解释；陀思妥耶夫斯基让人物在故事里把这些困难活出来。三者并非发现了完全相同的房间，再给家具换了名称。

但读者可以带着同一个疑问在这些书之间走动：我对自己的解释，是否已经把我希望别人看见的那个我，偷偷放在了答案里？这个问题不需要我们先同意某一套完整理论。它只要求，下一次说“我就是因为这个”时，稍微慢一点。

八、两座城，不是两张地图

410年，罗马遭到西哥特人的洗劫。有人把灾难归咎于离弃旧神、转向基督教。奥古斯丁从413年起写《上帝之城》，到426年前后完成二十二卷；回应这场震动的书，最终走得远远超过一次时事辩护。

他用两种爱的方向谈两座城：把自我抬高到轻蔑上帝，和爱上帝以至不再把自己的荣耀放在最高处。这里的“城”，不是一张把罗马涂红、教会涂蓝的地图，也不能简单等同国家与教会。

在历史中，两座城的人相互混杂，共同生活在可见的社会里。人不知道所有人的最终归属，也不能凭一张教会身份凭证就保证谁得救。这与“同一个人同时属于两座城”不是一回事；人内心的挣扎，更不能取消两种终极归向的区分。

上帝之城也不是现在全然不存在、到历史终点才突然出现。它在世间有朝圣中的生活，而最后的圆满属于他的信仰所盼望的未来。这样的区分，使罗马的命运不再等于基督教希望的命运：帝国不是永恒的保单。

这套思想留下的提醒很锋利：别让一种现存秩序占据最终答案的位置。不过，奥古斯丁本人后来也为强制异端回归教会作过辩护。不能因为他写出深刻的自省，就假定他对他人的自由始终同样宽厚。作品的洞见与作者造成的难题，需要一起读。

把黑格尔、马克思或奥古斯丁放在一起读，需要分别看他们的论证、相关的制度实践，以及他们寄托的希望。这些并不是同一种东西。我们可以追问一种希望怎样进入权力，怎样对待不同意的人；思想的比较，便从胜负名单回到了真正困难的生活里。

九、书仍然开着

430年8月28日，奥古斯丁在希波去世，七十五岁，汪达尔军队正在围城。那座城如今在阿尔及利亚。他不曾在386年的花园里把思想工作结束掉，此后还有关于三位一体、自由意志、经文与历史的大量写作。

到了晚年，他甚至回头检查自己的著作，留下《订正录》。信仰给了他方向，不等于从此每一句话都不必再想。后来的读者因此还能看见一个继续修订的思想者。

他的影响进入中世纪，也进入宗教改革；路德曾是奥古斯丁修会的修士。但对不准备追随整套教义的读者而言，最先留下来的，可能还是那个停顿：我愿意改变，但不要现在。

现在，让他走进这个系列想象的桥头。他抱着一本打开的《忏悔录》。维特根斯坦的两本书放在旁边。他们不必互相驳倒，也没有谁能替谁结束思考。一边是重新打开已经说出的答案，一边是重新审问已经解释过的自己。

我们不用先同意原罪，才被花园里的眼泪触动；也不用把人说成永远洗不干净，才承认改变的困难。认真承认一次伤害，放弃一次体面的借口，向人求助，都可以是开始，而不是合格之后才能领取的奖赏。

书仍然开着。今天未必能把一生整理好。但也许，那个说了很久的“以后”，可以先少往后推一点。

I. A Voice in the Garden

In the summer of 386, in Milan, Augustine wept beneath a fig tree. At least, that is how he would remember the garden many years later.

He was thirty-one. He had taught rhetoric, spent years among the Manichaeans and read the Platonists. Some of the intellectual obstacles to Christianity had fallen away. Yet understanding a position was not the same as consenting to the life it asked of him.

His mother Monica longed for his conversion. Her longing could not make the decision for him. Nor could an argument pack up his worldly ambitions, dismiss his accustomed pleasures and leave his old habits neatly outside the door. He had become persuaded of things he still could not bring himself to do.

He remembered hearing a childlike voice repeating Tolle, lege: take up and read. Taking it as a summons, he opened Paul's letters and read the passage in Romans urging the reader to turn away from revelry and lust and put on Christ. He describes the darkness of uncertainty clearing as he reached its end.

This is the conversion scene Augustine composed in the Confessions, not a recording made in the garden. Its force lies partly in what precedes the voice: a highly accomplished speaker has reached a place where eloquence can no longer get him past himself.

A conversion story could stop there. His life did not. The moment gave him a direction; it did not dispose of every question that would follow.

II. Writing Himself into the Question

The Confessions was written around the end of the fourth century. Its thirteen books do not all do the same work. The first nine look back over childhood, ambition, relationships, conversion and his mother's death. Book X turns toward the man doing the writing, his memory and his continuing temptations. Books XI–XIII explore Genesis, creation and time.

The book therefore does not end with the reassuring announcement that its author has become a better person. We have already followed him out of the garden when he takes us into memory and asks how well the man writing this account actually knows himself.

Self-examination already had ancient traditions: Zengzi's daily reflections in the Analects offer one example. Augustine's particular achievement was to turn an extended private history into sustained questioning, keeping even the person doing the remembering inside the question. That way of writing would exert an extraordinary influence on autobiographical literature.

He remembers a youthful theft, a long relationship with a woman who bore his son, and his appetite for distinction. He also recalls praying for chastity and restraint while quietly attaching a condition: not yet.

That condition catches the reader. I approve of change. I can imagine my better future self, and even feel moved by the prospect. Only do not take away today's advantages. A promise becomes a means of postponement. Sincerity itself can be recruited to keep things as they are.

Augustine addresses God, but he explicitly addresses possible human readers too. Confession here includes praise as well as the acknowledgment of wrongdoing; it is not news delivered to an uninformed deity. Where Wittgenstein reopened a book bearing his own name, Augustine reopened a life he had repeatedly managed to explain. Neither allowed an established version of himself to have the final word.

III. It Was Not About the Pears

A pear tree stands in Book II. As a teenager Augustine joined some companions in stealing its fruit at night. They ate a little and threw most of it to the pigs. He had not been hungry. Better fruit was available at home.

Years afterward, he still asks what he wanted that night. Wanting a pear would have been easier to explain. The attraction seems instead to lie in doing what was forbidden and getting away with it. The fruit becomes almost incidental.

But he does not leave us with the resounding declaration that he simply loved wrongdoing. His companions come back into the picture. Alone, he says, he would not have done it. The pleasure of shared mischief, the fear of looking timid, the embarrassment of not being shameless enough: these, too, belonged to the decision.

The scene is not so remote. Friends make jokes about someone who is absent. You do not particularly dislike the person, but you laugh along. Later, saying you meant no harm may be truthful; it still does not explain why you contributed to the humiliation.

Augustine understands the incident through sin and disordered love. He is not writing modern psychology, and we need not move the tree straight into Freud's consulting room. What is worth following is the refusal to finish too soon. One explanation proves insufficient, then another needs examining. A small theft can contain more of a person than a single motive will hold.

IV. Original Sin Is Not an Insult

Original sin became one of Augustine's most influential and contested teachings. He inherited earlier Christian traditions and, in later controversies, argued that our predicament cannot be explained solely through the wrong choices each individual makes. It belongs also to humanity's relationship with Adam's fall.

This is a theological claim, not an experimental conclusion established by observing a few people. The jealous infant described in the Confessions does not, by a look on its face, prove a whole doctrine. Readers can understand the steps of Augustine's thinking without accepting every conclusion.

Nor is the doctrine adequately rendered as people being inherently dirty. Augustine holds that created nature is good and that evil is not an independent substance alongside goodness. To speak of damage, disorder and a need for healing is not to declare a person fundamentally worthless.

Bringing Huineng into the conversation should not turn it into a contest between slogans about clean and dirty human beings. Awakening and nonattachment in the tradition associated with Huineng do not mean erasing everything in a person that exceeds an account of them. Augustine's original sin does not mean labeling all such excess evil. The traditions pose different questions and envision different forms of release.

We can take away a narrower, more immediate question: why does knowing what I should do still fail to make me do it? Augustine offers a theological answer. The question remains available to someone who rejects that answer. Acknowledging limitation need not become hatred of the person who has limits.

V. More Effort Is Not the Whole Answer

Then make a firmer resolution. We know the advice: break the habit, leave the damaging relationship, admit the wrong you have avoided admitting. When the resolution fails, the easiest prescription is another dose of effort.

Augustine finds that prescription inadequate. If the person resolving to change is already pulled by conflicting wishes, a louder command will not necessarily mend the division. His claim is not that willing has vanished. It is that a self-sufficient will cannot accomplish its own salvation.

His hope lies in grace. God's gift is not a prize earned through good performance, nor assistance available only after the recipient has become worthy of it. In Augustine's faith, even the turn toward goodness and the willingness to receive help depend upon grace.

His thinking on grace developed through prolonged controversy. How freedom and grace belong together, and how a person acts within that relationship, remained questions for later Christian traditions too. Augustine continued to exhort, pray and call for action. Dependence on grace did not make a human response irrelevant.

Nor should a comparison with Wittgenstein set up two cartoon alternatives: one man changes entirely by himself, the other has no self left. Wittgenstein's revisions owed much to criticism from other people; Augustine's God is more than psychological support. A useful question survives the contrast: when I change, can I honestly credit every enabling force to myself?

A nonbeliever need not rename grace friendship or therapy to find something here. We can preserve its religious meaning while recognizing a more ordinary experience: seeking help need not be the opposite of agency. Sometimes action begins when we stop pretending we can do everything alone.

VI. Finishing a Song

In Book XI the question becomes time. We speak effortlessly of being early, being late or waiting a moment. Asked what time is, however, we can find those familiar words surprisingly difficult to explain.

What has passed is no longer here; what is coming is not here yet. The present slips into the past as we try to name it. How, then, do we judge one interval longer than another? What are we measuring?

Augustine asks us to consider reciting or singing something familiar. Before the song begins, it lies ahead in expectation. Partway through, some of it has entered memory while the rest remains to be sung. Attention carries what was expected into what is remembered. We never hold the song as one simultaneous block, yet we do experience it.

Memory, attention and expectation become ways into the experience of time. Augustine describes a stretching or distension of the mind. That is not the simple assertion that external time does not exist, nor an early delivery of Kant's or Husserl's completed answers. Related questions illuminate one another without making their thinkers interchangeable.

This is closer to the man in the garden than it first appears. Saying not yet, he assigns hope to the future while letting habit govern the present. We do more than move through time. We understand ourselves between memory and expectation, making promises or negotiating extensions.

In his faith, God's eternity is not an infinitely lengthened timeline. We may disagree without pretending a quick objection has settled the entire question. At least the song gives us this: explaining a life and holding that whole life at once are different things.

VII. One Question, Different Paths

The rationalizations and incompatible wishes invite comparison with Freud, and with the people in Dostoevsky's novels. Sometimes the more solemnly someone explains himself, the more clearly another voice becomes audible beneath the explanation.

The encounter is worth preserving. But Augustine is not a psychoanalyst in ancient dress. Wanting and not wanting to change need not map neatly onto a battle between consciousness and the unconscious. Many of the desires in the Confessions are painfully well known to their owner.

The pear story includes the pleasures of transgression and companionship. Freud would also ask why people repeat painful experiences; the problems developed around repetition compulsion in Beyond the Pleasure Principle already take us beyond a choice between wanting the theft and wanting the fruit.

Augustine investigates will, love, sin and grace. Freud develops another account of conflict and mental processes. Dostoevsky makes characters inhabit their difficulties in stories. They have not simply discovered an identical basement and given its furniture different names.

A reader can nevertheless move among these books with a shared question: does my explanation of myself already contain the person I would like others to see? We do not have to adopt an entire theory before asking. We only have to slow down, next time, before declaring that we know exactly why we acted.

VIII. Two Cities, Not Two Maps

Rome was sacked by the Visigoths in 410. Some blamed the abandonment of the old gods for Christianity. Augustine began The City of God in 413 and completed its twenty-two books around 426. The work grew far beyond an immediate defense against those accusations.

He speaks of two cities through two orientations of love: self-exaltation reaching contempt for God, and love of God displacing the supremacy of one's own glory. City here does not mean a map with Rome colored red and the church blue. The distinction is not simply state versus church.

Their citizens are intermixed in history, sharing visible societies. We do not know everyone's final allegiance; visible church membership does not supply a guarantee of salvation. This is different from saying one person simultaneously belongs to both cities. Inner conflict does not erase the distinction between their ultimate orientations.

Nor does the city of God exist only at history's end. It has a pilgrim life in the present, while its fulfillment belongs to the future Augustine awaits in faith. Rome's fate and Christian hope are thereby separated. No empire provides an eternal insurance policy.

There is a sharp warning here against allowing an existing order to occupy the place of an ultimate answer. Yet Augustine also came to defend coercive measures to bring religious dissenters into the church. His searching self-examination did not make his treatment of other people's freedom consistently generous. We need to read the insight together with the difficulty its author leaves us.

Reading Hegel, Marx or Augustine alongside one another calls for distinctions among their arguments, related institutional practices and the hopes they hold out. These are different things. We can ask how a hope enters power and how it treats those who dissent. Comparison then returns from a list of winners to the difficulties of actual life.

IX. The Book Remains Open

Augustine died in Hippo on 28 August 430, aged seventy-five, while a Vandal army besieged the city in what is now Algeria. His thinking had not ended in the garden of 386. Extensive work on the Trinity, free choice, scripture and history still lay ahead.

Late in life he even revisited his own writings in the Retractations, reviewing and correcting earlier claims. Faith had given him a direction, not a blanket exemption from thinking again. Later readers can still encounter a thinker engaged in revision.

His influence extended through the Middle Ages into the Reformation; Luther had been an Augustinian friar. For a reader unready to follow the whole theology, however, what stays first may be a hesitation: I want to change, but not yet.

Now let him arrive at this series' imagined bridgehead, carrying an open Confessions. Wittgenstein's two books lie nearby. Neither man has to defeat the other, and neither can end the other's thinking. One reopens answers already given; the other questions again the self those answers had helped explain.

We need not accept original sin to be moved by tears in a garden. Nor must we pronounce people permanently unclean to acknowledge the difficulty of change. Owning a harm, relinquishing a respectable excuse, asking someone for help: these can be beginnings, not rewards reserved for those who already qualify.

The book remains open. Perhaps a whole life cannot be put in order today. But the date to which we keep postponing it might move a little closer.

Notes

The garden, pears, memory and time discussions draw on Confessions VIII, II, X and XI respectively. These are retrospective accounts. Original sin and grace are presented as Augustine's theological positions, not verdicts on human worth. The bridgehead is a literary invention.
