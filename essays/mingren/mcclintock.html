麦克林托克：她先感觉到了

McClintock: She Felt It First

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、三十年

一穗玉米上，有的籽粒颜色均匀，有的带着斑点。对芭芭拉·麦克林托克来说，那一点不同值得记下来，种下去，再看它的后代。斑点还不是答案，却可能是一个问题的开头。

1950年。她发表了多年追踪这些差异的成果：染色体上有些遗传元件，并不是一直待在原位的。它们会移动。位置的改变，还可能影响别的基因怎样表现出来。

其中两个被她称为Ac和Ds。Ds的某些活动需要Ac在场。后来，“转座子”“跳跃基因”这些名字走出了实验室，人们更熟悉后一个：基因会跳。

想象一串珠子。珠子的次序可以标在纸上，每一颗都有自己的位置。这是理解遗传的一种有力方式。麦克林托克追问的是：如果其中有些东西会换位置，那张图还够不够？

她不是一个突然闯进来的外行。1931年，她与哈丽特·克赖顿已把玉米的遗传重组和染色体片段的实际交换联系起来。1944年，她当选美国国家科学院院士；1945年，成为美国遗传学会会长。还没有发现跳跃基因的时候，她已经让同行学会了怎样看染色体。

可是，人们尊重你的眼睛，不等于马上能跟着你的眼睛走到更远的地方。

转座本身在五十年代就得到玉米遗传学家的确认。难以走出去的，是更大的问题：这只是玉米中的特殊现象，还是广泛存在于生命中的事情？这些元件的活动，又能在多大程度上解释基因的控制与发育？对于后一个问题，她的解释并没有随转座的确认一并成为共识。

从玉米到其他生物，从遗传图谱到分子机制，这条路慢慢延伸。没有一个时刻，全世界一起把旧图扔掉，换上新图。

1983年。她八十一岁，独享诺贝尔生理学或医学奖，因发现移动遗传元件获奖。

从1950年到1983年，三十三年。不是三十三年无人听见，也不是一张奖状终于证明了她所有的想法。只是当这个名字进入无数人的视野时，她已经和玉米待了大半辈子。

她谈起获奖，却觉得有些不好意思。那些年，向玉米提问题，看它怎样回答，本来就给了她那么多乐趣。现在还要为这件事奖励她。

别人看到迟来的奖。她说起的，还是这些年怎样过。

二、对有机体的感觉

也是1983年，伊芙琳·福克斯·凯勒出版了她的传记：《对有机体的感觉》。A Feeling for the Organism。

感觉。一个严格的细胞遗传学家，谈起自己怎样工作时，用了这个词。

她回忆看染色体的经验：看得久了，自己仿佛越来越小，眼前的东西越来越大。不再是一个站在外面的人隔着镜片看。她仿佛走了进去，来到它们旁边。那些染色体，她甚至称作朋友。

这段话动人的地方，不在于她获得了一种别人没有的神秘视力。而在于，一个人能熟悉一种事物，熟悉到原先看起来差不多的地方，渐渐全有了差别。

初学者在显微镜下找轮廓。她已经能辨认玉米的不同染色体，看出它们少了什么、多了什么，哪一段的位置不对。别人眼中的一片模糊，在她那里有了来历。

研究转座以前，她追过染色体的断裂—融合—桥循环：断裂的末端融合，分裂时又被拉开，再断裂。下一批细胞由此得到不同的染色体材料。她学着把显微镜下的变化，与种子和植株上出现的斑块对应起来。

一块颜色，可能留着一段细胞分裂的痕迹。但颜色不会自己说出原因。得种下去，做杂交，追后代，再回来核对。她的熟悉，就是在这些往返里长出来的。

然后，某一天，一粒籽让她停下来。

还没有完整解释，已经觉得这里值得追。眼前的差异，似乎与过去见过的某些东西连在一起。该挑哪株、怎样杂交、下一次到哪里找，她开始有了方向。

她先感觉到了。

这个“先”，前面已经站着许多年。不是先有感觉，才第一次观察；是观察与操作积累到某个时候，方向先于最后的说明浮出来。

感觉让她再看一眼。证据还要跟上。1953年的论文里有杂交、后代测试，也有暂时不能下定论的地方。

她把自己放得很近，却没有让玉米失去回答“不”的机会。

三、她和狄拉克

前面写过狄拉克。他沿着方程往下走，走到了实验尚未确认的地方。麦克林托克沿着玉米的差异往下走，也走到一个还没有被完整说明的地方。

两个人都曾先抓住一个方向，再等检验慢慢跟上。但他们手里拿的东西不一样。

狄拉克拿着方程。符号之间的要求、对称与不协调，会把问题推向下一步。你不用伸手摸到电子，才能发现纸上的东西值得继续追。

麦克林托克的手上有花粉。

她得种玉米，知道亲本是谁，认得眼前这一株。还得制片，把眼睛放到目镜后面，让田间的变化与染色体的变化一次次对上。一个季节不够，就等下一个季节。

她的感觉不是从天上掉下来的。它从身体跟世界长期待在一起的经验里长出来。

这也让上一章费希特的问题多了一幅画面。认知者不是可以从认知里删掉的括号。那么，认知者在哪里？

在这里。弯在显微镜前。走在田垄上。记得去年一株玉米，今天又看见它的后代。

费希特的“我”本来就有身体，也面对他人和限制；麦克林托克并不是替一个无身体的哲学补上四肢。她让我们看到的是：这个身体怎样一点点学会分辨。

后来在本系列里读到梅洛-庞蒂时，我们还会回到这里。不是用一个科学家的经历替哲学证明一条普遍定律，而是记住，认知有时候从这么具体的地方开始。

眼睛会累。手法会生疏。熟悉也会带来误认。但如果把这些全部擦掉，只剩下一条已经写好的命题，我们也就看不见，她怎样走到写下它的地方。

四、被否定

麦克林托克遭遇的阻力，和她是女性有关系吗？

这个问题不能拿一句“天才总是孤独”挡过去。她求学、找工作的时候，女性可以走的路并不和男性一样宽。一个人能不能进门，进门后能不能留下，能不能按自己的方向做事，本来就影响着我们最后读到什么科学。

但她的经历也不是一张可以随手填满的受难表。后来流传的“康奈尔不收她读植物育种研究生”“密苏里因她是女性拒绝给她终身教职”，经档案研究重新核查，都不能照原样讲。密苏里还曾提出更好的待遇与长期职位来挽留她。

她去了冷泉港。那里给她的研究条件，更接近她想要的生活。

思想被接受的过程，也比“她是对的，别人就是不肯听”曲折。有同行确认了转座，却不接受她更大的控制理论。听到了一个发现，与看见这个发现可能伸向哪里，是两回事；而伸向远处的猜想，也确实需要新的证据。

这仍然会让人失望。你以为终于把一件事讲清楚了，对方却只接住其中一小块。你想请他再向前看，他觉得事情到这里已经结束。

我想到前面写过的梵高、康托尔、特斯拉。不是说他们有同一段遭遇，更不是说只要不被理解，就一定是天才。

梵高的画怎样得到一双愿意停留的眼睛，康托尔的问题怎样得到继续讨论的空间，特斯拉的计划怎样遇到资金与商业的门槛——这些故事问的是：一件尚未被放进现成位置的工作，靠什么继续存在？

到了麦克林托克这里，问题变得很安静。你还在做。别人尊重你，却未必愿意追你正在追的那个问题。

尊重和理解之间，也可能隔着很多年。

我们不必为了写出这段距离，把她变成一个三十年没有同行、没有读者、没有支持的人。她的坚持不需要这样的背景才有分量。

五、她停了又没停

麦克林托克曾减少某些公开交流，不愿再把一些复杂的工作反复讲给尚未准备好接住它的人听。

但她没有在1953年停止发表。那年的长篇论文之后，1956年有《控制元件与基因》，1961年又有比较玉米与细菌基因控制系统的文章。翻开书目，会看见她仍在写，仍在试着把自己的问题带给别人。

她停下的，不是全部工作。甚至也不是全部解释。

一个人可以继续做自己认为重要的事，却不再把每一次不被理解，都变成一次必须当场赢下的辩论。

她继续种玉米。继续看后代。继续记录这一株和上一株之间的差异。关于明年要种什么的问题，不会因为今年的报告没有得到期待的回应，就跟着消失。

在访谈里，她说起一种很深的确信：觉得自己走在值得走的路上，别人就很难把自己赶下来。

这样的确信能让人留下来，却不能替留下来以后要做的实验交卷。她还得允许下一季的材料使自己改口。否则，所谓坚持，只剩下每天重复同一个答案。

冷泉港的长期研究职位也在托住这件事。土地、设备、时间，较少的教学和行政负担，让她可以一次次回来。她的独立不是靠没有任何支持换来的。有人给不急着见效的工作留了地方。

写到这里，我又想到屈原。

屈原与渔父之间，留下了一个难题：当周围的世界不能接住你，你怎样不把自己交出去？麦克林托克在另一种生活里，给我们看见一种漫长的坚持。

用时间凿。

这不是拿两个结局比赛，更不需要把死亡写成忠于自己的最高证明。她留下来。种下去。让自己认准的问题，再经历一个季节。也让它遇见自己尚未想到的回答。

承认慢慢变化。玉米也一季一季地长。她不必等到全世界点头，才开始过自己的日子。

六、tacit knowing

麦克林托克的“对有机体的感觉”，让人想到波兰尼说的tacit knowing：默会的认识。有些事情，我们会做，却不能把怎样做到的全部说清楚。

你会骑自行车。车身刚偏，手已经微调，身体已经找回平衡。让你逐项说出哪块肌肉用了多少力、重心移了几厘米，你说不出来。

这不妨碍你骑过一条街。反过来，把一份关于平衡的说明书背下来，也不保证你明天就骑得稳。

麦克林托克的工作当然比骑车复杂得多。但那个“会了，却未必能列尽步骤”的层次，也在里面。哪一种差异值得追，怎样把多年材料放在一起看，什么时候需要回去检查自己的手法——有些判断，很难装进一份操作清单。

“基因会移动”本身却不是说不出口的秘密。她已经写了出来，别人也能够重复、讨论。DNA双螺旋结构到1953年才提出，不等于那以前的人没有语言谈论遗传现象。

说不尽的，是走到那句话之前和旁边的许多功夫。不是等了几十年，才终于给身体里一个现成答案起了名字。

这使本乐章的路又往前伸了一点。

从孔德的整理、波普尔的划线，到狄拉克对方程的敏感、屈原的诗、费希特追问那个说“我”的人，我们一直在靠近一个问题：一段已经说出的知识，底下还站着什么？

这不是把孔德、波普尔写成只认条文、不认人的裁判。这里的次序，是我们读这些人的次序。读到麦克林托克，纸面下那双训练过的眼睛，变得更清楚了。

波兰尼在本乐章收篇时，会让这件事继续展开。能够说出的知识，依赖许多没有在同一句话里说尽的判断与能力。

可以把她的论文想成房子，把那些年养成的感觉想成地基。地基不都露在外面，但房子不能没有它。

这栋房子也会反过来让她检查地基。写出来，交给别人，发现一个比例对不上，再回到材料前面。感觉支持论文，论文和实验也训练、修正感觉。

不是一次神秘的到达。是一个人这样来来回回，越来越会看。

七、一个人

一个人跟玉米待了大半辈子。

这句话很容易让人先想到孤独：仿佛她退出了人的世界，把一种植物拿来填补生活。可是她谈起工作时，那种投入与快乐，又很难放进这个解释。

我们不必替她的私人生活补上一个缺口，再宣布玉米把它补好了。她有同行、朋友和自己的生活。她也确实能在看染色体的时候，感到自己就在它们中间。

这种亲近，让“客观”这个词有了另一种质地。

客观不是没有感情。你可以非常在意眼前的东西，同时认真记录它怎样与你预想的不一样。亲近之所以有用，有时恰恰是因为你认得它，才知道这一次不能混过去。

距离可以帮助我们看，也可能变成一堵墙。站得太远，每株都只是玉米，每粒都只是籽。走近以后，这一株不是那一株，这个斑点也不能直接塞进昨天的解释。

但走近，不等于让对象变成自己想要的样子。越熟悉，越要当心自己舍不得一个好解释。允许它不配合，才不只是把感情投在它身上。

下一篇，薇依会把我们带到注意力面前。

她面对人的苦难，麦克林托克面对实验材料。两件事并不相同。但这里有一个动作值得记住：先把那个急着替眼前事物作答的自己，往旁边让一点。

不是把自己抹掉。是别让自己的需要、预期和现成分类，把对方整个遮住。

麦克林托克说自己仿佛进入染色体，并不是说她真的从一株玉米的意识里向外看。她在描述一种投入：熟悉到足以接近，也耐心到还肯发现不熟悉的地方。

她没有因为知道得多，就把它看完了。

八、桥头

现在，让她走进本系列想象中的桥头。

麦克林托克走过来的时候，手里拿着一穗玉米。

不是超市里那种颜色整齐的甜玉米。上面有紫色，有黄色，有杂色。每一处不同，都可能让她想起亲本、杂交、某一年种过的材料。穗上的颜色不是现成的答案，是许多问题留下的痕迹。

她把玉米穗举起来。看了看。微笑了。

孔德扫了一眼。生物学。他在自己的知识次序里，为它找到了位置。

麦克林托克没有反对。她把有斑点的那一面转过来。放进一个格子以后，这一粒和旁边那一粒之间的差别，还在那里。

波普尔走近一点：“你对它的感觉，也会错吧？”

“会。”

“那怎么办？”

“再种。”

她说得很快。又添了一句：“再看后代。”

她不把玉米穗收回去。让他看。

狄拉克一直看着那些颜色。他们手里的东西不一样，却都认识那种时刻：还没有最后的答案，已经觉得有一条路值得追。

她看看他，笑了一下：“只是我的会发芽。”

费希特站在缝隙旁，看看她沾着泥土的手。那个不能从认知中删去的人，此刻有了手指、目光，以及一年一年留在身体里的工作。

屈原从水边抬起头。他闻到了什么。不是兰花。是泥土和花粉的气味，从她的手上飘过来。

他用诗跟世界待在一起。她用显微镜，也用田地。通道不一样，他们都没有把眼前的东西看成一件已经结束的事。

她在桥头找了一个位置。不是站着的。是蹲着的。

她蹲下来，把那穗玉米放在桥面上，一粒一粒地看。

紫色。黄色。杂色。杂色。紫色。

桥上的人还在说话。她的目光停在其中一粒上。

她还是蹲着。看着那些玉米粒。微笑着。

注释

转座在玉米遗传学中的早期确认、后来跨物种与分子层面的认识、麦克林托克较广的控制理论，须分别看待。“三十年”指发现走向广泛公众认识的漫长历程，不是三十年无人理解或她停止发表的时期；1953、1956、1961年的论文与冷泉港书目可核对。康奈尔、密苏里的具体任职传闻参Lee Kass的档案研究，不能据此把性别影响一概否认或归为唯一原因。

“感觉”“进入”“朋友”的叙述参凯勒的传记与所录访谈，描述长期实验训练中的投入，不指感觉在一切观察之前或无需验证。本文与狄拉克、费希特、屈原、波兰尼、梅洛-庞蒂和薇依的连读是作者的解释安排，不是同一理论的证明，也不是单线思想传承；“地基”是比喻。

第八节桥头的会面、动作与对话为文学想象。籽粒的颜色变化有不同机制，不能把所有杂色直接当作转座的证明；获奖也不使一种解释免于继续检验。

I. Thirty Years

On an ear of maize, one kernel is evenly colored; another is spotted. For Barbara McClintock, that difference was worth recording, planting, and following into another generation. A spot was not yet an answer. It might be the beginning of a question.

In 1950, she published results from years spent following such differences. Some genetic elements did not stay in a fixed position on the chromosome. They could move. A change of position could also affect how other genes were expressed.

Two of these elements she called Ac and Ds. Certain activities of Ds depended on Ac being present. Later, transposons and jumping genes became familiar names. The second was easier to picture: something in the genetic arrangement could jump.

Imagine beads on a string. You can map their order, assign each a position. It is a powerful way to understand inheritance. McClintock’s question was what happened to that map if some of its contents could change places.

She was no outsider arriving with an inspired guess. In 1931, she and Harriet Creighton had linked genetic recombination in maize to the physical exchange of chromosome segments. She entered the National Academy of Sciences in 1944 and became president of the Genetics Society of America in 1945. Before the jumping genes, she had already helped her colleagues learn how to see chromosomes.

But respecting someone’s eye does not mean being ready to follow it wherever it leads.

Maize geneticists confirmed transposition during the 1950s. The larger questions traveled less easily. Was this a peculiarity of maize or something widespread in living organisms? How much could these elements explain about gene control and development? Acceptance of transposition did not settle those questions or establish her broader theory of control.

The path extended gradually: from maize to other organisms, from genetic maps to molecular mechanisms. There was no morning when the entire scientific world threw out one map and took up another.

In 1983, at eighty-one, she received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, as its sole recipient that year, for discovering mobile genetic elements.

Thirty-three years from 1950 to 1983. Not thirty-three years without a listener, nor an eventual certificate that every idea of hers had been right. But by the time her name reached that much larger public, she had spent most of a lifetime with maize.

Speaking about the award, she seemed almost embarrassed. Asking maize questions and seeing how it answered had already given her so much enjoyment. Now she was to be rewarded for it as well.

Others saw the late arrival of a prize. She spoke about the years themselves.

II. A Feeling for the Organism

Also in 1983, Evelyn Fox Keller published her biography of McClintock: A Feeling for the Organism.

Feeling. A rigorous cytogeneticist used that word when describing how she worked.

She recalled looking at chromosomes until she seemed to grow smaller and they grew larger. She no longer felt like someone outside, peering through a lens. It was as though she had stepped in beside them. She even called the chromosomes friends.

What moves me in this account is not the suggestion of a supernatural faculty. It is how familiar someone can become with something: places that once looked much alike gradually fill with differences.

A beginner struggles to make out shapes under the microscope. McClintock could identify the different maize chromosomes and notice what was missing, added, or out of place. What blurred together for another observer had a history for her.

Before transposition, she had followed the breakage–fusion–bridge cycle. Broken chromosome ends could fuse; division could pull the resulting bridge apart, producing another break. Descendant cells received different amounts of chromosome material. She learned to connect these changes with patches appearing on kernels and plants.

A patch of color might carry the trace of a cell division. But color did not announce its own cause. Plant it, make crosses, follow the offspring, then check again. Her familiarity grew through those journeys back and forth.

Then, one day, a kernel made her stop.

There was no complete explanation yet, but something was worth pursuing. A difference seemed connected to things she had seen before. Which plant to select, what cross to make, where to look next: a direction began to emerge.

She felt it first.

Years of work stood behind that first. Feeling did not precede all observation. Observation and practice had accumulated until a direction emerged ahead of a finished account.

Feeling made her look again. Evidence still has to follow. Her 1953 paper contains crosses and progeny tests, as well as places where a firm conclusion could not yet be drawn.

She brought herself close without taking away the maize’s ability to answer no.

III. McClintock and Dirac

Earlier in this series, Dirac followed an equation beyond what experiment had confirmed. McClintock followed differences in maize toward something not yet fully explained.

Both could find a direction before the final test arrived. But they held different things in their hands.

Dirac held equations. Relations between symbols, demands of symmetry, an inconsistency: these could press a problem forward. You did not have to reach out and touch an electron to find something on the page worth pursuing.

McClintock had pollen on her hands.

She had to grow the plants, know their parents, recognize the one before her. She had to prepare slides, put her eyes to the microscope, and bring what happened in the field into relation with what happened in chromosomes. If one season was not enough, there was the next.

Her feeling did not fall from the sky. It grew out of a body spending a long time with the world.

That gives the previous essay’s question, Fichte’s question, another setting. The knower is not a parenthesis we can remove from knowing. Where, then, is the knower?

Here. Bent over a microscope. Walking between rows. Remembering a plant from last year while looking at its descendants.

Fichte’s I already has a body and encounters other people and limits; McClintock is not attaching limbs to a bodiless philosophy. What she lets us see is how a particular body gradually learns to distinguish.

When this series reaches Merleau-Ponty, we will return to that scene. Not to make a scientist’s life prove a universal philosophical law, but to remember how concrete the beginnings of knowing can be.

Eyes tire. Technique falters. Familiarity can mislead. Yet erase all of that and leave only the finished proposition, and we lose sight of how she reached the point where she could write it down.

IV. Denied

Did gender have something to do with the resistance McClintock encountered?

We cannot brush the question aside by saying that genius is always lonely. When she studied and looked for work, the paths open to women were not as wide as those open to men. Who gets through a door, who is allowed to stay, who can pursue a chosen problem: these decisions shape the science we eventually read.

Her life is not, however, a blank ledger into which every familiar injustice can be entered. Archival research has challenged stories that Cornell refused to admit her to graduate plant breeding and that Missouri denied her tenure because she was a woman. Missouri even offered improved terms and a permanent position to retain her.

She went to Cold Spring Harbor. The conditions there came closer to the working life she wanted.

The reception of her ideas was also more complicated than she was right and nobody would listen. Colleagues could confirm transposition without accepting her wider theory of control. Accepting a discovery and seeing how far it might reach are different things. And the further claims do require further evidence.

That can still be disappointing. You think you have finally made something clear, but the other person takes away only a small part. You want them to look further. They think the matter is finished.

I think of Van Gogh, Cantor, and Tesla, whom we have met earlier. Not because their lives were the same, or because being misunderstood is a certificate of genius.

How does a painting find eyes willing to linger? How does a mathematical question obtain room for continued argument? What happens when an inventor’s plan meets the requirements of funding and business? In different ways, these lives ask how work without an established place can continue to exist.

With McClintock the question grows quiet. You are still working. Other people respect you, but may not want to follow the problem you are following.

Years can lie between respect and understanding.

We do not have to remove every colleague, reader, and source of support from her life to make that distance felt. Her persistence carries weight without such a background.

V. She Stopped and She Didn’t

McClintock did reduce some public exchanges. She grew reluctant to explain complicated work again and again to listeners who were not ready to engage with it.

But she did not stop publishing in 1953. After that year’s long paper came Controlling Elements and the Gene in 1956, then her comparison of gene control in maize and bacteria in 1961. Open the bibliography and she is still writing, still looking for ways to bring her questions to others.

What stopped was not all the work. It was not even all the explaining.

Someone can go on doing what matters to them without turning every failure to be understood into an argument that must be won before the day is over.

She went on planting maize, examining offspring, recording differences between one plant and another. The question of what to plant next year did not disappear because this year’s presentation had failed to produce the response she hoped for.

In interviews, she spoke of a deep confidence: once you felt yourself on a path worth following, it was hard for other people to drive you off it.

That confidence could help her stay. It could not do the experiments that staying required. The next season’s material still had to be allowed to change her mind. Otherwise persistence would become the daily repetition of an answer.

Her long-term research position at Cold Spring Harbor helped sustain this life. Land, equipment, time, fewer teaching and administrative demands: these allowed her to return to the work. Her independence was not independence from all support. An institution had made room for research that did not promise a quick result.

Here I think again of Qu Yuan.

Between Qu Yuan and the fisherman lies a difficult question: when the surrounding world cannot receive what you bring, how do you avoid handing yourself over to it? In a very different life, McClintock shows us a long form of persistence.

Chiseling with time.

This is no contest between their endings. Death need not become the highest proof of fidelity to oneself. She stayed. Planted. Let a question she believed in pass through another season. Let it encounter answers she had not anticipated.

Recognition changed gradually. The maize grew season after season. She did not have to wait for the whole world’s agreement before living her days.

VI. Tacit Knowing

McClintock’s feeling for the organism brings us near what Michael Polanyi calls tacit knowing. There are things we can do without being able to give a complete account of how we do them.

You can ride a bicycle. As it tips, your hands adjust and your body recovers its balance. Ask which muscles exerted precisely how much force, or how far your center of gravity shifted, and you cannot say.

That does not prevent you from riding down the street. Conversely, memorizing a set of instructions about balance does not guarantee you will ride steadily tomorrow.

McClintock’s work was far more complex, but it contained that same layer of being able without being able to list every step. Which difference should be pursued? How can years of material be seen together? When should you go back and check your technique? Some judgments resist being packed into a complete checklist.

Genetic elements can move was not itself an unspeakable secret. She had written it down. Others could repeat the work and discuss it. The double-helical structure of DNA was proposed in 1953; that does not mean there had previously been no language for genetic phenomena.

What could not be exhaustively stated was much of the skilled work before and around that sentence. An already complete answer had not lain locked in her body for decades, waiting for someone to invent its name.

The path through this movement now reaches a little further.

From Comte’s ordering and Popper’s line, through Dirac’s ear for an equation, Qu Yuan’s poetry, and Fichte’s question about the person saying I, we have been approaching a question of our own. What stands beneath knowledge already put into words?

That does not make Comte and Popper judges who recognize only written rules and never people. This sequence is our reading itinerary. With McClintock, the trained eyes behind the page become harder to miss.

Polanyi will pursue this further when he closes the movement. Explicit knowledge depends on judgments and capacities that are not exhausted by the sentence in which it is expressed.

Think of her papers as a house, and the feeling developed through years of work as its foundations. The foundations are not all in view. The house nevertheless needs them.

The house also gives her reasons to inspect its foundations. Write the account, let others examine it, find that a ratio does not fit, return to the material. Feeling supports the paper; papers and experiments, in turn, train and correct the feeling.

Not one mysterious arrival. A person going back and forth, learning to see.

VII. Alone

A person spends most of a lifetime with maize.

It is easy to hear loneliness in that sentence, as though she had withdrawn from people and used a plant to fill the space. Yet the involvement and pleasure with which she spoke of her work do not readily fit that account.

We need not invent a gap in her private life and announce that maize repaired it. She had colleagues, friends, a life of her own. She also knew what it was to look at chromosomes and feel herself among them.

That closeness gives the word objective a different texture.

Objectivity does not mean having no feeling. You can care intensely about something and still record, carefully, how it differs from what you expected. Sometimes intimacy helps precisely because you know the material too well to let this difference pass.

Distance can help us see. It can also become a wall. Too far away, every plant is maize, every kernel a seed. Come closer: this plant is not that one, and this spot will not simply fit yesterday’s explanation.

Closeness does not mean making the object what you want it to be. The more familiar it becomes, the more you may need to watch your attachment to an attractive explanation. Allow it not to cooperate. Otherwise you are only projecting your feelings onto it.

In the next essay, Weil will bring us to attention.

She faces human suffering; McClintock works with experimental material. These are not the same encounter. Still, a gesture here is worth remembering: move aside, a little, the self already rushing to answer for what stands before it.

Not erase yourself. Do not let your needs, expectations, and ready-made categories hide everything else.

When McClintock described entering the chromosomes, she was not claiming to look outward from a plant’s consciousness. She was describing an absorption: familiar enough to draw close, patient enough to go on discovering what was unfamiliar.

Knowing so much had not brought her looking to an end.

VIII. The Bridgehead

Now let her enter this series’ imagined bridgehead.

McClintock arrives carrying an ear of maize.

Not the evenly colored sweetcorn of a supermarket. There is purple, yellow, mottling. A difference might recall a parent, a cross, material she planted in a particular year. The colors are not ready-made answers. They are traces left by many questions.

She raises the ear. Looks at it. Smiles.

Comte glances over. Biology. He finds a place for it in his ordering of knowledge.

McClintock does not object. She turns the spotted side toward him. Even after it has been given a place, the difference between this kernel and its neighbor remains.

Popper steps closer. “Your feeling about it can be wrong?”

“Yes.”

“Then what?”

“Plant again.”

She answers quickly. Then adds: “Look at the offspring.”

She does not take the ear away. He can look.

Dirac has been watching the colors. They hold different things in their hands, but both recognize a moment when there is no final answer and already a path seems worth pursuing.

She looks at him and smiles. “Mine happens to grow.”

Fichte stands beside the crack and looks at the earth on her hands. The person who cannot be removed from knowing now has fingers, a gaze, and years of work carried in her body.

Qu Yuan looks up from the water’s edge. There is a smell. Not orchids. Earth and pollen, coming from her hands.

He stays with the world through poetry; she through a microscope, and a field. Different passages. Neither treats what is before them as something finished.

She finds a place at the bridgehead. Not standing. Crouching.

She crouches, sets the ear on the bridge, and looks at it kernel by kernel.

Purple. Yellow. Mottled. Mottled. Purple.

The people on the bridge are still talking. Her gaze pauses on one kernel.

She is still crouching. Looking at the kernels. Smiling.
