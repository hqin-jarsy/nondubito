麦克林托克：她先感觉到了

McClintock: She Felt It First

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、一粒不同的玉米

一穗玉米上，有的籽粒颜色均匀，有的带着斑点。对芭芭拉·麦克林托克来说，那一点不同值得记下来，种下去，再看它的后代。斑点还不是答案，却可能是一个问题的开头。

1950年，芭芭拉·麦克林托克发表《玉米中易变位点的起源与行为》。她处理的不是一句后来才会流行起来的口号“基因会跳”，而是一套已经被她追踪多年的染色体异常：Dissociation（Ds）和Activator（Ac）能够改变位置，并在特定条件下改变邻近基因的表达。1953年的论文写得更直白：这些染色体单位“可以从一个位置移到另一个位置”，而Ds的某些作用依赖Ac存在。

这项工作不是凭一张杂色玉米穗突然猜出来的。早在1931年，她和Harriet Creighton已经用玉米把遗传重组与染色体片段的物理交换联系起来。到1944年，她当选美国国家科学院院士；1945年又成为美国遗传学学会第一位女性会长。发现控制元件以前，她已经是这个领域的重要细胞遗传学家。

这段训练还有一条后来容易被“跳跃基因”故事遮住的线。三十年代末到四十年代初，她反复追踪染色体断端怎样进入所谓断裂—融合—桥循环：断裂后的末端在复制后融合，形成具有两个着丝粒的结构；细胞分裂时两个着丝粒被拉向相反方向，染色体形成桥，再次断裂；下一代细胞于是继承不等量的染色体材料。她还利用9号染色体上的遗传标记，把某些断裂后果变成籽粒或植株上可以看见的斑块。于是，一块颜色不是装饰。它可能保存着一段细胞分裂史的痕迹。

这对后来理解Ac/Ds很重要。斑驳本身不会自动告诉她是哪一种机制起了作用。她得把那粒异常籽种下去，做杂交，追后代，定位位点，再到显微镜下看染色体；还要问，可见表型的变化是否真的跟染色体位置的变化一起发生。现代对这些实验的重建甚至能把几类不同籽粒图样，对回不同的断裂与转位事件。所谓“看见”，从来已经是一条从眼睛到田地、到显微镜、到谱系记录、再返回眼睛的链。

转座这件事并没有被玉米遗传学家整整否认三十年。历史学家Nathaniel Comfort根据档案、访谈和论文重新检查过这段历史：转座本身很快得到确认，五十年代末已经被视为经典发现；真正长期没有被广泛接受的，是麦克林托克更宏大的“控制系统”解释——这些移动元件怎样参与基因调控、发育与基因组重组，以及这种现象到底有多普遍。

1950到1983，三十三年。不是三十三年里没有一个人听见；而是从一个专业群体已经能确认的玉米现象，到移动遗传元件成为跨物种、进入分子遗传学中心的问题，再到诺贝尔奖把这一发现写进公共科学史，经历了很长的尺度变化。六十年代起，细菌和噬菌体中的插入序列等工作不断把“移动”从玉米带向更大的遗传学地图。

1983年，她八十一岁，独享诺贝尔生理学或医学奖，是第一位独享这个奖项的女性。她在获奖声明里没有把三十多年写成苦难的证明，而是说，因多年“向玉米提出具体问题并观看它的回答”而得到奖励，仿佛有点不公平——因为其中有太多乐趣。

怎样和一种材料相处，才能慢慢看出别人尚未注意到的差异？

二、对有机体的感觉

1983年，Evelyn Fox Keller出版《对有机体的感觉》。书名来自对麦克林托克研究方式的概括，也保存了她接受访谈时一些很特别的语言。她说，长期观察染色体时，她会觉得自己“不在外面”，而是“系统的一部分”；染色体甚至像“朋友”。

这些话很容易被写成神秘主义，也很容易被写成反神秘主义的新神话：仿佛她先闭上眼睛“感觉”出答案，后来才拿显微镜补一张证明。

两种写法都太快。

她所谓的感觉，长在技术里。

是识别每一条玉米染色体的形态，是设计杂交，是记住亲本，是判断一粒杂色籽粒值得不值得继续种下去，是在后代里重新检验，是把细胞学的结构和遗传学的性状一遍遍对回去。1953年的论文不是“我感觉如此”；里面有预测、有选择位点、有杂交数目、有后代测试，还有当结果不够时明确写下“不能作出确定结论”。

因此，一穗玉米既不是插图，也不是答案表。几百粒籽可以并排长在同一穗上，斑块的大小、数量与位置有时能给出一个体细胞事件大约发生在发育哪一阶段的线索；可是同一种肉眼差异，也可能由不同遗传路径造成。熟练的眼睛之所以重要，恰恰因为它知道：外观必须被带进杂交、定位和染色体工作，才能变成解释。

她早先研究断裂—融合—桥循环时已经练过同一种纪律：种子上能看见的图样，只有在亲本、标记、细胞分裂和后代都被追踪之后，才可以被读作染色体行为留下的痕迹。

所以标题里的“先感觉到”，不能理解成感觉在所有观察之前。

在时间上，观察已经进行了很多年。

“先”更接近另一种次序：在一项尚不能被一句规则完整说清的熟练活动里，研究者先感到“这里不对”“这个模式值得跟”“这些现象应该连在一起”；然后再把这种定向变成可以让别人检查的实验、图表、遗传关系和论文。

感觉不是证据的替身。

它更像注意力的方向感。

方向感可以极其重要，也可以错。

三、她和狄拉克

狄拉克的方程曾走在相应的实验发现前面。它带着已有理论和经验的积累，指向尚待确认的东西。

麦克林托克的“先”更不一样。

狄拉克面对的是已有量子理论、相对论和电子问题形成的数学约束；麦克林托克面对的是田地、显微镜、染色、杂交谱系和一株株不完全相同的玉米。两个人都可能在最终确认之前抓到一个尚未完成的方向，但不能因此说他们拥有同一种“先验知识”。

费希特笔下，自由的人有身体，也在行动中遭遇限制和他人。到了麦克林托克这里，身体有了一幅更具体的工作图景。

手上有花粉。

眼睛在显微镜目镜后面疲劳。

脚走过田垄。

记忆把今年这株和去年的后代接起来。

身体不是自动产生真理的器官。它也会误认，也会疲劳，也会把熟悉当成理所当然。

但科研实践从来不是只有一句命题在真空里接受检验。有人要学会怎样制片、怎样辨认、怎样选材料、怎样判断异常是噪音还是入口。麦克林托克让这个层次变得很难忽略。

她没有证明“身体先于概念知道”是一条普遍定律。

她留下的是一个更小、也更坚硬的事实：有些判断只有在长期熟练地与对象相处之后，才会变得可做。

四、到底什么被拒绝了

麦克林托克的故事常被讲成两个版本。

一个版本说：她是女性，所以男性科学界三十年不肯接受她。

另一个版本说：性别完全无关，只是她的理论证据不足。

档案使这两个整齐答案都变得困难。

女性在二十世纪上半叶的美国科学界确实面对制度性限制；麦克林托克自己的教育与职业也发生在这样的世界里。但一些后来广泛流传的具体故事——例如“康奈尔拒绝让她进入植物育种研究生项目”“密苏里因为她是女人拒绝给她终身教职”——被Lee Kass根据校档和人事材料重新检查后认为并不成立。密苏里后来甚至提出加薪和终身安排挽留她；她去了冷泉港，因为独立研究环境更合适。

同时，也不能因此说性别问题从她的一生消失。她所在时代的职位结构、对女性职业的预期、可获得的学术路径，本来就不是中性的。今天关于她与Jacob、Monod的1961年交流，仍有历史学家从“Matilda effect”等角度讨论性别是否参与了对其“控制”贡献的低估；Comfort则强调，转座被接受得早，争议主要在更广的控制理论。两种解释都需要具体证据，不能只选一边当寓言。

更关键的是：“被否定”也必须拆开。

移动元件是否存在？

Ac/Ds在玉米里怎样工作？

移动是否普遍？

这些元件是否构成一种普遍的基因调控理论？

基因组在压力下会不会重组自身？

这不是一个是非按钮。

1950年代，玉米遗传学家能够接受其中一些层面，而对另一些保持距离。1961年，麦克林托克主动把自己的系统和Jacob、Monod的细菌调控模型放在一起比较；她仍在发表、仍在争论、仍在调整语言。

一个人后来获奖，不反向证明她早先每一个解释都对。

一个共同体起初没有接受某个更大的解释，也不自动证明共同体只是因为狭隘。

真正值得看的，是具体哪一层证据已经够了，哪一层还没有。

五、继续种，继续写

1953年她在Genetics发表二十一页的《玉米中特定位点不稳定性的诱导》。1954、1955、1956、1957、1958仍有持续报告；1956年有《控制元件与基因》，1961年又发表《玉米与细菌基因控制系统的一些平行》。冷泉港数字库列出的她的作品一直延续到更晚。

冷泉港也不该被写成一个“孤独天才退到田里”的神话。1941年她先受邀在那里度过夏天，随后取得Carnegie的长期研究职位。这个环境给了她少见的独立性：没有固定的教学负担，也较少行政事务。1967年正式退休后，她仍以Distinguished Service Member身份继续在那里工作；从到来到晚年，她和这个机构的关系延续了大约半个世纪。换句话说，她的独立并不是“没有制度”。恰恰有一个制度，在物质上给了这种独立以时间和空间。

她确实曾因为同行理解困难而减少某些公开交流，也有同事回忆她在五六十年代对发表失去兴趣。可是“减少”“不再把某些复杂数据反复拿出去讲”和“1953以后停止发表”不是同一件事。

她继续种、继续观察，把自己认为有联系的现象交给新的材料检验。至于向谁解释、在哪个场合再讲一次，她也有自己的取舍。坚持研究，不等于必须回答每一个人。

1983年前后的采访保存了一句很有麦克林托克气质的话：如果你知道自己走在正确的路上，别人很难把你关掉；真相迟早会“在洗涤中显出来”。

这样的自信可以支持一个人继续做下去。至于方向对不对，仍要等下一次实验。

屈原与渔父的对话，留下了坚持与变通之间的难题。麦克林托克在另一种生活里，给它添了一条路。

她让自己的坚持一次次遇到新材料。留下来，继续工作，也可以意味着让自己的解释继续改变。

世界也不是某一天突然醒来。

承认是逐渐变化的。

六、默会的，不等于说不出来的答案

Michael Polanyi在《默会维度》开头写：“我们知道的，多于我们能够说出的。” 他举认脸作例子：我们能从许多人中认出一张熟悉的脸，却往往说不清自己究竟用了哪些特征。

这与麦克林托克很近。

1950年论文在说。1951年报告在说。1953年论文甚至直接写Ds和Ac可以发生transposition。1956年“controlling elements”仍然是公开的科学术语。

所以她的默会层，不是一个完整命题“基因会动”被锁在身体里，二十年后才终于获得语言。

默会的更可能是：

哪一种斑驳值得追？

哪一株“看起来不对”？

怎样把细胞学的图像、遗传比例和多年谱系放成一个整体？

什么时候一个结果只是制片或培育问题，什么时候它要求改问题？

这些判断可以部分教、部分写，却很难一次全部列成规则。它们支持明确知识，却不能替明确知识交卷。

把这样的经验带到孔德与波普尔面前，问题不再只是如何判断一条已经说出的命题。还有一段更早的工作：人怎样学会注意到它，怎样把它变成可以共同讨论的问题？

默会知识真正提出的压力更细：即使最后发表的知识必须尽可能公开、可批评，走到那个发表位置所依赖的技能、注意和背景判断，也未必能够在同一份论文里被穷尽。

这不取消检验。

它解释为什么“怎样想到要检验这里”本身也值得研究。

七、亲近并不取消对象

亲近对象，也有它的基础设施。冷泉港的工作不是只发生在一双眼睛和一株玉米之间：田地就在研究建筑周围，材料从田间进入制片、显微镜、笔记和论文，又在下一季回到新的杂交；实验室里还有同行、来访者和不断被她标画的重印本。这样的连续性很重要。“了解有机体”不只是内在同情，而是多年在不同技术里反复遇见同一批材料，同时有人持续给这项工作土地、设备和时间。

她确实用过很亲近的语言描述研究对象。她说每株玉米都有差异，工作久了会“对每一株都有感觉”；又说自己仿佛进入染色体之中，成为系统的一部分。

客观并不要求一个人对研究对象毫无感情。它要求的是：再亲近，也要留下别人能够检查的记录；再熟悉，也得容许下一株植物推翻自己的预期。

亲近有时反而让差异出现。

如果你认识每一株，就更容易发现这一株不像昨天那一株。

但亲近也可能让人舍不得放弃解释。

更准确的是：距离和亲近都是认识实践中的位置。它们各自让一些东西变清楚，也让另一些东西变模糊。

1983年的诺贝尔演讲把她几十年前的工作重新放到“基因组如何回应挑战”这个大问题里。她回顾四十年代的实验，同时也承认许多反应一开始不可预测，必须让同一种挑战反复发生，才能逐渐看清变化的性质。

“对有机体的感觉”在这里不需要成为一只神秘眼睛。

它可以只是极度训练后的注意：愿意让一个原本不在问题表里的差异，多停留一会儿。

下一篇会走到薇依和注意力。

八、桥头

现实中的麦克林托克1902年出生，1992年去世。她1941年到冷泉港的Carnegie遗传学部门工作，1967年正式退休后仍长期留在那里研究和交流。

她走来的时候，手里拿着一穗玉米。

有些紫。有些黄。有些杂色。它们的差异需要谱系、位点、发育时间和控制元件才能解释；颜色本身不是一张会讲话的实验报告。

孔德先看了一会儿。

他问：“你怎么知道哪一种差异值得留下？”

麦克林托克说：“看得久了，会有些地方不再像噪音。”

波普尔接着问：“那种感觉可能错吗？”

“当然。”

“错了怎么办？”

“再种。”

她说得很快。

“再交配。再看后代。再把我以为看见的东西拆开。”

波普尔点了点头，目光又回到那穗玉米上。

狄拉克看着玉米穗。

他说：“有时我先相信一个结构值得追。”

麦克林托克说：“我也是。只是我的结构会发芽。”

费希特问：“那个看得久了的人，也改变了吗？”

“当然。”

“那对象呢？”

麦克林托克低头看了一粒杂色籽。

“它也一直在长。”

屈原站在靠水的一侧。土和干叶的气味传来，他安静了一会儿。

麦克林托克蹲下来，把玉米穗放在木板上。

她把其中一粒指给他们看。

“先看这个。”

所有人都低下头。

没有哪一种感觉替他们看。

但如果没有人先注意到这一粒，它也许会一直只是背景里的一个小斑点。

感觉可以先到。

证据还要跟上。

而那个先到，也往往是许多年看、种、记、错、再看的结果。

注释

本篇区分转座现象的确认、跨物种推广与更广的控制理论，不把它们压成“无人相信三十年”。1950、1953及后续发表记录说明她持续公开研究；有关接受史与性别因素的不同解释仍需分别考察。“先感觉到”指多年训练后的注意与判断，不指感觉先于所有观察，也不以私人生活解释科研能力。

I. A Kernel That Is Different

On an ear of maize, some kernels have an even color; others carry spots. For Barbara McClintock, a difference could be worth recording, planting and following into another generation. A spot was not yet an answer. It might be the beginning of a question.

In 1950, Barbara McClintock published “The Origin and Behavior of Mutable Loci in Maize.” She was not offering the slogan that would later become familiar—“genes jump.” She was reporting a system she had followed through years of chromosomal work: Dissociation (Ds) and Activator (Ac) could change position, and under particular conditions they could alter the expression of nearby genes. Her 1953 paper said the point more directly: these chromosomal units could undergo transposition from one location to another, and some effects of Ds depended on the presence of Ac.

This did not begin with a single spotted ear of corn and a flash of intuition. In 1931, McClintock and Harriet Creighton had already connected genetic recombination in maize with the physical exchange of chromosomal segments. In 1944 she was elected to the U.S. National Academy of Sciences; in 1945 she became the first woman to serve as president of the Genetics Society of America. Before the controlling elements, she was already a major cytogeneticist.

There was an earlier line of training that the later “jumping genes” story can easily hide. In the late 1930s and early 1940s she followed broken chromosome ends through what became known as the breakage–fusion–bridge cycle: after replication, broken ends could fuse; a chromosome with two centromeres could be pulled toward opposite poles; a bridge formed, broke again, and daughter cells inherited unequal amounts of chromosomal material. By using genetic markers on chromosome 9, she could make some of those consequences visible as sectors in kernels or plants. A patch of color was therefore not decoration. It could preserve a trace of a history of cell divisions.

That mattered for Ac/Ds. Variegation by itself did not tell her which mechanism had acted. She had to grow the odd kernel, cross its descendants, map loci, inspect chromosomes, and ask whether a visible change in phenotype really traveled with a change in chromosomal position. Modern reconstructions of these experiments can match distinct kernel patterns to different breakage and transposition events. What looked like “seeing” was already a chain from eye to field to microscope to pedigree—and back to the eye.

Transposition itself was not rejected by maize geneticists for thirty uninterrupted years. Historian Nathaniel Comfort reexamined the archival record, interviews, and publications and found that the phenomenon of transposition was accepted relatively quickly and repeatedly confirmed; by the late 1950s it was already treated as a classic discovery. What remained far more controversial was McClintock’s broader account of “control”: how mobile elements might participate in gene regulation, development, genomic reorganization, and how general such mechanisms might be.

From 1950 to 1983 is thirty-three years. Not thirty-three years in which no one heard her, but a long change of scale: from a maize phenomenon that specialists could already confirm, to mobile genetic elements becoming a cross-organism problem near the center of molecular genetics, and finally to a Nobel Prize writing the discovery into public scientific memory. From the 1960s onward, work on insertion sequences in bacteria and phages helped move genetic mobility from maize into a much larger map.

In 1983, at eighty-one, McClintock received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine alone—the first woman to receive that prize unshared. In remarks surrounding the award, she did not turn the preceding decades into proof of suffering. She said, in effect, that being rewarded after so many years of asking the maize plant specific questions and watching its responses seemed almost unfair, because the work had given her so much pleasure.

How does long acquaintance with a material make differences visible that others have not yet noticed?

II. A Feeling for the Organism

In 1983 Evelyn Fox Keller published A Feeling for the Organism. The title named a quality she saw in McClintock’s way of doing research, and the book preserved some striking language from interviews. McClintock described long work with chromosomes as a state in which she no longer felt simply outside the system; chromosomes could become, in her words, like “friends.”

It is easy to turn such language into mysticism. It is equally easy to turn anti-mysticism into a new myth: she first closed her eyes and felt the answer, then used a microscope to attach proof afterward.

Both versions move too fast.

Her feeling grew inside technique.

It grew in learning the morphology of maize chromosomes, designing crosses, remembering parentage, deciding whether a variegated kernel deserved another generation, testing the progeny again, and repeatedly matching cytological structure to genetic phenotype. Her 1953 paper does not say “I feel this to be true.” It contains predictions, selected loci, numbers of crosses, progeny tests, and explicit statements that no definite conclusion can yet be drawn when the evidence does not permit one.

An ear of maize was therefore neither an illustration nor an answer key. Hundreds of kernels could sit side by side, and the size, number, and arrangement of sectors could sometimes preserve clues about when a somatic event occurred during development. Yet the same visible difference could arise by more than one genetic route. The trained eye mattered precisely because it knew that appearance had to be carried into crosses, mapping, and chromosome work before it became an explanation.

Her earlier work on breakage–fusion–bridge cycles had already taught the same discipline: a pattern visible in a seed could be read as a trace of chromosome behavior only because the parents, markers, cell divisions, and descendants had been followed.

So “she felt it first” cannot mean that feeling came before every observation.

Chronologically, years of observation had already happened.

“First” names a different ordering. In a skilled practice that cannot yet be exhausted by a set of explicit rules, a researcher may first register: something is wrong here; this pattern deserves pursuit; these phenomena may belong together. She must then turn that orientation into experiments, diagrams, genetic relations, and papers that other people can inspect.

Feeling is not a substitute for evidence.

It is closer to a sense of where to direct attention.

That sense can be extraordinarily important.

It can also be wrong.

III. She and Dirac

Dirac’s equation preceded the relevant experimental discovery. It carried accumulated theory and experience toward something still awaiting confirmation.

McClintock’s “first” is different again.

Dirac worked within mathematical constraints formed by quantum theory, relativity, and the electron problem. McClintock worked with fields, microscopes, stains, pedigrees, crosses, and maize plants that were never quite interchangeable. Both could grasp a direction before final confirmation. That does not make either case a possession of the same kind of a priori knowledge.

Fichte’s free individual has a body and encounters limits and other people through action. With McClintock, that body acquires the particulars of a working day.

Pollen on the hands.

Eyes tired behind microscope eyepieces.

Feet crossing rows in a field.

Memory connecting this year’s plant to descendants of last year’s cross.

A body does not manufacture truth automatically. It misidentifies, tires, overlooks, and can mistake familiarity for necessity.

But scientific practice has never consisted solely of propositions floating in a vacuum waiting to be tested. Someone has to learn how to make a slide, identify a chromosome, choose material, and judge whether an anomaly is noise or an entrance. McClintock makes this layer difficult to ignore.

She did not prove a universal law that “the body knows before the concept.”

What she left is smaller and harder: some judgments become available only after long, skilled acquaintance with a material.

IV. What, Exactly, Was Rejected?

McClintock’s story is often told in two clean versions.

One says: she was a woman, therefore a male scientific establishment refused her work for thirty years.

The other says: sex had nothing to do with anything; her theory simply lacked evidence.

The archives make both versions difficult.

Women in early- and mid-twentieth-century American science did face institutional restrictions, and McClintock’s education and career unfolded inside that world. But several specific stories later repeated as settled fact—for example, that Cornell rejected her from graduate plant breeding because she was a woman, or that the University of Missouri refused her tenure for the same reason—were reexamined by Lee Kass using university and personnel records. Her findings do not support those versions. Missouri, in fact, later countered another offer with comparable pay and job security; McClintock chose Cold Spring Harbor because it better suited the independent research life she wanted.

That does not make gender disappear from the history. The structure of academic appointments, expectations for women’s careers, and available paths were not neutral. Recent historical work on McClintock’s 1961 interaction with François Jacob and Jacques Monod revisits whether a Matilda effect contributed to the later minimization of her role in thinking about genetic control; Comfort, by contrast, emphasizes that transposition was accepted early and locates the main dispute in the broader control theory. These interpretations require evidence at the level of particular claims, not a ready-made morality play.

More important, the phrase “her work was rejected” has to be taken apart.

Did mobile elements exist?

How did Ac and Ds behave in maize?

Was mobility widespread?

Did these elements constitute a general theory of gene regulation?

Could genomes reorganize themselves in response to challenge?

Those are not one yes-or-no button.

In the 1950s, maize geneticists could accept some levels and remain unconvinced by others. In 1961 McClintock herself compared her maize systems with Jacob and Monod’s bacterial regulatory model. She was still publishing, still arguing, still adjusting the language in which the comparison could be made.

A later prize does not retroactively prove every earlier interpretation correct.

A community’s failure to accept a larger interpretation does not automatically prove that the community was merely narrow-minded.

The useful question is more precise: which layer of evidence was already strong enough, and which layer was not?

V. Planting and Writing On

Her twenty-one-page 1953 Genetics paper, “Induction of Instability at Selected Loci in Maize,” was followed by continuing publications in 1954, 1955, 1956, 1957, 1958, and beyond. In 1956 she published “Controlling Elements and the Gene”; in 1961 she published “Some Parallels Between Gene Control Systems in Maize and in Bacteria.” Cold Spring Harbor’s repository records work extending much later.

Cold Spring Harbor should not be turned into a myth of the solitary genius retreating into a cornfield either. McClintock first came for the summer of 1941 and soon secured a long-term Carnegie research position. The environment gave her unusual independence from routine teaching and administration. After formal retirement in 1967 she continued there as a Distinguished Service Member; from arrival to late life, her relationship with the institution lasted roughly half a century. Her independence, in other words, was not the absence of an institution. An institution materially gave that independence time and space.

She did at times reduce particular forms of public communication, and colleagues later recalled periods in which the difficulty of being understood made her less eager to publish some complicated material. But “publishing less,” “deciding not to keep presenting a certain body of results,” and “stopping publication after 1953” are different claims.

She kept planting and observing, exposing the relations she thought she saw to new material. She also chose whom to explain them to and which occasions deserved another attempt. Continuing the work did not require answering everyone.

A 1983 newspaper interview recorded her saying, in substance, that if you know you are on the right track it is hard for others to shut you down; eventually things will “come out in the wash.”

Such confidence can sustain the work. Whether the direction is right still has to face the next experiment.

The conversation between Qu Yuan and the fisherman leaves a difficulty between steadfastness and adaptation. In a very different life, McClintock opens another way through it.

She repeatedly let her persistence meet new material. Staying with the work could also mean allowing her explanations to keep changing.

And the world did not awaken on one morning.

Recognition changed gradually.

VI. Tacit Does Not Mean an Answer That Cannot Be Said

Michael Polanyi opens The Tacit Dimension with a famous proposition: we can know more than we can tell. One of his examples is facial recognition: we can pick a familiar face out of a crowd while being unable to enumerate all the features by which we did so.

That is close to something in McClintock’s practice.

The 1950 paper says it. The 1951 report says it. The 1953 paper explicitly describes transposition of Ds and Ac. By 1956 “controlling elements” was established terminology in her own published work.

So the tacit layer was not a complete proposition—genes move—sealed inside the body until language arrived decades later.

What was more plausibly tacit included judgments like these:

Which pattern of variegation is worth following?

Which plant “looks wrong” in an interesting way?

How do cytological images, segregation ratios, and years of pedigree information belong together?

When is an odd result a preparation or cultivation problem, and when does it require changing the question?

Such judgments can be taught in part and written in part, yet they may resist being exhausted by one rulebook. They support explicit knowledge; they do not submit the final answer on explicit knowledge’s behalf.

Bring this experience to Comte and Popper, and the question reaches beyond assessing an articulated claim. There is earlier work too: how does someone learn to notice it and turn it into a question others can discuss?

The pressure posed by tacit knowing is more exact: even when published knowledge must be made as public and criticizable as possible, the skills, attention, and background judgments by which one reaches the publishable claim may not be exhausted in the same paper.

That does not cancel testing.

It explains why “how did anyone come to test there?” is also a legitimate question.

VII. Closeness Does Not Abolish the Object

Closeness also had an infrastructure. At Cold Spring Harbor the work did not occur only between one pair of eyes and one maize plant: fields lay around the research buildings; material moved from field to cytological preparation, microscope, notebook, and paper, then returned the next season in new crosses; there were colleagues, visitors, and reprints she marked carefully. That continuity matters. “Knowing the organism” was not only inward sympathy. It was meeting the same material for years across different techniques while someone kept providing land, equipment, and time.

She did use intimate language for her research material. She spoke of individual maize plants as distinct, of acquiring a “feel” for each after long familiarity, and of entering the chromosome system so deeply that she could describe herself as part of it.

Objectivity does not require indifference to what one studies. It asks that closeness leave records others can inspect, and that familiarity allow the next plant to overturn an expectation.

Closeness may actually make differences visible.

If you know every plant, this plant’s departure from yesterday’s pattern is easier to notice.

But closeness can also make an interpretation harder to relinquish.

Distance and closeness are both positions within inquiry. Each clarifies some things and obscures others.

In her 1983 Nobel lecture McClintock reframed work from decades earlier around a larger question: how genomes respond to challenge. Recalling experiments from the 1940s, she noted that some initially unforeseen responses had to be produced repeatedly under the same challenge before their character could be appreciated.

“A feeling for the organism” does not need to become a mystical eye.

It can remain an extreme form of trained attention: the willingness to let a difference that was not on the original question sheet remain visible a little longer.

The next essay will move toward Simone Weil and attention.

VIII. The Bridgehead

The historical Barbara McClintock was born in 1902 and died in 1992. She came to the Carnegie Department of Genetics at Cold Spring Harbor in 1941; after formal retirement in 1967 she continued to work, think, and converse there for many years.

She arrives carrying an ear of maize.

Some are purple. Some yellow. Some variegated. To interpret their differences one needs pedigrees, loci, developmental timing, and controlling elements. Color by itself is not a laboratory report that speaks.

Comte looks for a while.

“How do you know which difference is worth keeping?” he asks.

McClintock says, “After you have looked long enough, some things stop looking like noise.”

Popper asks next: “Can that feeling be wrong?”

“Of course.”

“And then?”

“Plant again.”

She answers quickly.

“Cross again. Look at the progeny again. Take apart what I thought I saw.”

Popper nods and looks back at the ear of maize.

Dirac looks at the ear.

“Sometimes I first believe that a structure is worth pursuing,” he says.

“So do I,” McClintock replies. “Mine happens to grow.”

“Does the person who looks for a long time also change?” Fichte asks.

“Of course.”

“And the object?”

McClintock looks down at a variegated kernel.

“It has been growing the whole time.”

Qu Yuan stands near the water. The smell of soil and dry husk reaches him; he is quiet for a while.

McClintock crouches and lays the ear on the boards.

She points to one kernel.

“Look at this one first.”

Everyone bends closer.

No feeling sees on their behalf.

But if no one first notices this kernel, it may remain a small mark in the background.

Feeling can arrive first.

Evidence still has to follow.

And what arrives first is often the result of years of looking, planting, recording, being wrong, and looking again.
