达尔文，生命自己在凿

Darwin, Life Carves Itself

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、牧师

查尔斯·达尔文年轻的时候想当牧师。

这不是玩笑。他父亲是医生，希望他也学医。他去了爱丁堡，却受不了手术室里的痛苦场面——那是在现代麻醉术普及之前。他后来回忆，两次手术使他惊恐，其中一次的病人是个孩子。医学没有成为他的路。父亲提出另一条路，他便去了剑桥，准备取得学位，做一名英国乡村教区的牧师。

他不是把神学扔在一边的人。他认真读过佩利的宗教论证，也欣赏《自然神学》严整的推理。但他同时迷上了甲虫。按他晚年的回忆，有一次两只手各抓着一只甲虫，又看到了第三只，他把其中一只塞进嘴里，想腾出手。甲虫喷出刺激性的液体，他只好吐掉它，第三只也没抓住。

一个准牧师，嘴里含着甲虫。读论证，也蹲在田野里。后来改变人类对生命理解的那个人，是从这里出发的。

二、船

1831年。达尔文二十二岁。一个机会来了。

英国皇家海军的测量船“小猎犬号”将赴南美洲考察。船长菲茨罗伊希望有一位受过教育的绅士旅伴，植物学家亨斯洛推荐了达尔文。他以自费的博物学考察者和船长旅伴身份出航；观察、采集和记录，会逐渐成为这段旅程的中心。

他父亲反对。他的舅父乔赛亚·韦奇伍德帮助说服了父亲。他自己决定抓住这个机会，上了船。

这一走接近五年。从1831年12月到1836年10月，小猎犬号在南美洲海岸、火地岛一带和智利、秘鲁之间测量、航行，后来到达加拉帕戈斯群岛，再横渡太平洋，经塔希提、新西兰、澳大利亚，渡印度洋，绕过好望角，最终返回英国。

接近五年。一个准备当牧师的人绕了地球一圈。他并不一直待在船舱里，漫长的陆地考察同样塑造了这次航行。

他看到了什么？

在南美洲，他挖掘、收集巨大的灭绝哺乳动物化石。后来，欧文等人的鉴定帮助他看清：一些古老的有甲动物，与今天的犰狳既相似又不同。这些材料把一个问题推到眼前：为什么同一片大陆上，已经消失的生命与现存生命，会显出亲缘般的联系？

在加拉帕戈斯，岛屿间嘲鸫的差异先引起了他的注意。他也收集了后来著名的雀鸟，却没有当场认出它们的近缘关系，而且一些标本没有记清来自哪座岛。返英后，古尔德的鉴定和同船伙伴保存的标本，才帮助拼出图景：有的喙适合取食昆虫，有的适合啄食种子。后来研究者还观察到木雀用小枝或仙人掌刺取出木缝中的虫子；那不是达尔文在岛上已经看懂的一幕。

共同祖先，不同环境，不同形态。这个解释，是后来逐渐形成的。

他没有在船上想明白一切。航行给了他重要材料，也留下了问题；回国后的鉴定、育种研究和通信，还会不断改变他对材料的理解。一个准备当牧师的人，带着化石、鸟皮、笔记和越来越多的问题回到了英国。

三、二十年

回来之后，他陆续出版航行与地质研究，却没有立刻公布物种演变的理论。

从形成自然选择的核心想法到公开发表，又过了约二十年。

1837年，他在笔记本上画下一棵分枝的树，旁边写着“I think”——我想。那幅如今著名的图，试着把物种的关系画成共同祖先向不同方向分出的枝条。

证据逐渐积累。他研究育种者怎样通过选择改变鸽子的形态；花八年研究藤壶，写出四部专著。植物授粉和蚯蚓也进入他的视野，这些问题还会在《物种起源》出版之后继续生长。

1838年，阅读马尔萨斯的人口论，帮助他想到：在生存与繁殖受限制的条件下，有利的可遗传变异可能逐代积累。他在1842年写出提纲，1844年扩成论文，让少数朋友了解自己的思路；1856年又开始写一部大书。理论没有立即公开，却一直在被写下、讨论和检验。

为什么等了这么久？

他知道问题的分量。共同祖先的解释，冲击了每个物种分别被创造的观念；把这条路继续走下去，人类也进入生命的谱系，与其他猿类有共同祖先。人的独特能力仍然可以研究，却不能再把人放在自然史之外。

妻子艾玛有深厚的基督教信仰，他们的书信留下了信仰差异带来的担忧，也留下彼此的爱与照顾。至于“像承认谋杀”，是他1844年写给植物学家胡克的话：他逐渐相信物种并非不变。那是一位研究者向朋友承认危险想法的口气，不是一封向妻子宣布出版的信。

还有证据本身的重量。哪些现象符合解释，哪些会成为反例？他做实验、读书、通信，也受疾病和其他工作的牵制。谨慎、工作安排和思想上的压力交织在一起，不能由一种恐惧包办这二十年。

二十年。抽屉里的稿子不是早已完成的答案，而是一个不断长出枝条的研究。长久未公开的东西，也在长久地变化。

四、华莱士的信

1858年6月。达尔文收到了一封信。

寄信人是阿尔弗雷德·拉塞尔·华莱士，一个在马来群岛做田野调查的博物学家。他附来的论文，独立提出了通过自然选择解释物种变化的思路。

两个人走了不同的路，却在关键机制上相遇了。

达尔文震惊于思想的相似，也忧虑自己的多年工作失去优先性。他同时希望公平对待华莱士。那几周，家里的孩子正在患病，幼子查尔斯·沃林于6月28日去世。学术上的突变，落在一场家庭的悲痛之中。

莱尔和胡克安排把达尔文的旧稿节选、他1857年致阿萨·格雷的信，以及华莱士的论文，一起提交林奈学会，1858年7月1日宣读。两人都不在场：达尔文在丧子之后，华莱士还在海外。这个联合发表，使两位独立提出者共同进入了理论的公开历史。

达尔文随后转向写一部较短的“摘要”，大约十三个月完成主体。这不是从零起笔：1856年开始的大书和多年的材料都在背后。《物种起源》于1859年11月24日出版，首印1250册；出版前的书商订货已认购了可供销售的首批印量。书很快进入市场，不等于1250位读者在发行当天都买到了它。

一封信，改变了多年积累走向公众的速度。

这让人想起老子出关、尹喜请他著书的传说。两件事并非同一种“不可说”：达尔文的解释必须能够公开论证。相似的是那个时刻——一个人的思考，被另一个人的到来推向了交付。华莱士不只是催促者；他带来了自己独立完成的发现。

五、变异与选择

自然选择的核心思路，明白之后竟显得简洁。赫胥黎后来回忆自己的最初反应：怎么自己就没有想到呢？

可以把它先拆成三个事实和一个推论。

事实一：种群中的个体存在差异。同一窝小鸟，喙的长度、羽毛的颜色，都可能有所不同。

事实二：其中一部分差异能够遗传。假如喙长的差异具有遗传基础，亲代与后代的喙长便可能相关。达尔文还不知道后来遗传学揭示的机制。

事实三：生物有产生大量后代的潜力，但食物、空间和其他条件有限，后代不会都同样成功地存活、繁殖。关键不只是谁活得久，也是谁留下了后代。

推论：在特定环境下，某些可遗传差异使携带者平均而言更容易留下后代，这些差异就可能在种群中逐代变得常见。许多代之后，种群的特征发生变化。这就是自然选择的基本过程。

这个机制没有预先规定的目标。变异不会因为未来“需要”某种器官，才按图纸出现；选择则与当下条件中的繁殖差异有关，并非又一次纯随机抽签。漫长而有限的时间，让微小差异积累成复杂适应。这里解释的是生命怎样改变和适应，不是生命最初怎样出现，也不是关于宇宙是否有终极目的的判决。

六、生命的凿构循环

现在，借用这个系列的语言来看它。接下来的“凿”与“构”，是帮助思考的比喻，不是生物学的新定理。

变异像凿。用现代遗传学的语言说，一个碱基的改变、一段DNA的重复或缺失，都可能带来新的差异；重组又把已有变异重新组合。它们不以未来是否有利为目标，也不是每一次都会改变外形。原有的形态有了改变的可能——没有谁握着凿子，生命的材料自身在变化。

选择像构。假如在某座岛的条件下，稍长的喙能帮助取食，且这项差异能够遗传，它便可能因较高的繁殖成功而逐代积累。但这不是环境给每只鸟宣判：有利变异也可能偶然丢失。自然选择之外，随机的遗传漂变、种群间的基因流动等过程，也参与演化。

凿与构的图像，于是在这里来回出现：差异产生，某些差异积累，又成为后来变化的起点。没有一份等待交卷的最终设计。长久的相对稳定、分化和灭绝，都在这部历史之内。

回看秦始皇，书禁使不同的判断难以流通，坏消息和批评可能更难抵达权力中心。这给我们一个制度问题：一个秩序把修正自己的空间压到多小，会增加怎样的危险？秦亡有继承、战争、徭役与治理等复杂原因，不能把王朝当作一个因“拒绝变异”而灭绝的物种。

再看华盛顿留下的共和国。言论、批评和改制，为纠错保留了可能；废奴、女性投票权和民权运动，却不是偶然出现后被环境轻松选中的几个“好变异”。它们来自具体的人长期组织、抗争和承担风险。人的价值不取决于他们是否有助于制度存活，权利也不能等到被证明“适应环境”才成立。

帝国会压制异议，共和国也可能堵住耳朵；制度的名字不会自动保证开放。这里比较的是容纳批评、约束权力的安排，而不是拿两个国家的寿命做自然选择的实验。

达尔文曾从马尔萨斯那里获得启发，再把问题带到生物材料中检验。我们也可以跨过学科边界提出问题，却不能把比喻带回来，冒充政治正当性的证明。生命演化使“凿构”的图像变得丰厚。它帮助我们提问，答案仍须回到各自的证据中去找。

七、无目的性的合目的性

康德《判断力批判》中的“无目的的合目的性”，常被用来谈美：我们感到形式合宜，却不必先给它规定一个用途。

讨论有机体时，康德走的是另一条线。他把部分与整体相互依存的生命组织，作为“自然目的”来考察，讨论我们怎样作目的论判断。这与审美判断中的公式有关联，却不是同一个命题，更不是简单宣布自然界没有设计者。

《判断力批判》出版于1790年，《物种起源》出版于1859年。中间隔了将近七十年。

达尔文把另一种解释带到眼前：眼睛、翅膀等适应性的复杂结构，怎样在生命的历史中形成？这里不是给康德的一句直觉补上答案，而是把问题推进到遗传、繁殖与世代变化的机制。

“为了看”的眼睛，“为了飞”的翅膀，功能可以从当下的使用说起，来历却要放回历史。自然选择不必预先看见眼睛，才能让有助于感光的可遗传差异逐步积累。今天的功能也未必就是一个结构最初出现时的功能。

若压成一句文学性的式子：无预见的差异，加上有条件的积累，竟能长出仿佛按用途设计的形态。

蓝鲸、红杉、人类的大脑，都属于这漫长而分枝的自然史。解释适应，不必给每个器官另添一张外部设计图；然而几项词语相加，也还不是关于生命起源、全部演化过程或信仰问题的完整答案。

把这幅图放到庄子的混沌旁边，反差就出现了。寓言里的混沌被凿七窍，确实死了；个体遭受的伤害不会自动变成新物种。演化谈的是许多世代中的种群变化。我们借来的，是一个新的提问：形态怎样出现，又会把原有的可能带往哪里？无需让达尔文替庄子改写结局。

哥德尔追问特定数学体系中证明的界限，达尔文追问生命怎样变化。两者并不互相证明。它们在本系列中相遇，是因为我们不断问：一个已经形成的构，能否把此后的可能一并封住？

演化包含偶然与失去，也包含长久的相对稳定。改变可能失败，稳定也可能延续；被我们叫作“开放”的物种，同样没有永远存活的保证。

八、他自己的变异

达尔文成年后的许多年，一直受到疾病困扰。

他的信件和自述留下了恶心、呕吐、心悸、疲劳等症状。后人提出过多种病因解释，但没有一个可以在这里当作已经确定的诊断。那些痛苦是真实的，不能把它们缩成一个思想家不敢发表的身体寓言。

1842年起，他住在唐恩庄园，安排工作、休息和散步；有时一天只能工作几小时。他少参加公开集会，却并未与世界断开，标本、书信、访客不断抵达。赫胥黎以“达尔文的斗牛犬”自许，公开为演化论辩护，也有自己的判断，并不是替他发声的一件工具。

达尔文继续研究，也通过著作和通信参与争论。兰花授粉、攀援植物、食虫植物、蚯蚓对土壤的影响，一个问题接着一个问题。他1881年出版的最后一本书，写的是蚯蚓。

一个改变人类生命观的人，在晚年的书里俯身看蚯蚓。微小的动作，经过漫长年月，竟参与塑造脚下的土地。

若说现实把他凿成科学家，便让人想起杜甫在困顿中写成的诗。但被现实改变，不等于没有选择。他争取上船，收集材料，修改解释，回应华莱士；正如杜甫不只是苦难的被动产物。一个原想当牧师的人，在一次次遭遇中回应，才逐渐成为我们认识的达尔文。

1882年4月19日，达尔文在唐恩庄园去世，七十三岁。他在那里生活了近四十年，原拟安葬于本地教堂墓地。后来在亲友等人的推动下，葬礼于4月26日在威斯敏斯特教堂举行，墓地靠近牛顿。

一个改变了人们理解神创与自然关系的人，被葬在教堂里。科学争议、个人信仰和公共纪念，在同一座建筑中相遇；这幅图景，比一句科学战胜宗教的口号更复杂。

九、生命自己在凿

回看这个系列，“凿”一直随着不同的人改变形状。以下是本文把他们并置时形成的图像。

苏格拉底凿自以为知。孔子追问没有仁的礼。老子试探语言的边界。庄子的寓言让强加的形状受到追问。康德划定知识的界限。尼采举锤重估价值。王阳明把良知带回行动。释迦牟尼借筏渡河，也让人放下筏。耶稣的受难叙事，在被伤害时仍显出给予。哥德尔以数学揭示特定形式理论的界限。爱因斯坦改变牛顿物理的框架。杜甫在现实的压力下写诗。荷马传统把口传的声音带进文本。秦始皇以秩序压制异声，也留下未竟。华盛顿建立制度，又给个人权力设限。亚历山大的征服，碰到了人和世界的限度。

这些图像里，都是人在回应、在行动。

达尔文与华莱士让另一件事变得格外清楚：生命的改变，不需要先有一个人替它规定路线。

不需要苏格拉底先提出问题，也不需要康德先划出界限。在人类出现以前很久，遗传、变异和不同的繁殖成功已经在发生。只要这些条件仍在，过程就不以人的见证为前提。

若沿用本文的比喻，人类没有发明生命的“凿构”；人类本身也来自这段历史。

这个转身，比一句“人是猴子变的”更深：人类是生命谱系中的一个分支，与其他猿类共有祖先，并不是站在谱系之外的裁判。我们参与其中，却还要为有意识的行动承担责任。

苏格拉底以理由追问雅典人的确信，自然选择则通过不同的繁殖结果改变种群。前者能够反思自己的尺度，后者没有一个知道自己在“凿”的主体。这种差别不能抹掉；并置它们，是为了看见人的提问从怎样的生命中长出来，不是把批评、淘汰和人的价值混成一件事。

在我想象的桥头，又多了一个人。维多利亚时代的黑色大衣，长胡子，手里是一本关于蚯蚓的书。他暂时没有加入争论，先低下头看桥下的土地。他让我们注意的，不只是建桥者手里的图纸：

在这个比喻里，桥不只是人建的。桥自己在生长。

注释

本文按达尔文自传、通信与著作，区分航行时的观察和返英后的理论形成。加拉帕戈斯雀鸟的鉴定、岛屿信息的补全，以及后来记录的木雀工具使用，属于不同阶段。

1838年的自然选择思路、1842年提纲、1844年论文、1856年大书与1858年联合发表，显示研究并非二十年的完全沉默。“像承认谋杀”出自1844年1月11日致胡克信，所指是物种并非不变。

1858年联合发表包括达尔文旧稿节选、致格雷信与华莱士论文；华莱士没有预先参与具体安排，后来表示感谢。《物种起源》首印1250册，“售罄”须区分书商订购与读者零售。

自然选择涉及可遗传差异与不同的繁殖成功，是演化的重要机制而非唯一机制。突变的“随机”指不按将来的适应需要而发生，并非没有物理原因；自然选择本身不是纯随机过程。文中的凿构与政治对照是作者的解释性借喻，不能据以推导人的价值或政治权利。

康德的审美合目的性与有机体的目的论判断应分开理解，参见《判断力批判》§§10—17、64—75。混沌、哥德尔及桥头人物在此形成文学对照，不是跨学科的证明，也不是历史会面。哥德尔定理的适用条件包括有效公理化、足够表达算术及相应的一致性条件，不能直接移用于生物或政治制度。

达尔文的长期疾病在通信中有充分记录，确切病因仍无定论。唐恩庄园、蚯蚓研究与威斯敏斯特安葬的叙述，不把疾病浪漫化为天才的代价，也不替他的信仰作单一裁决。

I. The Clergyman

When Charles Darwin was young, he wanted to be a clergyman.

This is not a joke. His father was a physician and wanted him to study medicine. Darwin went to Edinburgh, but could not bear the suffering he witnessed in the operating theatre, before modern anaesthesia. He later recalled two horrifying operations, one on a child. Medicine did not become his path. His father proposed another, and Darwin went to Cambridge to obtain a degree and prepare for life as an English country parson.

He did not simply put theology aside. He read Paley's religious arguments carefully and admired the reasoning of Natural Theology. But he also became obsessed with beetles. In a story from his later autobiography, he had one in each hand when he spotted a third. He put one in his mouth to free a hand. It released an acrid fluid; he spat it out and lost the third beetle too.

A prospective clergyman with a beetle in his mouth. Reading arguments, crouching in fields. This was where the man who would transform humanity's understanding of life began.

II. The Ship

1831. Darwin was twenty-two. An opportunity arrived.

HMS Beagle, a Royal Navy survey vessel, was preparing to sail for South America. Captain FitzRoy wanted an educated gentleman companion, and the botanist Henslow recommended Darwin. He sailed as a self-funded naturalist and the captain's companion. Observation, collecting and recording would increasingly become the centre of his journey.

His father objected. His uncle Josiah Wedgwood helped persuade him. Darwin chose to seize the opportunity. He boarded the ship.

He was away for almost five years, from December 1831 to October 1836. The Beagle surveyed and travelled along South America's coasts, around Tierra del Fuego, and off Chile and Peru. It later reached the Galápagos before crossing the Pacific, calling at Tahiti, New Zealand and Australia, crossing the Indian Ocean, rounding the Cape of Good Hope, and eventually returning to England.

Almost five years. A prospective clergyman travelled around the world. He was not always aboard: long expeditions on land shaped the voyage just as profoundly.

What did he see?

In South America, he excavated and collected fossils of enormous extinct mammals. Later identifications by Richard Owen and others helped reveal resemblances between ancient armoured animals and living armadillos. The remains pressed a question upon him: why should vanished and living creatures on the same continent display something like family resemblance?

In the Galápagos, differences between mockingbirds on different islands first caught his attention. He also collected the now-famous finches, without recognising their close relationship on the spot; some specimens lacked clear island labels. Back in England, John Gould's identifications and specimens collected by Darwin's shipmates helped assemble the picture: some beaks suited feeding on insects, others on seeds. Researchers later observed woodpecker finches extracting prey from crevices with twigs or cactus spines. That was not a scene Darwin had already understood on the islands.

Common ancestry. Different environments. Different forms. The explanation took shape later.

He did not work everything out on the ship. The voyage supplied important materials and questions; subsequent identification, breeding studies and correspondence would keep changing his understanding of them. A man who had intended to become a clergyman returned to England with fossils, bird skins, notebooks and a growing collection of questions.

III. Twenty Years

After returning, he published on the voyage and on geology, but did not immediately publish a theory of species change.

Roughly twenty years separated his central insight into natural selection from its public presentation.

In 1837 he drew a branching tree in a notebook and wrote beside it: “I think.” The now-famous diagram tried to represent relationships among species as branches diverging from common ancestors.

Evidence accumulated. He studied how breeders altered pigeons through selection; he spent eight years investigating barnacles and produced four monographs. Plant pollination and earthworms also entered his field of attention. Those questions would continue to grow after the publication of the Origin.

Reading Malthus on population in 1838 helped him see how favourable heritable variations might accumulate when survival and reproduction were constrained. He wrote a sketch in 1842, expanded it into an essay in 1844, and shared his thinking with a few friends. In 1856 he began a large book. The theory was not immediately public, but it was being written, discussed and tested.

Why did it take so long?

He knew the stakes. Common descent challenged the separate creation of each species. Follow that path and humans, too, entered life's family tree, sharing ancestors with other apes. Distinctive human capacities remained subjects for inquiry, but humanity could no longer be placed outside natural history.

His wife Emma held deep Christian convictions. Their correspondence records both the anxieties surrounding their religious differences and their love and care for one another. The phrase about “confessing a murder,” however, comes from his 1844 letter to the botanist Hooker: he was coming to believe that species were not immutable. It was a researcher confiding a dangerous idea to a friend, not a letter announcing publication to his wife.

There was also the weight of evidence. Which observations supported the explanation, and which might contradict it? He experimented, read and corresponded, while illness and other projects competed for his time. Caution, work and intellectual pressures became entangled. No single fear explains those twenty years.

Twenty years. The manuscript in the drawer was not a finished answer, but a research programme continually putting out branches. What remained unpublished for so long was also changing for so long.

IV. Wallace's Letter

June 1858. Darwin received a letter.

The sender was Alfred Russel Wallace, a naturalist doing fieldwork in the Malay Archipelago. His enclosed essay independently proposed an explanation of species change through natural selection.

Two different journeys had converged on a central mechanism.

Darwin was startled by the resemblance and feared losing priority for years of work. He also wanted to treat Wallace fairly. During those weeks his children were ill; his youngest son, Charles Waring, died on 28 June. The sudden scientific upheaval arrived amid a family's grief.

Lyell and Hooker arranged a joint presentation to the Linnean Society: extracts from Darwin's earlier essay, his 1857 letter to Asa Gray, and Wallace's paper, read on 1 July 1858. Neither author attended. Darwin had just lost his son; Wallace was still overseas. The joint publication brought both independent discoverers into the public history of the theory.

Darwin then turned to a shorter “abstract,” completing the main writing in about thirteen months. He was not starting from nothing: the large book begun in 1856 and years of material lay behind it. On the Origin of Species appeared on 24 November 1859 in a first printing of 1,250 copies. Booksellers' advance orders had already taken up the copies available for sale. Rapid uptake by the trade is not the same as 1,250 readers buying the book on publication day.

A letter changed the speed at which years of accumulated work reached the public.

It recalls the story of Laozi leaving through the pass and Yin Xi asking him to write. These were not two instances of the same “unsayable”: Darwin's explanation had to be open to argument and evidence. The resemblance lies in the moment when another person's arrival moves a long meditation toward being handed over. Wallace was more than a summons to speak. He brought a discovery of his own.

V. Variation and Selection

Once grasped, the central idea of natural selection can seem remarkably simple. Huxley later recalled his first reaction: how had he failed to think of it himself?

We can begin with three facts and an inference.

First: individuals in a population differ. Even chicks in the same brood may vary in beak length or the colour of their plumage.

Second: some differences are heritable. If differences in beak length have a hereditary basis, the lengths of parents' and offspring's beaks may be related. Darwin did not yet know the mechanisms later uncovered by genetics.

Third: organisms have the potential to produce many offspring, but food, space and other conditions are limited. Offspring do not all survive and reproduce with equal success. What matters is not merely living longer, but leaving descendants.

The inference: in a particular environment, some heritable differences make their bearers, on average, more likely to leave offspring. Those differences can consequently become more common over generations. After many generations, the population's characteristics change. This is the basic process of natural selection.

The mechanism has no predetermined goal. Variants do not appear to a blueprint because an organ will be “needed” in the future. Selection, meanwhile, depends on differences in reproduction under present conditions; it is not another purely random draw. Long but finite stretches of time allow small differences to accumulate into complex adaptations. This explains how life changes and adapts, not how life first originated or whether the universe has an ultimate purpose.

VI. Life's Chisel-Construct Cycle

Now let us borrow the language of this series. The carving and construction that follow are images for thinking, not a new biological theorem.

Variation resembles carving. In modern genetics, a change in a DNA base, a duplication or a deletion can introduce a difference; recombination reshuffles existing variants. These processes do not aim at future usefulness, nor does every change alter an organism's appearance. An existing form acquires the possibility of being different. No one holds the chisel: the material of life itself changes.

Selection resembles construction. Suppose a slightly longer beak helps birds obtain food on an island, and that difference is heritable. Greater reproductive success may then allow it to accumulate across generations. But the environment is not sentencing each bird: even an advantageous variant can be lost by chance. Alongside natural selection, random genetic drift and the movement of genes between populations also contribute to evolution.

The images of carving and construction recur: differences arise, some accumulate, and the result becomes a starting point for further change. There is no final design awaiting submission. Long periods of relative stability, divergence and extinction all belong to this history.

Look back at Qin Shi Huang. Restrictions on books impeded the circulation of alternative judgments; bad news and criticism could become harder to bring to those in power. An institutional question follows: what dangers arise when an order constricts its capacity for correction? Qin's fall involved succession, war, labour demands and governance. A dynasty cannot be treated as a species that went extinct because it “refused variation.”

Now consider the republic Washington helped establish. Speech, criticism and reform preserve possibilities for correction. But abolition, women's suffrage and the civil rights movement were not a few lucky “mutations” effortlessly selected by an environment. They came from people organising, struggling and taking risks over many years. People's worth does not depend on their contribution to a regime's survival; rights need not first prove their adaptive usefulness.

An empire can suppress dissent, and a republic can close its ears. An institutional label guarantees no openness. We are comparing arrangements for receiving criticism and limiting power, not using the lifespans of two states as an experiment in natural selection.

Darwin drew inspiration from Malthus and tested a resulting question against biological evidence. We, too, can take questions across disciplinary borders. But we cannot return with a metaphor and pass it off as proof of political legitimacy. Evolution enriches the imagery of carving and construction. It helps us ask questions; the answers still have to be sought in the evidence proper to each field.

VII. Purposiveness Without Purpose

Kant's “purposiveness without purpose,” in the Critique of Judgment, is often invoked in discussions of beauty: we experience a form as fitting without first assigning it a use.

When considering organisms, Kant follows a different line. He examines living organisations, whose parts and wholes depend upon one another, as “natural purposes,” asking how we make teleological judgments. This is connected to the aesthetic formula but is not the same proposition, nor a simple announcement that nature has no designer.

The Critique of Judgment appeared in 1790; the Origin in 1859. Nearly seventy years separate them.

Darwin brought another kind of explanation into view: how could complex adaptations such as eyes and wings have arisen through life's history? He was not merely supplying the answer to an intuition of Kant's. The question now led into mechanisms of inheritance, reproduction and change across generations.

An eye “for seeing,” a wing “for flying”: we can begin describing a function through present use, but its origin must be placed in history. Natural selection need not foresee an eye for heritable differences that aid light sensitivity to accumulate. Nor need a structure's current function be its original one.

Compressed into a literary formula: differences without foresight, together with accumulation under particular conditions, can yield forms that appear designed for a use.

Blue whales, redwoods and human brains all belong to this long, branching natural history. Explaining adaptation does not require an additional external blueprint for every organ. Yet adding together a few terms is not a complete answer about the origin of life, every evolutionary process, or questions of faith.

Set this picture beside Zhuangzi's Hundun and a contrast appears. In the fable, Hundun really dies when the seven openings are bored. An injury to an individual does not automatically produce a new species; evolution concerns populations across generations. We borrow a question: how do forms arise, and where do they take possibilities that existed before? Darwin need not rewrite Zhuangzi's ending.

Gödel asked about the limits of proof in particular mathematical systems; Darwin asked how life changes. Neither proves the other. They meet in this series because we keep asking whether a construction, once formed, can enclose every subsequent possibility.

Evolution includes chance and loss, as well as long periods of relative stability. Change can fail; stability can persist. A species we call “open” still has no guarantee of surviving forever.

VIII. His Own Variation

Illness troubled Darwin through many years of his adult life.

His letters and personal accounts record nausea, vomiting, palpitations, fatigue and other symptoms. Later writers have proposed various explanations, but none can be treated here as a settled diagnosis. The suffering was real. It cannot be reduced to a bodily allegory of a thinker afraid to publish.

From 1842 he lived at Down House, organising his days around work, rest and walks. Sometimes he could work for only a few hours. He seldom attended public gatherings, but remained connected to the world through specimens, letters and visitors. Huxley, who called himself “Darwin's bulldog,” defended evolution publicly while retaining judgments of his own. He was not merely an instrument through which Darwin spoke.

Darwin kept investigating, and took part in controversy through books and correspondence. Orchid pollination, climbing plants, insectivorous plants, the effects of earthworms on soil: question followed question. His last book, published in 1881, was about earthworms.

A man who transformed our understanding of life bent down, in a late book, to look at worms. Small actions, extended over long spans of time, helped shape the ground beneath our feet.

To say that reality carved him into a scientist recalls Du Fu writing poetry amid hardship. But being changed by reality does not mean having no choices. Darwin sought the voyage, gathered materials, revised explanations and responded to Wallace, just as Du Fu was not simply the passive product of suffering. A man who had intended to be a clergyman gradually became the Darwin we know by answering one encounter after another.

Darwin died at Down House on 19 April 1882, aged seventy-three. He had lived there for nearly forty years and was initially to be buried in the local churchyard. Instead, following efforts by friends, family and others, his funeral took place at Westminster Abbey on 26 April, and he was buried near Newton.

A man who changed how people understood creation and nature was buried in a church. Scientific controversy, personal belief and public commemoration met within one building. The picture is more complicated than a slogan about science defeating religion.

IX. Life Carves Itself

Throughout this series, “carving” has changed shape with each person. What follows is the montage this essay makes of them.

Socrates questions presumed knowledge. Confucius asks what ritual becomes without humaneness. Laozi tests the limits of language. Zhuangzi's fables question imposed forms. Kant marks the limits of knowledge. Nietzsche raises a hammer to revalue values. Wang Yangming returns innate moral knowing to action. Shakyamuni offers a raft and asks that it be put down. The narrative of Jesus' Passion finds giving even in suffering. Gödel reveals the limits of particular formal theories through mathematics. Einstein changes the framework of Newtonian physics. Du Fu writes under the pressure of reality. The Homeric tradition carries oral voices into texts. Qin Shi Huang imposes order upon dissent but also leaves things unfinished. Washington establishes institutions and limits personal power. Alexander's conquests encounter the limits of people and the world.

In these images, human beings respond and act.

Darwin and Wallace made something else especially clear: life's changes do not require a human being to lay out their route beforehand.

Socrates need not first ask his questions; Kant need not first draw his boundaries. Long before humanity appeared, inheritance, variation and differences in reproductive success were already occurring. Wherever those conditions continue, the process does not depend on a human witness.

To keep the essay's metaphor: humanity did not invent life's carving and construction. Humanity itself comes from that history.

This change of standpoint goes deeper than the phrase “humans came from monkeys.” Humans are a branch of life's family tree, sharing ancestors with other apes, not judges standing outside the tree. We participate in it, while still bearing responsibility for conscious actions.

Socrates uses reasons to question Athenian certainties; natural selection changes populations through differences in reproduction. The former can reflect on his standards. The latter has no subject who knows it is “carving.” The distinction matters. We place them together to see the living history out of which human questioning grows, not to collapse criticism, elimination and human worth into one thing.

In my imagined scene at the bridgehead, another figure arrives. A black Victorian coat, a long beard, a book about earthworms in his hand. For the moment he does not join the argument. He looks down first, at the earth beneath the bridge. He directs our attention beyond the plans in the builders' hands:

In this metaphor, the bridge is not only something people build. The bridge is growing by itself.

Notes

The voyage's observations are distinguished from later interpretation, drawing on Darwin's autobiography, correspondence and publications. Identification of the Galápagos finches, reconstruction of their island provenance and subsequent records of tool use by woodpecker finches belong to different stages.

The 1838 insight, the 1842 sketch, the 1844 essay, the large book begun in 1856 and the 1858 joint publication were not twenty years of complete silence. “Confessing a murder,” in the letter to Hooker of 11 January 1844, refers to accepting that species are not immutable.

The 1858 presentation combined Darwin's earlier writings and letter to Gray with Wallace's essay. Wallace had not participated in arranging it beforehand and later expressed appreciation. The Origin's first printing comprised 1,250 copies; booksellers' orders and retail sales to readers are different measures.

Natural selection involves heritable differences and differential reproductive success. It is an important, not exclusive, mechanism of evolution. Mutation is random with respect to future adaptive need, not without physical causes; selection is not a purely random process. Carving, construction and the political comparisons are the author's interpretive metaphors, not grounds for deriving human worth or political rights.

Kant's aesthetic purposiveness should be distinguished from teleological judgments about organisms: see Critique of Judgment, §§10–17 and 64–75. Hundun, Gödel and the figures at the bridgehead form literary comparisons, not interdisciplinary proofs or historical meetings. Gödel's results concern effectively axiomatized theories with sufficient arithmetic strength under the relevant consistency conditions; they do not apply directly to biological or political systems.

Darwin's chronic illness is amply documented in his correspondence; its precise cause remains unsettled. The account of Down House, earthworms and Westminster Abbey neither romanticises illness as the price of genius nor delivers a single verdict on his faith.
