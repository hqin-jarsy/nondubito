狄拉克：方程比观察先到

Dirac: The Equation Arrived First

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、最安静的人

物理学界流传着一个非官方的计量单位，叫“一个狄拉克”。

一个狄拉克，等于每小时说一个词。

这是同事间的玩笑，倒也留下了一个很鲜明的人。有些人用说话理清思路，保罗·狄拉克常常让别人等很久，最后只等到一个“是”，或者“不是”。围绕他的寡言，故事越讲越多。有时，一个安静的人就这样被热闹地讲完了。

但我们还没有走到他工作的地方。

他在布里斯托尔长大。父亲教法语，要求儿子跟自己说法语。狄拉克后来回忆，想说的话表达不出来，他便宁愿不说。这段回忆值得听，却不能替我们解释完他的沉默，更不能替我们判断他有没有爱人的能力。

在物理学里，他找到了一种格外有力量的表达。不是英语，不是法语。是方程。

1928年，他发表了一个描述电子的方程。论文在1月2日收稿，不等于他在那一天忽然写完了一切。接下来的几年，他还要一遍遍追问：方程里那些自己也没准备好接受的解，究竟意味着什么？

1931年，他明确提出反电子。1932年，卡尔·安德森在云雾室里留下了后来被认作正电子的径迹。1933年发表的进一步研究，使那个带正电、质量与电子相当的粒子更清楚地站到了人们面前。

方程先到。解释并没有同时到。实验又从自己的路上走来。

这才是这个故事奇异的地方。不是一个天才闭上眼睛，凭空看见了宇宙。量子力学、相对论、光谱实验，前人的工作都在他手里。可他把这些要求放进同一个方程之后，方程带他走到了没有正电子观测可供指认的地方。

他本来是在写电子。写出来的东西，却向他提出了一个他没有事先安排的问题。

孔德在问，知识怎样落到可靠的地面。波普尔在问，怎样让世界有机会说不。走到狄拉克这里，我想再停一停：一个问题已经可以交给世界之前，人是怎么走到它面前的？

谁先到的？在这个故事里，方程先到的。

二、美比正确更重要

狄拉克在1963年的一篇文章里说过，比起让方程符合实验，更重要的是让方程具有美。

这句话很容易把人吓住。实验不符合也没关系？物理学家的审美，可以命令自然服从？

如果这样读，狄拉克方程反而被读轻了。那不是一句“我觉得美，所以是真的”。他面对的是一些不能随便丢掉的要求：电子的量子行为、相对论，以及怎样让波函数随时间演化。已知的物理在给他出题，他要找到一种能把这些要求放在一起的形式。

薛定谔的非相对论方程还不能完成这件事。狄拉克不满足于在旧形式外面贴补丁。他要一个对时间是一阶的方程，又要让它满足相对论的要求。时间与空间的关系，不只是纸面上的匀称。

自由电子的方程可以写成这样：

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = (c α·p + βmc²)ψ

不必会算。看见这一行就够了：那个看似简洁的形式，里面装着彼此牵制的条件。α和β不是普通的数，而是矩阵；p在这里是动量算符。为满足这些条件，最小的矩阵表示是四乘四，波函数有四个分量。

不是“要线性”三个字独自变出了反物质，也不是四个分量一出现，就已经贴好了两个电子、两个反电子的标签。形式到了，读懂它还要时间。

狄拉克起先也想把陌生的东西安顿在熟悉的世界里。他试过用质子解释空穴。外尔关于质量的论证，奥本海默关于电子与这种“质子”湮灭的反对，使这条路越来越难走。一个人没有单靠自己的审美把答案找全。方程、同事的反驳、已知的物理，一起把他逼到了更陌生的门前。

美在这里不是奖章。是他舍不得放下的一种整合：这些东西似乎应该能够一起说清楚，不必东补一块、西补一块。

他相信那种整合值得追下去。

相信，不等于担保。他后来提出的某些漂亮想法并没有获得他期待的实验支持。自然没有签过一张保证书，说这位物理学家觉得美的东西都是真的。

但没有这份对美的信任，他也许会更早停下来。危险和力量，在同一个地方。

三、第一道裂缝

狄拉克方程在这一组文章里打开了第一道裂缝。

不是科学之外突然出现了一个不受检验的真理。裂开的是我们把认识过程讲得过于平整的习惯：先有观察，再总结规律；或者先抛出一个想法，再等待证伪。用这几句话讲完，路上最费力的部分就不见了。

孔德并不认为观察可以脱离理论。波普尔也不会把狄拉克赶出科学。他完全可以把这看作一个大胆而可检验的理论。问题不在于他们有没有一个词可以收下结果。

问题在结果出现之前。

狄拉克没有先想好“宇宙里也许有正电子”，再找一个方程替这个愿望穿上衣服。他为电子寻找合适的描述，描述里却多出了他不容易安顿的东西。他得让原来的意图退一点，听听自己写下的形式还要求什么。

我把这个姿态叫作听方程说话。

不是方程有意志。是写方程的人也不能随意决定它的后果。某些条件既然放在了一起，就会彼此约束。你可以改条件，却不能只因为不喜欢一个结果，就装作它没有跟来。

这里有两种“必须”，不能混成一种。接受这些前提，就必须接受相应的数学后果；但自然是否接受这套前提，仍要问自然。纸上的必然，还不是世界已经作出的承诺。

知道这一点，“听”才不是迷信。狄拉克可以被方程带到意料之外，也必须允许实验把他带回来。

美是一种认知的引导。它使人愿意往某个方向多走一段，愿意在别人已经厌倦的地方继续看。它还不是结论。

指南针不是目的地。可没有指南针，有些路根本不会有人去走。

那道裂缝里传来的，是发现尚未完成时的声音。

四、他和爱因斯坦

爱因斯坦给狄拉克留下了一道绕不过去的要求。

一个描述电子的量子理论，也得认真面对相对论。不能因为电子已经在原来的理论里工作得很好，就让它一到高速运动那里便换一套世界。

敬重一个人的工作，有时就是这样发生的。你把他的要求带进自己的问题，带到一个他没有替你走完的地方。

这比把两个人的名字并排写在伟人名册上更亲近，也更不省事。相对论不会递给你一张现成的量子方程。两者都不能轻轻放下，你就得在它们之间工作。它们挤压你，不让你用一个好听的答案过关。

1933年的诺贝尔演讲里，狄拉克谈的正是这种挤压：给量子力学加上相对论的要求，会限制粒子可以有什么性质。限制没有只把路堵住。它也让本来不知道的东西显出了轮廓。

不是爱因斯坦已经把狄拉克的答案藏在口袋里。是一个人把世界问深了一点，另一个人接过来，再问下去。

我不想用狄拉克有没有为谁流泪，来替这份关系称重。没有看见眼泪，不等于没有悲伤；几则回忆，也装不下一生的亲近与疏远。把他写成只有方程、没有日常感情的人，仍然是在替一个寡言的人说得太多。

我们能清楚看见的，是工作留下的回应。

爱因斯坦没有替他决定怎样走。他也没有因为敬重爱因斯坦，就只许自己重复爱因斯坦。

有一种承接，是把别人打开的门继续打开。门后出现了连开门的人也没有预先看见的东西。

五、沉默即认知

孔德在铺地面。波普尔在画线。狄拉克坐下来，继续做他的方程。

把这个场景叫作“沉默即认知”，容易引起误会。沉默不能证明任何物理结论。一个人不说话，不会比说话的人天然更接近真理。

但沉默可以给某种工作留出地方。

不用立刻把还没想清的东西说圆。不用为了让别人满意，马上宣告“我猜到了什么”。一个符号暂时不顺，一项推导还过不去，就容许自己停在那里。还没有好听的故事。先不要有。

狄拉克的方程当然也是语言。它精确得可以被别人检查，可以被指出错误。这里没有一个绕开表达、直接握住自然本身的神秘人。

只是表达也有不同的速度。有时，一个人还说不出自己的判断来自哪里，手里的工作已经逼他不能照旧做下去了。他先调整一个式子，删掉一条路，重算一次。后来才有可以交给别人讨论的理由。

我们平常把成品叫作知识。论文发表了，推导排好了，结论可以引用了。那些还不成句子的停顿，就退到了后面。

可如果没有那些停顿，成品从哪里来？

狄拉克紧接孔德、波普尔出场，是要把我们的目光拉回这里。不是宣布前两个人的学说失败了，而是别让知识的资格审查遮住知识怎样发生。

他不说多余的话。我们也先不替他把话填满。

六、他和本轮其他人

狄拉克的方程用数学打开了缝隙。后面的人，会从别的地方靠近。

屈原用美。你不能把《离骚》交给实验室，要求它像电子理论一样提供可重复的预测。但读到某一句，忽然认出自己不愿放弃什么，那也不是一件没有发生的事。

波普尔并不需要否认诗的意义。可承认诗有意义，与读到那一句时自己的生活动了一下，仍不是同一层经验。这组文章要追的，不止是给诗在门外留一个合法的位置。

狄拉克的美感会进入一个接受物理检验的方程。屈原的美不以此为归宿。两条路不能因为都叫美，就被铺成同一条；它们也不必等变成同一种知识，才值得我们跟着走。

麦克林托克会带来对有机体的感觉。那不是先有神奇预感，后来到显微镜里看见一个转座子。种植、观察、遗传分析，做了又做，她才越来越知道什么差异值得追问。感觉长在工作里，又使她看见别人略过的东西。

狄拉克也不是没有论证就知道自己正确。他的判断受到数学训练，受到已知物理的磨炼。我们要留住的，是论证还没有全部写出来时，他为什么愿意沿这条路走。

波兰尼会在本乐章的后面接住这条线。你知道怎样骑自行车，却未必能把身体每一刻如何调整说成一份可照抄的说明书。说不全，不等于没有学会；写出了说明书，也不等于别人已经会骑。

这并不给直觉免检的资格。自行车照样会摔，漂亮方程照样可能失败。只是人在学会、判断、发现时做的事，比最后说出的规则多。

从狄拉克到波兰尼，那条线会越来越清楚：说不出来的，不全是知识剩下的渣滓。有些东西，正在支撑知识抵达。

七、那个负号

回到那个方程。

相对论给自由粒子的能量和动量留下这样一个关系：

E = ±√(|p|²c² + m²c⁴)

正。负。两条路。

问题不是“能量绝不许是负数”。能量零点常常可以选择。这里麻烦的是负能量那一支向下没有尽头，而理论又允许跃迁。一个普通电子如果能一路往下掉，我们熟悉的稳定世界该怎样存在？

不能只拿橡皮擦掉那个负号。你删掉的，也许正是方程其他部分离不开的东西。

狄拉克看着它。

然后跟着它走。

他提出，让负能量态几乎都被电子占满，留下的空穴表现得像带正电的粒子。最初，他希望这个粒子是已经熟悉的质子。世界就不必多出一位新住客。

可那位住客太重了。外尔指出，空穴应当具有与电子相同的质量。奥本海默又追问，如果它就是质子，电子与质子的湮灭会怎样威胁普通物质。两条反对意见，不是同一句“数学证明原子不能存在”。它们一起使那个熟悉的名字放不进来。

1931年，狄拉克走向反电子。1932年，安德森的实验从另一头走来。

后来，量子场论重新讲述了这个故事。今天的正电子，是相对于真空的正能量激发，不是一个带着负能量到处跑的电子。理论也会改写自己用来理解成功的语言。

那个负号没有直接把一切说完。它使一个问题再也不能被随手略去。

故事还往外展开。1933年，狄拉克谈到反质子的可能，同时承认电子的理论不能原封不动地解决质子的全部问题。1955年发现反质子，1956年发现反中子。后来的人用新的理论、新的实验，把镜像世界一点点打开。

不能把几十年的工作都塞回1928年那一页，仿佛后来的人只是在依次点名。也不是每种粒子都有一个与自身不同的伙伴：有些粒子本身就是自己的反粒子。

方程不只是比观察先到。它还会比写方程的人先到。

人写下了一组条件，条件之间的联系却超过了他起初的打算。接下来的工作，是发现自己究竟写下了什么。

这不是用数学取消检验。是让检验有机会走向此前没有想过的地方。方程把人带到门口，门外有没有那个世界，还得继续开门。

我们常喜欢福尔摩斯式的结论：排除所有不可能，剩下的就是真相。可在物理里，还要问一句，你真的列全了吗？前提本身会不会需要改？狄拉克故事的力量，不在于替我们免掉这些问题。

而在于一个人没有因为答案陌生，就先替世界关上门。

八、桥头

现在，让他走进本系列想象中的桥头。

狄拉克走过来的时候，几乎没有声音。他没有急着看任何人。他在看远处。远处什么也没有——至少别人这么觉得。

波普尔试图跟他说话。“你是先猜到反电子，再去找方程的吗？”

狄拉克看了他一眼。沉默了很久。然后说：“不是。”

然后又沉默了。

波普尔等着。他还想问怎样检验。狄拉克没有拦住那个问题，只是手里的笔还没有停。

孔德翻着日历。他在找狄拉克的位置——物理学家，应该在牛顿那个月附近。格子当然可以添进去。可添进去之后，那个负号带来的陌生感，好像还留在格子外面。

他给一个结果找到了位置。那个结果怎样把人带出原来的安排，却没有一起装进去。

康德在远处站着。这一次，他看着那支笔，没有急着替它命名。纸上必须如此，世界是否如此。两句话之间，还有一段路。

狄拉克找了一个安静的位置站下来。他从口袋里掏出笔，在空气中写了一个方程。那个方程有一个负号。

没有人看清那个负号。安德森留下的径迹，却仿佛在远处亮了一下。

他站在那里。安静。完全安静。

但如果你把耳朵贴在他站的位置的桥面上，会听到一种极轻的嗡鸣。那是方程的声音。从缝隙里传上来。从孔德灌的水泥下面，从波普尔画的线那边。

缝隙里有东西。方程知道。

I. The Quietest Man

Physics has an unofficial unit of measurement: one dirac.

One dirac equals one word per hour.

A joke among colleagues, but it leaves a vivid figure. Some people think by talking. Paul Dirac often made people wait, only to give them a yes or a no. Stories accumulated around his reticence. Sometimes a quiet person gets talked about so much that we think there is nothing left to discover.

We have not yet reached his work.

He grew up in Bristol. His father taught French and required his son to speak French with him. Dirac later recalled preferring silence when he could not express what he wanted to say. The recollection deserves a hearing. It does not explain his entire silence, much less tell us whether he could love.

In physics he found an exceptionally powerful form of expression. Not English. Not French. Equations.

In 1928 he published an equation describing the electron. The paper was received on January 2; that does not mean he suddenly wrote the whole thing that day. For years afterward he would still be asking what its unfamiliar solutions meant.

In 1931 he explicitly proposed an anti-electron. In 1932 Carl Anderson obtained the cloud-chamber tracks that would be identified with the positron. Further work published in 1933 brought this positively charged particle, with a mass comparable to the electron’s, more clearly into view.

The equation arrived first. Its interpretation did not arrive all at once. Experiment approached along a road of its own.

That is what makes the story remarkable. Not a genius shutting his eyes and seeing the universe from nowhere. Quantum mechanics, relativity, spectroscopy: other people’s work was already in his hands. But when he brought their demands together, the equation led him somewhere no observed positron could yet point out.

He had set out to write about the electron. What he wrote asked him a question he had not arranged in advance.

Comte asks how knowledge can find reliable ground. Popper asks how the world can be allowed to say no. With Dirac, I want to pause at an earlier moment: before a question is ready to be put to the world, how does someone reach it?

What arrived first? In this story, the equation.

II. Beauty Matters More Than Being Right

In a 1963 essay, Dirac placed mathematical beauty ahead of agreement with experiment as a guide to equations.

It is a startling claim. Does experimental disagreement not matter? Can a physicist’s taste order nature to obey?

Read that way, the claim actually diminishes his achievement. The equation was not a declaration that whatever he found beautiful must be true. There were demands he could not lightly abandon: the electron’s quantum behaviour, relativity, and the evolution of a wave function through time. Known physics was setting the problem. He wanted a form that could hold its demands together.

Schrödinger’s nonrelativistic equation did not yet do that. Dirac was not satisfied with attaching patches to its exterior. He wanted an equation first-order in time that also met relativistic requirements. The relationship between space and time was not merely a matter of looking balanced on paper.

For a free electron, the equation can be written:

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = (c α·p + βmc²)ψ

You need not calculate anything. Just notice how much that short line contains. The α’s and β are matrices, not ordinary numbers; p here is the momentum operator. Meeting the combined conditions requires, in the smallest matrix representation, four-by-four matrices and a wave function with four components.

Linearity alone did not conjure antimatter. Nor did four components arrive neatly labelled as two electrons and two anti-electrons. The form had arrived. Understanding it still took time.

Dirac, too, tried to accommodate the unfamiliar inside the familiar world. He attempted to identify holes with protons. Weyl’s argument about mass and Oppenheimer’s objection about annihilation made that route increasingly difficult. One man’s taste did not supply the whole answer. Equations, colleagues’ objections, and established physics together pushed him toward a stranger door.

Beauty here is not a medal awarded afterward. It is an integration he cannot easily relinquish: these things seem capable of being understood together, without another patch at every turn.

He believes that integration is worth pursuing.

Belief is not a guarantee. Some of his later attractive ideas did not receive the experimental support he hoped for. Nature never signed a promise that everything this physicist found beautiful would be true.

Without that trust in beauty, though, he might have stopped sooner. The danger and the power occupy the same place.

III. The First Crack

Within this group of essays, the Dirac equation opens the first crack.

Not a truth outside science, exempt from testing. What cracks is our habit of telling too smooth a story about knowing: first observe, then generalize; or first propose an idea, then wait for refutation. Told in those few words, the hardest part of the journey disappears.

Comte did not think observation could dispense with theory. Popper would not expel Dirac from science. He could readily describe this as a bold, testable theory. The question is not whether either philosopher has a term that accommodates the result.

The question lies before the result.

Dirac did not first decide that the universe might contain positrons and then find an equation to dress his wish. He sought a description of the electron. The description contained something he could not comfortably accommodate. He had to let his original intention retreat a little, to hear what else the form demanded.

I call this listening to the equation.

Not because equations have wills. Because their authors cannot arbitrarily choose their consequences. Once certain conditions are brought together, they constrain one another. You can change the conditions. You cannot retain them and pretend an unwelcome consequence has not followed.

There are two necessities here. Accept the premises and you must accept the mathematical consequence. Whether nature accepts those premises is a question for nature. Necessity on paper is not yet a promise made by the world.

That distinction keeps listening from becoming superstition. The equation can take Dirac beyond his expectations. Experiment must still be allowed to bring him back.

Beauty can guide cognition. It makes a person willing to go farther in one direction, to keep looking where others have grown tired. It is not yet a conclusion.

A compass is not a destination. Without a compass, however, some roads would never be travelled.

What comes through the crack is the sound of discovery still happening.

IV. Dirac and Einstein

Einstein left Dirac a demand he could not go around.

A quantum theory of the electron would have to take relativity seriously. It could not give the electron one world at low speed and a wholly separate arrangement when it moved fast.

Respect for another person’s work sometimes takes this form. You carry its demands into your own problem, beyond the point to which that person has already accompanied you.

It is closer, and much less convenient, than placing two names side by side in a register of great men. Relativity does not hand you a ready-made quantum equation. Neither set of demands can lightly be abandoned. You work between them. They press on you. An agreeable answer will not get you through.

In his 1933 Nobel lecture, Dirac described precisely such pressure: imposing relativistic requirements on quantum mechanics constrains the properties a particle can have. The constraints do not merely block a road. They give something previously unknown a shape.

Einstein was not carrying Dirac’s answer in his pocket. One person had deepened a question about the world. Another took it up and continued.

I do not want to weigh that relationship by counting whom Dirac wept for. Not seeing tears does not establish an absence of grief. A handful of recollections cannot contain a lifetime of closeness and distance. To give this quiet man equations but no ordinary affections would still be to say too much on his behalf.

What we can see clearly is the response left in his work.

Einstein did not decide his next move for him. Dirac’s admiration did not require him to repeat Einstein.

One way of receiving another person’s work is to keep opening the door they opened. Something appears beyond it that even the first person through had not foreseen.

V. Silence as Cognition

Comte lays a floor. Popper draws a line. Dirac sits down and continues with his equation.

Calling this “silence as cognition” can mislead. Silence proves no physical result. Someone who does not speak is not, for that reason, closer to the truth than someone who does.

But silence can make room for a kind of work.

No need to round an unfinished thought into an explanation immediately. No need to please an audience by announcing what you have guessed. A symbol is troublesome. A derivation will not go through. You can stay there. There is no good story yet. Let there be none yet.

Dirac’s equation is itself a language: exact enough for other people to inspect, exact enough for them to find a mistake. No mysterious figure has bypassed expression and taken hold of nature itself.

Expression has different speeds, though. Sometimes a person cannot yet explain the source of a judgment, while the work already prevents them from continuing as before. First they adjust a formula, abandon a route, calculate again. Only later do the reasons become ready for discussion.

We usually call the finished thing knowledge. The paper is published. The derivation is in order. The conclusion can be quoted. The pauses that never formed sentences recede behind it.

Without those pauses, where did the finished thing come from?

Dirac follows Comte and Popper here to draw our eyes back to that question. Not to announce that their philosophies have failed. To keep the examination of knowledge’s credentials from hiding how knowledge happens.

He does not fill the air with unnecessary words. For now, neither should we fill his silence for him.

VI. Dirac and the Others in This Round

Dirac’s equation opens a crack through mathematics. Others will approach from elsewhere.

Qu Yuan through beauty. You cannot deliver the Li Sao to a laboratory and require it to produce repeatable predictions like a theory of electrons. But when a line makes you recognize something you will not give up, that is not nothing happening.

Popper need not deny poetry’s significance. Recognizing that poetry has significance, however, is not the same experience as having your life shift while you read a line. This group of essays seeks more than a legitimate place for poetry outside a scientific boundary.

Dirac’s aesthetic judgment enters an equation exposed to physical tests. Qu Yuan’s beauty has no such destination. Two paths do not become one because we use the word beauty for both. Nor must they become the same kind of knowledge before either is worth following.

McClintock brings a feeling for the organism. Not a magical hunch followed by sighting a transposable element through a microscope. Planting, observing, genetic analysis, repeated work: she increasingly learned which differences deserved pursuit. Feeling grew inside that work and helped her notice what others passed over.

Dirac, likewise, did not know he was right without argument. Mathematical training and established physics educated his judgment. What matters here is why, before the whole argument was written down, he was willing to go this way.

Polanyi will take up this thread later in the movement. You know how to ride a bicycle without being able to state every adjustment your body makes in instructions someone else could simply copy. Not being able to say it all does not mean you have not learned. Writing down instructions does not mean another person can already ride.

Intuition gets no exemption from testing. Bicycles fall. Beautiful equations fail. But learning, judging, and discovering involve more than the rules eventually stated.

From Dirac to Polanyi, a thread grows clearer: what we cannot fully articulate is not all the residue of knowledge. Some of it supports knowledge’s arrival.

VII. The Minus Sign

Back to the equation.

Relativity gives this relationship between a free particle’s energy and momentum:

E = ±√(|p|²c² + m²c⁴)

Plus. Minus. Two branches.

The problem is not that energy can never have a negative value. Its zero can often be chosen. The difficulty here is a negative-energy branch without a lower bound, together with transitions allowed by the theory. If an ordinary electron could keep falling down it, what would become of the stable world we know?

You cannot simply erase the minus sign. What you remove may be inseparable from the rest of the equation.

Dirac looks at it.

Then he follows it.

He proposes that almost all the negative-energy states are occupied by electrons. A vacancy, a hole, behaves like a positively charged particle. Initially he hopes to identify it with the familiar proton. The world need not acquire another inhabitant.

But that inhabitant is too heavy. Weyl argues that the hole must have the electron’s mass. Oppenheimer asks how annihilation between electrons and these supposed protons would affect ordinary matter. These are distinct objections, not one proof that atoms cannot exist. Together they make the familiar name harder to fit.

In 1931 Dirac moves to the anti-electron. In 1932 Anderson’s experiment approaches from the other end.

Quantum field theory later retells the story. Today a positron is a positive-energy excitation relative to the vacuum, not an electron carrying negative energy around. A theory can change the language in which it understands its own success.

The minus sign has not said everything at once. It has made a question impossible to brush aside.

The story widens. In 1933 Dirac discusses the possibility of antiprotons while acknowledging that electron theory cannot simply be transferred to solve every problem of the proton. The antiproton is discovered in 1955; the antineutron in 1956. Later researchers open the mirror world further through new theories and experiments.

Decades of work cannot all be folded into the page of 1928, as though everyone afterward merely called the roll. Nor must every particle have a distinct partner: some particles are their own antiparticles.

Yet the thought remains powerful: the equation arrived before the person who wrote it.

A person sets down conditions. Their connections exceed his original intention. His next task is to discover what he has actually written.

Mathematics has not abolished testing. It has given testing somewhere previously unimagined to go. The equation brings a person to the door. Whether that world exists beyond it still has to be investigated.

We like the Sherlock Holmes conclusion: exclude every impossibility, and what remains must be true. In physics one more question remains. Have you really listed the possibilities? Could the premises themselves need changing? The power of Dirac’s story does not lie in exempting us from those questions.

It lies in a person refusing to close the door simply because the answer is unfamiliar.

VIII. The Bridgehead

Now let him enter this series’ imagined bridgehead.

Dirac arrives almost without sound. He is in no hurry to look at anyone. He is looking into the distance. At nothing, or so the others think.

Popper tries to speak to him. “Did you first guess there was an anti-electron, then look for the equation?”

Dirac glances at him. Silence. A long silence. Then: “No.”

Then more silence.

Popper waits. He still wants to ask about testing. Dirac does not obstruct the question. His pen simply has not stopped moving.

Comte leafs through his calendar. He is looking for Dirac’s place: a physicist, somewhere near Newton’s month. Of course a square can be found. Afterward, though, the strangeness brought by the minus sign seems to remain outside it.

He has found a place for a result. How that result took someone beyond his original arrangements has not fitted inside with it.

Kant stands at a distance. This time he watches the pen without hurrying to name what it does. It must be so on paper. Is it so in the world? Between those sentences, a stretch of road remains.

Dirac finds a quiet place. He takes a pen from his pocket and writes an equation in the air. The equation has a minus sign.

No one sees the sign clearly. Far away, Anderson’s track seems briefly to shine.

He stands there. Quiet. Completely quiet.

But put your ear to the bridge where he stands and you will hear a faint hum. The sound of the equation, rising through the cracks. From beneath Comte’s grout. From the other side of Popper’s line.

There is something in the cracks. The equation knows.
