柏格森：绵延

Bergson: Duration

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、那场辩论

一九二二年四月六日。巴黎。法国哲学学会。

两种理解时间的方式，在这里迎面相遇。

爱因斯坦四十三岁，相对论已经让他举世闻名。他的1921年度诺贝尔物理学奖要到1922年稍后才宣布，主要表彰光电效应定律；不能把这场四月的会面写成他刚凭相对论领完奖。

柏格森六十二岁，是当时声望很高的哲学家，著作和演讲吸引过大批听众。他1913年在纽约的讲学尤其轰动；“百老汇第一次堵车”则是过于好记、却不宜照单全收的传奇。

辩论的题目是时间。

柏格森的发言追问：物理学处理时间的方式，是否已经把我们经验时间的全部问题处理完了？他把那种前后相渗、不能像珠子一样分开摆放的活时间，叫作“绵延”（durée）。关于这一场的完整发言与争论，应当回到记录，而不是凭想象添上精确的三十分钟独白。

爱因斯坦的回应，把心理时间与物理时间区分开。

那句后来被反复引用的话，就在这个区分里。

按会议记录，他否认一种独立的“哲学家的时间”，同时承认心理时间与物理时间的区别。

“哲学家的时间不存在。”这句话很锋利，但不能因此改写成“人的主观经验不存在”。

两人争的，不只是钟表和心情能否并存。柏格森也对相对论中的同时性、实际与可能的时间提出解释；这些解释确有技术上的争议。不能为了替经验保留位置，就预先判定他在物理学上一定没错。

后来的讲述喜欢把这场会面压成一句话：科学赢了，哲学输了。

这句话比两个人真正提出的问题简单得多。

柏格森的声望后来变化，有思想潮流、政治与学术制度等多种原因。1927年的诺贝尔文学奖也不是什么正式的科学降级处分。他既没有因一句反驳从此不存在，也没有因为今天重新受到关注就自动赢回所有论点。

但是。

一个人怎样活过时间，仍然值得问。承认这一点，不需要先击败相对论。

二、绵延

先把时间拨回柏格森年轻时。

1878年，他进入巴黎高等师范学院，与让·饶勒斯同届。他受过很好的数学训练，也发表过一道几何问题的解答。这一点重要：他不是因为不会算，才来问计算究竟把什么当作时间。

毕业后，他在昂热、克莱蒙费朗等地教哲学。后来关于绵延的思考，在这些年的工作中逐渐成形。

我们可以借一块黑板，把那个问题放到眼前。以下不是某一天课堂的实录。

一个物体从A到B。位置如何随时间变化？怎样计算某一时刻的速度？

这是个标准的微积分问题。导数。瞬时速度。柏格森自己受过严格的数学训练，他知道怎么算。

但是那一天他停下了。

他看着自己写在黑板上的东西。他突然意识到一件事——

导数把瞬时变化率表达出来。这个数学操作不要求宇宙有一份最小时间颗粒，也不是先找到一小段不能再分的时间再相除。连续模型恰恰容许不断细分。

可以吗？

可以计算和切分时间的表示，不等于已经把活过这一段的经验说尽。柏格森想追问的是这一步，不是给微积分挑一个它并没有的漏洞。

那时间本身是什么样的？

让我们想象他在黑板前停了一会儿。停顿属于这个场景，不是他的笔记已经证明的一次顿悟。

把时间画成线，有极大的用途。但当线上的点被当成彼此之外的单位时，某种经验会从图上滑出去。

但他自己经验到的时间不是这样。

他自己经验到的时间是一个流动。他正在经验的此刻里，包含着他刚才经验的一切（不是作为分开的另一段，是作为此刻的一部分），也包含着他对下一刻的预期（同样是作为此刻的一部分）。这个流动不是一条线。这个流动里没有可以被分开的"段"。这个流动里也没有可以被倒回去的方向——他经验过的不能被取消，他没经验的不能被预先经验。

他叫这个流动为"绵延"（durée）。

这个词在法语里就是"持续""延续"的意思，没有什么神秘含义。柏格森用它来指意识里那种活的时间——不是被分段的，不是被空间化的，不是被钟表测量的——那种只能在自己内部被经验的时间。

这个问题不是一堂课上的漂亮惊叹。它在后来的著作里被反复论证、修正、推进。

他的博士论文（一八八九年发表，叫《时间与自由意志》）专门讲这个。他后来的所有重要著作——《物质与记忆》（一八九六），《创造的进化》（一九零七），《道德与宗教的两个来源》（一九三二）——都是在这个最初的直觉上展开。

在柏格森的论述中，绵延不是给钟表读数另加一种快慢感，而是要认真面对经验本身怎样连续。

物理学用可操作的关系比较时间，包括钟与钟怎样比较；相对论也不能被简化成所有人共用一个外部大钟。这样的工作不可缺少，却不等于一段等待的第一人称叙述。

绵延是从里面经验的——你不需要工具，你的意识本身就是绵延。你想起昨天你笑过，那个笑不是消失了的过去，那个笑还在你此刻里。你期待明天要见的人，那个人不是不存在的未来，那个期待已经在你此刻里。绵延的此刻里有时间的厚度。

把某个现在表示成坐标点，点没有长度。这是表示的性质，不足以判定物理学没有能力研究记忆或神经活动。

绵延的此刻有厚度——你的此刻里有你的过去和你的未来。

两种描述没有简单互相替代。至于它们是否要求两个彼此独立的实体，则是下一步的哲学问题，不能由比喻提前证明。

1922年的争执，发生在柏格森已思考这个问题几十年之后。爱因斯坦不是否认有人会等待、记忆、感到漫长；他质疑的是这些经验如何进入关于物理时间的论证。

这是值得认真回答的质疑，不能只用“他不懂体验”来打发。

反过来，也不能因为物理学的时间概念有严格用途，就认为人的等待与记忆不再值得自己的描述。

我想保留的不是一场反科学的胜利，而是一个不急着由谁吞并谁的问题。

三、笑

让我们暂时离开时间的问题，看一看柏格森的另一面。

1900年，他出版《笑：论滑稽的意义》。也是这一年，他到法兰西学院任教。一本谈时间的哲学家写的小书，这回谈的是喜剧。

谈喜剧。柏格森。

这件事乍看有点不协调——一个讲意识深处的时间结构的哲学家写了一本谈笑的书。但读了之后你会发现这两件事是同一件事的两面。

柏格森问：什么让人发笑？

他研究的主要是滑稽，不是宣布喜悦、紧张或亲密关系里的所有笑都出自同一个原因。他提出一个很有穿透力的方向——

机械的东西嫁接在生命之上。

书里有走路跌倒的例子：身体还沿着惯性往前，没能及时适应眼前的情境。我们若用香蕉皮来重演它，是熟悉的生活化改写，不是原文必须有的道具。笑声看见了活人的动作忽然像一个失灵的装置。

笑就是对这种"生命堕落为机械"的反应。

柏格森用这个原理分析喜剧的几乎全部类型。不断重复的口头禅是机械嫁接在语言上。一根筋的人物（莫里哀的吝啬鬼）是机械嫁接在性格上。错认（双胞胎弄混了）是机械嫁接在身份上。模仿一个人（特别是模仿他的小动作）是把他还原为可重复的机械模式。

这条线能照亮许多喜剧，也可以被后来的读者质疑、补充。它不是一张让所有笑声都必须报到的清单。

我愿意把它放到绵延旁边读，但不把两本书说成一个公式。

绵延提醒我们：经历过的会进入下一刻，同一个动作也会带着不同的过去。因此，一个人的生活不只是外部可重复动作的串联。

笑学说说：当一个本来应当是流动的东西（一个人的行为，一个人的语言，一个人的反应）变得不流动了——变得可以预测、可以重复、可以被机械模式描述——我们的意识立刻指认这件事，并以笑作为反应。

如果借一个比喻，笑像在提醒生命别太僵硬。但“绵延的免疫系统”是我们的说法，不是他的命题。

而且提醒也可能变成纠正与惩罚。柏格森注意到笑的社会作用：群体用笑把不合拍者拉回来。那未必总是自由在获胜。

这本书里柏格森还讲了一件更深的事。他说喜剧之所以有效，需要观众在一瞬间放下自己的同情心。如果你看见那个滑倒的人，你立刻意识到他可能受伤了，你不会笑。你会担心。喜剧效果出现，是因为你在那一秒把他当成了一个可观察的机械装置，不是当成一个跟你一样的有意识的人。

所以笑不自动是恶意，也不自动善良。短暂地把同情放开，可能让我们看见僵硬，也可能让我们错过一个人的疼。

书的冷静分析留下这种不舒服：我们笑的时候，那个具体的人有没有从眼前暂时消失？这不必被扩大成“所有笑都否认人格”。

这与本乐章的“他者”主题相接。但不能据此把爱因斯坦的一句理论反驳写成不承认柏格森是人。争论、笑声与暴力有不同的层次，不宜用同一顶帽子盖住。

柏格森继续阐述自己的问题，我们也可以继续追问他的答案。让一个人的思想得到认真对待，不等于保证它不会被反驳。

他守住了自己的位置。

四、两种时间

让我们回到时间。

“科学击败哲学”的胜负故事，常让问题比原来更窄。我们不必替柏格森赢回一场比赛，才能看看一种普通经验：钟表记录了很久，我回想起来却像没有中间。

一个容易接近、也容易被用过头的例子，是全身麻醉之后的回忆。

麻醉。

想象一次手术：用药，意识中的场景淡出去，再醒来时，已经在恢复室。这是某些人的回忆方式，不是每一次麻醉都一模一样的报告。

中间过了三个小时。

钟表记录了三个小时。仪器工作，身体经历治疗，细胞仍有活动。在外面等的人，也许觉得每一分钟都很长。一个间隔，从不同的位置被描述出来。

而在你的回忆里，那三个小时可能没有形成一段能讲述的过程。

不是一段很快的电影，更像前一个场景直接接上后一个。

这已经是一个值得认真听的第一人称报告。

但它不是意识完全不存在的独立证明。麻醉会影响意识与记忆；没有术后记忆，不能单独确定期间每一刻是否毫无体验。大脑也并非整体停止工作。

因此，“我的三个小时去了哪里”可以是一个有力的问题，却不能偷偷变成已经答完的结论。

我们可以问经历怎样中断，记忆怎样保存，也可以问一个人为何会把这段时间叙述成空白。

物理主义也不要求“只要物理时间经过，人就必须感觉到它”。依赖某种脑活动的体验，可以在活动改变时改变或消失；这与钟表继续走并无逻辑冲突。

所以不能靠这一个例子，就宣布物理主义被反驳。

也不能因为神经科学能研究它，就说那句“对我而言没有中间”毫无意义。

外部记录告诉我们经过了多久；回忆告诉我们现在能怎样取回那一段。它们回答的问题不同。

区别值得保留，结论需要节制。

这不是柏格森形而上学的现代实验证明。

它让我们更容易理解他为什么不满足于把体验直接换成刻度。理解一个问题的分量，与证明提出者所有答案，是两件事。

麻醉在1922年当然已经存在，不能说爱因斯坦无从知道这个例子。我们也不应替他虚构一段反驳，再宣布自己已经赢了。

把问题重新问得小一点——

这段时间，怎样成为我的经历，或没有成为我能回忆的经历？

这里有机制的问题，也有描述的问题。两种工作可以互相纠正，不必谁先宣布另一个不存在。

你可以告诉医生自己醒来时的断裂感，医生可以解释药物与记忆。解释不会替你重新活出那三个小时，你的叙述也不因此成为神经科学的反例。

钟表不能独自说完一段生活，第一人称也不能独自说完身体。

先把两者放在桌上，不急着宣布谁拥有全部。

这是我们读绵延时，可以带回日常的一点谨慎。

五、《道德与宗教的两个来源》

1932年，柏格森出版最后一部主要著作。

《道德与宗教的两个来源》。

这部书酝酿多年。晚年的风湿疾病使他的活动受到严重限制，工作已经不像早年那样轻便。这里不替每一页补出唯一的口授程序。

这本书谈道德和宗教。但它的核心论点跟他一辈子讲的时间问题是连续的。

他说有两种道德，也有两种宗教。

封闭道德，以群体的习惯、义务与压力维持共同生活。它容易把义务的重心放在“我们”之内，战争尤其显露它的边界。这不是说现实中的每一个封闭社会对外人都绝无一丝善意。

开放道德则以召唤与感动越过既定群体。苏格拉底、基督与圣徒等人物进入他的讨论；他也讨论佛教，却不把各传统都判成完全等价。重要的是：一个人的行动可能使旁人愿意朝更广的人类关怀迈出一步，而非只因群体命令。

这不只是把小圈子逐步画大。开放所要求的转变，不能简单靠增加成员人数完成。

它与绵延有相通的问题意识，却不是同一个概念换了名字。

可以拿僵硬的切分作比喻，但不能由此说物理时间在道德上“封闭”，或使用钟表的人就在排斥陌生人。

也不能因为某个体验流动而新鲜，就断定它善良。开放涉及关怀朝向谁，不能只靠“不可分”“有创造力”这些词替它完成伦理判断。

柏格森讲两种宗教也是这个结构。

静态宗教回应恐惧、死亡与社会维系的需要，借由仪式、信念和他所谓的虚构功能保持生活的秩序。它有作用，也有边界。

动态宗教则与神秘主义的创造性动力相连，在行动中向人类展开。他特别重视基督教神秘主义；其他传统在书里得到的评价并不相同。我们可以不接受他的宗教排序，同时看清他想区分维持惯性与打开新路。

现实里的宗教也会混合这两种倾向，不是一本教派名册上两种固定颜色。

柏格森这本书在一九三二年出版的时候，欧洲正在迅速滑向他说的"封闭"那一面。德国民族主义在崛起，反犹运动在加剧，"我们"和"他们"的区分在每个国家变得越来越严酷。柏格森看见了这个趋势。这本书不是抽象哲学，是写给那个时代的警告。

封闭的道德不会自动开放。 静态宗教不会自动变成动态宗教。 要让封闭打开，需要有人付出代价。

他年过七十，身体越来越不方便。欧洲的危险已经可见，但不能说他预知了后来每一步。

后来他怎样面对迫害，可以与这本书相对照，却不是一场证明全部哲学的实验。

六、登记

一九四零年六月。德国军队进入巴黎。

占领区要求犹太人登记的命令，来自1940年9月27日的德国法令；维希的第一部《犹太人地位法》则在同年10月颁布。不能用1941年后来的法令解释柏格森已经去世之前的行动。

柏格森已经年老多病。他是法兰西学术院院士、诺贝尔奖得主，但头衔没有把他与反犹的处境隔开。关于特殊待遇与豁免的叙述有不同版本，不能把一封具体的“荣誉非犹太人”来信当作已经找到的档案。

可以确定的方向，是他没有因此与受迫害的犹太人切割。

他的名字进入了1940年的登记故事。至于怎样到场、穿什么、谁推轮椅、等了几小时，需要比一个动人的版本更多的证据。

把这些细节拿掉，场景会少一点电影感。但尊重一个人，不需要替他安排恰好能让我们落泪的天气。

还有一份更适合把思想放稳的材料。

在1937年的遗嘱里，他解释自己为何没有正式改宗天主教：尽管思想上越来越接近它，他仍愿留在即将遭受迫害的人中间。有关遗嘱的发表与措辞也有复杂历史，但这个选择，比一段未经确证的雪中排队镜头更有依据。

我们不能把登记、受寒、肺炎与死亡剪成一条已经证实的因果链。

1941年1月4日，柏格森去世，八十一岁。同时代悼词提到肺部病症；它没有证实“一月排队，三天后去世”的版本。

他的死发生在被占领的巴黎。保罗·瓦莱里留下悼词。那个时代的沉重，不需要靠多添一个未经核实的细节来成立。

为什么不把自己单独划出去？

不是完全没有解释。遗嘱把他的宗教趋向与对受迫害者的连带，放在同一个决定里。我们不必替他说一篇从绵延演绎登记的论文。

从这里回看开放道德，问题变得很近。

我可以有自己的信念，可以走向与原来不同的思想。但当别人因出身被排除时，我是否愿意让自己的声望成为一道只保护我的门？

把名望与功绩拿来区分谁值得被救，是迫害秩序很有诱惑力的一种例外逻辑。

拒绝这种逻辑，可以是一种个人选择。

但不能反过来要求所有受迫害者都放弃可能的逃生办法。

利用豁免活下来，不自动意味着赞同迫害；活着的人不该因没有按照我们的英雄剧本行动，再受一次审判。

柏格森的选择之所以值得看，不是因为他替所有人发布了一道命令。

而是因为，在自己信念与处境交会的地方，他不愿把自己从那些人中间抽走。

这可以与开放道德相照。不能因此说他的每一个判断都已获得证明。

不必有睡衣、大衣和几小时的寒冷，他的选择仍然有重量。

它让一个抽象词走到人和人的关系里。

绵延与伦理不是同一个东西。一个人的过去、承诺与眼前的人相遇，却可能使某个当下变得不再只是钟表上的一点。

这个当下值得记住，不需要一个传奇的死因替它盖章。

七、他不是输家

如果只留下“1922年柏格森输了”，我们会错过他继续提出的问题。若反过来说“后来他的生命证明爱因斯坦错了”，又会把一种简化换成另一种。

我们今天讲了他一篇。我们没有把他写成"被错杀的天才"。我们也没有把他写成"被时代误解的先知"。

我们看他做过的事，也标出不能确定的地方。

他把绵延发展成一生的思想。在相对论的解释上，他的论点可以受到批评；在迫害面前，他的连带也可以被记住。两种判断不必用一张总分表彼此抵消。

这就够了。

他不需要先赢，才值得我们读。他做过的工作，与他在这件事上守住的位置，可以分别被看见；这不是替他的全部人生盖上无过的印章。

阿奎那留下庞大的著作，也留下“稻草”的停顿。柏格森留下绵延的论述，也留下不愿与受迫害者切割的决定。

两件不同的事，被我们放在“不把自己当成全部”的问题旁边。

不能把阿奎那的神学改成柏格森的伦理，也不能让柏格森的伦理替他的物理解释辩护。

他们都不是已经完成的范本。我们从他们那里得到问题，也保留继续判断的责任。

钟表可以继续精确地走，一个人也仍要问自己怎样活过这一刻。没有哪个测量结果会单独替他承担这个问题。

那段被他活过的时间，不必先赢得一场辩论，才值得被理解。

八、桥头

桥头上的人又多了一个。

希帕蒂娅在桥的中段。星盘在她手里。 阿奎那在她旁边。手里没有东西。 柏格森走过来。

他个子不高。他穿着深色的西装，戴着金属框眼镜。他的胡子很整齐。他的手不太能动——关节炎让他每个动作都很慢。他用一根拐杖。

他走得比阿奎那还慢。

他看见希帕蒂娅，也看见阿奎那。我们不再替他编一份经她之手传来的阅读书单，也不让他宣布与阿奎那学说相同。今晚相遇的，是三个各有工作、也各有限度的人。

他到了桥的中段。

他站住。他对希帕蒂娅微微颔首。他对阿奎那微微颔首。两个人也对他点了点头。

他没有立刻找位置。他先站在桥头看了一会儿。

远处苏格拉底坐着，柏拉图蹲着画图。中间有人写方程、看玉米、读诗、翻星图、写小说。有些人他读过，有些是桥头才让他遇见的陌生人。

他看见了爱因斯坦。

爱因斯坦在桥头的另一段，在画一个时空图——四个维度的坐标系，一些光锥。他在专注工作，没有看柏格森。

柏格森看着他工作了一会儿。

然后他做了一件事。

他没有走过去。他没有说话。他只是在自己站着的位置上微微对爱因斯坦的方向点了点头。

动作很轻。我们让旁边的人注意到它，爱因斯坦还专注于图。

在这个想象里，它是一次和解的尝试。

不是“我赢了”，不是“你错了”。更像是：你的工作有严格的要求，我的问题也仍值得提出。我们的回答可能冲突，但不必因此取消彼此继续说话的位置。

希帕蒂娅看见了那个动作。她把星盘的角度稍微调了一下，让月光更准地落在刻度上。

阿奎那也看见了。他说了一句拉丁文，柏格森笑了一下，试着回应。桥头的想象不必靠把柏格森写成只懂一半拉丁文来制造距离。

六百多年隔在中间，相近的问题没有把两人变成同一个人。

桥的最远那一头，那个一直看着远方的人，看了希帕蒂娅，看了阿奎那。

这次他看的是柏格森。

柏格森微微低头。这可以是答谢，也可以是确认；答谢本身不必意味着低人一等。在这个镜头里，它像是在说：我看见你了。

那个一直看着远方的人也轻轻地点了一下头。

这是本乐章里第三次，远处的目光落到一个具体的人身上。这一次，有了回应。

柏格森继续往桥的中段走。他到了希帕蒂娅旁边，停下来。

星盘上的刻度泛着冷光，月亮慢慢移动。可以测量的关系还在，众人也各自活过这个晚上。我们不让月光替任何一种形而上学完成证明。

柏格森手里没有任何工具。

但他有自己的此刻：年轻时反复思索的问题，1922年没有结束的争执，面对反犹浪潮作出的选择，还有桥上这一秒。过去不是几个散落的标签，它正在改变他怎样站在这里。

他站着。

他的此刻很厚。[1][2][3]

I. The Debate

April 6, 1922. Paris. The French Society of Philosophy.

Two ways of understanding time meet here.

Einstein was forty-three, already famous worldwide for relativity. His Nobel Prize for 1921 would be announced later in 1922, especially recognizing the photoelectric law. He had not just arrived at this April meeting with a prize for relativity.

Bergson was sixty-two, a philosopher whose books and lectures had drawn large audiences. His New York lectures in 1913 attracted particular attention. The tale that he caused Broadway’s first traffic jam is more memorable than securely established.

The subject of the debate was time.

Bergson’s intervention asks whether physics’s treatment of time exhausts the questions raised by experiencing it. Duration, durée, names a lived continuity whose moments interpenetrate rather than sit apart like beads. The meeting’s record matters more than an imagined, precisely timed thirty-minute speech.

Einstein’s reply distinguishes psychological time from physical time.

The sentence repeatedly quoted afterward belongs inside that distinction.

In the record he denies a separate “time of the philosophers,” while acknowledging a distinction between psychological and physical time.

“The time of the philosophers does not exist.” It is a sharp sentence, but it cannot be rewritten as “subjective experience does not exist.”

Their disagreement is not merely about whether clocks and feelings can coexist. Bergson also interprets simultaneity and actual or possible times in relativity, and those interpretations face real technical objections. Making room for experience cannot begin by declaring him necessarily right about physics.

Later retellings like the verdict: science won, philosophy lost.

The verdict is much simpler than the questions either man raised.

Bergson’s later standing changed amid intellectual fashions, politics and institutions. The 1927 Nobel Prize in Literature was no formal scientific demotion. One objection did not make him disappear, nor does renewed interest make every argument of his correct.

But.

How a person lives through time remains worth asking. Recognizing that does not require defeating relativity first.

II. Duration

First, go back to the young Bergson.

He entered the École normale supérieure in 1878, in the same cohort as Jean Jaurès. He had strong mathematical training and published a geometrical solution. That matters: his question about what calculation treats as time did not arise simply from an inability to calculate.

After graduating he taught philosophy at Angers and Clermont-Ferrand. His thinking about duration took shape during these working years.

A blackboard can bring the question into view. What follows is not a transcript of one documented lesson.

An object moves from A to B. How does its position vary with time? What is its velocity at a particular moment?

This is a standard problem in calculus. Derivatives. Instantaneous velocity. Bergson had been rigorously trained in mathematics; he knew how to compute it.

But that day he stopped.

He looked at what he had written on the blackboard. He suddenly realized something —

A derivative expresses an instantaneous rate of change. It does not require the universe to contain a smallest grain of time, nor first locate an indivisible interval to divide by. A continuous model allows intervals to be subdivided.

Can it?

Being able to calculate and divide a representation of time does not already describe everything about living through an interval. That is the step Bergson challenges, not a flaw calculus does not contain.

Then what is time itself like?

Let us imagine a pause before the blackboard. The pause belongs to the scene, not to a revelation certified by a notebook.

Drawing time as a line is enormously useful. But treating points as units external to one another can let something in experience slip out of the drawing.

But the time he himself experienced was not like that.

The time he himself experienced was a flow. The present moment he was experiencing contained everything he had just experienced — not as a separate segment, but as part of this present — and contained also his anticipation of what was coming next, also as part of this present. This flow was not a line. There were no separable segments inside this flow. There was no direction in this flow that could be reversed: what he had experienced could not be uncrossed; what he had not yet experienced could not be experienced in advance.

He called this flow duration. Durée.

The French word means simply "lasting" or "continuing"; there is nothing mystical about it. Bergson used it to refer to that lived time inside consciousness — the time that is not segmented, not spatialized, not measurable by clocks — the time that can only be experienced from inside itself.

This question was more than a brilliant classroom exclamation. Later books argued, revised and developed it.

His doctoral thesis (published 1889, Time and Free Will in English) was devoted to it. All his major later works — Matter and Memory (1896), Creative Evolution (1907), The Two Sources of Morality and Religion (1932) — built on the same initial intuition.

For Bergson, duration is not merely a feeling of speed added to clock readings. It asks how experience itself continues.

Physics uses operational relations to compare times, including relations among clocks; relativity is not simply one great external clock shared by everybody. That indispensable work is not the same as a first-person account of waiting.

Duration is experienced from inside. You need no instrument. Your consciousness itself is duration. When you remember laughing yesterday, that laughter is not a vanished past — that laughter is still in your present. When you anticipate someone you will see tomorrow, that person is not a non-existent future — that anticipation is already in your present. The present of duration has thickness.

A present represented as a coordinate point has no length. That property of a representation does not establish that physics cannot study memory or neural activity.

The present of duration has thickness. Your present contains your past and your future.

The descriptions do not simply replace one another. Whether they require two independently existing entities is a further philosophical question, not something a metaphor has already proved.

The dispute of 1922 came after decades of this work. Einstein was not denying that people wait, remember or experience length. He challenged how those experiences enter an argument about physical time.

That challenge deserves an answer, not dismissal as an inability to understand experience.

Conversely, the rigor of a physical time concept does not make waiting and remembering undeserving of their own description.

I want to retain not an antiscientific victory, but a question that need not immediately end with one account swallowing the other.

III. Laughter

Let us leave the question of time for a moment, and look at another side of Bergson.

In 1900 he published Laughter: An Essay on the Meaning of the Comic. That year he also joined the Collège de France. This small book by a philosopher of time concerns comedy.

Comedy. Bergson.

The combination is at first slightly odd: a philosopher who speaks of the deep time-structure of consciousness writes a book about laughter. But once you read it, you see the two are two faces of one thing.

Bergson asks: what makes us laugh?

He primarily studies the comic, not every laugh arising from joy, nervousness or intimacy. He offers a penetrating line of thought—

The mechanical encrusted upon the living.

The book considers a person stumbling while walking: inertia carries the body onward without adequate adjustment to the situation. A banana peel is a familiar adaptation, not a necessary prop from the original. Laughter catches a living movement suddenly resembling a malfunctioning mechanism.

Laughter is the response to this falling of life into machinery.

Bergson uses this principle to analyze nearly every type of comedy. The recurring catchphrase is the mechanical encrusted upon language. The single-trait character (Molière's miser) is the mechanical encrusted upon personality. The mistaken identity (mixed-up twins) is the mechanical encrusted upon identity. Mimicry, especially of someone's small mannerisms, is the reduction of a person to a repeatable mechanical pattern.

The approach illuminates much comedy and remains open to criticism and extension. It is not a register at which every instance of laughter must report.

I want to read it beside duration, without turning two books into one formula.

Duration reminds us that what has been lived enters the next moment. Even the same movement can carry a different past. A life is not merely a chain of externally repeatable motions.

The doctrine of laughter says: when something that should be flowing — a person's behavior, a person's language, a person's reaction — becomes unflowing, becomes predictable, repeatable, describable in mechanical terms — our consciousness immediately registers this, and answers with laughter.

As a metaphor, laughter can remind life not to grow rigid. Calling it duration’s immune system would be our phrase, not his proposition.

A reminder can also become correction or punishment. Bergson notices laughter’s social role in pulling the out-of-step person back into line. Freedom is not necessarily the winner.

There is also something deeper that Bergson noted in this book. Comedy, he said, requires that the audience set aside its sympathy for a moment. If you see the slipping man and instantly think he might be hurt, you will not laugh. You will be worried. The comic effect arises only because, in that second, you treated him as an observable mechanical apparatus, not as a conscious being like yourself.

Laughter is neither automatically malicious nor automatically kind. Temporarily suspending sympathy can reveal rigidity, or make us miss someone’s pain.

The analysis leaves an uncomfortable question: when we laugh, has the particular person temporarily slipped from view? It need not become the claim that every laugh denies personhood.

That question meets this movement’s concern with the other. It does not make Einstein’s theoretical objection a denial of Bergson’s humanity. Argument, laughter and violence occupy different registers; one label should not cover them all.

Bergson continued to formulate his question; we can continue to challenge his answers. Taking a thinker seriously is not guaranteeing immunity from refutation.

He held his position.

IV. Two Times

Let us return to time.

The victory story of science defeating philosophy can narrow the original question. We need not win a match for Bergson to consider a familiar experience: the clock records a long interval, but in recollection there seems to be no middle.

An accessible example, easily pushed too far, is recollection after general anesthesia.

Anesthesia.

Imagine an operation: medication, the scene fading, then waking in recovery. This is how some people remember it, not a uniform report of every anesthetic.

Three hours have passed.

The clock records three hours. Equipment operates, the body undergoes treatment, cells remain active. Someone waiting outside may find each minute long. One interval receives descriptions from different positions.

In your recollection, those three hours may form no narratable sequence.

Less like a fast film than one scene joined directly to another.

That is already a first-person report worth hearing carefully.

It is not independent proof that consciousness was wholly absent. Anesthesia affects both consciousness and memory; missing postoperative recollection does not by itself establish the absence of every experience throughout the interval. Nor does the brain simply stop working as a whole.

So “Where did my three hours go?” can be a powerful question without secretly becoming an already established answer.

We can ask how experience is interrupted, how memory is retained, and why a person narrates an interval as a blank.

Physicalism does not require people to experience every interval that passes physically. Experience dependent on particular brain activity can alter or cease when that activity changes, without contradicting the continued movement of clocks.

This example alone therefore does not refute physicalism.

Nor does its availability to neuroscience make “for me there was no middle” meaningless.

The external record tells us how long elapsed; recollection tells us how the interval can now be retrieved. They answer different questions.

The difference matters; the conclusion needs restraint.

This is not a modern experimental proof of Bergson’s metaphysics.

It helps explain why replacing experience directly with graduations on a scale did not satisfy him. Understanding the weight of a question differs from proving all its author’s answers.

Anesthesia certainly existed in 1922; the example was not unavailable to Einstein. Nor should we invent his response and then declare victory over it.

Make the question a little smaller—

How did this interval become my experience, or fail to become experience I can recall?

There are questions of mechanism and questions of description. The inquiries can correct each other without first declaring the other nonexistent.

You can describe a gap on waking; a clinician can explain medication and memory. The explanation does not make you live those three hours again, and your account does not thereby become a counterexample to neuroscience.

A clock cannot alone tell the whole of a life; a first-person report cannot alone tell the whole of a body.

Put both on the table before declaring either the owner of the whole.

That is one caution we can carry from reading duration into everyday life.

V. The Two Sources

In 1932 Bergson published his last major work.

The Two Sources of Morality and Religion.

It had developed over years. Rheumatic illness severely restricted his later life; work no longer came with the physical ease of youth. We need not invent one dictation procedure behind every page.

The book is about morality and religion. But its central argument is continuous with what he had been saying his whole life about time.

He says there are two kinds of morality, and two kinds of religion.

Closed morality maintains shared life through habit, obligation and social pressure. Its center of gravity lies within “us,” with war especially exposing its boundaries. That does not mean every actual closed society shows outsiders absolutely no kindness.

Open morality reaches beyond the established group through appeal and inspiration. Socrates, Christ and saints enter his discussion; he also discusses Buddhism without treating all traditions as equivalent. An individual’s action can draw others toward wider human concern, rather than merely secure obedience to the group.

This is not simply enlarging a small circle. The change openness requires cannot be completed merely by increasing membership.

It shares concerns with duration, without being the same concept renamed.

Rigid divisions can serve as an analogy. They do not make physical time morally closed, or clock users guilty of excluding strangers.

Nor is an experience good merely because it flows or feels new. Openness concerns whom care reaches; indivisibility and creativity cannot perform the ethical judgment for us.

Bergson's account of two religions follows the same structure.

Static religion responds to fear, death and the needs of social cohesion through practices, beliefs and what he calls the fabulatory function. It has a role and a limit.

Dynamic religion involves the creative impetus of mysticism opening outward in action toward humanity. He particularly values Christian mysticism; other traditions receive differing evaluations. We can reject that ranking while recognizing his distinction between maintaining an established pattern and opening a new path.

Actual religions can mix both tendencies; the categories are not fixed colors in a directory of denominations.

When this book was published in 1932, Europe was sliding rapidly toward the closed side. German nationalism was rising. Antisemitic movements were intensifying. The distinction between "us" and "them" was hardening in every country. Bergson saw this trend. The book was not abstract philosophy. It was a warning to its time.

Closed morality does not open of itself. Static religion does not turn dynamic of itself. For closure to open, someone has to pay a cost.

He was past seventy and increasingly limited physically. Europe’s dangers were visible, but he did not foresee every step that followed.

His later response to persecution can be read beside the book. It was not an experiment proving his entire philosophy.

VI. Registration

June 1940. German troops entered Paris.

The order requiring registration of Jews in the occupied zone was German, dated September 27, 1940. Vichy’s first Jewish Statute followed in October. Later legislation in 1941 cannot explain an action taken before Bergson’s death.

Bergson was old and ill, a member of the Académie française and a Nobel laureate. Titles did not remove him from antisemitism’s reach. Accounts of exceptional treatment or exemption vary. A particular letter offering “honorary non-Jew” status must not be treated as a recovered document.

The clearer direction is that he did not separate himself from persecuted Jews.

His name belongs to the story of registration in 1940. How he appeared, what he wore, who pushed a wheelchair, or how many hours he waited require more evidence than a moving version of the story.

Removing those details makes the scene less cinematic. Respecting someone does not require arranging exactly the weather that will bring us to tears.

There is another document on which to rest the thought more firmly.

In his 1937 will he explained why he had not formally converted to Catholicism: despite his growing intellectual proximity to it, he wished to remain among those facing persecution. The publication and wording of the testament have a complicated history too. The choice is nevertheless better grounded than an unverified scene of queuing in the snow.

We cannot edit registration, cold, pneumonia and death into an established causal chain.

Bergson died on January 4, 1941, aged eighty-one. A contemporary tribute mentions pulmonary illness; it does not establish the story of a January queue followed by death three days later.

He died in occupied Paris. Paul Valéry left a tribute. The weight of the time does not depend on one more unverified detail.

Why not draw himself apart?

He did not leave us wholly without an explanation. The will places his religious inclination and solidarity with the persecuted inside one decision. We need not invent a treatise deducing registration from duration.

From here, open morality becomes a close and practical question.

I can have my beliefs and move toward ideas different from those I began with. But when others are excluded for their ancestry, will I let my reputation become a door protecting only me?

Using distinction and achievement to decide who merits rescue offers a tempting logic of exception within persecution.

Refusing that logic can be a personal choice.

It cannot become a demand that every persecuted person forgo a possible means of survival.

Surviving through an exemption does not automatically endorse persecution. Survivors should not face another trial for failing to act out our heroic script.

His choice matters not because it issues an order for everybody else.

It matters because, where conviction met circumstance, he did not wish to detach himself from those people.

That can illuminate open morality without proving every judgment he made.

Without pajamas, an overcoat or hours of cold, the choice still has weight.

It brings an abstract word into a relation between people.

Duration and ethics are not identical. Yet when a person’s past and commitments meet the people before him, a present can matter as more than a point on a clock.

That present deserves remembering without a legendary cause of death certifying it.

VII. He Was Not the Loser

Keeping only “Bergson lost in 1922” misses his continuing question. Saying instead that his later life proved Einstein wrong replaces one simplification with another.

We have written one essay on him. We have not written him as a "wronged genius." We have not written him as "a prophet misunderstood by his time."

We look at what he did, and mark what cannot be established.

He developed duration across a life’s thought. His interpretation of relativity can be criticized; his solidarity amid persecution can be remembered. These judgments need not cancel each other on one scorecard.

That is enough.

He need not win before his work and this particular choice can matter. We can recognize both without certifying a life entirely free of failure.

Aquinas left immense writings and the interruption expressed in straw. Bergson left a philosophy of duration and a decision not to detach himself from the persecuted.

Two different acts, placed by us beside the question of not taking oneself for the whole.

Aquinas’s theology must not be rewritten as Bergson’s ethics; Bergson’s ethics must not defend his interpretation of physics on its behalf.

Neither is a finished model. We receive questions from them while retaining responsibility to judge.

Clocks can continue to run accurately while a person must still ask how to live this moment. No measurement alone assumes that responsibility for him.

The time he lived through need not first win a debate to deserve understanding.

VIII. The Bridge

There is one more figure on the bridge tonight.

Hypatia stands in the middle of the bridge, the astrolabe in her hand. Aquinas stands beside her, his hands empty. Bergson walks up.

He is not tall. He wears a dark suit, and metal-rimmed spectacles. His beard is neatly trimmed. His hands cannot move very well — the arthritis makes every motion slow. He uses a cane.

He walks even more slowly than Aquinas.

He sees Hypatia and Aquinas. We do not invent a reading list passed down to him through her, or make him announce an identical doctrine with Aquinas. Tonight brings together three people with different work and different limits.

He reaches the middle of the bridge.

He stops. He nods slightly to Hypatia. He nods slightly to Aquinas. They nod back to him.

He does not immediately find a place to stand. He stands first and looks for a moment at the bridge.

Socrates sits in the distance and Plato crouches over a drawing. Others write equations, watch corn, read poetry, consult star charts, write novels. Some he has read; others are strangers only this bridge lets him meet.

He sees Einstein.

Einstein is at another section of the bridge, drawing a spacetime diagram — a four-dimensional coordinate system, light cones. He is absorbed in the work. He does not look at Bergson.

Bergson watches him work for a moment.

Then Bergson does something.

He does not walk over. He does not say anything. He only, from where he is standing, nods slightly in Einstein's direction.

The gesture is slight. We let the nearby figures notice it while Einstein remains intent on his drawing.

In this imagining it is an attempt at reconciliation.

Not “I won,” not “you were wrong.” More like: your work has rigorous demands, and my question remains worth asking. Our answers may conflict without canceling each other’s place to keep speaking.

Hypatia sees the gesture. She tilts the astrolabe slightly so that the moonlight falls more precisely across the markings.

Aquinas sees it too. He says something in Latin; Bergson smiles and tries a reply. Our imagined bridge need not manufacture distance by making Bergson understand only half the Latin.

More than six centuries lie between them. A neighboring question has not made them the same person.

At the far end of the bridge, the figure who has always been looking into the distance has, in this round, twice looked at someone — at Hypatia, and at Aquinas.

This time, he is looking at Bergson.

Bergson lowers his head slightly. It may be thanks or acknowledgment; gratitude need not imply inferiority. In this scene it seems to say: I see you.

The figure at the far end nods, lightly, in return.

For the third time in this movement, that distant gaze rests on a particular person. This time it is answered.

Bergson keeps walking, slowly, toward the middle. He reaches Hypatia and stops.

The astrolabe’s markings catch a cold light as the moon moves. Measurable relations remain, and each person lives through the evening. Moonlight does not finish the proof of any metaphysics.

Bergson holds no instrument.

But he has his present: the question he worked through in youth, the unfinished dispute of 1922, the choice made amid antisemitism, and this second on the bridge. The past is not a scatter of labels. It is changing how he stands here.

He stands.

His present is thick.[1][2][3]
