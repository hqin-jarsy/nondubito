达芬奇，还要打开一扇门

Da Vinci, One More Door to Open

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、门外的孩子

列奥纳多·达·芬奇出生时，父母没有结婚。

1452年，芬奇附近。父亲皮耶罗是公证人，母亲名叫卡特琳娜。这个孩子在父系家庭中长大。他没有沿着父亲的职业往下走，也没有接受以拉丁文为中心的大学教育。

“达·芬奇”意为“来自芬奇”。名字把他带回一个地方，也把后来的传奇带回一个真实长大的孩子。

少年时，他进入佛罗伦萨的韦罗基奥工坊。画画、雕塑、金属加工，在这里不是互相隔绝的学科：一双手要认识颜料，也要认识重量、材料和工具。到1472年，他已加入当地画家的圣路加兄弟会。

工坊不是知识的后门。它有自己的训练、合作和判断。没有走进大学的年轻人，在另一种学校里学着看。

把他放在李白旁边，会看见一种不肯只沿着既定道路行走的自由。但出身不是自由的自动起点，障碍也不会因为后来出了天才，就变成恩赐。

杜甫有一个反复诉说的入世方向：致君尧舜上。达芬奇的纸上却不断出现新的方向。一个人的痛苦可以来自道路受阻，也可以来自道路太多；他还必须面对委托、钱、战争，以及一生只有这么长。

二、笔记本

数千页纸留了下来，常被概称为约七千页。

翻开它们，很难只说这是一本什么书。

解剖。他观察骨骼、肌肉、血管和脏器，一生解剖的人体可能约有三十具，也研究了许多动物。心脏研究中，他把水流、模型和绘图带到瓣膜的问题旁：血流中的旋涡怎样帮助瓣膜关闭？这里有惊人的洞察，也有当时生理学留下的误认。

水。他画波纹、旋涡、冲击障碍后的回流。河流不是一条蓝色的线，而是彼此牵动的运动；眼睛追着它，手要想办法把运动留在纸上。

飞行。他画扑翼机、螺旋形的“空中螺旋”和降落伞。纸上的翅膀可以展开，人的力量却未必足够使机器离地。它们是探索飞行的方案，还不是等待装配的现代航空器。

战争。他设计巨弩、装甲车，也研究水下装备。写给卢多维科·斯福尔扎的求职信，先用大部分篇幅陈说军事本领，随后谈和平时的建筑、水利、雕塑和绘画，又提出青铜骑马纪念像。那个后来以画家闻名的人，先请掌权者看看他的桥梁和武器。

光影、透视、颜色。山中的贝壳化石与大地的过去。树枝怎样分出树枝。教堂、桥梁、城市中的水道。还有音乐：传记留下了他演奏里拉琴的形象。

人体与河流、画面与机器，有时挤在同一张纸上。不是每一行都在宣布发现；还有计算、提醒、试错和没有答案的问题。

他常用左手，从右向左写镜像文字。熟悉字形的人可以直接辨读，不一定需要镜子。纸张也不都从一开始就属于今天所见的册子；散页经过折叠、编订，后来的收藏者又重新排列。

他曾计划整理绘画、解剖等论述，却没有在生前把这些研究系统出版。身后，梅尔齐保存并整理部分文稿，绘画论述后来以《绘画论》等形式流传；另一些纸页散失或分散到不同收藏中。

图像、比较、局部论证，本身已经在构成知识。只是读者看见的这座工作室，许多门仍开着。

三、不同的未完成

未完成，是接近达芬奇的一条路。不是给他一生盖下的印章。

传世而被普遍接受为他所作的绘画很少，确切数目随归属判断而变。壁画、合作以及有争议的作品，也使这份清单不容易整齐地排定。

《最后的晚餐》，1490年代中期至1498年。他在干燥的墙面上作画，好让自己有时间推敲光与表情。画完成了，却因技法与环境而很早受损。完成与保存，是两件不同的事。

《蒙娜丽莎》，约1503年开始，多年留在他身边，持续加工。我们看见的是一件经过长久劳动的作品；“他到底何时觉得够了”，却不能只靠起讫年份回答。

《安吉亚里之战》，为佛罗伦萨市政厅准备的大型委托，留下研究和构图的回声，没有成为原定的完整壁画。墙上究竟实施到哪一步，后来发生了什么，仍有讨论。

斯福尔扎骑马纪念像。他完成了巨大的泥模型，青铜巨像却没有铸成。1494年，原定用于雕像的青铜被转去制造火炮；1499年法军占领米兰后，模型遭到破坏。这里有技术难题，也有战争和赞助者的命运。

飞行器、装甲车、水下装备，许多方案停在纸上。解剖与水的研究，没有变成他所设想的出版物。这些未实现的计划，与一幅完成后受损的画，是两回事。

数千页手稿，少量传世绘画，大量中断的工程。旁边还要放上已经兑现的委托，以及那些无法简单算成“成功”或“失败”的长期研究。

他开始了很多。有些做成了，有些改了又改，有些没有走到终点。留下的不是一张空白的成绩单，而是一生与完成之间的摩擦。

四、为什么还要打开门

为什么总还有下一件事？

我们容易说：因为他分心。画画把他带到肌肉，肌肉把他带到力，力又把他带到水和飞行。桌上的工作似乎永远不能收拢。

但这些方向也彼此相通。要画出转身的人，就去看骨骼怎样转；要理解心里的血，就去看水怎样旋。好奇心既可能打断工作，也可能把工作往深处带。

在这个系列的词汇里，可以叫作凿。一个看似熟悉的东西，被重新打开，露出尚未问过的问题。

门内有心脏瓣膜、河流的回旋、远山在空气中变淡的颜色、鸟翅和空气之间的作用。每一扇门都要求观察，也要求他承认，眼前的解释还可能不够。

他不是从来不构。他画，比较，设计试验，安排论述；有时多年返回同一个问题。只是局部的构经常又成为新问题的入口，难以收束为一部可交付的书。

把牛顿放在旁边，我们可以看见《原理》那样的公开论证，与达芬奇纸页间不断延伸的探究。牛顿也研究许多领域，达芬奇也能集中劳动。

若要把这一刻写成两种姿态：一个在门后建立可供别人走入的建筑，一个仍听见隔壁传来的声音。他放下刚画好的线，想再去看看。

五、他和这个系列的每个人

凿与构，是本系列用来观看人的两束光，不是把人分成两种的户籍。

苏格拉底追问，柏拉图让对话继续并建立思想的形式；亚里士多德整理知识。牛顿的数学论证、巴赫的复调、秦始皇的帝国工程、康德的批判，又让“构”显出完全不同的重量。

达芬奇站在这些人之间，一会儿是提问者，一会儿是制作者。他不愿只待在一门手艺里，却也没有脱离手艺：新的问题仍须由眼睛和手来承担。

苏格拉底的追问，使回答者不能轻易安住；达芬奇的好奇，使他自己不能轻易安住。“那边还有一扇门。”这是我们从他的纸页里听到的话，不是替他补写的名言。

也可以想起达尔文的变异：尝试很多，结果并不预先知道。但人的设计是有意图的，生物变异并不朝着未来的需要准备答案。这个比喻，只照亮探索中不能预订成功的一面。

他并不是把莱特兄弟、现代医学、纳维尔—斯托克斯方程都提前装进了笔记。后来的飞行与科学有自己的问题、证据和集体劳动，不是等待一位先知的图纸终于被看懂。

真正动人的，不是“每一次都对”。是一个人愿意把不确定画得这样具体：翅膀怎样连，水怎样转，手怎样抬起来。可能出错的线，也能让后来的人继续问。

六、那几幅画

再回到画前。不要让“未完成”遮住已经在那里发生的事情。

《最后的晚餐》《蒙娜丽莎》《岩间圣母》《抱银鼠的女人》。餐桌上的惊动、岩石间的手势、女子怀中的动物，各自构成了一个可以反复进入的世界。

《蒙娜丽莎》的微笑尤其难以抓牢。达芬奇把渐隐法推到极细密的程度，薄薄的色层使轮廓缓缓过渡。目光移向嘴角、眼睛，再退回整张脸，表情仿佛也跟着变。

不必宣布所有观看者都产生同一种错觉。重要的是：画不把一个表情像标签一样钉在脸上。你已经看见，又还想再看一次。

借用本系列的词，这像余项。它不在画外藏着，而在已经画出的关系之间，让一句“她在笑”仍显得不够。庄子的混沌也在远处回响：有些东西一经强行切清，反而失去了原来的活气。

这不是没有画完嘴角。模糊是经过劳动形成的清楚选择，未定的感受可以属于完成的艺术。那种不肯被一句话取走的表情，本身就是形式。

哥德尔提醒过我们去想形式系统的边界；这里回来的只是“不要急着封口”的阅读感受。微笑不服从一个关于算术证明的定理。它不需要数学担保，就足以让人停下。

七、镜像

从右向左的字，像把纸翻到了世界的另一面。

这是一个可以借用的隐喻，不是一把破解天才的钥匙。

看树，他还想看分枝的关系；看河，他还想看水怎样绕过障碍；看人，他追问骨骼和肌肉如何配合；看画，他又回到照亮脸的光。

他没有离开可见的世界，去占有藏在背后的最后答案。他是在可见之中，寻找平常目光漏掉的联系。

因此，康德可以来到这个对照里，却不能被一把解剖刀驳倒。身体内部一旦被观察，仍是经验中的对象，不是哲学意义上已经被看见的“物自体”。

康德追问我们能够认识的条件和界限。达芬奇追问这一处究竟怎样运作。一个问题不能代替另一个，却可以使我们听见“看见”这个词有多深。

看见之后，他画下来。绘图不是看见的废料，而是检验看见、把它交给别人的方式。镜像文字旁边的线条，仍然朝向另一个读者。

他晚年在法国继续工作，1519年在昂布瓦斯附近的克洛吕塞去世，六十七岁。没有五百年的时间。桌上的纸，也没有在那一天自动变成一本写完的书。

八、还要打开一扇门

现在，把他请到本系列想象的桥头。

手上有颜料，有墨水，也仿佛有碰过工具和模型的痕迹。一双长期工作的手。

苏格拉底站着，老子已不见，孔子还在等。杜甫在哭，李白在飞。巴赫听着，贝多芬握着拳，司马迁背着书，鲁米在旋转。

达芬奇四处看。桥的承重怎样分配？桥下的水为什么在那里卷回去？人群中一个转身，肩膀和手臂怎样一起动？天上的鸟又怎样停在风里？

他走几步，停一下，抽出纸，画几条线。别人说完一句话，他可能还在看那个人说话时嘴角的变化。

这和鲁米的旋转不同。这里的脚步一次次转向新的对象，每一处停留，都可能成为另一扇门。

他开始过那么多。

其中有看准了的，有猜错了的，也有把问题留给下一张纸的。求真的工作，不靠每一次都正确才成立。

没有人知道，再给他多少年就能“全部完成”。新的答案还会生出新的问题；五百年也不是一份可以算清的工期。

他有过六十七年。在那些年里，他让许多原本容易掠过的东西获得了被仔细观看的时间。

桥头的手仍沾着那些观看留下的痕迹。他没有碰过一切，却使人觉得，任何一件东西都值得再看。

蒙娜丽莎在微笑。画已经在那里，观看还没有结束。

他还要打开一扇门。

6. 艺术与比较：渐隐法在此指达芬奇高度发展的技法，不宣称由他独自发明。牛顿、达尔文、庄子、哥德尔、康德等是本文的阅读对照；不把生物变异理解成预知未来，不把微笑当成数学定理，也不把解剖见到的内部当成康德的物自体。桥头为虚构群像。

7. 晚年与死亡：1519年5月2日去世于克洛吕塞。昂布瓦斯王室城堡说明“死在弗朗索瓦一世怀里”是瓦萨里叙述及后世绘画发展出的传说，国王当时不在现场；正文未把它作为史实，也不对晚年身体状况作远程诊断。

I. The Child Outside the Door

Leonardo da Vinci was born to parents who were not married.

It was 1452, near Vinci. His father, Piero, was a notary; his mother was named Caterina. He grew up within his father's family. He did not follow the paternal profession, nor receive a university education centred on Latin.

“Da Vinci” means “from Vinci.” The name points towards a place, bringing the later legend back to a child who grew up somewhere real.

As a young man he entered Verrocchio's Florentine workshop. Painting, sculpture and metalwork were not sealed departments there: a hand needed to understand pigment, but also weight, materials and tools. By 1472 he belonged to the city's confraternity of painters, the Company of Saint Luke.

The workshop was not knowledge's back entrance. It had disciplines, collaborators and standards of its own. Outside the university, he was learning to see in another kind of school.

Set him beside Li Bai and we may glimpse a freedom unwilling to follow only an assigned road. But birth outside marriage does not automatically confer freedom. Obstacles do not become gifts because a remarkable person later encounters them.

Du Fu repeatedly named his wish to serve and guide a ruler beyond Yao and Shun. On Leonardo's sheets, directions keep multiplying. A life may hurt because a road is blocked, or because there are too many roads. Commissions, money, war and the length of a lifetime were also real.

II. The Notebooks

Thousands of pages survive, often loosely described as about seven thousand.

Open them, and it is difficult to say what kind of book this is.

Anatomy. Bones, muscles, vessels, organs: he probably dissected some thirty human bodies, as well as many animals. To investigate the heart he brought drawings, models and flowing water together. How might eddies help a valve close? Brilliant insight sits beside errors inherited from the physiology of his time.

Water. Ripples, vortices, currents curling behind an obstacle. A river becomes more than a blue line: it is motion acting upon motion. The eye follows; the hand must find a way to keep that movement on paper.

Flight. Ornithopters, an aerial screw, a parachute. A wing can spread across a drawing while the power available to a human body remains insufficient to raise the machine. These are investigations of flight, not modern aircraft awaiting assembly.

War. Giant crossbows, an armoured vehicle, underwater equipment. His letter offering services to Ludovico Sforza devotes most of its space first to military skills, then turns to architecture, water engineering, sculpture and painting in peacetime, and proposes a bronze equestrian monument. The man remembered as a painter first asks a ruler to consider his bridges and weapons.

Light, shadow, perspective, colour. Shell fossils in the mountains and the past of the earth. Branches dividing into branches. Churches, bridges, urban waterways. Music, too: early biography preserves the image of a player of the lira da braccio.

Bodies and rivers, pictures and machines sometimes crowd the same sheet. Not every line announces a discovery. There are calculations, reminders, false starts and questions without answers.

He commonly wrote with his left hand, from right to left, in mirror script. A practised reader does not necessarily need a mirror. Nor did every sheet originally belong to the volume that holds it now: loose leaves were folded, bound and rearranged by later owners.

He planned treatises on painting, anatomy and other subjects, without systematically publishing these studies during his lifetime. After his death Melzi preserved and organised part of the papers; writings on painting eventually circulated in compilations including the Treatise on Painting. Other sheets disappeared or travelled into separate collections.

Images, comparisons and local arguments already make knowledge. But in the workshop that remains for us, many doors are still open.

III. Different Kinds of Unfinished

The unfinished is one way into Leonardo. It is not a stamp to press upon his whole life.

Few surviving paintings are generally accepted as his, and the precise count depends on attribution. Murals, collaborations and disputed works also make the list difficult to settle neatly.

The Last Supper, made from the middle of the 1490s to 1498. Working on dry plaster allowed him time to reconsider light and expression. He completed the painting; technique and environment caused early deterioration. Completion and preservation are different questions.

The Mona Lisa, begun around 1503, remained with him for years of further work. What we encounter is an intensely worked painting. Dates alone cannot tell us when he thought he had done enough.

The Battle of Anghiari, commissioned for Florence's town hall, left studies and echoes of its composition, but never became the complete mural intended. How much reached the wall, and precisely what happened there, remain debated.

The Sforza equestrian monument. He made a colossal clay model, but the bronze statue was never cast. In 1494 the bronze intended for it was diverted to cannon; after the French occupation of Milan in 1499, the model was destroyed. Technical difficulties belong here, but so do war and the fortunes of a patron.

Flying machines, the armoured vehicle, underwater apparatus: many proposals remained on paper. Anatomical and hydraulic research did not become the publications he envisaged. Unrealized plans and a painting damaged after completion are different problems.

Thousands of manuscript pages, a small surviving body of paintings, many interrupted undertakings. Beside them we must put commissions delivered, and long investigations not easily entered into either a success or a failure column.

He began much. Some things he made, others he repeatedly revised, others never reached their destination. What remains is not a blank record of accomplishment, but the friction between a life and its endings.

IV. Why Open Another Door?

Why is there always another undertaking?

We readily answer: distraction. Painting leads him to muscles, muscles to forces, forces to water and flight. The work on the table appears impossible to gather up.

Yet these directions also connect. To paint a turning body, look at how its bones turn; to understand blood inside a heart, look at swirling water. Curiosity can interrupt work, and it can take the work deeper.

In this series we might call that carving: something apparently familiar is opened again, exposing a question not yet asked.

Behind the doors are heart valves, circling currents, distant mountains fading through the air, the action of a bird's wing upon that air. Each asks for observation, and for the admission that an explanation may not yet be enough.

He did construct. He drew, compared, devised experiments, arranged arguments; he could return to one question for years. But a local construction often opened onto another problem, resisting closure into a book ready to be delivered.

Beside him we can place Newton: the public argument of the Principia alongside investigations spreading across Leonardo's sheets. Newton also pursued many subjects; Leonardo also sustained concentrated work.

To give the contrast a momentary shape: one erects a building others can enter beyond the door; the other still hears something in the next room. He sets down the line he has just drawn. He wants to go and look.

V. Leonardo and the Others

Carving and construction are two lights this series casts upon people, not two kinds of citizenship.

Socrates questions; Plato gives continued conversation a form and builds philosophical arguments; Aristotle organises knowledge. Newton's mathematics, Bach's counterpoint, Qin Shi Huang's imperial projects and Kant's critiques then give construction very different weights.

Leonardo stands among them, now asking, now making. He will not remain inside one craft, yet never leaves craft behind. Eyes and hands must answer for the new question.

Socratic questioning prevents an answerer from settling too easily; Leonardo's curiosity prevents him from settling. “There is another door.” We hear that in the papers: it is our phrase, not a recovered quotation.

Darwinian variation offers another association: many possibilities, outcomes not known beforehand. But human designs have intentions; biological variations do not prepare answers for future needs. The resemblance illuminates only the uncertainty of exploration.

Leonardo did not pack the Wright brothers, modern medicine and the Navier–Stokes equations into his notebooks in advance. Later aviation and science had their own problems, evidence and collective labour. They were not merely waiting to understand a prophet's plans.

What moves us is not that every attempt was right. It is his willingness to make uncertainty so specific: how the wing attaches, how the water turns, how the hand rises. A line that can be wrong can also invite the next question.

VI. Those Few Paintings

Return to the paintings. Do not let the unfinished hide what has already happened there.

The Last Supper, the Mona Lisa, the Virgin of the Rocks, the Lady with an Ermine. The disturbance around a table, gestures between rocks, an animal in a woman's arms: each establishes a world we can enter again.

The Mona Lisa's smile is particularly elusive. Leonardo brought sfumato to extraordinary refinement, using thin layers of paint to make contours pass gently into one another. As our gaze shifts between mouth, eyes and face, the expression seems to shift too.

Not every viewer must report the same illusion. What matters is that the painting does not fasten an expression to a face like a label. Having seen it, we want to look once more.

In this series' vocabulary, it resembles remainder. Nothing needs to hide outside the painting: relations already painted make “she is smiling” feel insufficient. Zhuangzi's Hundun echoes from a distance. Some things lose their animation when we insist on cutting every boundary sharply.

This is not simply a mouth left unpainted. Softness is a choice achieved through labour; an unsettled experience can belong to finished art. The expression that refuses to be carried away in a sentence is itself a form.

Gödel has invited us elsewhere to think about the limits of formal systems. What returns here is only a reader's reluctance to close things too quickly. A smile is not governed by a theorem about arithmetic proof. It needs no mathematical guarantee to make us pause.

VII. Mirror

Writing from right to left can look as though the page has turned to the other side of the world.

It is a metaphor we may borrow, not a key that unlocks genius.

A tree draws his attention to branching relations; a river to water travelling around an obstacle; a person to the cooperation of muscle and bone. Looking at a painting, he returns to the light that illuminates a face.

He has not left the visible world to possess a final answer concealed behind it. Within the visible, he looks for relations an ordinary glance may miss.

Kant can enter this comparison, but a dissection knife cannot refute him. Once observed, the body's interior is still an object of experience, not a philosophical thing-in-itself finally caught in view.

Kant asks after the conditions and boundaries of our knowing. Leonardo asks how this particular thing works. Neither question replaces the other; together they make the word “seeing” less simple.

Having seen, he draws. A drawing is not the waste left over from perception: it tests that perception and offers it to someone else. Beside the mirror script, the lines still turn towards another reader.

He continued working during his last years in France, and died at Clos Lucé near Amboise in 1519, aged sixty-seven. He did not have five hundred years. Nor did the papers on his table turn themselves into a finished book that day.

VIII. One More Door

Now let us invite him to this series' imagined bridgehead.

Paint on his hands, ink too, and perhaps the marks of tools and models. Hands that have worked for a long time.

Socrates stands. Laozi has disappeared; Confucius waits. Du Fu weeps and Li Bai flies. Bach listens, Beethoven clenches his fist, Sima Qian carries books, Rumi turns.

Leonardo looks about him. How does the bridge carry its weight? Why does the water curl back just there? Someone turns in the crowd: how do shoulder and arm move together? How does that bird hold itself in the wind?

He walks a few steps, stops, takes out paper, draws a few lines. When someone has finished speaking, he may still be looking at the movement of their mouth.

These steps differ from Rumi's turning. Again and again they lead towards another object; each pause may become a door.

He began so much.

Some observations were right, some guesses mistaken, some questions passed to the next page. Work directed towards truth does not require an unbroken record of being correct.

No one can say how many more years would have let him finish everything. Answers would have produced new questions. Even five hundred years is not a schedule we can calculate.

He had sixty-seven. Within them, he gave things we might otherwise pass by the time of an attentive gaze.

His hands at the bridgehead still bear its traces. He did not touch everything. He makes us feel that anything might deserve another look.

The Mona Lisa is smiling. The painting is there; the looking has not ended.

He wants to open one more door.

1. Life and training: the National Gallery and Royal Collection Trust support his birth near Vinci in 1452, notary father, upbringing in the paternal family and membership of the painters' confraternity by 1472. Caterina's precise background remains debated. The essay does not present illegitimacy as a universal ban on universities, guilds or established occupations, or fix his entry into the workshop at fourteen.

2. Manuscripts: “about seven thousand pages” is a popular approximation, not a precise inventory using consistent units of pages and leaves. The V&A describes loose sheets and later binding and rearrangement. Mirror script is readable without a mirror; secrecy is not an established motive. Melzi's compilation and the later Treatise on Painting distinguish posthumous publication from both lifetime publication and wholly unorganised notes.

3. Anatomy and water: approximately thirty human dissections is an estimate; animal studies were also important. Insights into valves, models and flow did not amount to the modern theory of blood circulation. Older beliefs, including the separation of arterial and venous systems, persisted. Water drawings are not modern turbulence equations, and not every drawing records a correct observation.

4. Engineering: proposals, models, construction and later reconstructions differ. Milan's science museum explains why the aerial screw, as interpreted in its model, could not work with its weight and human power. Later feasibility cannot be claimed for every design. The Sforza letter offers skills, including architecture, hydraulics, sculpture and an equestrian monument as well as painting; it is not a delivery record.

5. Works: the Last Supper was completed and deteriorated early. Keeping and reworking the Mona Lisa does not prove sixteen years of continuous daily painting. Bronze for the horse was diverted in 1494, before the model's destruction after 1499. Accounts differ on how far the Battle of Anghiari reached execution; the Uffizi and Royal Collection emphasise different aspects. The commission was not realised as a complete work.

6. Art and comparison: sfumato is a technique Leonardo developed to exceptional refinement, not an invention claimed for him alone. Newton, Darwin, Zhuangzi, Gödel and Kant enter as literary comparisons. Variation does not foresee future needs, a smile does not instantiate an arithmetic theorem, and an observed body's interior is not Kant's thing-in-itself. The bridgehead is imagined.

7. Final years: he died at Clos Lucé on 2 May 1519. The Château Royal d'Amboise identifies the death in Francis I's arms, transmitted through Vasari and later paintings, as a legend; the king was elsewhere. It is not used here as historical fact, and no retrospective diagnosis is made.
