日本天皇制，空的王座

The Japanese Emperor System, The Empty Throne

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、比人长久的位置

日本历史上有一个位置，往往比坐在上面的人更醒目。

将军打仗，幕府执政，大名争地盘；明治维新的力量推翻幕府，近代国家以天皇之名扩张，军部把战争推向亚洲和太平洋；战败后，占领当局与日本政治力量共同重写制度。

天皇这个位置一直在，位置上的人却并非始终没有行动，也并非始终拥有同一种权力。

可靠的历史记录可以把皇统追到古代；《日本书纪》又把神武即位定在公元前660年，但那一段属于神话化的王统叙事，不能当作连续档案。此后朝廷、幕府、帝国与战后民主先后出现，皇位却以不断改变的形式延续。

这是一种罕见的制度连续性，却不能简单宣布世界上绝无第二例。

与英国、中国、罗马或奥斯曼比较，很容易把王朝、家系、君主职位和现代宪法制度混成同一种东西。这里真正需要解释的，不是争一项世界纪录，而是一个位置为何能在权力结构大变时反复获得新含义。

天皇制没有以同一种形态原封不动地保存下来；它靠改变而延续。

为什么？

二、治理与象征

本文把它称为"空"，不是说天皇从来没有权力，而是说实际统治与象征中心经常能够分开。

平安时期有藤原摄关，也有院政和亲政；此后镰仓、室町、江户幕府掌握大量实权，朝廷仍参与官位、仪式、文化与合法性的生产。到了明治宪法下，天皇又被规定为主权中心，近代国家以他的名义运作。

仪式、任命、诗歌与宫廷文化不是"什么都没做"；它们本身就在塑造秩序。至于具体天皇，也有介入政治深浅不同的时期。

所以"不构"只能是制度分析的比喻：这个位置往往不必亲自承担全部日常治理。

它能把内容交给不同权力来填，因而比某一套具体政策更容易跨过断裂。

一套具体政策会遇到具体的反对。秦始皇统一文字、度量衡，也以征役把国家伸进无数人的生活；秦的迅速崩溃有许多原因，不能用“做得太多”包办解释。但那个高度集中的政治形象，确实使政策的代价与皇帝的权威紧紧捆在一起。

如果把位置与决定它所授权、象征或掩护的政策分开，批判就容易落到首相、幕府、军部或内阁，而位置本身继续留下。但这不表示天皇个人没有决定，也不表示制度不可批判。

所谓空构的力量，不是里面真的什么都没有，而是内容与责任可以被转移、重写。

三、谁能借用这个名字

上一章让我们留意场所与占据场所的事物。读到这里，也会忍不住问：皇位有时是否更像一处让权力登场的位置，而不只是某个人手中的权力？

藤原氏、源氏、足利氏、德川氏，各有自己的权力结构，也各有与朝廷往来的办法。到了近代，天皇、内阁、议会和军部之间又出现新的制度关系。不能把这段历史排成几拨人轮流拿走同一件东西。

姑且说，掌权者像演员，皇位像舞台。

这个比喻的用处，是让我们看见演员换了，舞台还可能留下；它的限度，则是历史中的演员也会拆改舞台。朝廷不是任人摆布的木板，皇位的意义也在每一次争夺中变化。

许多新权力都向朝廷寻求官位或授权。源赖朝、德川家康获得征夷大将军之职，倒幕力量也曾高举"尊皇"。这些过程各有战争、谈判和既有制度，不能缩成每一次政权更替都按同一剧本向天皇借章。

耐人寻味的是，相反的政治诉求都可能举起同一个名字。一个阵营凭它维护现存秩序，另一个阵营凭它要求推翻秩序。连续的符号，能够参与不连续的行动。

皇位因此常成为争夺合法性的资源。资源不是无辜的死物：谁能使用它、它替什么行动加上庄严、事后如何追究，都是制度的一部分。

四、1945年

最极端的考验来了。1945年。日本战败。

麦克阿瑟来了。占领军。新宪法。整个国家的政治体制要重写。

要不要废除天皇制？要不要追究裕仁的战争责任？盟国内部与日本国内都有争论。侵略战争以天皇为主权中心的国家体制展开，皇位不能只凭“象征”二字便站到战争之外。

最后，制度被保留了，但并不是原样留下。

占领决策中，维持秩序、推动占领改革与利用裕仁配合政策，是保留制度的重要理由；盟国内部与日本国内都存在不同意见。这不是麦克阿瑟一人的抽象实验，也不是"日本只剩一种凝聚力"可以概括的。

1946年1月1日，昭和天皇发布后来通称"人间宣言"的诏书，否定天皇与人民关系建立在天皇神格之上的虚构观念。它并不是一句现代口语式的"我不是神"。新宪法第一条随后规定：天皇是日本国与日本国民统合的象征，其地位来自拥有主权的国民之总意。

从明治宪法中的主权结构转到国民主权下的象征制度，这是制度断裂，也是皇位延续。

注意这个词：“象征”。它没有使皇位变成无意义的摆设。仪式、探访与公开言行仍会影响人们怎样理解国家；但这种影响，不是自行决定战争、法律或政府政策的权力。影响与统治权，需要分开。

宪法第四条规定天皇没有国政权能；第三条规定，国事行为须经内阁建议与承认，由内阁负责。这些不是“空”字自带的美德，而是写进制度的限制。今天的象征地位，也不能倒投回去，替历代天皇解释一切。

五、断裂中的延续

这个系列写了很多碎了的构。

秦朝短暂，却有许多制度被后继王朝继承。一个王朝结束，不等于它做过的一切同时归零。 罗马的政治疆域改变、分裂，它留下的法律与政治想象却继续影响后来的人。 黑格尔和马克思的体系也经历反驳、重读与挪用。不能用一场政权的失败，替一整套思想盖上“已证伪”的印章。 所以，延续与碎裂，不总是非此即彼。我们得问：到底是什么留下了，又是什么已经变了？

天皇制能够延续，部分原因正在于它可以把许多具体内容留给别的权力承担。

内容越具体，越能被反驳、推翻或改正。位置若保留解释空间，就能在内容失败以后宣称：失败的是那一次填充，不是位置本身。

这可以帮助我们理解制度怎样处理余项：它不必把一切都纳入一套政策，而可以把相互冲突的内容先后收入同一象征。不过，血统、仪式和国家身份本身已经是内容，不是真空。

皇位的情形尤其醒目：推翻一种治理方式，并不总是需要取消这个位置。 人们可以更换赋予它的解释，继续称它为“同一个”制度。 天皇制则不断改写自己所声称的连续性。余项并没有消失，而是一次次迫使制度换一种解释。

空构并非不可凿，只是能把凿痕说成上一种形态的历史。

六、代价

但空构有代价。

代价是：可以重新解释，并不保证每一次新解释都在保护人。

幕府秩序保留朝廷，明治国家把天皇置于主权中心，侵略战争又借助帝国的忠诚与服从来动员。战后制度则必须把皇位放在国民主权之下。这不是四次换装，而是四种不同的权力与责任关系。

同一个天皇制。四种完全不同的内容。

制度不会像一个有良知的人那样开口，但占据位置的人、内阁、官僚、军人和国民都会行动。把一切都说成舞台不在乎，反而可能再次让行动者从责任中退场。

最危险的时候，是所有人都能借这个位置行动，却没有人肯为行动负责：执行者说自己只是奉命，位置又被说成不问政事的象征。

侵华战争与南京大屠杀发生在以天皇为主权中心的帝国体制下。战后宪法则把主权交给国民，并由天皇公布。两者都经过国家机关与具体的人，不能只用"同一个名义"抹平责任结构。

倘若只剩下“都是以天皇之名”这一句，我们反而看不见命令怎样发出、谁能够干预、谁选择服从。

追究责任，恰恰要把这些具体关系重新打开，而不是在一个抽象的空位前停下。

战后象征天皇制受到国民主权、基本权利与和平主义的宪法原则约束，所以它并非永远只有位置、没有原则。真正的问题是：原则由谁执行，制度又是否帮助社会面对自己的历史。

一个位置活得久，不会替它自动长出良知。人得把限制、记忆与追责放进去。

七、它和"绝对无"

上一篇写了西田几多郎的"绝对无の場所"。

天皇制可以和"绝对无の場所"作一个危险但有启发性的比喻。

两者都容易使人联想到一个区别：位置不等于某一次占据它的内容。 但从这里再向前走，就得格外小心。

它们不能被说成同一种结构的两个版本。

西田追问主客与各种规定怎样形成；皇位却已有明确的历史规定。谁能继承、谁主持仪式、它在宪法中的位置，都不是“无”。 天皇制是政治制度——它在历史中运行。它被人使用。它被权力利用。

西田的"绝对无"不是政治权力可以直接占据的容器；天皇制却是由法律、血统、仪式和行动者组成的历史制度。

因此不能把两者说成一个干净、一个被污染的同一种空。比喻只能提示"场所怎样容纳内容"，不能替代对制度行为的分析。

八、制度作为一种构

这是这个系列第一次写一个制度而不是一个人。

写人的时候，问题是：这个人凿了什么，构了什么，余项是什么。 写制度的时候，问题变了：这个制度本身就是一个构。它怎么运行？它为什么不碎？它的余项在哪里？

天皇制告诉我们：有一种构不靠固定同一套内容来维持，而靠保留可重新解释的位置。别人不断填入内容，容器也随之变化；它不是永远不碎，而是善于把断裂改写成连续。

这跟这个系列一直说的"构不可闭合"是什么关系？

通常"构不可闭合"提醒我们：任何制度都不能无损覆盖所有人。天皇制没有绕过这个问题。它的继承规则、身份叙事和战争记忆同样留下被排除者，只是把很多具体治理交给别的机关。

可以改变，不等于对每一个人都敞开。

若一个制度允许自身被重新解释，它确实较少因某一次政策失败而整体崩溃；这是一种韧性，不是不可闭合定律自动推出的永生。

这也不是解决方案。开放的位置既可能容纳民主约束，也可能曾替侵略提供庄严。它保护谁，要看具体法律与行动，不能由"空"字预先回答。

九、空的王座

2019年5月1日。令和时代开始。德仁天皇即位。

古老的仪式与象征进入现代法律安排。眼前让人感到连续的场景，已经不是古代原封不动的一天。

德仁继承皇位。战后宪法使这个位置没有国政权能，却仍承担国事行为与象征劳动。所谓空，不是没有人也没有行动，而是政治决定必须由民选机关承担责任。

这个系列写了四十个人。他们凿了，构了，碎了，笑了，哭了，沉默了，跪了，站起来了。每一个人都做了什么。

天皇制并非从来不构；它参与构成了不同时代的合法性。只是它常把具体治理交给别人，从而让位置比政策活得更久。

现在，来到这组文章想象的桥头。这一次先看见的不是人物，而是一把椅子。

椅子上有人，而那个人的选择不能被制度比喻抹去；可是椅子又总想让人相信，个人不如连续性重要。政权来了又走了，战争打了又停了，宪法改变，位置仍在。

其他人忙着画、写、辩论。椅子似乎不说话，却改变了围绕它站立的人怎样排列。制度的作用，有时正在这种不显眼的安排里。

它通过少构、转移和改写而延续，不是因为历史从未凿过它。

它可以为秩序提供连续性，也可能让责任沿着缝隙滑走。

这一篇的发现因此不是"空构不可凿"，而是：制度可以靠保留空位来承受一次次改造。延续本身不证明正当。

空位有时稳定生活，有时遮住责任；是否保护人，要看围绕它的法律、行动与追问。

我们把王座称作“空”，终于不是为了宣布它无辜，而是为了看清：谁在填它，谁被它代表，谁没有机会说话。

至于王座应不应该在——这个问题，王座自己不回答。[1][2]

I. A Position That Outlasts Its Occupants

There is a position in Japanese history that often appears more prominent than the person who occupies it.

Shoguns waged war, bakufu governed, and domain lords fought for territory. Restoration forces overthrew the shogunate; a modern state expanded in the Emperor's name; military leaders carried war across Asia and the Pacific. After defeat, occupation authorities and Japanese political actors remade the system together.

The imperial position remained, but its occupants were neither always inactive nor always invested with the same power.

Reliable records carry the line far into antiquity. The Nihon Shoki dates Jimmu's accession to 660 BCE, but that belongs to a mythologized genealogy, not to an unbroken archive. Courts, warrior governments, empire, and postwar democracy followed one another while the throne continued in repeatedly altered forms.

This is a rare institutional continuity, not a world record that can be declared without qualification.

Comparisons with Britain, China, Rome, or the Ottomans easily collapse dynasties, bloodlines, offices, and modern constitutional systems into one category. The question is not who wins a longevity contest, but how one position repeatedly acquired new meanings as structures of power changed.

The institution did not preserve one unchanged form. It endured by changing.

Why?

II. Governing and Symbolizing

When this essay calls the institution “empty,” it does not mean emperors never held power. It means that practical rule and a symbolic center could often be separated.

Heian Japan had Fujiwara regents, but also cloistered rule and periods of direct imperial action. Later warrior governments held extensive power while the court still produced offices, ritual, culture, and legitimacy. Under the Meiji Constitution, the Emperor was again made the center of sovereignty and the modern state acted in his name.

Ritual, appointment, poetry, and court culture are not “doing nothing”; they help shape an order. Individual emperors also intervened in politics to different degrees.

“Not constructing” can therefore be only an analytical metaphor: the position often did not have to carry all ordinary government itself.

It could leave content to other powers and therefore cross ruptures more easily than any single policy.

A concrete policy encounters concrete resistance. Qin Shi Huang standardized scripts and measures while demands for labor brought the state deep into ordinary lives. The Qin collapse had many causes, not simply “constructing too much.” Yet its concentrated political image bound the costs of government closely to imperial authority.

When the position is separated from the policies it authorizes, symbolizes, or shelters, criticism can fall on a shogunate, prime minister, military command, or cabinet while the throne remains. That does not mean an emperor made no decisions or that the institution cannot be criticized.

The force of an “empty construction” lies not in containing literally nothing, but in allowing content and responsibility to be transferred and rewritten.

III. Who Can Invoke the Name

The previous essay asked us to notice places as well as what occupies them. Here that invites a question: can the throne sometimes function as a place on which authority appears, rather than simply the power held by one person?

The Fujiwara, Minamoto, Ashikaga, and Tokugawa operated through different arrangements and different relations with the court. Modern institutions produced further relations among emperor, cabinet, parliament, and military. This is not a procession of groups taking turns to hold one unchanged object called power.

As a first image, rulers are actors and the throne a stage.

The image helps us see how a stage may survive a change of cast. Its limit is just as important: historical actors rebuild their stages. The court was not passive timber, and the meaning of the throne changed in the struggle over it.

Many new powers sought titles or authorization from the court. Minamoto no Yoritomo and Tokugawa Ieyasu received the office of shogun; anti-bakufu forces also raised “Revere the Emperor.” Each transition involved its own wars, negotiations, and inherited arrangements. They were not one repeated script in which every regime simply borrowed an imperial seal.

Opposed political projects could invoke the same name: one to defend an existing order, another to demand its overthrow. A continuous symbol could participate in discontinuous actions.

The throne thus repeatedly became a resource in struggles over legitimacy. Such a resource is not an innocent dead tool: who may use it, what actions it dignifies, and how responsibility is pursued afterward are part of the institution.

IV. 1945

The most extreme test came. 1945. Japan defeated.

MacArthur arrived. Occupation forces. A new constitution. The entire political system to be rewritten.

Should the imperial institution be abolished? Should Hirohito face prosecution? These questions were disputed among the Allies and within Japan. Aggressive war had been waged through a state centered legally on imperial sovereignty; calling the throne a symbol could not place it outside that history.

The institution was retained, but not unchanged.

Maintaining order, using Hirohito to assist occupation policy, and advancing reforms were important arguments for retention. There were disagreements among Allied governments and within Japan. The outcome was not MacArthur's abstract experiment, and “Japan had only one cohesive force left” is too simple an explanation.

On January 1, 1946, Hirohito issued the rescript later nicknamed the “Humanity Declaration.” It rejected the fictitious idea that the bond between Emperor and people rested on his divinity; it was not the plain modern sentence “I am not a god.” Article 1 of the new Constitution then made the Emperor the symbol of the State and the unity of the people, deriving his position from the will of the people in whom sovereign power resides.

The move from the sovereignty of the Meiji Constitution to a symbol under popular sovereignty was both institutional rupture and imperial continuity.

The word “symbol” does not turn the throne into a meaningless ornament. Ceremonies, visits, and public conduct still affect how people understand their country. That influence is different from authority to decide war, legislation, or government policy. Influence and governing power must be distinguished.

Article 4 denies the emperor governing powers; Article 3 requires cabinet advice and approval for acts of state and assigns responsibility to the cabinet. These are legal constraints, not virtues contained in the word “empty.” The modern symbolic position cannot be projected backward to explain every earlier emperor.

V. Continuity Through Rupture

This series has written about many constructions that shattered.

The Qin dynasty was brief, yet later dynasties inherited many of its institutions. A dynasty’s end does not reduce everything it made to nothing. Rome’s political territories changed and divided, while its laws and political imagination continued to affect later societies. Hegel’s and Marx’s systems underwent criticism, rereading, and appropriation. The failure of a regime is not a stamp declaring an entire body of thought disproved. Continuity and rupture are therefore not simple opposites. What exactly survived, and what has already changed?

The institution endured partly because it could leave many concrete contents to other powers.

The more specific the content, the more directly it can be refuted, overturned, or corrected. A position that keeps room for reinterpretation can say, after a failure, that the filling failed rather than the position itself.

This helps explain how an institution handles remainder: instead of forcing everything into one policy, it can successively receive conflicting contents under one symbol. Yet bloodline, ritual, and national identity are already content. This is not a vacuum.

The throne makes this particularly visible: overthrowing one form of rule need not abolish the position. People can replace the interpretation attached to it and continue calling it the same institution. The imperial institution repeatedly rewrites what its continuity is said to mean. Remainder does not vanish; it keeps forcing a new interpretation.

An empty construction is not beyond carving. It is simply able to rename a scar as the history of an earlier form.

VI. The Price

But an empty construction has a price.

The price is that reinterpretability does not guarantee a humane interpretation.

Warrior government retained a court; the Meiji state centered sovereignty on the emperor; imperial loyalties helped mobilize aggressive war. The postwar order had to place the throne under popular sovereignty. These are not four costumes on an unchanged body, but different distributions of authority and responsibility.

The same Emperor system. Four completely different contents.

An institution does not speak like a person with a conscience, but emperors, cabinets, officials, soldiers, and citizens act. To say only that the stage does not care can once again let actors leave the scene of responsibility.

The danger becomes acute when people invoke the position to act, then use it to avoid answering for the act: the official was only obeying, while the throne was only a symbol.

The war of aggression and the Nanjing Massacre occurred under an imperial order centered legally on the Emperor. The postwar Constitution placed sovereignty in the people and was promulgated by the Emperor. State organs and particular people acted in both histories; “the same name” cannot flatten their structures of responsibility.

If “in the emperor’s name” becomes the whole explanation, we lose sight of who issued orders, who could intervene, and who chose to obey.

Accountability requires opening those concrete relations to scrutiny, not stopping before an abstract empty place.

The postwar symbolic monarchy is constrained by constitutional commitments to popular sovereignty, basic rights, and peace. It has not always been a position without principles. The harder questions are who enforces those principles and whether the institution helps a society face its history.

Longevity does not give an office a conscience. People must build limits, memory, and accountability into its workings.

VII. The Emperor System and "Absolute Nothingness"

The previous essay wrote about Nishida Kitarō's "place of absolute nothingness."

The imperial institution can be placed beside the “place of absolute nothingness” in a risky but suggestive analogy.

Both can suggest a distinction between a place and whatever occupies it at a particular time. But the next step requires particular care.

These are not two editions of one structure.

Nishida investigates how subject, object, and determinations arise. A throne already has determinate historical features: succession, ritual, and constitutional status are not nothing. The Emperor system is a political institution — it operates inside history. It is used by people. It is exploited by power.

Nishida's “absolute nothingness” is not a container that political power can occupy. The imperial institution is a historical arrangement made of law, genealogy, ritual, and actors.

They are therefore not one kind of emptiness, one clean and one stained. The analogy can illuminate how a place receives content; it cannot replace analysis of institutional action.

VIII. Institution as Construction

This is the first time in this series that the subject is an institution rather than a person.

When writing about a person, the question is: what did this person carve, what did they construct, what is the remainder? When writing about an institution, the question changes: the institution itself is a construction. How does it operate? Why does it not shatter? Where is its remainder?

The institution shows us a construction maintained not by fixing one content forever but by preserving a position open to reinterpretation. Others repeatedly fill it, and the container changes with them. It is not immortal; it is skilled at rewriting ruptures as continuity.

What does this have to do with this series's consistent argument that "no construction can close"?

“No construction can close” means that no institution can include every person without loss. The imperial institution does not escape the problem. Its succession rules, identity narrative, and war memory also leave people out; it merely assigns much ordinary government to other organs.

Being changeable is not the same as being open to everyone.

An institution that permits reinterpretation is less likely to collapse with one failed policy. That is resilience, not immortality deduced from a philosophical law.

Nor is it a solution. An open position can receive democratic limits, and it once lent solemnity to aggression. Whom it protects depends on law and action, not on the word “empty.”

IX. The Empty Throne

May 1, 2019. The Reiwa era began. Emperor Naruhito ascended the throne.

Inherited rites and symbols enter a modern legal arrangement. A scene that conveys continuity is not an ancient day reproduced without change.

Naruhito inherited the throne. The postwar Constitution withholds governing power, while leaving acts of state and symbolic labor. “Empty” means neither personless nor inactive: political decisions must be made, and answered for, by elected institutions.

This series has written about forty people. They carved, constructed, shattered, laughed, wept, fell silent, knelt, rose again. Every one of them did something.

The institution has constructed legitimacy in different eras. It often handed ordinary rule elsewhere, allowing the position to outlive particular policies.

Now we return to this series’ imagined bridge. This time we notice a chair before we notice a person.

Someone sits in the chair, and that person's choices cannot be erased by an institutional metaphor. Yet the chair repeatedly invites people to believe that continuity matters more than its occupant. Regimes pass, wars begin and end, constitutions change; the position remains.

Others paint, write, and argue. The chair appears silent, yet its presence changes how people arrange themselves around it. An institution’s work may lie in precisely such unobtrusive arrangements.

It endured by constructing less, transferring functions, and rewriting itself—not because history never carved it.

It can lend continuity to public life, and it can let responsibility slip through institutional seams.

The discovery is therefore not that an empty construction cannot be carved. An institution can survive repeated reconstruction by preserving an open position. Survival does not prove legitimacy.

An empty place sometimes steadies life and sometimes hides responsibility. Whether it protects anyone depends on the law, action, and questioning around it.

We call the throne empty not to declare it innocent, but to ask who fills it, whom it represents, and who has no opportunity to speak.

As for whether the throne should remain — that is a question the throne itself does not answer.[1][2]
