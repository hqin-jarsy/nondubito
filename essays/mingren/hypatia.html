希帕蒂娅：一个圆锥被切开

Hypatia: A Cone, Cut

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、讲台

五世纪初。亚历山大里亚。让我们把镜头放在一间想象中的课堂，而不是冒认自己见过她最后一次授课。

一个女人在讲圆锥曲线。

她的学生来自远方，也跨越信仰的边界。我们没有这一堂课的名册，不能把城里每一个社群都直接安进教室。但我们知道其中一个名字：辛尼修斯。这个后来成为基督教主教的人，在病中的信里仍称她为母亲、姐妹、老师。

她叫希帕蒂娅。

她讲的是阿波罗尼乌斯的圆锥曲线论。一个圆锥被一个平面切开，截面可以是圆，可以是椭圆，可以是抛物线，可以是双曲线。同一个圆锥，切的角度不一样，得到的形状完全不同。

这个女人一生教数学、天文和哲学。她父亲席昂也是数学家；古代文献把她的名字与丢番图、阿波罗尼乌斯和天文著作的整理相连。辛尼修斯谈星盘时也提到她的教导。至于她是否发明或改良了某一件仪器，我们知道得没有传说那么多。

她在这座城市教了许多年。城市里有仍在工作的学者，也有日益尖锐的宗教与政治冲突。412年，西里尔成为主教。她生命的最后几年，将被卷进他与总督的争斗。

她每天讲课。她不知道还能讲多久。但今天还能讲。今天讲圆锥曲线。

讲台前坐着她的学生。一个圆锥被切开。截面是椭圆。她在画板上画那个椭圆，画得很慢，像在画一个她认识的人。

二、星盘和圆锥

我们对希帕蒂娅的工作了解得不多。没有一部完整著作以她的名字明确地传到今天。辛尼修斯留下同时代的见证；较晚的《苏达辞典》保存书目。她参与过哪些传世文本的编校、具体留下哪些段落，研究者仍在争论。

《苏达辞典》说她为丢番图的《算术》作过注释。那是一部研究数与方程问题的书，寻求的常是正有理数解，不能直接等同于今天以整数解为中心的丢番图方程。注释的细节大多失落，但这个工作本身很清楚：一个老师，要让难的东西可以继续被读。

天文学这一头，席昂《天文学大成》第三卷注释的题记提到了女儿希帕蒂娅的校订。她究竟整理父亲的注释还是托勒密的正文，学者有不同解释。我们能把她放进这项工作，却不能把整部书后来的存续都算在她名下。

辛尼修斯献星盘的文字，把自己的相关知识归于老师的教导。星盘把天球投影到平面，帮助使用者从刻度读懂天体的位置。教学、数学与手艺在这里连在一起；这比未经证实地给她安上“发明者”的头衔，更具体。

她做的是一个数学家在做的事。但她做这些事的方式——读丢番图，注释托勒密，画星盘——有一个共同的特点：把一个复杂的东西打开，让别人能进去。

圆锥曲线就是这件事的一个完美的例子。

阿波罗尼乌斯的《圆锥曲线论》是希腊数学的重要著作。一个不经过顶点的平面切双圆锥：垂直于轴线可得圆；倾斜而只截一叶可得椭圆；平行于母线可得抛物线；截到两叶可得双曲线。这里说的是非退化情形，不是随便切一下都得到这四种形状。

四种熟悉的曲线。同一个圆锥，切的位置与方向改变，截面跟着改变。

我愿意借这个图形来读她的课堂：同一个对象，从不同角度进入，会看见不同的样子。这是本文的隐喻，不是从她失落的讲义里找出来的一条政治主张。

这个隐喻在她活着的那个城市里有非常具体的含义。亚历山大里亚是一个被很多个角度切开的城市。希腊人有希腊人的亚历山大里亚。犹太人有犹太人的亚历山大里亚。基督徒有基督徒的亚历山大里亚。埃及本地人有埃及本地人的亚历山大里亚。这些不同的截面在彼此叠加，彼此摩擦，彼此越来越不能互相看见。

至少，辛尼修斯的经历让我们看见：信仰不同的人，可以在同一种学习中相遇。课堂没有解决整个城市的问题，但它给“我们”以外的人留下了一个坐下的位置。

不是说他们因此就和解了。不是说数学能解决宗教问题。是说——在这一节课里，他们看的是同一个东西。

她做了几十年这件事。

三、辛尼修斯

昔兰尼的辛尼修斯，三七零年左右生，四一三年左右死。

他是昔兰尼加的富裕希腊文化贵族。年轻时到亚历山大里亚求学，后来回乡，娶妻生子，经营庄园，处理地方事务，最终出任托勒密斯主教。他的宗教转变没有简单整齐的一条日期线，哲学训练也没有在任职时一笔勾销。

离开课堂之后，他继续给希帕蒂娅写信。

存世的辛尼修斯书信共有一百多封，其中数封直接写给她，其他信里也有她的身影。海隔开他们，职务改变了他的日常，但老师没有因此成为一个必须从往事里删掉的人。

他叫她"母亲，姐妹，老师"。

信里既有敬意，也有求助。

那些称呼把亲情与师生之情放在一起。

他在一封信里说："即便在地下世界忘川的水让人忘记一切，我在那里也不会忘记我亲爱的希帕蒂娅。"

信当然有那个时代的修辞。但一个基督教主教仍这样写给异教哲学家，已经比一个抽象的“宽容”口号更具体。他不只是给她贴一个可以接受的标签；他在向一个认识自己的人说话。

他病了，写信；失去孩子，写信；被地方事务压得疲惫，也写信。他请她帮助制作测量液体的仪器，请她关照友人，向她寻求判断。教义之间并非全无紧张，但这份关系没有因宗教身份的变化而被取消。

他不是把她当成"还没皈依的可怜人"。他是把她当成他自己的老师。终生的。

辛尼修斯的存在告诉我们一件事：希帕蒂娅讲台前的那些基督徒学生不是一个抽象的群体。他们当中有人是这样爱她的。

后来杀她的那群人是基督徒。在他们之前，先爱她的也是基督徒。

因此，亚历山大里亚的基督徒不能被写成同一个人。一种关系能养出辛尼修斯的信，另一种动员能走向杀戮。后来记下并谴责杀戮的历史学家苏格拉底，也是基督徒。

辛尼修斯通常被认为死于413年前后，早于她遇害。如果他在场，他会怎样做？他能拦得住吗？

我们不知道。

我们不能替历史安排一个救援者。留下来的只是这些信：在杀戮发生之前，有人已经这样敬爱她。这件事不会因后来发生了什么而失效。

四、西里尔

四一二年，西里尔成为亚历山大里亚的主教。

他继承叔叔提奥菲勒斯的主教职位，日后成为基督论争论中的重要人物，受到多个基督教传统尊崇。神学史不能绕过他；谈希帕蒂娅之死，也不能因为他的地位而绕过他。

他也是一个政治家。

城里并非只有两派人。帝国总督、主教、犹太社群以及不同基督徒群体各有位置。苏格拉底记述了犹太人与基督徒的流血冲突、随后的驱逐，以及西里尔与总督奥列斯特斯的对立。奥列斯特斯本人也是基督徒。尼特里亚修士袭击总督，与希帕蒂娅遇害是相关背景中的不同事件，不能把参与者直接混成同一群人。

希帕蒂娅同奥列斯特斯往来，受到城市显要的敬重。她不是一个躲在象牙塔里的人。苏格拉底说，流言把主教与总督不能和解的责任推到了她身上。一个老师于是被写成了政治障碍。

流言替一个复杂的冲突找到了一个可以指认的身体。

西里尔是否下令，不能当成已经结案的事实。较早的苏格拉底记述与更晚、更敌视他的记述并不相同；它们需要辨别，而不是拼成一个无所不知的旁白。

苏格拉底明确写道，此事给西里尔和亚历山大里亚教会带来耻辱。这足以要求我们追问主教权力、煽动与保护责任；却还不足以把命令链、事前知情或具体凶手身份补成确证。问责与证据的边界，应当同时保留。

也不能因为我们不掌握他的内心，就把问题放下。

一旦一种权力把“让城市正确”理解成“让不属于我的声音消失”，老师就不再作为老师被看见。她与谁谈话、谁愿意听她，会被改写成罪证。这是我们能从这场冲突中追问的危险，不是替西里尔写一段未经留下的自白。

危险在于：人先被缩成障碍，暴力才更容易成为办法。

学问不会自动挡住这个动作，虔诚也不会。一个体系完全可以说得很有道理，却把一个实际的人排除在自己的照顾范围之外。我们不必声称知道西里尔脑中只有哪三种人，才能看见这种可能。

辛尼修斯的信提供了另一种实际发生过的关系：我有自己的信仰，而你仍是我的老师。

这里不是给两个基督徒的信仰深浅打分。问题是：不同的人，能不能在同一个世界里继续有位置？

五、通道

希腊数学怎么传到了我们手里？

阿波罗尼乌斯的圆锥曲线论，丢番图的算术，托勒密的天文表，欧几里得的几何原本——这些书是怎么从公元前几百年的希腊，穿过古代晚期的混乱，穿过中世纪，到了文艺复兴的欧洲人手里的？

不是直线传过来的。

古书靠手抄、注释、修订、翻译和教学活着。亚历山大里亚图书馆的命运不能压成几次大火的简单年表；一部书的失传也未必有一个戏剧性的日期。常常只是下一份抄本没有被抄出来。

希帕蒂娅属于这项工作。书目、题记和学生的文字，让我们看见她参与数学与天文的传授。哪些段落经她之手走到后世，不能逐行认领；但不能逐行认领，不等于那一代的教学没有发生。

之后的路是一张网，不是一条直线。希腊语的保存、叙利亚文与阿拉伯文的翻译、拜占庭学者的整理、阿拉伯语学者自己的创造，以及直接从希腊文到拉丁文的翻译，共同进入欧洲后来的知识世界。巴格达和托莱多都重要，却不是每一部书都经过同样几个站。

她在这张网里有一个位置。我们不必把她说成唯一的接力者。

她不知道自己是。她只是在讲课。她做的是一个老师做的事。她在自己的城市里讲她爱的东西，讲了几十年。这些课的回声，她活着的时候听不到，死了以后也听不到。

但是回声在那里。

后来，费马在丢番图的拉丁译本页边写下了著名的数论断言，开普勒用椭圆理解行星轨道。这些遥远的回声，能让我们感到传统的寿命；却不能证明他们手里的某一行文字，必定经过希帕蒂娅的注释。

她不是把这些东西"传"下去。她做的事比"传"更小，也更大——她让她那一代的学生看见了这些东西，让这些东西在那一代里继续活着。下一代是不是看得见，下下一代是不是看得见，她管不到。她不是历史的工程师。她是一个老师。

至少在她教过的那些人那里，困难的知识有过一次成为活知识的机会。

一个人不能"成为"通道。一个人只能站在自己脚下那一段路上，把它走完。那一段路成不成为通道，不取决于这个人。取决于下一段路上有没有人接着走。

留下来的证据让我们看见她走过的一段；它没有把之后每一段都照亮。

如果没有人接着走呢？那项工作可能被忘记。历史里有多少失去名字的教师，我们无法逐一列出；更不能凭想象给古代图书馆补出一整份女性教师名单。但没有名字留下，并不是一个人没有价值的证据。

她们和他们做过的事，不能只用后世是否成功继承来评分。通道断了，损失是真实的；断裂也不是前一个人的罪。

希帕蒂娅也接近这种失落。我们主要靠他人的见证重建她，数学工作的具体面貌仍有争议。她的死格外醒目，但她不应当只有死法，没有一生。

她差一点就是一个被忘记的女人。

六、蚌壳

415年3月，基督教大斋期。

据苏格拉底记述，她在乘车回家的途中遇袭。她的出生年份并不确定，我们不为这个场景补上一个精确年龄。

一群人在街上拦下了她。

较早的记述把领头者称为读经员彼得。参与者是基督徒暴民，但不能据此断言全是修士或全部属于照护病人的parabalani团体。她没有得到足以救她的保护；这不等于我们拥有每一个官员在那一天行动的完整记录。

他们把她从马车上拖下来。他们把她拖到一座叫凯撒里翁的教堂里。这座教堂是一座旧异教神庙改建的。

在教堂里他们用ostraka杀死了她。

这个词在译本里出现过陶片、瓦片或贝壳等译法。不能仅凭它，就还原杀戮的时长与每一个动作。我们知道已经足够残酷的部分，不需要用猜出来的细节把它再演一遍。

她死了以后他们把她的遗体拖到城外烧掉。

这就是她死的方式。

我们写到这里的时候有一个诱惑：把她的死写得更详细，让这种残忍渗透出来，让读者愤怒。

我不打算这样写。

不是因为这件事不残忍。是因为残忍本身不是这一节要说的东西。残忍是表面。表面下面有更需要看见的东西。

下面有什么？

那一群人在那一天做的事，他们以为他们在做的是什么？

他们当中可能有人觉得自己在保卫信仰。有人觉得自己在执行主教的暗示。有人可能只是被人群带着往前走。有人可能跟希帕蒂娅毫无个人恩怨，只是听说"那个女异教徒"在阻碍主教，需要被处理。

当“老师”被一个敌意标签盖住，她教过什么、帮助过谁，便可能退到看不见的地方。我们不能逐个读出凶手的心，但可以问：什么使这个标签有了取消一个人的力量？

觉得自己在做正确的事，也可能成为伤害别人的理由。

这是这一节要说的东西。

不是说“有些人是恶魔”，也不是说“宗教必然使人发疯”。更值得警惕的是：流言、权力争夺与群体热情相遇时，人能够把残酷解释成职责。

这不是一切暴力唯一的原因。有的人明知作恶，有的人逐利，有的人服从。但只要他者被先写成需要清除的障碍，停止伤害他的理由就会变得越来越弱。

障碍要被清除。

一个人就这样被一个词盖住了。

希帕蒂娅死的时候没有留下话。我们不知道她最后说了什么。她可能什么都没来得及说。她可能说了很多但没有人记下来。我们不会知道。

那一天，被杀的是一个具体的人。她自己的声音停止了；不能因此宣告城里所有的哲学与数学也在当天停止。

给她写信的辛尼修斯，通常被认为已先她去世。

七、空地

希帕蒂娅死后，亚历山大里亚还是亚历山大里亚。

教堂继续建，神学继续发展。西里尔活到444年，并在431年的以弗所会议中扮演关键角色。城市没有因为一个人的死停下来。

但有一个人的讲台，确实空了。

这里需要说清楚：空下的是她自己的位置，不是整个亚历山大里亚的学术。后来的阿蒙尼乌斯、约翰·斐洛波努斯等人仍在这里讲学、作注。用她的死给古代学术写一个整齐的句号，会抹掉后面实际活过、工作过的人。

642年的征服又是两个多世纪以后的事，不能把它与415年的杀戮拉成一根必然衰落的直线。城市、语言和学术机构发生了复杂的变化。伊斯兰世界的翻译与创造，也不是替一片完全死掉的空地简单补课。

巴格达、托莱多和后来的欧洲城市开出了新路；亚历山大里亚自身也并非只剩废墟。新的工作可以出现，失去的那个人却不会因此回来。

不需要一个城市永远衰亡，她的损失才算数。

我们读历史的时候有一个习惯：把空地解释成"被取代了"。亚历山大里亚的希腊学术被基督教神学取代了。中世纪的拉丁神学被文艺复兴的人文主义取代了。文艺复兴被科学革命取代了。一切空地都被新东西填满。所以好像没有什么真正失去。

但是有些东西没有被取代。它们就是消失了。

她课堂上的声音没有完整留下。归于她的数学与天文工作，只能从残存的题记、书目和文本争议中追索。没有一件星盘能被稳妥地指认为她亲手制作；她同学生的许多对话，也没有记录可找。

我们能找回的，是一个名字，一个轮廓，一个被她讲过课的主教写的几封信，一个让她不至于完全被遗忘的死。

她差一点就是一个完全消失的人。

她还留下了名字，许多人没有。保存史并不是一张公平的成绩单；谁进入后世的视野，并不总由谁做得最好来决定。

空地不一定都被填上。有些空地永远是空地。我们走过去的时候不知道脚下原来是什么。

苏格拉底死后有柏拉图和亚里士多德，希帕蒂娅死后也有继续工作的学者。两座城都没有立刻沉默。但后来有人说话，不等于被杀的那个人的声音已被补上。

不是比较谁更幸运，而是不把“传统仍在”当成“没有真正失去”。她站在那个不能由别人代替的位置。

她教了许多年，然后被杀。学生可以另找老师，城市可以继续运作；这些都不是她继续活着的同义词。

她所在传统中的一些书继续流传，另一些失落。希腊文、阿拉伯文和拉丁文的路径把知识带往别处。她自己的贡献究竟隐藏在哪些传世版本中，仍不能作整齐的确认。

她不是一条直线。她是一条断了又被另一条线接上的线。

通道有时候是这样工作的。它在一个地方断掉。它在另一个地方被接上。你以为它死了的时候，它在另一个地方还活着。但你不能依赖这一点。你不能说"反正会有人接上的"。每一次断掉都是真的断掉。每一次接上都是另一群人下了功夫。

课堂的现场没有回来；某些知识传统继续被人教。延续与失落，可以同时是真的。

她不知道哪些会被接上，哪些不会。她活着的时候不可能知道。她只是讲她的课，注释她的书，做她那一段路上能做的事。

通道不是一个人能完成的东西。通道是一代一代人共同做出来的事。每一代都可能是最后一代。每一代都可能不是。

希帕蒂娅不是亚历山大里亚最后一个学者。但她是她自己的最后一个人。她的工作可能有回声，她的死亡仍不可代偿。

八、桥头

桥头上有很多人。

有些蹲着。有些站着。有些坐着。有些在画图，有些在写方程，有些在看玉米，有些在读诗。这些人是一片一片的人影，分布在桥的不同距离上。康德在最远的那一头，几乎看不清，但每个人都知道他在那里。

希帕蒂娅走过来。

她是一个上了岁数的女人，灰发，瘦，眼睛很亮。她的衣服是希腊式的长袍。她手里拿着一个东西——一个圆形的金属盘，刻着精细的刻度和星图。是一个星盘。

她走得很稳。

桥头上的人朝她看了一眼。有个穿希腊袍子的老人——苏格拉底——走过来站在她旁边。他比她矮一点，鼻子很大，赤脚。两个人没有说话。两个人都被自己的城市杀过。两种杀法不一样。两个人之间有一种不需要说出来的东西。

不远处站着奥古斯丁。两人的生命年代相近。他看着她，像想说什么，又没有说出来。这是桥头的想象，不是替他补写悔词。他与西里尔同属基督教传统，却不是同一个人；她也从未以变成基督教神学家为目标。我们让他们相遇，不替他们取消差别。

更远处，柏拉图蹲着画图。希帕蒂娅看了他一眼。她的哲学教导属于柏拉图与普罗提诺的传统，不只是一个实用数学家的手艺。桥上这一刻，我们让她把目光停在椭圆上：一个具体的图，也能通向很远的思考。

桥头中央有一片人——画方程的，看玉米的，写诗的，读星图的。希帕蒂娅看了看他们。她不认识其中的大多数人。但他们都在做她做过的事——在自己那一段路上把它走完。她对他们点了点头。他们也对她点了点头。

她没有找一个固定的位置。她在桥头慢慢走着。她手里的星盘在月光下泛着光。

夜色在加深。她低头看了看星盘上的刻度。她抬头看了看天空。

天空和星盘对得上。

她笑了一下。

她没有笑给谁看。她只是确认了一件她讲了一辈子的事——天空在那里，星盘在这里，它们之间有一个对应关系。这个对应关系不需要任何人的同意。它就在那里。

她继续慢慢走。星盘在她手里。月光在她身上。

桥的最远那一头，那个一直看着远方的人，这一次没有看向远方。

他在看她。

她不知道。她不需要知道。她讲过的圆锥曲线，正在桥下面一片看不见的地方，被人接着讲。[1][2][3]

I. The Lectern

Early fifth-century Alexandria. Let us enter an imagined classroom, not pretend to have witnessed her final lesson.

A woman is teaching conic sections.

Her students came from elsewhere and crossed boundaries of belief. We have no roll call for this particular room, and cannot seat every community in the city here as a matter of fact. But one pupil has a name we know: Synesius. Later a Christian bishop, he still addressed her from his sickbed as mother, sister, teacher.

Her name is Hypatia.

She is teaching the Conics of Apollonius. A cone is cut by a plane. Depending on the angle, the cross-section is a circle, an ellipse, a parabola, or a hyperbola. The same cone. Different angles. Entirely different shapes.

She taught mathematics, astronomy and philosophy. Her father Theon was a mathematician too. Ancient notices connect her with work on Diophantus, Apollonius and astronomical texts; Synesius acknowledged her teaching when discussing an astrolabe. Whether she invented or improved a particular instrument is less certain than the familiar stories suggest.

She taught in the city for many years. Scholars still worked there, while religious and political conflicts sharpened. Cyril became bishop in 412. Her final years would be caught up in his struggle with the imperial prefect.

She teaches every day. She does not know how much longer she will be able to. But today she still can. Today she teaches conic sections.

Her students sit in front of her. A cone is cut. The cross-section is an ellipse. She draws the ellipse on her board, slowly, the way you would draw someone you know.

II. Astrolabe and Cone

No complete work securely survives under Hypatia’s name. Synesius supplies contemporary testimony; the much later Suda preserves a list of writings. Exactly which surviving texts she edited, and which passages might carry her work, remain disputed. That uncertainty belongs in the story.

The Suda credits her with a commentary on Diophantus’s Arithmetica. Its problems often sought positive rational solutions, not simply the integer solutions associated with modern Diophantine equations. Most details of her commentary are lost. But the kind of labor is recognizable: a teacher making difficult work available to another reader.

A heading attached to Book III of Theon’s commentary on the Almagest names his daughter Hypatia in connection with a revision. Scholars disagree about whether it concerns the commentary or Ptolemy’s text. We can place her within that work without crediting her alone for the entire book’s later survival.

In his text accompanying the gift of an astrolabe, Synesius connects what he knows with his teacher’s instruction. The instrument projects the celestial sphere onto a plane and helps its user read the heavens. Teaching, mathematics and craft meet here. That is more specific than giving her an unverified inventor’s title.

She did the work of a mathematician. But the way she did it — reading Diophantus, annotating Ptolemy, working the astrolabe — shared a single quality. She took something complicated and opened it, so that someone else could enter.

The conic sections are a perfect instance of this.

Apollonius’s Conics asks what happens when a plane cuts a cone. For a double cone and a plane that misses its vertex, a cut perpendicular to the axis gives a circle; an oblique cut confined to one half can give an ellipse; a plane parallel to a generator gives a parabola; a plane crossing both halves gives a hyperbola. These are the nondegenerate cases, not the outcome of every possible cut.

Four familiar curves. One cone. Change the position and direction of the plane, and the section changes.

I want to borrow the figure to read her classroom: one object can appear differently when approached from different directions. This is our metaphor, not a political doctrine recovered from her missing lecture notes.

This metaphor had a very specific resonance in her city. Alexandria was a city cut by many planes. The Greeks had their Alexandria. The Jews had theirs. The Christians had theirs. The native Egyptians had theirs. These different cross-sections lay over one another, rubbing, becoming less and less able to see each other.

At least Synesius’s life shows that people of different beliefs could meet in a shared practice of learning. The classroom did not solve the city. It did leave a place to sit for someone outside a religious “we.”

This does not mean they were reconciled. It does not mean mathematics solved religion. It means that, in this hour of class, they were looking at the same thing.

She did this for decades.

III. Synesius

Synesius of Cyrene, born around 370, died around 413.

He belonged to the wealthy, Greek-speaking elite of Cyrenaica. After studying in Alexandria, he returned home, married, raised children, managed estates and dealt with public affairs. Eventually he became bishop of Ptolemais. His religious development resists a neat conversion date, and his philosophical education did not disappear with his new office.

After leaving her classroom, he kept writing to Hypatia.

More than a hundred of his letters survive. Several address her directly; others also bring her into view. The sea separated them and public office changed his days. Neither required him to erase his teacher from his life.

He calls her mother, sister, teacher.

The letters contain appeals for help as well as reverence.

The language of family and the language of learning sit together.

In one letter he writes: "Even if the dead are allowed to forget all in Hades, even there I shall remember my dear Hypatia."

Of course the letters use the rhetoric of their time. Still, a Christian bishop writing this way to a pagan philosopher gives us something more particular than an abstract slogan of tolerance. He is speaking to a person who knows him, not merely classifying an acceptable outsider.

He wrote in illness and after the loss of his children, and when public affairs exhausted him. He asked her help in having an instrument made for measuring liquids, sought her judgment, and asked her to assist friends. Doctrinal tensions were real. The relationship was not therefore canceled.

He did not treat her as a regrettable pagan who had not yet seen the light. He treated her as his teacher. For life.

The existence of Synesius tells us something. Those Christian students sitting in Hypatia's classroom were not an abstract group. Some of them loved her like this.

The people who would later kill her were also Christians. Before them, the people who first loved her were also Christians.

The Christians of Alexandria cannot be written as one person. One relationship could produce these letters; another kind of mobilization could lead to murder. The historian Socrates, who recorded and condemned the killing, was himself a Christian.

Synesius is generally thought to have died around 413, before her murder. Had he been there, what would he have done? Could he have stopped it?

We do not know.

We cannot furnish history with a rescuer. We have the letters: before the murder, someone had loved and respected her in this way. Nothing that happened afterward makes that relationship unreal.

IV. Cyril

In 412, Cyril became bishop of Alexandria.

He succeeded his uncle Theophilus and became a major figure in the disputes over Christ, revered in several Christian traditions. The history of theology cannot go around him. Neither can the story of Hypatia’s death go around him because of that eminence.

He was also a politician.

The city contained more than two opposing camps: imperial authorities, the bishop, Jewish communities and different Christian groups. Socrates describes communal violence, expulsions and the conflict between Cyril and Orestes. Orestes was a Christian too. The attack on him by Nitrian monks belongs to this background, but it was a separate event; its participants cannot simply be substituted for Hypatia’s killers.

Hypatia met Orestes and enjoyed respect among the city’s prominent people. She was not sealed away from public life. According to Socrates, a rumor blamed her for preventing a reconciliation between bishop and prefect. A teacher had been recast as a political obstruction.

A complicated conflict had acquired a body on which to fix its blame.

Whether Cyril ordered her death is not a settled fact. The earlier account by Socrates differs from later, more hostile accounts. They need to be distinguished, not combined into an omniscient narrator.

Socrates explicitly says the affair disgraced Cyril and the Alexandrian church. That warrants questions about episcopal power, incitement and responsibility for protection. It does not establish a precise chain of command, prior knowledge or the affiliation of every killer. Accountability and the limits of evidence must remain together.

Not possessing his inner life does not mean putting the question away.

When power takes making a city right to mean silencing voices outside its ownership, a teacher ceases to be seen as a teacher. Whom she speaks to, and who listens, can become evidence against her. This is a danger we can examine in the conflict, not an unrecorded confession we may place in Cyril’s mouth.

First a person shrinks into an obstacle. Then violence becomes easier to imagine as a solution.

Learning does not automatically prevent that move. Neither does devotion. A system may speak with impressive coherence while excluding a living person from its care. We need not claim to know every category in Cyril’s mind to recognize the possibility.

Synesius’s letters show a different relationship that actually existed: I have my faith, and you remain my teacher.

This is not a ranking of two Christians by depth of belief. The question is whether different people can continue to have a place in one world.

V. Conduit

How did Greek mathematics reach our hands?

Apollonius's Conics, Diophantus's Arithmetica, Ptolemy's Almagest, Euclid's Elements — how did these books travel from a few centuries before Christ, through the disorder of late antiquity, through the Middle Ages, and into the hands of the Europeans of the Renaissance?

Not in a straight line.

Books lived through copying, annotation, revision, translation and teaching. The fate of Alexandria’s library cannot be reduced to a tidy list of fires; the loss of a book may have no spectacular date at all. Sometimes the next copy is simply never made.

Hypatia belonged to this labor. Book lists, headings and a student’s testimony locate her in mathematical and astronomical teaching. We cannot identify every later line that passed through her hands. That does not mean her generation’s teaching never happened.

What followed was a network, not a straight line. Preservation in Greek, translations into Syriac and Arabic, Byzantine scholarship, the original work of Arabic-speaking scholars, and direct Greek-to-Latin translation all entered later European learning. Baghdad and Toledo mattered enormously. Not every book stopped at the same stations.

She had a place in that network. She need not have been its only relay.

She did not know she was. She was teaching her classes. She was doing what a teacher does. She taught what she loved, in her own city, for decades. The echoes of those lessons she could not hear during her life, and could not hear after her death.

But the echoes were there.

Much later Fermat wrote his famous claim in the margin of a Latin Diophantus, and Kepler used ellipses to understand planetary orbits. These distant echoes suggest a tradition’s long life. They do not prove that a particular line either man read came through Hypatia’s commentary.

She did not "transmit" these things. What she did was smaller, and larger. She let her own generation see them. She let them go on living in her generation. Whether the next generation would see them, whether the generation after that would see them, was not in her power. She was not an engineer of history. She was a teacher.

At least among the people she taught, difficult knowledge had an opportunity to become living knowledge.

A person cannot become a conduit. A person can only stand on the stretch of road under their own feet and walk it through. Whether that stretch becomes a conduit does not depend on that person. It depends on whether someone walks the next stretch.

The evidence illuminates a stretch of road she walked. It does not illuminate every stretch after it.

What if nobody follows? Work can be forgotten. We cannot name every lost teacher, or invent an entire roster of women teaching in an ancient library. But the failure of a name to survive is not evidence that its bearer had no value.

Their work cannot be graded solely by the success of later inheritance. A broken passage is a real loss, not necessarily the fault of the person who came before.

Hypatia came close to such disappearance. We reconstruct her mainly through others, while the details of her mathematical work remain disputed. Her murder is conspicuous. It should not leave her with a death and no life.

She came that close to being a forgotten woman.

VI. Shells

March 415, during the Christian season of Lent.

Socrates says she was attacked while returning home in a carriage. Her birth date is uncertain; the scene does not require us to invent a precise age.

A crowd stopped her in the street.

The earlier account names a reader called Peter as the leader. It describes a Christian mob, but does not establish that all were monks or members of the parabalani, the body associated with caring for the sick. She was not protected sufficiently to survive. We do not possess a complete record of what every official did that day.

They pulled her from the carriage. They dragged her to a church called the Caesareum. The Caesareum had been a temple for Caesar's cult, repurposed as a church.

Inside the church, they killed her with ostraka.

The word has been translated as potsherds, tiles or shells. It does not let us reconstruct the length of the attack or every movement in it. What we know is already cruel enough. We need not stage the rest out of conjecture.

After she was dead, they dragged the body outside the city and burned it.

This is how she died.

There is a temptation, when writing this, to give the death more detail, to let the cruelty seep through, to make the reader furious.

I will not write it that way.

Not because what happened was not cruel. Because the cruelty itself is not what this section is for. The cruelty is the surface. Underneath it is something that needs more to be seen.

What is underneath?

What did the people in that crowd think they were doing, on that day?

Some of them probably believed they were defending the faith. Some believed they were carrying out the bishop's wishes. Some were probably just being carried by the crowd. Some had no personal grievance with Hypatia at all; they had only heard that "the pagan woman" was obstructing the bishop, and that she needed to be dealt with.

Once a hostile label covers the teacher, what she taught and whom she helped can disappear from view. We cannot read each killer’s mind. We can ask what enabled a label to acquire the power to cancel a person.

The conviction of doing right can also become a reason to hurt someone.

This is what this section is for.

Not “some people are demons,” nor “religion inevitably makes people mad.” The more useful warning is that rumor, struggles for power and collective fervor can allow cruelty to be understood as duty.

This is not the sole cause of every act of violence. Some perpetrators know they are doing wrong; some seek advantage; some obey. But when another person has already become an obstacle to remove, reasons to stop hurting that person become harder to hear.

Obstacles are to be removed.

A word has covered a person.

Hypatia left no last words. We do not know what she said at the end. She may not have had time to say anything. She may have said many things that no one wrote down. We will not know.

A particular person was killed that day. Her own voice stopped. It does not follow that all philosophy and mathematics in the city stopped with it.

Synesius, who had written those letters, is generally thought to have died before her.

VII. Empty Ground

After Hypatia's death, Alexandria was still Alexandria.

Churches continued to be built and theology continued to develop. Cyril lived until 444, having played a central role at Ephesus in 431. A city did not stop because one person died.

One person’s place at the lectern was nevertheless empty.

Her place, not the whole of Alexandrian scholarship. Ammonius and John Philoponus were among those who later taught and wrote there. Making her death a neat full stop to ancient learning would erase people who actually lived and worked after her.

The conquest of 642 came more than two centuries later. It cannot be joined to the murder of 415 as one inevitable line of decline. Cities, languages and institutions changed in complicated ways. Translation and original work in the Islamic world were not simply remedial instruction for an entirely dead landscape.

Baghdad, Toledo and later European centers made new paths; Alexandria itself was not merely rubble. New work can be done without restoring the person who was lost.

A city need not perish forever for her loss to matter.

We have a habit, when reading history, of treating empty ground as "replaced." The Greek learning of Alexandria was replaced by Christian theology. The Latin theology of the Middle Ages was replaced by Renaissance humanism. The Renaissance was replaced by the Scientific Revolution. Every empty ground gets filled with something new. So nothing seems truly lost.

But some things are not replaced. Some things just disappear.

The sound of her teaching was not fully preserved. Mathematical and astronomical work attributed to her must be sought through surviving headings, book lists and disputed texts. No extant astrolabe can securely be identified as one she made. Many conversations with students have left no record.

What we can recover is a name, an outline, the letters of a bishop who once sat in her classroom, and a death too cruel to be entirely erased from the record.

She came close to being a completely vanished person.

She retained a name; many did not. Preservation is not a fair examination. Entering posterity’s field of vision is not always the reward for having done the best work.

Empty ground is not always filled. Some empty ground remains empty. We walk over it and do not know what was once there.

After Socrates came Plato and Aristotle; after Hypatia, scholars also continued to work. Neither city immediately fell silent. Yet a later voice is not a replacement for the murdered person’s voice.

The point is not to compare their luck. It is to resist mistaking “the tradition continued” for “nothing was truly lost.” Her place could not be occupied on her behalf.

She taught for years and was killed. Students could find another teacher; the city could continue functioning. Neither is another way of saying she remained alive.

Some books in her tradition survived; others disappeared. Greek, Arabic and Latin paths carried knowledge elsewhere. Exactly where her own contributions lie within surviving versions still resists a tidy verdict.

She was not a straight line. She was a line that broke and was picked up by another line.

This is sometimes how a conduit works. It breaks in one place. It is picked up in another. Just when you think it is dead, it is alive somewhere else. But you cannot rely on this. You cannot say "someone will pick it up." Every break is a real break. Every pickup is the work of another group of people.

The classroom itself did not return; some traditions of knowledge continued to be taught. Continuity and loss can both be real.

She did not know which would be taken up and which would not. She could not have known, in her lifetime. She only taught her classes, annotated her books, did the work that her stretch of road allowed.

A conduit is not something one person completes. A conduit is something many generations make together. Each generation may be the last. Each generation may not be.

Hypatia was not Alexandria’s last scholar. But there was no second Hypatia who could live her life for her. Her work may echo; her death remains uncompensated.

VIII. The Bridge

There are many people on the bridge.

Some are crouching. Some standing. Some sitting. Some are drawing diagrams, some writing equations, some watching corn, some reading poems. They are figures distributed at different distances along the bridge. Kant is at the far end, almost out of sight, but everyone knows he is there.

Hypatia walks up.

She is an aging woman, gray-haired, thin, with very bright eyes. She wears a Greek robe. In her hand is something — a circular metal disc, finely etched with markings and a star map. An astrolabe.

She walks steadily.

The people on the bridge glance at her. An old man in a Greek robe — Socrates — comes over and stands beside her. He is shorter than her, with a large nose, barefoot. They do not speak. Both of them have been killed by their own city. The two killings were not the same. Between them is something that does not need to be said.

Augustine stands nearby. Their lives overlapped. He looks as if he would speak, but does not. This is an imagined encounter, not a recovered confession of remorse. He shared a Christian tradition with Cyril without being the same person; she never set out to become a Christian theologian. Bringing them together must not abolish their differences.

Farther away Plato is drawing. Hypatia glances toward him. Her philosophy belonged to the tradition of Plato and Plotinus; she was not merely a practical mathematician. At this moment on our bridge, we let her attention rest on an ellipse. A particular figure can open onto distant thought.

In the middle of the bridge stands a cluster of figures — those drawing equations, watching corn, writing poems, reading star charts. Hypatia looks at them. She does not know most of them. But they are all doing what she did: walking the stretch of road under their own feet. She nods to them. They nod back.

She does not look for a fixed place. She moves slowly along the bridge. The astrolabe in her hand catches the moonlight.

The night deepens. She glances down at the markings on the astrolabe. She looks up at the sky.

The sky and the astrolabe match.

She smiles, briefly.

She is not smiling for anyone. She is only confirming what she taught all her life — that the sky is there, the astrolabe is here, and there is a correspondence between them. The correspondence does not require anyone's consent. It is simply there.

She keeps walking. The astrolabe is in her hand. The moonlight is on her.

At the far end of the bridge, the figure who has always been looking into the distance is, this once, not looking into the distance.

He is looking at her.

She does not know. She does not need to know. The conic sections she once taught are, in some unseen place beneath the bridge, being taught by someone else.[1][2][3]
