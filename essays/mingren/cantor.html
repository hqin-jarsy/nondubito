康托尔，比无穷更大

Cantor, Larger Than Infinity

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、无穷有多大

数到一百万，还能再加一。再数下去，还是能再加一。

我们通常这样理解无穷：前面永远有路。因此，“两个无穷哪个更大”听起来像一句说错了的话。既然都走不到头，还能怎样比较？

康托尔把问题换了一种问法。先别管走不走得到头。如果两边的东西能够一个不漏地配成对，它们是不是就一样多？

这个问法没有声势，甚至有点像分椅子。可是，十九世纪的数学会从这里走进一片新地方。无穷不再只是手指向远方的动作，它开始有可以辨认的形状。

1874年，康托尔发表了实数不可数的结果。1891年，他又给出了著名的对角线论证。两个年份之间，还有许多尝试、通信与修改，不是一声惊雷以后，世界才开始思考无穷。

先跟他算一小段。不要急着把这一生读成“天才因看见太多而发疯”的寓言。数学需要证明，一个生病的人则需要照顾。这两件事不该互相冒充。

二、一把椅子配一个人

假设屋里的人和椅子都很多。你不必先数出总数：让每个人坐下一把椅子，没有人站着，也没有空椅子，两边就一样多。这是一一对应的直觉。

把自然数1、2、3、4依次配给偶数2、4、6、8。每一个自然数都有伙伴，每一个正偶数也会被配到。规则可以一直用下去，不会在哪一步突然失效。

偶数确实只是自然数的一部分。可是，在“能否一一配对”这个意义上，它们一样多。有限集合里管用的直觉——拿走一些就必定变少——到了这里需要重新检查。

分数更像一片挤满人的广场。任意两个不同的有理数之间，还能放进别的有理数。这样密，怎么排得成一条队？

把正分数按分子、分母排进格子，沿斜线一层层走，遇到1/2与2/4这样的重复就跳过。再加入零和负数，每个有理数最终都能轮到。这里的斜线是编排办法，别和后面用来证明无法列全的对角线混在一起。

这种大小叫ℵ₀，读作阿列夫零。自然数、正偶数、有理数，都能进入这支可数无穷的队伍。你开始有理由怀疑：也许无穷只有一种大小。接下来的一步，才真正出人意料。

三、队伍外面那一个

设想有人递给你一份名单，说0与1之间的实数已经全在上面。第一行一个小数，第二行一个小数，一直往下，每行都有自己的自然数编号。

你不必读完整份名单。沿对角线看：第一行第一位，第二行第二位，第三行第三位。每一行只取出一个位置，作为你要改变的地方。

我们只用1和2来写新数。如果看到的那一位是1，就写2；不是1，就写1。这样写出的新小数，第一位与第一行不同，第二位与第二行不同，第n位与第n行不同。

只用1和2，还避开了小数表示的小陷阱：0.4999…与0.5000…其实相等。不能仅仅让纸上的数字看起来不同，却不小心写出同一个数。

现在问：新数在哪一行？放在第一行不行，第一位不对；放在第二行也不行，第二位不对。不管你指哪一行，总有一个已经安排好的差别等在那里。

它仍是0与1之间的实数，却没有出现在名单中。“已经列全”这句话破了。不是名单还不够长：任何按自然数编号的名单，都可以这样找出漏掉的数。

这是1891年论证的一种现代小数说明，不是原论文逐字照搬。1874年的证明走的是另一条路。真正重要的不是哪一种写法最漂亮，而是从“我们暂时没列出来”走到了“这种列法不可能列全”。

图灵后来使用的反向构造，与这里有家族相似性。但停机问题不只是再写出一个遗漏的小数；那里要构造程序，让一个假定万能的判定程序与自己的结果冲突。工具会迁移，问题仍各有边界。

四、楼梯怎么长出来

自然数的大小是ℵ₀，实数的大小通常记作c。c更大。那它会不会就是最后一个无穷？

康托尔给出了一个可以反复使用的办法：从一个集合出发，考虑它所有子集组成的集合，叫作幂集。比如两个人的小组，可以选谁都不选、只选甲、只选乙、两人都选，共四种。

对任何集合，幂集都比原集合大。证明也带着刚才的转身：假设每个元素都已经领到一个子集，就收集那些“不属于自己领到的子集”的元素。这个新子集不可能恰好是其中任何一个人的配额。

于是，只要你递来一个集合，就还有更大的。这里没有最大的集合大小，可以让楼梯在某一级正式封顶。

但符号也会使人产生错觉。ℵ₀、ℵ₁、ℵ₂依次表示越来越大的无限基数；取一次幂集，却不一定正好走到紧邻的下一级。把这两种往上走的方法画成同一列台阶，会把一个尚待回答的问题偷偷画成答案。

无穷因此变得更精确，并不是变得什么都懂了。康托尔给我们的，不是掌握一切的最高位置，而是到了一个位置以后，还能认真问下一步在哪里的办法。

五、两级之间

ℵ₁是比ℵ₀大的最小无限基数。实数的大小c，会不会刚好等于ℵ₁？也就是说，在可数无穷与连续统之间，还有没有别的大小？

这就是连续统假设。康托尔长年尝试解决它，没有成功。可以想象一页页计算重来的疲惫，却不能从一个难题直接写出一份精神疾病的病因报告。

1900年，希尔伯特把这个问题列在著名问题清单的首位。它没有随着康托尔的失败消失。后来接手的人，连“怎样才算回答”也一起改了。

哥德尔在1938年得到相对相容性结果，1940年发表专著；科恩在1963年完成另一方向。合起来说：如果通常使用的集合论公理系统ZFC是一致的，那么它既不能证明连续统假设，也不能证明其否定。

“在ZFC内”不能省略。这不是宣布任何可能的数学都永远不能讨论答案。加入新的公理、研究不同模型，正是后来的工作；它们也需要理由，不能为了喜欢某个结论就随手选定。

有时候，没找到答案是因为人还不够熟练；有时候，是现有规则没有决定答案。把这两种情形分开，本身就是一种进展。未完成不总是个人失败，更不必成为羞辱人的凭据。

六、通信桌的两边

克罗内克是康托尔的重要反对者。他对哪些对象、哪些论证可以进入数学，有严格得多的要求。这是实质性的学术冲突，也伴随着令人受伤的权力关系。

不过，不能把康托尔一生写成一位恶人把门、其余所有人都拒绝他的故事。魏尔斯特拉斯等人曾支持他，他在哈勒工作多年，参与建立数学共同体。批评、支持、职业受挫与疾病交错，不归一个原因管。

还有德德金。1873年的通信不是天才给听众发布成果，而是两个数学家交换问题、证明与改进。纸页从一个人的桌上出发，到另一个人的桌上，思路才继续往前。

这里有一笔不能因为敬佩而抹去的署名账。德德金留下记录，指出自己的证明及改进进入了康托尔1874年的论文，公开发表时却没有得到应有承认。康托尔的数学贡献和这笔欠账，可以同时成立。

2026年《Quanta》的报道又把一封长期被认为失佚的信带回公众视野：德米安·古斯在2025年于哈勒查见的1873年11月30日信，其中包括德德金关于代数数可数性的证明。应把新近报道、保存的信件与对动机的推测分开。

这不是“原来一切都是偷来的”的反转剧。实数不可数与超穷数研究的贡献仍需逐项判断；德德金也不应只是用来给康托尔增加人物复杂度的配角。他的名字应在他做过的工作旁边。

受过不公的人，也可能没有公正地对待另一个人。这值得直说，但它不是某种伤害守恒定律：受伤不会必然使人伤人，更不能替后来造成的伤害免责。

理解一个人，比替他辩护到底难一些。你要保留他的工作、他受的痛，也保留那个没有得到署名的人。

七、三种不同的边界

康托尔、哥德尔、图灵，放在一起很有诱惑力。三个人都让一种“应该可以全部完成”的愿望遇到了严格的限制。

康托尔证明实数无法用自然数列全；哥德尔研究足够表达算术、可有效列举公理的形式系统；图灵追问能否用一个通用算法判定所有相关实例。他们不是把同一个定理说了三遍。

对角构造、自我指涉与编码，把这些工作连成重要的思想线索。但名单、证明系统和程序不是同一种对象，连续统假设的独立性也不等于停机问题的一个换皮例子。

我们可以从中读出一种谦逊：工具很有力量，也值得问它适用于哪里。这是哲学上的阅读，不是说数学已经替所有哲学主张开具了证明。

尤其不能把三个人的疾病、迫害与死亡，写成“看见边界的人注定被边界压碎”。图灵遭受国家迫害，康托尔经历反复疾病；他们的生活不是定理附带的寓言。

证明有局限，并不妨碍生活需要朋友、工作、休息和照料。把一个人的痛苦当成思想深刻的证书，是我们在纪念他时还可以避免的一种伤害。

八、没有顶，不等于没有路

现在可以回到那座楼梯。你站在哪一级，都能问是否还有更大的集合；答案不会因为站得够高就变成没有。

这容易让人想到别的宏大思想：柏拉图的善、黑格尔的体系。但那是比较的起点，不是比较已经结束。基数定理不会直接证明理念论错误，也不会替我们判定历史是否有终点。

康托尔本人还有强烈的宗教关切，并把数学中的超穷与神学意义上的绝对无穷区分开。把他写成宣布所有终极问题一律取消的人，也会错过他自己的声音。

更贴近他工作的比喻，也许不是“终于证明什么都不可能”，而是发现尺子可以造得更好，问题可以问得更清楚。可数、不可数、幂集，各给了下一步一个落脚处。

人不必先拥有全部，才有资格做眼前这一段。读者也不必懂完集合论，才看得见这里发生的转变：一句含混的“永远”，被拆成了可以争论、校正和接着工作的事情。

楼梯没有顶，和你今天不能上一级，是两句话。康托尔的工作留下了前一句，并没有要求我们接受后一句。

九、把笔放下来一会儿

1918年1月6日，康托尔在哈勒的精神病院去世，七十二岁。他晚年反复住院，战争也使生活更艰难。最后的日子应当被记住，不必再添上“无穷终于夺走他的理智”的旁白。

希尔伯特关于康托尔所创造的天堂的名言，出现在他身后。它说的是数学的开阔，不是证明创造它的人曾经生活在天堂里。学科的胜利不会倒流回去，使一个病人免于孤独。

在这个系列想象的桥头，康托尔仍然抬头看。他手里的笔划过空气，写下ℵ₀、ℵ₁，后面还有地方。

这一次，我们不必替他画一双“有点疯”的眼睛。他也可以只是一个想把事情讲清楚、说到兴头上的人。

契诃夫走近一点。休谟把双陆棋收好。图灵拿着一张写有符号的纸，没有人催他把故事变成结论。

康托尔可以接着说，也可以停一下。有人愿意听，不是因为他一定还能证明一条惊人的定理。

那些台阶不会因他休息而消失。一个人也不必把自己耗尽，才配得上自己开出的路。

上面还有。这里，也可以有一把椅子。

I. How Large Is Infinity?

Count to a million and you can still add one. Keep going: there will always be another number waiting.

That is how infinity usually enters our lives, as a road without an end. Asking which of two infinities is larger seems to misunderstand the word. If neither journey can be finished, what could comparing their lengths possibly mean?

Cantor approached from another direction. Forget reaching the end for a moment. If everything on one side can be paired with exactly one thing on the other, leaving nothing unmatched, would that make the two collections equally large?

It sounds more like arranging chairs than overturning mathematics. Yet a new territory opens inside the question. Infinity begins to acquire a shape we can investigate, rather than remaining a gesture toward the distance.

Cantor published his result on the uncountability of the real numbers in 1874; the celebrated diagonal argument followed in 1891. Between those dates were calculations, letters, revisions, and other people's work. Infinity did not enter human thought in a single flash.

Follow the argument before supplying the tragedy. A mathematical claim requires a proof; someone experiencing mental illness requires care. The story becomes less truthful, not more profound, when we make either one stand in for the other.

II. A Chair for Everyone

Imagine a crowded room. You can discover whether there are as many chairs as people without first counting either: seat everyone, one person to a chair. Nobody standing, no vacant seat. That is the everyday intuition behind a one-to-one correspondence.

Now pair the positive integers 1, 2, 3, 4 with 2, 4, 6, 8. Every integer gets its even partner, and every positive even number is reached. The rule does not stop working merely because we cannot finish performing it.

The even numbers remain a proper part of the positive integers. Nevertheless, the two sets have the same cardinality. A reliable intuition about finite collections—that removing some members must reduce their size—cannot simply be carried across unchanged.

Fractions look more troublesome. Between any two different rational numbers there are others. How could this crowded landscape ever become a queue in which each person receives a whole-number ticket?

Arrange positive fractions in a grid by numerator and denominator, then traverse successive diagonals, skipping repetitions such as 1/2 and 2/4. Include zero and negative fractions too. Every rational eventually receives a place. This diagonal is a route through a grid, not the later argument showing that a list must omit something.

The size is called ℵ₀, aleph-null: countable infinity. Integers, positive evens, rationals—all can join it. Perhaps every infinite collection will turn out to be this size. We now have a good reason to ask, rather than merely assuming an answer.

III. The Missing Entry

Suppose someone hands you a list containing, they say, every real number between zero and one. A decimal occupies the first row, another the second, and so on, each row carrying its own positive-integer number.

You need not inspect the entire list. Read along its diagonal: the first decimal place in row one, the second in row two, the third in row three. Each row gives you one place at which to disagree with it.

Write a new decimal using only the digits 1 and 2. Where the inspected digit is 1, write 2; otherwise write 1. Your new number differs from the first row in its first place, from the second in its second place, and from row n in place n.

Choosing only 1 and 2 avoids a small but consequential trap: 0.4999… and 0.5000… are the same real number. Different-looking digit strings are not always different numbers. A beautiful argument still owes attention to this detail.

Which row contains your new decimal? Not the first; its first digit is wrong. Not the second; its second digit is wrong. Point to any row and a difference has already been arranged for precisely that row.

The newcomer is still a real number between zero and one. Yet it is absent. The difficulty is not that this particular list needs more paper: any list numbered by the positive integers can be defeated in the same way.

This is a modern decimal presentation of the 1891 argument, not a transcription of Cantor's original wording. His 1874 proof took another route. What matters is the passage from a failure to finish listing to a proof that this kind of listing cannot succeed.

Turing's later reversal has a family resemblance, but it concerns programs and a supposed universal decision procedure, not merely another missing decimal. The method travels. The objects, assumptions, and conclusions must still be identified afresh.

IV. Making Another Step

Let ℵ₀ name the size of the natural numbers, and c the size of the reals. We know c is larger. Could it be the last infinity?

Cantor supplies a repeatable operation: take all the subsets of a set. This new collection is its power set. From a two-person group you can select nobody, the first person, the second, or both: four possible selections.

For any set, the power set is strictly larger. Suppose every member had been assigned a subset in a proposed complete matching. Gather the members that do not belong to their own assigned subsets. This new subset cannot be assigned to any member without a contradiction about that member's inclusion.

Give us a set, then, and there is a larger size. No greatest cardinality waits at the summit to end the procedure.

But the symbols can deceive us. ℵ₀, ℵ₁, ℵ₂ name successive infinite cardinalities; taking a power set need not move exactly to the next aleph. Drawing both processes as one staircase quietly turns a difficult question into an answer.

Infinity has become more precise, not entirely mastered. The achievement offers no final viewing platform above everything. It gives us something better suited to continued work: an exact way of asking what might come next.

V. Between Two Steps

ℵ₁ is the smallest infinite cardinal larger than ℵ₀. Is c equal to ℵ₁? Put differently, does any size lie strictly between the countable infinity and the continuum?

This is the continuum hypothesis. Cantor tried to resolve it for years. We can imagine the fatigue of starting again, but a difficult problem is not, by itself, a medical explanation for the life of the person working on it.

Hilbert placed the question first in his famous list of problems in 1900. It did not disappear when Cantor failed to settle it. Later mathematicians would change our understanding of what settling it might require.

Gödel obtained a relative-consistency result in 1938 and published his monograph in 1940; Cohen supplied the complementary result in 1963. Together they show that, if the usual set-theoretic axioms ZFC are consistent, ZFC proves neither the hypothesis nor its negation.

Within ZFC is indispensable. The result does not forbid every conceivable mathematical framework from addressing the question. New axioms and different models became part of the subsequent investigation; choosing them also requires reasons, not merely a preference for an answer.

Sometimes the obstacle is an unfinished piece of reasoning. Sometimes the adopted rules do not determine the answer. Distinguishing those situations is progress. An unfinished problem need not become evidence of a person's inadequacy, still less a warrant for humiliation.

VI. Two Desks

Kronecker was an important opponent. His requirements concerning admissible mathematical objects and methods were much more restrictive. The disagreement had genuine intellectual substance, and it also unfolded through unequal professional power.

Yet Cantor's career cannot be reduced to one malicious gatekeeper and universal rejection. Weierstrass and others supported him; he worked at Halle for decades and helped build a mathematical community. Support, hostility, disappointment, and illness do not all answer to one cause.

There was also Dedekind. Their correspondence in 1873 was not a genius broadcasting discoveries to an audience. Two mathematicians exchanged questions, proofs, and improvements. Thinking continued because paper moved between their desks.

An unpaid debt of acknowledgment belongs in this story. Dedekind recorded that his proof and improvements entered Cantor's 1874 paper without adequate public credit. Cantor's lasting achievement does not make that record disappear.

Quanta reported in 2026 on a letter long thought lost, located by Demian Goos in Halle in 2025. Dated 30 November 1873, it includes Dedekind's proof of the countability of algebraic numbers. The recent reporting, the surviving documents, and speculation about motives should not be treated as interchangeable kinds of evidence.

This is not a twist revealing that everything was stolen. The contributions to uncountability and transfinite numbers still need to be considered individually. Nor should Dedekind exist merely to make Cantor a more interesting character. His name belongs beside the work he did.

A person treated unfairly may fail to treat somebody else fairly. That deserves plain acknowledgment, but it is no law conserving injury: being hurt neither makes later wrongdoing inevitable nor excuses it.

Understanding a life is harder than defending it without limit. We must leave room for the work, the suffering, and the person whose contribution went unacknowledged.

VII. Three Different Boundaries

Cantor, Gödel, Turing: placing the names together is tempting. Each makes a powerful expectation of completeness encounter a rigorous limit.

Cantor rules out listing all reals by the natural numbers. Gödel investigates effectively axiomatized formal systems strong enough for arithmetic. Turing asks whether a universal algorithm can decide all instances of a certain problem. These are not three statements of one theorem.

Diagonal constructions, coding, and self-reference form an important intellectual connection. But lists, proof systems, and programs are different objects. The independence of the continuum hypothesis is not simply the halting problem wearing another name.

A philosophical reader may find an invitation to humility: understand a powerful tool, and ask where its power applies. That is a reading of the mathematics, not a mathematical proof of every philosophical conclusion we would like to draw.

Above all, illness, persecution, and death must not become the corollary: those who discover limits are destroyed by them. Turing faced state persecution; Cantor endured recurrent illness. Their lives are not illustrations attached to theorems.

Limited methods coexist with the need for friends, rest, work, and care. To treat suffering as the certificate of an insight's depth is to impose another injury on the person we claim to remember.

VIII. No Summit Is Not No Road

Return to the staircase. At any stage, ask whether a larger set exists. Reaching a sufficiently lofty position will not change the answer to no.

We may think of other grand intellectual architectures: Plato's Good, Hegel's system. Comparison can begin there, but cannot end there. A theorem about cardinality does not directly refute the Forms or settle the question of history's destination.

Cantor himself had profound religious concerns and distinguished the mathematically transfinite from the absolutely infinite of theology. Casting him as the man who abolished every ultimate question would silence an important part of his own thought.

His work is closer to learning how to make a better measuring instrument than to discovering that nothing is possible. Countability, uncountability, power sets: each distinction supplies somewhere more secure to put a foot.

You need not possess everything before doing the piece in front of you. Nor must a reader master set theory before noticing the transformation: a vague forever has become something people can dispute, correct, and investigate together.

The staircase has no highest step. That does not mean you cannot climb a step today. Cantor's mathematics leaves us the first proposition; it does not demand that we accept the second.

IX. A Place to Sit

Cantor died in a psychiatric hospital in Halle on 6 January 1918, aged seventy-two. Recurrent illness marked his later life; war made living conditions harder. We can remember this without adding a voice-over in which infinity finally claims his sanity.

Hilbert's celebrated image of the paradise Cantor created belongs to the years after Cantor's death. It describes an opening in mathematics, not the daily circumstances of its maker. A discipline's eventual triumph cannot travel backward and keep a patient company.

At this series' imaginary bridge, Cantor still looks upward. His pen traces ℵ₀, ℵ₁ in the air. There is more room beyond them.

We need not give him the supposedly mad eyes of a man who has seen too much. He might simply be someone warming to an explanation, eager for another person to understand.

Chekhov comes a little closer. Hume puts away his backgammon. Turing holds a sheet of symbols; nobody asks him to reduce his life to a final moral.

Cantor can continue, or stop for a while. Someone will listen without requiring another astonishing theorem as the price.

The steps will not vanish while he rests. Neither does a person owe complete exhaustion to the road he opened.

There is more above. There can also be a chair here.
