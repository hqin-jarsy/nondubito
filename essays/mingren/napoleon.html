拿破仑，世界精神骑在马上

Napoleon, the World Spirit on Horseback

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、世界精神

1806年10月13日。耶拿。

黑格尔看见拿破仑骑马出城侦察。就在《精神现象学》接近完成的时候，战争来到了他生活的城市。他给朋友尼特哈默写信，称马上的皇帝为“世界灵魂”：一个人聚集在这一点上，却仿佛把手伸向了整个世界。

世界精神骑在马上。后来的人记住了这个画面。

从远处看，确实容易这样想。革命推倒的东西，拿破仑似乎正在重新组织起来：军队、行政、法典。观念不再只在书页上争论，它们变成了命令，变成了人们明天必须遵守的规则。

黑格尔并不是在信里宣布历史从此停止。绝对精神也不能简单换算成一个皇帝的帝国。但这个画面会诱惑我们：那么复杂的一段历史，好像终于有了一张脸，有一只握住缰绳的手。

问题也从这里开始。我们看见骑马的人时，还看不看得见被马蹄扬起的尘土遮住的人？

二、革命的儿子

1769年，拿破仑出生在科西嘉。前一年，热那亚把对这座岛的权利让给法国；法国随后以武力确立统治。这个孩子来到世上时，家乡刚刚换了统治者。

他的家庭属于当地小贵族，并非毫无门第。父亲获得法国对贵族身份的承认，帮助他进入军校。但出身于科西嘉的小贵族，与站在巴黎权力中心，毕竟是两回事。他说法语带着口音，早年的归属也曾在科西嘉与法国之间拉扯。

我们可以想到达芬奇、李白那些难以放进整齐格子的出身。不过，相似的是边缘位置带来的张力，不是三个人有同一份命运。

他选择了炮兵。数学、距离、地形，决定炮弹落在哪里。年轻军官先学会的，是怎样使一种力量准确地发生。

然后革命来了。1789年，旧制度的特权遭到冲击，《人权宣言》出现；到1793年，路易十六被处决。这不是几个月内一切整齐归零，而是一段不断翻动、也不断流血的岁月。

拿破仑不是革命的发动者，却抓住了革命和战争打开的道路。旧的晋升秩序松动了，军队需要能打仗的人。不能断言没有革命他必定止步于某一级军衔，但没有这场巨变，很难想象同样的上升速度。

1793年，土伦。年轻的炮兵军官在夺回港口的行动中崭露头角。1796年，他统率意大利方面军，接连取胜，名声迅速扩大。1798年，他远征埃及，随军还有一批学者与科学家。

埃及远征最终失败了，却留下复杂的学术遗产。法军在1799年发现罗塞塔石碑；石碑后来在法军投降后被英国接收，并不是拿破仑亲手带回法国的战利品。知识在战争中获得了材料，也不能让掠夺因此变得无辜。

1799年，雾月政变。拿破仑成为第一执政。三十岁，从炮兵军官走到了法国权力的中心。

革命拆掉了一些墙。他穿过墙的缺口，开始建自己的房子。

三、法典

1804年，法国《民法典》颁布，后来又以《拿破仑法典》之名广为人知。

想象两个人签一份契约。他们首先需要知道的，不该是谁祖上有爵位，而该是双方承担什么、承诺什么，出了纠纷到哪里讲理。一部通行的法典，就是要把这类日常关系放到相对共同的规则里。

它整理并巩固了革命以来的一部分变化：摆脱封建身份特权，保护财产，让契约和民事身份有统一的法律框架。不同地区不必永远被彼此冲突的旧习惯法隔开。这是实在的建设，不只是皇帝的宣传。

但走进同一部法典里的妻子，会发现自己并没有得到与丈夫一样的地位。家庭内部，服从仍被写进规则。再把目光移向殖民地，拿破仑政府在1802年维持或恢复奴隶制的政策，更直接撕开了普遍平等的承诺。

民事婚姻也不等于现代意义上的政教分离。拿破仑与教皇订立宗教协约，将教会重新纳入国家安排。他既继承革命，也挑选、约束和倒转革命留下的东西。

法典随法国势力进入比利时、莱茵地区、意大利等地，实施的方式和持续时间并不相同。有些地方在帝国退出后仍保留它，有些修改或废止它。不是军队走到哪里，同一套生活就完整复制到哪里。

想到秦始皇统一文字、度量衡和行政，会看见一种相似的雄心：让广大的地域使用共同的标准。但两段历史不是同一张图。秦的制度并非他一人凭空发明，拿破仑的法典也来自法学家、革命改革和漫长的法律传统。

帝国倒了，某些制度却没有跟着倒。这件事值得看。它说明一条规则可以脱离创建它的强人，进入后人的日常；不说明强人因此做的一切都有了理由。

今天法国的民法仍以这部法典为重要基础，却不是把1804年的每一条原封不动供起来。后人删除、修改、扩展权利。法典能够继续活着，也因为人们没有停止改它。

四、称帝

1804年12月2日，巴黎圣母院。教皇庇护七世在场，拿破仑自己戴上皇冠。

这不是临场从惊愕的教皇手里夺走皇冠的戏剧性突袭，而是事先安排过的仪式。正因为安排过，姿态才格外清楚：皇权需要祝圣，却不愿显得只是由教皇赐予。

革命曾砍下国王的头。现在，一位从革命中升起的人，把皇位留给了自己和自己的家族。旧名字改变了，权力向一个人聚拢的动作又出现了。

贝多芬听到拿破仑称帝的消息后怎样发怒，主要来自里斯多年后的回忆。保留下来的乐谱题名页上，“波拿巴”被用力涂去，纸张留下破损。我们不必再替这一页补一声撕裂，愤怒已经在纸上。

想到华盛顿，会看见另一种选择：胜利之后交出军事权力，后来又离开总统职位。那不是一幅完美自由的画像，奴隶制和排斥仍在；但权力可以交接，而不必随一个人的生命捆到底，这个区别很具体。

问题不是一部民法典里漏写了皇帝的名字。民法典本来就不是限制最高政治权力的完整宪法。问题在于，谁能够有效地对最高统治者说不？谁能公开反对他，而不先担心警察与审查？

法条可以让许多关系变得可预期。倘若决定规则的人又能压住质疑，纸上的平等就无法独自保护纸外的人。

那个自己戴上皇冠的动作，让我们同时看见了才能与危险：他能组织庞大的世界，也越来越不愿让世界离开他的手。

五、莫斯科

1812年，拿破仑向俄国进军。这不是只有法国人的军队，而是一支从欧洲多地集合起来的庞大联军。计入后续部队，参战总数常以约六十万计；不是六十万人一起走到了莫斯科。

道路先开始吃人。疾病、缺粮、掉队，早在严寒来到之前，就使队伍不断缩小。地图上一条线，到了地上，是走不完的路，是必须喂饱的马，是运不过来的面包。

俄军不断退却，也确实作战。9月7日，博罗季诺的激战留下大量伤亡。拿破仑得以继续前进，却没有摧毁俄军，更没有得到他想要的政治结局。

9月，他进入莫斯科。那时俄国的政治首都是圣彼得堡；莫斯科却有古老而沉重的象征分量。大部分居民已经撤走，城市随后被大火吞噬，起火与蔓延的责任不能用一句“俄国人烧了自己的首都”说尽。

他到了。可是，抵达并没有兑现地图许下的东西。

沙皇没有求和。

10月，拿破仑离开莫斯科。接下来的撤退，饥饿、寒冷、战斗与追击交织在一起。许多人死去，许多人被俘，许多人再也无法归队。胜负表上的一个失败，落到路上，是一个个回不了家的人。

想到卡夫卡的《城堡》，会生出一种遥远的回声：K到不了那个仿佛握着最后确认的地方；拿破仑到了，却发现占领一座城并不能迫使另一个人交出意志。这是阅读中的比较，不是小说在预言战史。

命令能够推动军队，不能命令粮食凭空长出来，不能命令距离缩短，更不能保证对方按你的剧本认输。世界没有因为他握着缰绳，就只剩下那一只手。

六、滑铁卢

1814年，联军进入巴黎。拿破仑退位，被送往地中海的厄尔巴岛。

1815年，他离开厄尔巴，3月回到法国。士兵重新聚到他身边，路易十八出逃。那个仿佛已经结束的故事，突然又翻开一页。

百日王朝。这个名字里已经藏着后来人的倒计时。

6月18日，滑铁卢。威灵顿统率的联军与布吕歇尔率领的普军击败了他。不是一位英雄单独败给另一位英雄，是许多部队、许多人的行动，把这一次返回关上了。

随后是圣赫勒拿。南大西洋上的火山岛。与厄尔巴不同，这一次，他离欧洲太远了。

1821年5月5日，拿破仑在那里去世，五十一岁。当年的尸检记录更支持胃癌这一解释；围绕砷的传说不能和这些证据不加区分地并列。

亚历山大三十二岁死在巴比伦，身后帝国被继承者争夺。拿破仑在四十多岁就失去了帝国，后来死于流放。两个人的时间线不同，远离故土的结尾也不是同一种失败。

他们都留下了超过自己身体的影响。拿破仑尤其留下了一部后来仍被使用、争论和改写的法典。但一本法典，并不能把他的一生结算成净收益。

七、他和每个人

把秦始皇、华盛顿、亚历山大和拿破仑并放，会看见四种不同的政治遗产：行政与标准，宪政和交接，征服后交错的文化，成文法与国家机器。

这些遗产并不是四个伟人各自在纸上画好，再由后世照着施工。无数官员、士兵、劳动者、法学家和反对者在其中行动。有人继承，有人反抗，有人把留下来的东西改成了创建者未必肯认的样子。

一项制度为什么活得久？可能因为有用，因为人们离不开，也可能因为掌握强制力量的人不准它改变。活得久本身，不是正当性的证明。

拿一份后来修订的民法典来看，这件事就容易懂了：财产与契约的框架还在，妻子却不必永远停在1804年安排给她的位置。我们珍惜的，不只是制度保存下来的能力，也包括人改变制度的能力。

杜甫留下诗，贝多芬留下音乐，司马迁留下历史。帝王留下的制度同样可能超过个人的寿命。但不能因此把人叫作制度的耗材，仿佛作品、法典或帝国才是值得保存的目的。

恰好相反。一个制度值得被保存，是因为它还能服务其中具体的人；当它伤害人时，人的反对不该被算作需要清除的故障。

征服者会死。留下来的东西，还要由活着的人重新判断。

八、英雄变成暴君

再看那张被涂破的乐谱题名页。

贝多芬曾在波拿巴身上看见某种希望。称帝使那个形象裂开了。但他的看法并非此后永远只有一个颜色，《英雄交响曲》“纪念一位伟人”的措辞，也不能被锁成唯一的政治讣告。

更值得留下的，是那个拒绝继续照旧命名的动作。你曾经相信一个人代表自由，不等于你必须替他后来做的每件事辩护。作品可以离开曾经寄托过它的人，听众也可以不再接受同一张英雄画像。

于是，拿破仑又与黑格尔站到一起。不是因为一位哲学家证明了历史结束，另一位皇帝照着他的书作战。是因为他们会共同唤起一种危险的诱惑：既然我已经看见整体，剩下的阻力是不是都可以被解释为通往整体的代价？

理解冲突，不等于替冲突中的牺牲者签字。组织法律，不等于获得无限征服的许可。辩证思考与军事能力，都不能替一个人自动取得最后裁决的资格。

拿破仑不是戴上皇冠后就失去了所有别的选择。说他“只能继续征服”，反而替他的选择免了责。胜利会使退让越来越难承认，难承认却不是不可能。

这才是那种力量真正令人不安的地方：成功过许多次，就开始把下一次成功当作世界欠自己的东西。工具很强，不等于终点已经在那里等你。

九、火山岩上

在这个系列想象的桥头，我们给拿破仑留一个位置。可是先不让他到来。

先把画面停在圣赫勒拿。火山岩，海风，远得看不见的欧洲。一个曾经被称作世界灵魂的人，如今被限制在一座岛上。

这不是我们知道他某一天确实摆出的姿势，而是一个用来回望他的画面：他站着，手里没有缰绳，海面不接受命令。

另一边的大陆上，人们仍然结婚、继承、签约、走进法庭。法典继续参与他们的生活。有些人从中得到保护，有些人仍在为改变不平等的条文而争取。

还有一些人没有回来。他们留在远征的道路上，留在被战火穿过的城镇里。不能因为我们终于看见孤岛上的寂寞，就忘了那些没有获得一座雕像的生命。

拿破仑的帝国已经结束，争论没有结束。他不是纯粹的法典，也不是纯粹的一顶皇冠。留下建设与留下伤害，可以是同一个人的两件事，不能拿其中一件把另一件擦掉。

桥头上，我们可以让他与黑格尔谈一谈，与贝多芬谈一谈。但还应给那些被宏大故事略去的人留出说话的位置。

海风吹过来。一个人的手终于握不住整个世界。

法典还在。更重要的是，后来的人还在，仍然有权决定它应该成为怎样的法典。

Han Qin (秦汉) · @hqinjarsy · 名人系列

I. The World Spirit

October 13, 1806. Jena.

Hegel saw Napoleon riding out to reconnoitre. The Phenomenology of Spirit was nearing completion, and war had arrived in the city where its author lived. Writing to his friend Niethammer, Hegel called the emperor the “world-soul.” Here was one man, concentrated at one point, who seemed to reach out across the world.

The world spirit on horseback. It is an image with a remarkable afterlife.

From a distance, you can see its appeal. Napoleon appeared to be organizing what the Revolution had unsettled: armies, administration, law. Ideas were no longer arguing on the page. They had become orders and rules that people would have to live by tomorrow.

The letter does not announce that history has stopped. Nor can Hegel’s Absolute Spirit simply be identified with an emperor’s dominion. But the scene offers a temptation: a bewildering stretch of history suddenly seems to have a face, and a hand holding the reins.

When we look at the rider, can we still see the people disappearing into the dust behind his horse?

II. Son of the Revolution

Napoleon was born in Corsica in 1769. The previous year Genoa had ceded its rights over the island to France, which then established control by force. His birthplace had only just acquired a new ruler.

His family belonged to the local minor nobility. Recognition of that status by France helped secure his education: he was not a penniless commoner excluded from every advantage of birth. Yet a Corsican noble family stood a long way from the centre of Parisian power. He spoke French with an accent, and his early allegiances were pulled between Corsica and France.

Leonardo’s illegitimacy and the uncertainties surrounding Li Bai’s origins come to mind. What these lives share is the tension of not fitting comfortably into a category, not an identical destiny.

Napoleon trained in artillery. Mathematics, distance, terrain: calculations decided where a projectile would land. Before he organized countries, he learned to make a particular kind of force arrive precisely where he wanted it.

Then came the Revolution. Privilege came under attack in 1789, alongside the Declaration of the Rights of Man; Louis XVI was executed in 1793. The old order did not disappear cleanly within a few months. Change kept changing direction, and blood kept being shed.

Napoleon had not started the Revolution, but he seized the openings created by upheaval and war. Old patterns of promotion loosened. Armies needed effective commanders. We cannot know the highest rank he might otherwise have reached, but his extraordinary ascent is hard to imagine without that rupture.

Toulon, 1793: the young artillery officer distinguished himself in the operation to retake the port. Italy, 1796: a succession of victories expanded his reputation. Egypt, 1798: an expedition carrying scholars and scientists as well as soldiers.

The Egyptian campaign ultimately failed, leaving a complicated scholarly legacy. French soldiers discovered the Rosetta Stone in 1799; after the French surrender, it passed into British hands. Napoleon did not bring it home to France. Scholarship gained new materials, but that gain did not make conquest or appropriation innocent.

In 1799 the coup of Brumaire made him First Consul. Thirty years old: an artillery officer had reached the centre of French power.

The Revolution had knocked holes in the walls. He went through one and began building a house of his own.

III. The Code

The French Civil Code appeared in 1804. It would also become known, around the world, as the Napoleonic Code.

Imagine two people making a contract. The first question should not be whose ancestor held a title. It should be what each has promised, what each owes, and where they can take a dispute. A common code puts such everyday encounters within a relatively shared framework.

The Code consolidated important revolutionary changes: the removal of feudal status privileges, protection of property, a framework for contracts and civil identity. Regions need not remain indefinitely divided by conflicting customary laws. This was substantial institutional work, not merely imperial publicity.

But a married woman entering the same legal framework found that she did not enter on her husband’s terms. The family remained hierarchical. Across the sea, the government’s policies maintaining or restoring colonial slavery in 1802 tore an even more direct hole in any promise of universal equality.

Civil marriage did not mean a modern separation of church and state, either. Napoleon’s Concordat with the pope brought the Church into a new state arrangement. He inherited the Revolution selectively: consolidating some changes, containing others, reversing others still.

The Code travelled with French power into Belgium, the Rhineland, Italy and elsewhere. How it was introduced, and how long it lasted, varied. Some places retained it after the empire withdrew; others altered or abandoned it. An army’s arrival did not reproduce an entire society in its own image.

Qin Shi Huang’s administrative standards, measures and script offer a comparison: the ambition to make a large territory operate through common forms. But neither ruler invented his institutions alone. Napoleon’s code also belonged to jurists, revolutionary reform and a long legal inheritance.

An empire can fall while some of its arrangements survive. A rule may detach itself from a strongman and become part of ordinary life. That is worth noticing. It does not justify everything the strongman did.

French civil law still rests importantly on the Code, but not by preserving every provision of 1804 untouched. Later generations amended it, removed rules and extended rights. It stayed alive partly because people refused to leave it alone.

IV. The Crown

December 2, 1804. Notre-Dame de Paris. Pope Pius VII was present; Napoleon placed the crown on his own head.

This was not an improvised snatching of a crown from a startled pope. The ceremony had been negotiated in advance. Its planning makes the gesture more, not less, revealing: imperial authority wanted consecration without appearing merely to receive its power from the Church.

The Revolution had beheaded a king. A man who rose through that revolution now established an imperial throne for himself and his family. The name had changed; power was gathering around one person again.

The story of Beethoven’s fury at Napoleon’s assumption of imperial power comes largely from Ferdinand Ries’s later recollections. On a surviving score’s title page, Bonaparte’s name was forcefully erased, damaging the paper. We need not supply the sound of an entire page being torn out. The wound in the paper is enough.

Washington suggests another possibility: relinquishing military command after victory, and later leaving the presidency. This was no flawless picture of freedom; slavery and exclusion remained. Yet the transfer of power, rather than fastening it permanently to one man, was a real difference.

The problem was not that the Civil Code forgot to list the emperor among its citizens. A civil code is not a complete constitutional restraint on supreme political power. The practical question was who could effectively refuse him. Who could oppose him publicly without first thinking about police and censorship?

Legal rules can make many relationships more predictable. But if the person directing the rules can suppress challenges, equality on paper cannot by itself protect people off the page.

The self-coronation shows talent and danger together: a man capable of organizing an immense world, increasingly unwilling to let that world out of his hands.

V. Moscow

In 1812 Napoleon invaded Russia with a multinational army drawn from across Europe. Counting later forces, the campaign involved roughly six hundred thousand men in many estimates. That does not mean six hundred thousand marched together into Moscow.

The roads began consuming the army first. Disease, hunger and straggling reduced its strength long before the worst cold. A line on a map becomes something else on the ground: distances that feet must cover, horses that must be fed, bread that cannot reach the column.

Russian forces retreated, but they also fought. The terrible battle of Borodino on September 7 let Napoleon continue his advance without destroying the Russian army. It did not deliver the political outcome he needed.

In September he entered Moscow. Russia’s political capital was then Saint Petersburg, but Moscow carried enormous historical and symbolic weight. Most inhabitants had left. Fires devastated the city; responsibility for their origins and spread cannot be settled by saying that Russia burned its own capital.

He had arrived. Arrival had not delivered what the map seemed to promise.

The tsar did not ask for peace.

Napoleon left Moscow in October. The retreat entangled hunger, cold, fighting and pursuit. Men died, were captured, disappeared from their units. One defeat in a military table became, on the road, innumerable people who would never get home.

Kafka’s Castle offers a distant echo. K. cannot reach the place that seems to hold the final confirmation he needs. Napoleon reaches his destination and discovers that occupying a city cannot force another person to surrender his will. This is a reader’s comparison, not a novel predicting a campaign.

Orders can move an army. They cannot order food into existence, shorten the distances or make the enemy accept the allotted part in your story. Holding the reins did not reduce the world to the hand that held them.

VI. Waterloo

In 1814 allied armies entered Paris. Napoleon abdicated and went into exile on Elba, in the Mediterranean.

In 1815 he left Elba and returned to France in March. Soldiers rallied to him. Louis XVIII fled. A story that seemed finished suddenly turned another page.

The Hundred Days: posterity’s countdown is already hidden in the name.

On June 18, at Waterloo, the allied army under Wellington and the Prussians under Blücher defeated him. Not one solitary hero overcoming another, but the actions of many units and many people closing this return to power.

Then Saint Helena, a volcanic island in the South Atlantic. Unlike Elba, it put Europe almost impossibly far away.

He died there on May 5, 1821, aged fifty-one. The contemporary post-mortem reports support an explanation of advanced stomach cancer; arsenic stories should not be placed beside that evidence as though all explanations had equal weight.

Alexander died at thirty-two in Babylon, leaving an empire fought over by his successors. Napoleon lost his empire in his forties and died later in exile. Their timelines differ, and dying far from home does not make their endings the same kind of defeat.

Both left effects exceeding their own lives. Napoleon particularly left a code that others continued to use, dispute and rewrite. But a code cannot settle the account of a life as an unambiguous net gain.

VII. Napoleon and Everyone Else

Place Qin Shi Huang, Washington, Alexander and Napoleon alongside one another. Four different inheritances appear: administration and standards; constitutional government and transfers of power; cultures intermingling after conquest; codified law and the machinery of a state.

These were not four plans drawn by four great men and simply executed by posterity. Officials, soldiers, workers, jurists and opponents acted within them. Some inherited; some resisted; some transformed what remained into arrangements their founders might never have approved.

Why does an institution last? It may be useful, difficult to replace, or protected by people with the force to prevent change. Longevity alone is not a certificate of justice.

An amended civil code makes the point tangible. A framework for property and contracts can remain without a wife having to stay in the position assigned to her in 1804. What matters is not only the institution’s capacity to survive. It is people’s capacity to change it.

Du Fu left poetry, Beethoven music, Sima Qian history. Political institutions can also outlive their makers. But that does not make human beings expendable vessels for poems, codes or empires, as though the creation were the real end and the person merely its packaging.

The relation runs the other way. An institution deserves preservation insofar as it serves the concrete people who live within it. When it harms them, their objections must not be treated as faults to be eliminated.

Conquerors die. What they leave behind remains for the living to judge.

VIII. The Hero Becomes a Tyrant

Return to that damaged title page.

Beethoven had invested hope in Bonaparte. Imperial power fractured that image. Yet his views did not thereafter have only one colour, and the Eroica’s reference to the memory of a great man cannot be locked into a single political obituary.

The important gesture is a refusal to keep using the same name in the same way. Believing that someone represented freedom does not oblige you to defend every later act. A work can depart from the person who once carried its hopes; listeners can refuse the old heroic portrait.

Here Napoleon stands beside Hegel again. Not because the philosopher proved history complete and the emperor fought from his instructions. They suggest a shared temptation for us as readers: if I believe I can see the whole, may every remaining objection be classified as a cost of reaching it?

Understanding conflict does not authorize us to sign on behalf of those it sacrifices. Organizing law grants no unlimited licence to conquer. Neither dialectical intelligence nor military ability automatically confers the right to a final verdict.

Napoleon did not lose every alternative the moment he put on the crown. Saying he could only go on conquering would excuse his choices. Repeated victory can make retreat harder to acknowledge. Harder is not impossible.

That is the unsettling power of success: after winning often enough, a person may begin to treat the next victory as something the world owes him. A powerful instrument is no guarantee that its imagined endpoint exists.

IX. On Volcanic Rock

At this series’ imagined bridgehead, let us leave Napoleon a place. But do not bring him there just yet.

Hold the image at Saint Helena: volcanic rock, sea wind, Europe beyond sight. Someone once called the world-soul is confined to an island.

This is not a documented pose on a particular day. It is an image for looking back at his life: he stands without reins in his hands, facing a sea that takes no orders.

On the distant continent, people still marry, inherit, sign contracts and enter courtrooms. The Code remains part of their lives. Some find protection in it; others struggle to change its unequal provisions.

Other people never returned. They remain along the roads of campaigns and in towns through which war passed. The loneliness of one famous exile must not make us forget lives that were never awarded a monument.

His empire is over; the argument is not. Napoleon is neither a code alone nor a crown alone. One person can leave useful institutions and grievous injuries. Neither legacy erases the other.

At the bridgehead he might speak with Hegel, and with Beethoven. But there must also be room for the people that grand historical pictures leave out.

The wind moves over the rock. One man’s hand can no longer hold the world.

The Code remains. More importantly, other people remain, with the right to decide what kind of code it ought to become.
