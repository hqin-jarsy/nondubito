蒯因，没有那条线

Quine, There Is No Such Line

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、两个教条

你说一件事错了，人们会请你拿出证据。你说一件事根本不配接受讨论，往往只需要给它贴一个标签。蒯因让我们停下来看看贴标签的那只手：你凭什么这样分类？

1951年，他发表《经验主义的两个教条》。文章不长，问题却一路追到了现代经验主义习以为常的地方。有些句子被认为仅凭词义就是真的，另一些则要由事实裁定；两类之间，真有一道性质截然不同的界线吗？

另一个问题与此相连：一句关于世界的话，能否独自带着一份经验清单，只要逐项核对，就能决定它的意义与真假？还是说，我们每次检查一句话，都已经借用了许多别的话？

这些问题不是从门外砸进来的。蒯因受过严格的逻辑训练，也深受卡尔纳普影响。他不是嫌分析太细，转身投奔神秘；他是在分析最有把握的地方，请分析再往前走一步。

论文激起的争论延续至今。说他用二十几页证明了所有界线都不存在，反而会抹去这场争论。批评有力量，不等于对手已经没有话可说，更不等于世界从此不用区分。

这篇文章的题目“没有那条线”，最好带着一个问题来读：你以为不必再解释的那条线，究竟靠什么站住？我们先别急着喊它不存在，也别急着拿它去划掉别人。

二、单身汉和桌上的猫

“单身汉没有结婚。”听见这句话，我们大概不会拿着问卷去采访一千个人。通常用法已经把答案放在词里。所谓分析命题，最容易从这样的小例子进入。

“猫在桌上。”这就不同了。你把“猫”和“桌子”解释得再清楚，也不能据此知道厨房里正在发生什么。最好推门看看；也许桌上只有一条盘起来的围巾。

于是我们仿佛有了两只抽屉：一只装靠意思成立的话，一只装要看世界才知道的话。把句子放好，哲学的房间立刻整齐起来。整齐很诱人，尤其当房间已经乱了很久。

但康德的区分不能简化成“综合命题都来自经验”。他最重要的问题之一，恰恰是先天综合判断如何可能。他认为有些知识增加了内容，却又不只是把观察到的事情汇总一下。

分析与综合、先天与后天、必然与偶然，也不是三种说法指同一条线。维特根斯坦关于可说与不可说的讨论，更不能直接塞进这两只抽屉。把不同哲学家的问题画成一张图，图会清楚，问题却可能丢了。

蒯因质疑的是两类真理之间被赋予的根本鸿沟。他并不需要否认我们日常能够区别查词典和看厨房。他问的是：这种方便的区别，够不够承担我们后来交给它的全部哲学重量？

三、词典还没有回答

我们很自然地说：因为词典把“单身汉”解释成“未婚男子”。问题似乎结束了。蒯因却在这里继续问：编词典的人凭什么把这两个说法放在一起？

编者不是随手立法，而是在记录他们认为已经存在的语言用法。因此，词典条目通常依赖“这两个说法意思相同”的判断，不能再拿这个条目，当作同义关系最终的根据。

这里的困难不是“词典靠观察编成，所以词典不可靠”。词典当然好用。困难在于，我们请它解释同义，它交还给我们的答案，却已经用上了同义。钥匙很好，只是未必能打开正在问的这把锁。

那么，换一种办法：如果两个说法可以互换而不改变真假，它们是不是就同义？想象柜子里恰好所有红杯子都缺了口，所有缺口杯子也都是红的。在这个柜子里，两种说法找出的杯子完全一样，“红”却没有因此变成“缺口”的意思。

光是指到同一批东西还不够。若再加一句“两者必然一样”，就得说明这里的“必然”是什么意思。要是它的解释又是“凭意思就能知道”，我们便回到了出发的地方。蒯因追的，是这笔被悄悄借用的账。

他并不拒绝一切定义。我们可以明确约定一个新符号，用来缩写一串表达式；这种关系是当场设立的。他不肯轻易接受的是：几个清楚的约定，已经替整个语言世界划好了一道永远不会动的边界。

四、猫后面的那张网

再回到厨房。你看见桌上有一团毛，于是说猫在那里。这个判断很平常，但它不是光秃秃地面对世界：你相信自己没有看花眼，灯光没有骗你，也相信自己认得那只猫。

走近才发现，那是一只毛绒玩具。你会改掉“猫在桌上”，不必同时推翻光学、逻辑和自己的全部童年。大多数修正就是这样局部发生的，生活才不至于每天重新开机。

可是，经验与预期不合时，究竟改哪里，并非由单独一句观察报告替我们选好。也许认错了物体，也许仪器坏了，也许原来的理论确实需要修改。一项测试通常连着背景知识和辅助假设。

信念之网这个比喻，抓住的就是这种牵连。1951年的论文把整体性说得很强；不必把它理解成检查水壶是否烧开，就得让宇宙中的每条知识一起重新应试。蒯因后来也对这样过强的整体说法作了调整。

靠近中心的信念，改起来会牵动很多别处，因此我们通常先保留它们。但修改代价大，不等于它们拥有永远不得检查的特权。蒯因愿意把问题推到逻辑那里，正说明他问的是整个安排，而不只是一次看错。

这也不是给固执的人发许可证：既然哪里都能改，我就永远不认错。修补后的说法仍须承受经验，比较解释力、简洁性和改动的代价。一张把所有反例都挡在外面的网，很可能已经不再帮助我们看世界。

五、卡尔纳普没有退场

把这段历史写成蒯因打倒一群维特根斯坦的追随者，会省去许多麻烦，也会省掉真正值得读的争论。卡尔纳普有自己的工作，不是一位替别人看守门槛的人。

他关心怎样建立清楚的语言框架：规定使用什么符号、接受什么规则，然后区分哪些结论依据规则成立，哪些还要查经验。在一个选定的框架里，这个区别可以很明确。

选择哪套框架，又是另一个层次的问题。它是否方便、是否有用，能不能帮助科学工作？承认框架可以更换，并不使卡尔纳普惊慌。他不必认为自己每写一条规则，都发现了一条天上的永恒刻痕。

蒯因仍然不满意。他怀疑，把修改框架与修改框架中的信念分得如此根本，是否说明了真实的认识活动。双方不是一个懂得变化、一个死抱教条；他们争的是，变化之中这个区分到底解释了什么。

格赖斯和斯特劳森等批评者也提醒：没有找到一种完全不借助相关概念的定义，不等于日常所作的区别毫无根据。否则，我们使用的许多基本概念，都会被同一把扫帚扫出去。蒯因的要求本身也值得追问。

这里仍能听见苏格拉底的回声：你说你知道，请再说清楚一点。但真正的对话不该只允许蒯因问别人。不然我们刚刚拆掉一座讲台，又立起一座更高的，让新的名字替问题收尾。

六、休谟去下棋了

把蒯因和休谟放在一起，很容易听见一种相近的语气：你经常这样想，并不自动说明你已经替它找到了不可动摇的根据。熟悉会让问题安静下来，却不一定已经回答了问题。

休谟追问我们凭什么从已经观察到的事情，走向还没有发生的事情。太阳过去升起，不是一份用纯粹推理签好的合同，保证明天一定如此。这并不是说人间没有原因，或者推理从此毫无用处。

蒯因的具体问题不同。他面对的是意义、经验与理论之间怎样分工。更有意思的是，休谟自己也区分观念关系与事实问题；蒯因质疑的边界，和这份传统恰好有关。他们不是隔了两百年复述同一篇论文。

相近之处在于，地基受到追问以后，两个人都没有因此停止生活。休谟写过，吃饭、交谈、下双陆棋会把他从哲学的困顿中带回来。棋盘不是反驳怀疑论的证明，却是一个活人仍在世界里的事实。

蒯因也没有烧掉逻辑书。他继续研究、授课、修改论证。逻辑不必先领取一张绝不可能出错的保证书，我们才能用它认真工作；但认真工作也不是一句“反正都会错”就可以交差。

这份平静不是轻率。它需要同时握住两件事：没有最终担保，以及仍然需要给出理由。少掉前者，人会变得武断；少掉后者，谦逊就容易沦为逃避判断的漂亮名字。

七、不要替他们排好队

在这一百零八种人生里，蒯因让我们重新看看自己的阅读习惯。我们很喜欢给每个人留一个位置：柏拉图在洞外，康德守边界，黑格尔把一切收入体系，维特根斯坦站在沉默旁边。

这些标记能帮我们记住一条路，却不能替我们读完一个人。柏拉图没有只写一个洞穴，康德的限制也不是简单筑墙。把黑格尔概括成一个什么都能吞下去的胃，很快就不必再听他如何论证。

蒯因可以帮助我们怀疑地图画得太满，但他并没有用一篇论文驳倒这些人的全部问题。他的整体论也不等于本系列关于人的价值的判断。两种思考互相照亮，不需要先被宣布为同一件事。

真正值得带走的是一种阅读动作：每当一个概念顺利安置了太多人，再问问它漏掉了谁。说一个人“理性”，是否就不再听他的恐惧？说他“脆弱”，是否连他的判断也一起取消了？

这已经是我们把阅读带回生活的尝试，不是从蒯因的逻辑直接推出的伦理定理。一个分类会出错，也不能单独证明我们应当怎样对待别人。责任还要由具体的理由、伤害和关系来承担。

不过，愿意重新检查分类，至少能腾出一点位置：让对方不只以我们写好的那个名字出现。地图还可以拿着，只是不要拿地图挡住站在面前的人。

八、三个问题，不是一个答案

我们也会想到海森堡。物理学曾让一些看似稳固的直觉失去把握：在量子理论里，位置与动量的统计散布受到不确定关系约束，不能让两者同时任意收窄。这不是简单说仪器还不够精密。

但它也不是一句“观察者与世界没有区别”，更不证明所有概念界线都是幻觉。量子理论有自己的数学、实验和适用问题。若拿它给任何哲学主张盖章，恰好把应当细查的区别再次抹平了。

蒯因问一个判断怎样连着其他判断；不确定关系说的是特定物理量之间的限制。两者都可以使人反省一种过于轻松的确信，但触动了相近的感受，不意味着已经证明同一个命题。

西田几多郎又从另一个方向进入。在他关于纯粹经验的思考中，我们后来才作出的主客区分，不应被匆忙当成全部经验的原始样子。先是投入地听见音乐，再说“我正在听一个声音”，这可以作为进入问题的例子。

这同样不是蒯因把自然科学视作认识工作一部分的立场换了日语说一遍。西田、蒯因、海森堡，各自的方法和所要回答的问题并不相同。愿意把他们放在一起，也要愿意把他们重新分开。

比较最有用的时刻，不是三个人终于齐声替我作证，而是我发现原来有三种不同的困难。于是下一次说“这当然是两回事”或者“这根本是一回事”以前，我会稍稍停一下。

九、逻辑学家也会旅行

蒯因的文字确实有一种不慌不忙的劲头。他可以把对方最放心的一句话拿起来，翻到背面，再请对方看看。读者有时会觉得，没有发生什么戏剧，房间的家具却已经挪了位置。

走出论证，生活里的蒯因又很开阔。哈佛同事记得他喜欢交往、旅行、语言和地理，也有人谈起他含蓄的幽默。他的好奇心不只在黑板上寻找去处，还会沿着地图上的地名走出去。

1908年出生于俄亥俄州阿克伦，1930年来到哈佛，他此后很长的人生都与这所学校相连。1932至1933年的欧洲之行让他接触维也纳学派、卡尔纳普和塔尔斯基。这些相遇进入了他后来追问的方式。

旅行提供另一种尺度。一句话在自己的语言里那么顺手，到了别处，别人会从哪里开始理解？不同语言怎样接上同一个世界？这也是他后来持续研究的问题。我们不必把哲学归因于旅途，却可以听见两种好奇心在一个人身上相遇。

课堂又把他带回另一种耐心：怎样让初学者走过一段推理，而不只是站在结论前面点头。他的逻辑教材与教学相互生长。1978年退休后，他仍继续写作；早年的著名论文并没有替后来几十年的思考关上门。

读到这里，最吸引我的不是把别人驳倒的胜利。是一个人愿意反复回到一句话旁边，看看它究竟说了什么。这样细的工作也能做成很长的一生；窗外有旅途，桌上还有一个没有问完的问题。

十、桥上的粉笔

2000年12月25日，蒯因去世，九十二岁。现在，让他走向本系列那座想象中的桥。书还在，争论也还在。桥上的相遇，不需要等待争论终于分出了最后的胜负。

在本系列虚构的桥头，他来到大家中间。休谟收起双陆棋，苏格拉底让出半步，康德仍然有问题要问。没有人因为蒯因到了，就必须放下自己的书，承认一切讨论已经结束。

桥面上有我们画过的许多线。老师与学生，影响与反对，爱人和同行。苏格拉底与柏拉图之间的一根，萨特与波伏瓦之间的一根。它们帮助我们记住故事，也可能把故事里的人捆得太紧。

蒯因蹲下来，看看粉笔印：这条线是在记录一次真实的相遇，还是在说这个人永远只能站在另一人旁边？如果发现新材料，愿不愿意改？他问得具体，并没有挥手把所有关系都宣布不存在。

波伏瓦也可以反问：有些线保护一个人说不，有些线却不许她走出去，难道因为都是人画的，就可以一样擦掉？同意与强迫之间的区别，不能用“分类都能修改”轻轻带过。

蒯因得留在对话里。提出一个重要的批评，不会使提问者从此免受提问；他不必拥有我们替他准备的完美回答。桥上如果只能有一个人最后说话，它依然只是一间换了主持人的教室。

也许最后，大家没有把桥擦成一片空白。他们留下该留下的标记，修掉一处太武断的箭头，给一条不确定的联系加上问号。远处还有人走来，不能因为图已经画满，就说没有他的位置。

蒯因把粉笔递回来。不是所有线都该消失；只是下一条线落下去以前，我们得知道自己在做什么。它帮助我们看见了什么，又会让谁从我们的眼睛里消失？

I. Two Dogmas

Tell people that a claim is wrong and they may ask for evidence. Tell them that it does not qualify for discussion and sometimes a label will suffice. Quine makes us look at the hand applying the label. What authorizes this division?

In 1951 he published “Two Dogmas of Empiricism.” One target was a fundamental separation between truths grounded in meaning and truths dependent on fact. The other was the idea that each meaningful statement could, individually, be reduced to immediate experience.

Imagine every sentence arriving with its own inspection sheet. Check the appropriate experiences, tick the boxes, and its credentials are settled. Quine asks whether we can inspect any sentence without quietly borrowing assistance from a great many others.

This challenge came from inside the workshop. He was a formidable logician, deeply influenced by Carnap. He was not abandoning analysis because it demanded too much care. He was asking analysis to examine a tool it normally took for granted.

The resulting dispute remains a dispute. Twenty-four pages did not establish that all distinctions are illusions. Treating the essay as a demolition after which nobody could answer would make the history tidier and the philosophy considerably less interesting.

“There is no such line,” the title of this portrait, should therefore carry a question. Where is the boundary you say needs no further explanation? Before using it to exclude someone else's question, show us how it works.

II. Bachelors and the Kitchen Table

Bachelors are unmarried. We do not ordinarily establish this by interviewing a thousand bachelors. The familiar meaning already appears to settle it. Such examples offer the easiest entrance to what philosophers call an analytic statement.

The cat is on the table. That takes something else. Explain cats and tables as carefully as you like: you still need to look in the kitchen. Perhaps the curled object there is a scarf, not a sleeping animal.

Two drawers suggest themselves, one for truths supplied by meanings, another for truths requiring an encounter with the world. The philosophical room begins to look reassuringly orderly. Order is especially seductive after a long period of confusion.

Yet Kant did not equate every synthetic judgment with knowledge obtained from experience. Synthetic a priori knowledge was central to his project: knowledge extending our understanding without merely collecting observations. Leave that out and we have misplaced the philosopher before meeting Quine.

Analytic and synthetic, a priori and a posteriori, necessary and contingent are not interchangeable pairs. Nor is Wittgenstein's distinction between saying and showing another name for these drawers. A convenient chart can quietly exchange somebody's problem for a different one.

Quine need not deny that checking a dictionary differs practically from checking a kitchen. He challenges the fundamental gulf assigned to two kinds of truth. Can a serviceable everyday contrast carry all the philosophical weight subsequently loaded onto it?

III. The Dictionary Has Not Finished the Job

The dictionary defines a bachelor as an unmarried man. That sounds decisive until we ask why the lexicographer paired those expressions. Usually the entry records an existing judgment about usage; it does not create that judgment from nothing.

The difficulty is not that dictionaries rely on observation and must therefore be unreliable. They are useful. But when asked to explain synonymy, this dictionary entry already presupposes it. We have borrowed precisely the relationship we promised to explain.

What if synonyms are expressions we can exchange without changing truth? Imagine a cupboard where all the red cups happen to be chipped and all the chipped cups happen to be red. Both descriptions pick out exactly the same collection.

Red does not therefore mean chipped. Agreement in what expressions apply to is weaker than sameness of meaning. This is more troublesome than the practical impossibility of testing every sentence: the proposed test may establish the wrong kind of agreement.

Add that the expressions must necessarily agree, and another bill comes due. What does necessarily mean here? If its explanation appeals to truth guaranteed by meaning, we have returned to the very notion whose credentials were being investigated.

Quine does allow explicit stipulations introducing new abbreviations. We can create a symbol for an expression and say exactly what we are doing. His resistance concerns turning such clear local arrangements into a settled account of an absolute boundary throughout language.

IV. The Web Behind the Cat

Back in the kitchen, a furry shape produces an ordinary judgment. Less visibly, that judgment relies on background assumptions: your eyesight is working, the lighting is adequate, and you recognize the animal. The sentence does not face the world entirely alone.

Closer inspection reveals a stuffed toy. You revise the cat report without discarding optics, arithmetic, or your childhood. Most repairs are local. Otherwise getting breakfast would require reopening every scientific argument before the kettle could be judged to have boiled.

Still, a disappointing prediction does not identify its own culprit. The object may have been misidentified, an instrument may have failed, or a theory may need revision. Testing ordinarily involves a claim together with assumptions supporting its application.

The web of belief captures this dependence. The 1951 essay states holism forcefully; Quine later moderated its sweeping formulation. We should not turn every small observation into an examination that every belief in the universe must sit simultaneously.

Central commitments are costly to change because much else depends on them. That is different from granting them permanent exemption from examination. Quine's willingness to consider even logic shows how far he presses the question of revisability.

None of this licenses refusing every unwelcome result. A repaired account still owes us empirical adequacy, explanatory economy, and reasons for its adjustments. A web arranged to catch every objection before it reaches the favored claim may have stopped catching anything else.

V. Carnap Is Still in the Room

It would be convenient to cast the debate as Quine defeating Wittgenstein's obedient followers. Carnap, however, was doing work of his own. He was not merely guarding a threshold established by another philosopher and awaiting the arrival of a liberator.

He developed linguistic frameworks with specified expressions and rules. Within a chosen framework, one could distinguish consequences secured by the rules from questions requiring empirical investigation. Such a distinction did not need to masquerade as a groove carved in heaven.

Choosing a framework was a further, practical matter. Would it serve inquiry? Was it useful? Carnap could accept changing frameworks without treating that possibility as a catastrophe. Revisability was not a discovery Quine had to introduce to an uncomprehending opponent.

Quine nevertheless questioned the fundamental division between changing a framework and changing beliefs within it. The disagreement concerns what this division explains, not whether one man understood change while the other clung helplessly to permanence.

Grice and Strawson also challenged the inference from difficulty defining a distinction to its illegitimacy. Many basic concepts are explained through related concepts. Failure to find a definition using none of that family does not automatically abolish the difference we recognize.

There is a Socratic sound here: you say you understand, so please explain further. But the questions must travel both ways. Otherwise we dismantle one lectern only to build a higher one where Quine's name ends the discussion.

VI. Hume Returns to the Game

Hume and Quine share a way of interrupting familiarity. Having always expected something does not mean we have secured an unshakable warrant for expecting it. Habit can quiet a question without actually having supplied its answer.

Hume asks what justifies moving from observed cases to unobserved ones. Yesterday's sunrise does not furnish a deductive contract guaranteeing tomorrow's. This is not the declaration that events have no causes or that ordinary reasoning has become worthless.

Quine's question concerns meaning, experience, and the organization of theory. Moreover, Hume himself distinguished relations of ideas from matters of fact. Their kinship contains a disagreement: part of the tradition Quine challenges runs through the philosopher we have placed beside him.

Neither stopped living when foundations became difficult. Hume described dining, conversation, and backgammon bringing him out of philosophical perplexity. The game does not refute skepticism. It shows a person remaining involved in life without first having defeated every skeptical argument.

Quine likewise continued doing logic, writing, teaching, and answering objections. Serious reasoning need not wait for a certificate guaranteeing that error is impossible. Equally, saying that everybody is fallible is not a substitute for doing the reasoning properly.

This composure demands two things together: no ultimate guarantee, and a continuing obligation to give reasons. Lose the first and conviction becomes dogmatic. Lose the second and intellectual modesty becomes a graceful way of avoiding the work of judgment.

VII. Do Not Arrange Them Too Neatly

In this series we give thinkers memorable positions: Plato outside the cave, Kant at a boundary, Hegel gathering everything into a system, Wittgenstein near silence. These markers help us find our way, but they can also become assigned seats.

Plato wrote more than a cave. Kant's limits are not simply walls. Once Hegel becomes a stomach capable of swallowing everything, we no longer need to hear his arguments. A vivid image can save memory by quietly replacing attention.

Quine helps us notice an overcrowded map. He has not thereby refuted these thinkers' entire projects. Nor is his holism identical to this series' concern for human beings as ends. Ideas can illuminate one another without becoming interchangeable authorities.

A useful reading habit survives: when a category comfortably accommodates many people, ask whom it leaves out. Call someone rational and perhaps their fear disappears from hearing. Call someone fragile and perhaps their judgment disappears along with their strength.

That is our extension of a reading into life, not an ethical theorem deduced from Quine's logic. Showing that classifications are revisable cannot alone establish how anyone should be treated. Concrete reasons, injuries, and obligations still require consideration.

Reopening a category can nevertheless make room for another person to answer. We may continue using the map. We should only take care not to hold it so close to our faces that it conceals the person standing before us.

VIII. Three Questions, Not One Answer

Heisenberg offers a tempting comparison. Quantum theory unsettled familiar expectations: uncertainty relations constrain the statistical spreads of position and momentum in a quantum state. Both cannot simply be made arbitrarily narrow. The issue is not merely inadequate equipment.

But this does not say that observer and world are indistinguishable, or prove every conceptual boundary fictitious. A physical theory has mathematical content and experimental responsibilities. Borrowing its prestige for any philosophical claim discards precisely the distinctions requiring attention.

Quine investigates how judgments depend on other judgments. Uncertainty relations concern particular physical quantities. Both can disturb complacency, but producing a similar feeling in a reader is not evidence that two thinkers have established the same result.

Nishida Kitarō approaches another problem through pure experience. The later division into subject and object need not be treated as experience's original form. Being absorbed in music before describing oneself as a listener offers one accessible way into his question.

This is not Quine's naturalistic outlook restated in Japanese. Nishida, Quine, and Heisenberg use different methods and seek answers to different questions. If we bring them into conversation, we must also allow them to separate again.

Comparison succeeds when we discover three difficulties, not when three famous men finally speak our preferred answer in unison. Perhaps afterward we will pause before saying either that two things are obviously separate or that they are really the same.

IX. A Logician Goes Traveling

Quine's prose has an unhurried force. He picks up a reassuring sentence, turns it over, and invites its owner to examine the underside. Nothing theatrical seems to happen, yet the furniture in the room has changed position.

Outside the argument, his curiosity had plenty of room. Harvard colleagues remembered a sociable traveler interested in languages and geography; others recalled his understated humor. A place-name on a map could draw his attention as readily as a symbol on a blackboard.

Born in Akron, Ohio, in 1908, he arrived at Harvard in 1930. His European fellowship in 1932–33 brought encounters with the Vienna Circle, Carnap, and Tarski. He learned from people whose ideas he would also question.

Travel offers another scale for familiar words. A sentence comes easily in your own language; where would another speaker begin understanding it? How do different languages reach a shared world? Such questions also occupied his later work. Two kinds of curiosity can meet without one explaining away the other.

Teaching required a different patience: helping beginners follow a line of reasoning rather than merely nod before its conclusion. His logic textbooks grew with his classroom work. Writing continued after his retirement in 1978; the famous early essay had not closed the door on subsequent thought.

What holds my attention here is less the victory of refuting somebody than the willingness to return to a sentence and ask what it actually says. Such delicate work can occupy a long life. Beyond the window there are journeys; on the desk, a question still unfinished.

X. Chalk on the Bridge

Quine died on December 25, 2000, aged ninety-two. Now let him approach this series' imagined bridge. The books remain, and so do the disagreements. A meeting on the bridge need not wait until somebody has won the last argument.

Hume puts away the backgammon board; Socrates makes room; Kant still has questions. Nobody has to close a book merely because Quine has arrived. A meeting of minds would be strangely disappointing if the latest arrival ended every conversation.

Across the boards run our penciled connections: teacher and pupil, influence and resistance, partners and colleagues. Socrates and Plato; Sartre and Beauvoir. Such lines help us remember stories. They may also fasten people too tightly to somebody else's story.

Quine crouches beside a mark. Does this record an encounter, or decree that one person must forever stand beside another? Would new evidence change it? He is asking about a particular line, not waving all relationships out of existence.

Beauvoir can question him in turn. Some boundaries protect a person's refusal; others keep her from leaving. Does their human origin authorize erasing both alike? The distinction between consent and coercion cannot be dismissed by announcing that classifications are revisable.

Quine must remain in the conversation. An important criticism does not exempt its author from criticism, and he need not possess the perfect answer prepared for him by our admiration. Otherwise the bridge is just a classroom with a replacement lecturer.

Perhaps they do not wipe the boards clean. They preserve useful marks, correct an overconfident arrow, and put a question mark beside an uncertain connection. Someone else is approaching; a full diagram is no reason to deny that person room.

Quine hands back the chalk. Not every line should disappear. Before drawing the next one, however, we should know what we are doing. What does this mark help us see, and whom might it cause us to stop seeing?
