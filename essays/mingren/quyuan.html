屈原：美是一种认知

Qu Yuan: Beauty Is a Form of Cognition

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、他问了天

《天问》没有先交出一套关于天地的学说。它先问：关于最初的那些事，是谁传下来的？天地尚未成形，又凭什么考察？

“遂古之初，谁传道之？”

“上下未形，何由考之？”

问题从天上的日月，走到洪水、山川、古代君王与传说中的身体。问句接着问句，篇末没有一张逐题对应的答案表。但没有附答案，不等于不想知道答案。他连“我们凭什么说自己知道”也问进去了。

后来的王逸讲过一个来历：被放逐的屈原看见楚地祠庙墙上的天地山川与古人图像，于是题壁发问。这幅画面很有力量，却是数百年后的注家叙述，不是我们手里的一份当日记录。我们可以看见问句，不能替它补出一段已经证实的孤身对天独白。

《史记》把屈原写为楚国宗族出身的官员，曾任怀王左徒，参与议政与外交；又叙述他被疏远、遭到指斥、再次被迁逐的经过。传记有自己的叙述与判断。它没有给我们一册精确到每天的流放日记。

于是，不能把《离骚》《天问》《九歌》全塞进同一段“汨罗江畔九年”。《九歌》的祭祀、歌舞与多重声音，也不能全部收成一个被遗弃者的自言自语。王逸自己的序就把它连在楚地祭神的传统上。

不需要那样收拢。

一个被既有位置排除的人，仍可能把问题提出来；这已经足够重要。苦难不是诗的生产许可证。朝廷没有给他一个位置，不等于天地只剩一种已经替他安排好的解释。

《天问》把解释重新放到了问号后面。

二、“离骚”里站着谁

司马迁以“离忧”解释“离骚”；王逸把“离”解为别，把“骚”解为愁。这些解释把题目引向不同的愁苦经验，却不能替整首诗预先说完它在做什么。

先听开头：

“帝高阳之苗裔兮，朕皇考曰伯庸。”

这里有人交代自己的来历、父亲、出生与名字。他不是从空无中制造一个“我”。祖先、称谓、语言，已经在他之前。他用这些承来的东西，说出此刻由自己承担的话。

这不是中国文学第一次出现“我”。《诗经》也不全是没有任何作者标记的集体声音。《节南山》有“家父作诵”，《巷伯》有“寺人孟子，作为此诗”。这些句子不足以恢复每一首诗的全部作者史，却足以挡住“屈原之前没有人站出来说我”的断言。

无名，也不等于无人。

《离骚》的特别，不必靠清空前人才能成立。它让同一个发言的位置，长久穿过一连串变化：修饰自己，等待，受伤，辩解，出发，回望；想离开，又不能把所牵挂的东西简单放下。

读者不只是知道“某人遇到了某事”。读者被带进他如何承受、如何区别、如何反复选择。诗中的“我”仍是文学声音，不等于一份可以逐行核对的私人日记；但这个声音不能被换成一份无人的条目而毫无损失。

认识者在这里有位置。

并非站在一切经验之前。是站在自己要为所说的话负责的地方。

而在传统归入《九章》的《涉江》里，那个“我”继续带着身体移动：“朝发枉陼兮，夕宿辰阳”，再到“入溆浦余儃佪兮，迷不知吾所如”。 地名、舟行、天气和迟滞，不是给“忠贞”这个抽象名词加一层地方色彩。正因为路真的会绕、船真的会慢、山会遮日、雨雪会改变一天，人所谓“不改其志”才不是一张悬空标语。

这也使“认识者的位置”更具体：不是只问他站在什么理论坐标上，还要问他从哪一条河、哪一座山、哪一种季节里说话。一个位置既会限制视野，也会提供别人没有的细节。把位置承认下来，不是把一切判断都相对化；恰恰是让判断把自己的来路一起交出来。

三、美的认知

《离骚》里有许多香草。江离、芷、蕙、兰，还有木兰的露与秋菊的花。它们被种下、采来、编结、佩在身上，也会衰败、变质。这里的“兰”先当作古代香草来读，不急着给它安上今天花店里兰科植物的身份证。

“朝饮木兰之坠露兮，夕餐秋菊之落英。”

可以将这两句释作一种洁净的自我要求。王逸也确实沿着香草与品行、求偶与君臣关系展开解释。但注释给出的对应，不是诗的全部；也不是每一株植物、每一个美人，都只能兑换成同一个概念。

如果把“朝”“夕”删去，把露与花换成“美德”，句子的命题摘要或许还在。可清晨与黄昏不在了，入口之物的轻微不在了，一天又一天维持某种生活的动作也不在了。

少掉的不是包装纸。

那是读者据以认识这个人的一部分：他把什么带在身边，为了什么等待，又为什么害怕时节已经过去。美在这里参与认识，不是因为鼻子能检验一个人是否正直，而是因为诗的组织，使原本抽象的坚持有了可进入的质地。

而这组织并不只会确认。

“兰芷变而不芳兮，荃蕙化而为茅。”

曾经可以依靠的芳草，如今也会失去芳香。意象并非一枚盖上去便永远有效的德性印章。失望改变了它的位置。读者跟着这种变化，可能发现：我先前信任的名称，并没有替对象担保。

这并不需要把孔德与波普尔赶成两个拒绝诗的人。孔德专门讨论过诗与艺术，甚至给诗以重要位置；问题在于，他为艺术安排的职分，是否穷尽了诗可能提出的问题。 波普尔的科学划界不等于意义划界，他讨论的人类作品也包括故事、歌曲与艺术。

因此，不是“实验不能证明这枝兰草，所以它一无所知”；也不是“实验管不到，所以它说什么都对”。文本怎样写、一个解释是否删掉反证、借诗说出的历史判断是否可靠，仍可以追问。

波兰尼所说的，我们能够知道多于我们能说出的东西，为明说未尽之处留了位置。它不是让一切未能说明的感觉自动升级为真理。

《九章》传统所收《橘颂》，把这种关系写得更近。橘树不是一个抽象的“坚定”图标：诗中的它生在南国，根深难移，叶青、花白、枝有刺、果实圆。王逸把这些性质一一引向人格解释，但诗之所以能承担这样的解释，恰恰因为对象先有自己的形状、颜色、气味与生长条件。

“受命不迁，生南国兮。深固难徙，更壹志兮。”

如果只保留“坚定”二字，当然也能写一句格言。可格言不会告诉你，为什么是橘树，为什么在诗和王逸的注释里，橘树难以离开南方的处境，会成为人的自我比照。这里的美不是替概念装饰边框；它让一个判断借具体对象取得阻力。对象越具体，解释越不能任意。

这也给“香草美人”传统加上一道限制。我们可以沿着王逸去读比兴，却不能因为知道了“香草常被解释成美德”，就把每一株草从此只读成密码。一个比喻若已经知道答案，读者只剩解码；而诗真正有力量的地方，常常是对象还会反过来修改答案。
美可以使人看见，也可以使人误认。称它为一种认知，不是给它免检，是不先把它逐出认识活动。

四、他在岸边

先不要让后来那次死亡，把屈原的一生都提前泡进江水里。

他在岸边。他写过长路。

“路曼曼其脩远兮，吾将上下而求索。”

这不是一张已知终点的地图。诗里有寻找、有耽搁、有想走与回头；行路者并没有在第一步就拿到全程担保。把这样的诗读成一套命题，会留下东西；说它根本没有任何可讨论的主张，同样会失去东西。

路上真正困难的，有时还不是选哪一条路。是认出什么值得自己回头，什么不能轻易丢下，什么又可以放手。诗把这些迟疑和选择留在了句子里。

认识不止有“答案是什么”，还可能有“为什么是这个问题，使我不能随手翻过去”。诗让那种分量可以被别人接近。

但方向不是命令。一个人可以继续，可以修改，也可以暂时停下，寻找能够一起想的人。我们不能从一句“求索”，替他颁布一生不得休息的任务。

眼前的安排还没有说完这个人。但安排之外剩下的路，也不会替他作决定。走不走、往哪里走，仍然是他的事。

美有时使这道区别变得可感。

并不是替我们走路。是让我们在走得太快时，重新看见脚下。

五、江水不能替诗证明

《史记》把《怀沙》放在江边对话之后，接着记述屈原死于汨罗。这里有一条影响深远的叙述次序，却没有注明他死亡的确切年月日。后人常讲的都城失守、诗人闻讯、节日之日，也不能不经核查便拼成一个已获目击证明的下午。

《怀沙》可以带着末路的重量来读。它在传记中的位置，却不等于我们握有一封带日期、由在场者认证的最后留言。楚国的衰败、都城的失守与国家最终灭亡，也不是同一个时刻；《史记》还写到，屈原死后数十年，楚才终于被秦所灭。

我们不能替那个人轻易开出一张穿越两千年的处方。也不能把他的死安排成本文最漂亮的一步。

倘若把死亡当成最后的完成，就等于替他宣布：所有别的路都没有了，而后世传诵的诗，可以补偿那个不再继续的人。

这两步，都不能由诗替我们证明。

古代的悼念也并非只有一个答案。《史记》所录贾谊的吊文，一面哀伤，一面问“何必怀此都”；司马迁在篇末既写自己的悲恸，也记下读到吊文时生出的不解。这里本来就有哀悼与质问，不必抹成两千年一致的赞成。

端午的纪念同样由许多地方、许多实践构成。纪念屈原是重要的一支，但还有伍子胥等纪念传统，以及节令、饮食、行舟与祈愿生活的活动。它不是一个全国同日重复的单一论证。

一首诗留下来，需要有人记诵、抄写、编订、讲解、翻译；也需要有人愿意再打开它。作品的延续，是后来者持续在做的事。它不能倒签一张证明，说诗人当时必须死去。

《离骚》不需要江水来取得最后的资格。

一个人也不必以消失，证明自己没有被说尽。

六、他和系列里的其他人

杜甫也写花，也写鸟。

“感时花溅泪，恨别鸟惊心。”

这两句不只是把景物抄进报告。关于泪与花、惊心与鸟的关系，可以有不同读法；无论怎样读，把它压成“先看见，再记录”的摄像程序，都太快了。感觉、记忆、语言与眼前之物已经在句子里彼此改变。

同样，我们也不能进入屈原未留下记录的一个瞬间，宣布他必定先有完整的美德概念，后来才偶然看见兰草。两位诗人不必分守“经验”与“先验”两座岗亭，才能让我们看见他们的不同。

图灵在本系列里带来另一种相遇。关于可计算数的论文，早在1930年代就已发表，远早于1952年的刑事追诉，以及随后作为法院处置条件接受的激素治疗。不能把计算思想写成遭受这一处置之后才长出的礼物。

诗人的失位与数学家的遭遇，也不能被折成同一张受难者画像。他们有不同的时代、制度、工作与关系。这里能相问的，不是“谁的苦难产生了更大成就”，而是：我们是否总要先列出一个人的贡献，才肯承认他不是供我们使用的东西？

狄拉克与屈原，则在“美可以指引工作”这一点上相遇。上一篇已经分开数学推导、物理解释与实验检验：实验支持某项理论后果，不等于实验测定了那个方程有多美。

诗也不靠传得比某条理论久，取得更高的真理名次。流传可能包含教育、选择与偶然；遗失的作品不能因此被判没有价值。

后来的批评史本身也说明，美从来没有替《离骚》换来一张免审通行证。刘勰在《文心雕龙·辨骚》里把几种非常不同的前人判断并排放在一起：有人把《离骚》举到接近经典的位置，班固却批评其中的自我申说与怪异材料，王逸又努力把它重新接回经义。刘勰没有简单选一边；他反而说这些褒贬都有“抑扬过实”的地方，要“核其论，必征言焉”。

这件事对“美是一种认知”很重要。所谓美的认知，不是说一首伟大的诗一旦出现，后世就只能越来越正确地赞美它。恰恰相反，一个作品持续存在，常常意味着它持续改变问题：它与经典是什么关系？神话的怪异是缺点还是资源？香草是伦理符号、感官材料，还是两者同时发生？不同年代会给出不同答案。

刘勰后来写《楚辞》“论山水，则循声而得貌；言节候，则披文而见时”，又说它“衣被词人，非一代也”。 这里比“传了两千年所以真”精确得多。作品留下的，不只是一项结论；还可能是一套让后来者重新看山水、节候、声音与人的方法。

这也是传承真正进入认识的地方：不是时间替作品盖章，而是后来的人仍然能够从中做新的区分。

这里没有一场诗与物理的比赛。方程关于自然提出主张，需要接受相应检验；诗把经验组织成一种可被进入的形式，也必须让读者能够反问它怎样组织、遗漏了谁。

“先到”不是同一种先，“知道”也不是同一种工作。

相遇的价值，恰在没有把它们磨成相同。

七、渔父

《楚辞》里的《渔父》，让两个声音在江边碰面。它在传统中归到屈原名下，但成篇与传述的问题，不宜用“现场实录”四字盖住。王逸的序既讲屈原作篇，也讲楚人叙其辞而相传；《史记》的相关对话与《楚辞》的收尾还不完全一样。

诗中的屈原说，世人皆浊，自己独清；众人皆醉，自己独醒。他将被逐的遭遇，放在这道区别里。

渔父问的则是另一件事：与处境相处，是否一定等于把自己交给处境？水有清浊，人能否随之改变使用它的方式，而不把生活整个交出去？

屈原不接受。他以洁净衣冠说明自己的拒绝，话语终于走到死亡的选择。

我们可以听见拒绝中的分量，不必接着宣布，那是所有拒绝唯一应有的归宿。也可以认真问渔父，顺应有没有可能变成替伤害找理由，而不把他的生存直接叫作空白。

因为他唱了歌。

“沧浪之水清兮，可以濯吾缨；沧浪之水浊兮，可以濯吾足。”

说“渔父不产生诗”，恰好删掉了文本最后留下的声音。这几句也见于《孟子·离娄上》，那里是一个孩子歌唱，孔子作出回应。相近的歌进入不同篇章，承担不同的谈话；不能把它们都并成一次有唯一见证人的会面。

《渔父》最后写他离去，不再交谈。没有一句由全知裁判宣布谁赢了。

屈原的坚持没有被渔父的歌抹去。渔父的歌也不该被屈原的后来之名抹去。

这两种声音甚至可以看作两种认识姿态：屈原害怕适应最终变成同化；渔父则追问，环境已经改变时，行动方式是否也可以改变。把一切调整都叫投降，会看不见保存主体的策略；把一切顺势都叫智慧，也可能替权力制造的污浊免除责任。

所以这场对话不必分出“更成熟”的一方。它真正逼人的地方，是要求双方说出自己不能接受的代价。歌声之所以不能被一句“随遇而安”替代，正因为清水、浊水、冠缨与双足形成了具体动作；美让一个立场更可记，也让它更容易被反问。
本篇说美可以是一种认知，也必须接受这首歌的追问：如果我只肯在赞同我的声音里听见美，我究竟认识了什么？

八、桥头

屈原沿着河岸走来，衣角被雾气打湿，头发有些散。他手里没有书卷。香草的气息却像随行的文字，从衣褶里慢慢出来。

孔德打开日历，看了看新来的人，又看了看纸页。一个名字可以在格子里找到位置，接下来要说的话却未必放得进去。

他把日历合在膝上，挪出一块干燥的地方。

波普尔问：“你要我们检查什么？”

屈原说：“先听完。”

狄拉克站在一旁。一个人曾沿着方程追下去，一个人沿着香草与远游写下去。他们不必因此知道对方心里的全部。纸上的关系可以请人复算；一句诗也可以请人重读。两种邀请，各有等待。

河面传来渔父的歌。

屈原听着，没有接唱，也没有请谁把那声音赶走。

水中有他的倒影。一片落叶经过，脸便短暂地散开，又合拢。秋天了。

他沿石阶走到浅水边，把衣上的一枝香草放进水里，然后回到干燥的石头旁。

兰草随水漂去。

人还在岸上。

下一句，还可以由他自己说。

I. He Questioned Heaven

Tianwen, or Questions to Heaven, does not begin by supplying a theory of the universe. It asks who handed down the stories about the beginning. Before heaven and earth had taken shape, on what evidence could anything about them be established?

Who transmitted the account of the most ancient beginning? How could what preceded all formation be examined?

The questions travel from sun and moon to floods, mountains, rulers, and the bodies of legend. One follows another, with no matching answer sheet waiting at the end. Yet not supplying answers is different from not wanting them. The poem includes the question of what entitles us to say that we know.

Writing centuries later, the commentator Wang Yi offered a memorable account of its origin. The exiled Qu Yuan saw paintings of the cosmos and ancient figures on the walls of shrines in Chu, and inscribed his questions there. That is a story about the poem's making, not a contemporary record of the event. We possess the questions. We cannot turn them into a verified scene of one solitary man addressing the sky.

Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian presents Qu Yuan as an official from the royal kin of Chu, serving King Huai in deliberation and diplomacy. It narrates estrangement, accusations, and renewed banishment. The historian's account has its own judgments and arrangement; it is not a daily journal of exile.

There is therefore no warrant for packing Li Sao, Tianwen, and the Nine Songs into one precisely measured nine-year stay beside the Miluo. Nor should the ritual performances and varied voices of the Nine Songs all become one abandoned man's monologue. Wang Yi himself connects that collection with local traditions of song and worship.

Nothing requires us to close the story so tightly.

Someone deprived of an established place may still put a question. That matters without making deprivation a prerequisite for poetry. The court's failure to give him a place does not mean that the world has only one interpretation left for him to accept.

Tianwen puts interpretation back on the far side of a question mark.

II. Who Is Speaking in Li Sao?

Sima Qian glosses Li Sao through sorrow; Wang Yi explains its two terms through separation and grief. These readings open different approaches to the title. Neither can tell us in advance everything the poem does.

Listen first to its beginning. The speaker identifies himself as a descendant of Gao Yang and names his late father, Bo Yong. Ancestry, birth, and naming enter before his journey has begun. This “I” is not made out of nothing. Language and relationships already precede him. He uses what he has inherited to speak words for which he now takes responsibility.

This is not the first “I” in Chinese literature. Nor is the earlier Book of Songs entirely devoid of authorial identifications. “Jie Nanshan” names Jiafu as the maker of its verses; “Xiangbo” names the palace attendant Mengzi as the maker of its poem. Those statements do not settle the complete authorship history of either work. They do, however, prevent us from claiming that nobody stepped forward before Qu Yuan.

An unknown name does not mean an absent person.

The distinctiveness of Li Sao need not depend on erasing its predecessors. What it sustains is a speaking position through a long sequence of changes: self-adornment, expectation, injury, argument, departure, and the backward glance. A wish to leave does not make attachment easy to put down.

We are not simply informed that someone underwent an event. We encounter how he bears it, distinguishes one possibility from another, and chooses again. The poetic speaker is still a literary voice, not a private diary that can be checked line by line against an external record. Yet replacing that voice with an impersonal catalogue would lose something essential.

The knower has a place here.

Not before all experience. At the point where the words become his to answer for.

In She Jiang, traditionally included among the Nine Declarations, that “I” keeps moving in a body. He travels by river, reaches named places, hesitates at Xupu, encounters mountains that block the sun and weather that changes the day. Geography is not merely local color pasted onto an abstract idea called integrity. The route can bend, a boat can slow, and rain can alter what is possible; only within such conditions does refusing to change one's direction become more than a floating slogan.

This makes the knower's position more concrete. We need to ask not only where someone stands in a theoretical map, but from which river, mountain, season, and political distance the words are spoken. A position can restrict vision and also disclose details unavailable elsewhere. Acknowledging position does not make every judgment equally relative. It asks a judgment to disclose its own route.

III. Cognition Through Beauty

Li Sao is full of fragrant plants. There are lan and other aromatic herbs, magnolia dew and chrysanthemum petals. Plants are cultivated, gathered, woven, and worn. They also wither or cease to be what the speaker once trusted. We should first let lan be an ancient aromatic plant, rather than immediately assigning it the botanical identity of a modern florist's orchid.

At dawn, the speaker drinks the dew fallen from magnolia; at evening, he eats the fallen blossoms of autumn chrysanthemums.

One may read these lines as a demand for an untainted life. Wang Yi does interpret aromatic plants through conduct, and certain scenes of courtship through relations between ruler and minister. But an interpretive correspondence is not the whole poem. Every plant and every beautiful figure need not exchange for one fixed concept.

Replace dew and petals with “virtue.” Delete dawn and evening. A summary of the proposition may remain. But the day has disappeared. So has the delicacy of what enters the mouth, and the repeated act of sustaining a way of living.

What has gone missing is not wrapping paper.

It is part of how the reader comes to know this person: what he keeps close, what he waits for, why the passing season troubles him. Beauty participates in cognition here not because a nose can test moral character, but because the poem gives an otherwise abstract commitment a texture that another person can enter.

And the pattern does more than confirm.

Later, the aromatic plants lose their fragrance; what once belonged among them turns into coarse grass. The image is not a certificate of virtue, valid forever after its first application. Disappointment changes its place. A reader following that change may recognize something about an earlier trust: the name I relied on did not guarantee the thing.

To acknowledge this, we need not invent a Comte or a Popper who refuses poetry. Comte devoted substantial attention to poetry and art. The question is whether the function he assigns them exhausts the questions they may raise. Popper's demarcation of science is not a boundary around meaning, and his account of human creations includes stories, songs, and art.

Neither “a laboratory cannot establish this, so it knows nothing” nor “a laboratory has no jurisdiction here, so anything goes” will do. We can still ask how the text is written, whether a reading has suppressed contrary details, and whether historical claims made through the poem are reliable.

Polanyi's observation that knowing can exceed what we manage to say leaves room for what explicit formulation has not exhausted. It does not make every unarticulated feeling true.

The Ode to the Orange Tree, traditionally included in the Nine Declarations, brings the relation still closer. The orange is not an abstract icon of steadfastness. In the poem and commentary, it grows in the south and is difficult to transplant, has green leaves, white blossoms, thorns, and round fruit. Wang Yi turns these features one by one toward character, but the interpretation works because the object first has a shape, color, and conditions of growth of its own.

Reduce the poem to “steadfastness,” and a maxim remains. What disappears is why an orange tree matters: the poem and commentary make the tree’s attachment to its habitat available for human comparison. Beauty here does not decorate a concept. It lets a judgment meet resistance from a particular thing.

The same point limits any automatic decoding of “fragrant plants and beautiful persons.” We may follow Wang Yi's allegorical reading, but if “fragrant herb” always already means “virtue,” the reader has only a key to a cipher. Poetry becomes cognitively interesting precisely when the thing can still modify the answer we expected.

Beauty can bring something into view. It can also make us misrecognize it. Calling it a form of cognition grants it no exemption from questioning. It declines to expel it from cognitive life before the questions have begun.

IV. He Is on the Bank

Let us not allow the death that came later to submerge an entire life in advance.

He is on the bank. He has written about a long road.

The road stretches far; he will search above and below.

This is not a map with a guaranteed destination. The poem contains searching and delay, the impulse to depart and the backward turn. Its traveler has not been given an assurance covering the whole journey. Reducing the poem to propositions leaves something out. Saying it contains no discussable claims at all would lose something too.

Sometimes the hardest part of a journey is not choosing a road. It is recognizing what deserves a backward glance, what cannot lightly be left behind and what may be released. The poem keeps those hesitations and choices in its lines.

Cognition may concern not only “What is the answer?” but “Why does this question carry a weight that prevents me from casually turning the page?” A poem can make that weight accessible to someone else.

But a direction is not a command. A person may continue, revise a course, pause, or seek someone with whom to think. A line about searching does not authorize us to impose a lifetime without rest on its speaker.

The arrangements before him have not said everything about this person. Yet the roads left outside them cannot choose for him. Whether to walk, and where, remain his questions.

Beauty sometimes makes that distinction felt.

It does not walk in our place. It may make us look down again when we have begun to walk too quickly.

V. The River Cannot Prove the Poem

The Records places Huai Sha after the riverside dialogue and then recounts Qu Yuan's death in the Miluo. This is an influential narrative sequence. It is not accompanied by an exact date for the death. Familiar accounts of a capital's capture, news reaching the poet, and a festival day cannot simply be spliced into a witnessed afternoon.

We may read Huai Sha with the weight of an ending. Its position in the biography does not mean we possess a dated final letter authenticated by someone who was there. The decline of Chu, the loss of its capital, and the state's eventual destruction are not the same moment. The Records itself places the final conquest decades after Qu Yuan's death.

We cannot lightly prescribe a solution across two millennia to the predicament of that person. Neither should we arrange his death as this essay's most beautiful step.

To treat his death as a final completion is to declare on his behalf that every other road was gone, and that poetry surviving afterward could compensate for the person who could no longer continue.

The poetry establishes neither proposition.

Nor did ancient mourning speak with one voice. Jia Yi's lament, preserved in the Records, grieves but also asks why the poet had to remain attached to that particular capital. Sima Qian's concluding reflection records both sorrow and perplexity. Mourning and questioning are already together in the text. We need not flatten them into two thousand years of unanimous approval.

The Dragon Boat Festival likewise contains several commemorative and seasonal traditions. Qu Yuan is an important focus, but not the only one: Wu Zixu also appears among its figures of remembrance. Food, boats, seasonal practices, and wishes for life belong to its varied regional forms. It is not one proposition repeated by an entire civilization on the same day.

For a poem to survive, people must remember, copy, edit, teach, translate, and open it again. Continuity is work that later people keep doing. It cannot retroactively issue a certificate declaring that the poet had to die.

Li Sao does not need the river to qualify it at last.

Nor must a person disappear to establish that no account has exhausted him.

VI. Qu Yuan and the Others

Du Fu, too, wrote about flowers and birds.

In “Spring View,” flowers and tears meet in grief for the times; birds and a startled heart meet in the pain of separation.

There are different ways to understand those relationships. None makes the lines a simple transfer of scenery into a report. Feeling, memory, language, and the visible world are already altering one another. Calling this “first observe, then record” turns a poem too quickly into a camera.

Equally, we cannot enter an undocumented instant in Qu Yuan's mind and announce that he first possessed a complete concept of virtue, then happened to recognize it in a plant. The poets need not stand guard at opposite checkpoints labeled “empirical” and “a priori” for their differences to matter.

Turing brings a different encounter into this series. His work on computable numbers was published in the 1930s, long before the prosecution of 1952 and the probation made conditional on hormonal treatment. We cannot present the idea of computation as a gift that grew only after this treatment.

The poet's loss of office and the mathematician's treatment should not fold into one generic portrait of a sufferer. Their periods, institutions, work, and relationships differ. What their juxtaposition can ask is not whose suffering produced the greater achievement, but whether we must first list someone's contributions before acknowledging that he is not simply ours to use.

Dirac and Qu Yuan meet at another point: beauty may orient work. The preceding essay distinguished mathematical derivation, physical interpretation, and experimental testing. Evidence for a theory's consequences is not a measurement of how beautiful its equation is.

Nor does poetry gain a higher rank of truth by circulating longer than a physical theory. Transmission involves education, selection, and contingency. Lost works are not thereby proved worthless.

The history of criticism shows that beauty never bought Li Sao immunity from examination. In The Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons, Liu Xie set sharply different earlier judgments beside one another: some raised the poem toward the Classics, Ban Gu objected to its self-assertion and strange materials, while Wang Yi worked to reconnect it with classical norms. Liu Xie did not simply choose one camp. He warned that praise and blame had both overshot and insisted that an argument be tested against the words themselves.

That matters for the claim that beauty can be a form of cognition. A great poem does not appear and leave later readers only the task of praising it more accurately. It keeps changing the questions: What is its relation to inherited norms? Is strangeness an error or a resource? Are fragrant plants ethical signs, sensory things, or both at once?

Liu Xie later remarked that the Chuci could give form to mountains and waters through sound and make a season visible through writing, and that its influence clothed writers of more than one age. That is more precise than saying “it lasted for two thousand years, therefore it is true.” What survives may be not one conclusion but a way of making later readers see distinctions again.

There is no contest between poetry and physics here. An equation making claims about nature owes the relevant tests. A poem organizing experience into an inhabitable form must also leave readers able to ask how that form works and whom it omits.

“Arriving first” need not always name the same relation. “Knowing” need not always name the same task.

The encounter matters because the differences have not been ground away.

VII. The Fisherman

In the Songs of Chu, “The Fisherman” brings two voices together beside a river. Tradition assigns the piece to Qu Yuan, but its composition and transmission cannot be settled by calling it a transcript. Wang Yi's preface speaks both of Qu Yuan's composition and of people in Chu passing on his words. The related dialogue in the Records also does not have exactly the same ending.

The poem's Qu Yuan says that everyone else is muddy while he alone is clear, everyone drunk while he alone is awake. He places his banishment within that distinction.

The fisherman asks something different. Must living with one's circumstances mean surrendering oneself to them? As water changes, could one change the use one makes of it without handing one's entire life away?

Qu Yuan refuses. He explains his refusal through newly washed clothes and an unsoiled body, and the speech reaches the choice of death.

We can hear the weight of refusal without announcing that this is where every refusal should end. We can also ask the fisherman whether accommodation may become an excuse for harm, without declaring survival an empty activity.

For he sings.

When the Canglang waters are clear, I can wash my cap-strings; when they are muddy, I can wash my feet.

To say that the fisherman produces no poetry deletes precisely the voice the text leaves at its end. Related lines appear in Mencius, too, where a child sings and Confucius responds. A song enters different works and different conversations. These are not all one meeting with a single authenticated witness.

“The Fisherman” ends with departure and no further exchange. There is no omniscient judge declaring a winner.

The song has not erased Qu Yuan's insistence. Qu Yuan's later fame should not erase the song.

An essay claiming that beauty can be a form of cognition must let this voice question it as well: if I can hear beauty only in the voices that agree with me, what have I actually come to know?

The two voices can also be heard as two cognitive postures. Qu Yuan fears that adaptation may eventually become assimilation. The fisherman asks whether a changed environment may require a changed way of acting. Calling every adjustment surrender can hide strategies that preserve a subject; calling every accommodation wisdom can excuse pollution produced by power.

The dialogue therefore need not identify a “more mature” winner. Its pressure comes from asking each side to state the price it refuses to pay. The song cannot be replaced by the slogan “go with the flow,” because clear water, muddy water, cap strings, and feet form concrete actions. Beauty makes a position memorable—and for that very reason, easier to question.

VIII. The Bridgehead

He arrives along the riverbank, the hem of his robe damp with mist, his hair a little loose. He carries no scroll. The scent of herbs comes slowly out of the folds of his clothes, as though words had accompanied him without becoming a book.

Comte opens his calendar, looks at the newcomer and then at the page. A name can find a place in a square. What the person will say next may not fit.

Comte closes the calendar on his knees and makes room on a dry stone.

“What would you like us to examine?” Popper asks.

“Listen to the end first,” Qu Yuan says.

Dirac stands nearby. One man followed an equation; the other wrote through fragrant plants and distant journeys. That does not give either access to everything in the other's mind. Relations on paper can invite recalculation. A line of poetry can invite rereading. Both invitations involve waiting, differently.

The fisherman's song comes across the water.

Qu Yuan listens. He does not join in. Nor does he ask anyone to silence it.

His reflection lies below. A leaf passes; for a moment the face breaks apart, then gathers again. Autumn.

He follows the stone steps to the shallow water, lays a sprig from his robe on the surface, then returns to the dry stone.

The herb drifts downstream.

The man remains on the bank.

The next sentence can still be his to speak.
