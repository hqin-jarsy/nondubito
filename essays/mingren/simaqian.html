司马迁，活着比死难

Sima Qian, Living Is Harder Than Dying

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、太史令

司马迁的父亲司马谈做过太史令。管天文，管历法，也整理典籍与旧闻。看星辰怎样运行，看人间留下什么。

父亲有一项没有完成的事业：论次旧闻，写出贯通古今的历史。公元前110年，汉武帝举行封禅。按《太史公自序》的叙述，司马谈留滞河洛一带，不能参与，忧愤将死；出使归来的司马迁赶去见他。

父亲握着儿子的手哭了。他追述家族与周代史官的渊源，说自己死后，儿子要继承这份工作，不能忘记他想写的东西。一份尚未完成的事业，就这样交到了儿子手上。

司马迁俯首流泪，答应了：“小子不敏，请悉论先人所次旧闻，弗敢阙。”——我虽不聪敏，愿把您整理的旧闻接着论述，不敢遗漏。

父亲死后三年，司马迁任太史令，得以整理、阅读宫廷收藏的文献。后来他参与太初改历，也继续撰述。旅途中的见闻，库藏里的文字，父亲整理过的材料，一点点进入这部书。

这首先是一份继承来的托付。但继承并没有替他写出判断。哪些材料可信，哪些人物值得留下，怎样把不同的时代连接起来，后来都要由他继续选择。父亲交给他的是工作，不是一部已经完成的书。

二、李陵

公元前99年。司马迁已经写了几年。

然后发生了一件事，改变了这份工作的代价。

李陵率五千步兵出击匈奴。汉代记载叙述他们与优势兵力苦战、边战边退，终于箭尽，救援不至。突围失败以后，李陵投降。

消息传回宫廷。司马迁在后来写给任安的信里回忆：先前为战报举杯祝贺的人，转而抓住李陵的失败攻击他。皇帝悲愤，群臣惶恐。

武帝召问，司马迁说了不同的话。他与李陵并不是亲密朋友；他认为这个将军以少敌多，已经尽力，不能因为一次失败抹去从前的作为。他还推测李陵留命，是想等待机会报汉。

这番话没有被听成忠告。按他的自述，武帝以为他贬损贰师将军李广利、替李陵游说。李广利是武帝宠妃李夫人的兄长，正在皇帝的倚重之中。

司马迁被交付司法官吏，最终以“诬上”的罪名受处置。一次在召问中说出自己判断的回答，进入了牢狱与刑罚的机器。

他想保留一个败将的复杂性。现在，他自己的生命也被压成了一项罪名。

三、选择

到了牢狱里，可走的路越来越窄。

在《报任安书》中，他说家贫，财物不足以自赎；“交游莫救，左右亲近不为一言”。朋友没有救他，左右亲近的人没有替他说一句话。

宫刑，也叫腐刑：国家施加于身体的残酷伤害。他受了刑，并没有以自杀结束生命。谈他的“选择”，必须让这个被强制压窄的处境留在句子里。

为什么还要活着？

他给任安的回答是：书还没有写完。那封后来流传为《报任安书》的长信，不只是人生宣言，也是在回应朋友要他推举贤士的劝告：一个处在这种身份和位置的人，还能做什么？

“人固有一死，死有重于泰山，或轻于鸿毛，用之所趋异也。”人终有一死，轻重取决于它所指向的东西。面对当时士人社会所要求的死，他为自己留下来寻找另一种解释。

他设想自己若伏法受诛，不过“若九牛亡一毛，与蝼蚁何异”。旁人未必把他看作死节者，反会认为他罪尽而死。死亡并不会自动让他夺回解释自己一生的权利。

他之所以隐忍，是“恨私心有所不尽，鄙没世而文采不表于后也”。心里尚有未能说尽的东西，他害怕它随着自己无声消失。

沿着自己所理解的“发愤著书”谱系，他列出一串前人：文王受拘而演《周易》；孔子困厄而作《春秋》；屈原放逐而赋《离骚》；左丘失明而有《国语》；孙子膑脚而论兵法；吕不韦迁蜀而《吕览》传世；韩非囚秦而有《说难》《孤愤》。

“此人皆意有所郁结，不得通其道，故述往事，思来者。”道路堵住了，他们把尚未说尽的话交给未来。

他把自己也放进这个未来。父亲的托付仍在，但“成一家之言”已经是他自己的追求。他承认人会贪生、畏死、顾念亲人；留下来不需要先证明自己没有这些感情。

他没有让未完成的书随自己一起消失。对这个人而言，留下来承担那份工作，成了一条比死更难、却仍愿意走的路。

四、活着比死难

受刑以后的司马迁，在信里写下了这样的日常：

“每念斯耻，汗未尝不发背沾衣也。”每次想到这种耻辱，汗都会从背上浸透衣服。

“肠一日而九回。”痛苦不是过去发生一次便结束的事。

“居则忽忽若有所亡，出则不知所如往。”在家恍惚，出门不知往哪里去。这里没有平静地克服一切的胜利者，只有一个在信里承认自己仍然很痛的人。

身体的损伤和社会的羞辱纠缠在一起。他在信中反复诉说刑余之人被轻贱的位置。可是社会以“非人”的目光看一个人，不等于这个人真的失去了人格。耻辱属于施暴和羞辱的秩序，不是受害者应得的身份。

他后来担任中书令，仍在宫廷任职。工作继续着，伤害过他的权力也仍在身旁。那封信让我们听见，外在职位与内心感受之间，可以有多深的距离。

苏格拉底在柏拉图笔下拒绝逃走，走向死亡；司马迁忍受刑罚，留下来写书。一种坚持常被纪念为壮烈，另一种坚持却曾被当作苟且。把两个人放在一起，是为了看见这种评价的不对称。

不是死者“干净”、生者“肮脏”，也不是宣布司马迁比苏格拉底更伟大。一个被羞辱的人仍然有尊严；一段必须继续的生活，也值得被理解。

死亡的故事常有一个收束的场景。活下去的故事却要容纳明天、后天，以及还没有写完的下一篇。我们不知道他的每一天，却知道他后来仍写作，并留下那封不肯粉饰痛苦的信。

贝多芬听力严重受损以后仍作曲；司马迁受刑以后仍撰史。身体受到限制，工作没有因此全部停下。两种持续的劳动，在这里互相照亮。

两者也不能混同。疾病与司法暴力不同，而两个人的痛苦又都不能被切成纯粹“生理的”或纯粹“社会的”。贝多芬也承受孤立，司马迁也承受身体的创伤。

不能说身体的痛一定能适应，社会的痛永远不能适应。本文只愿意停在这个更小、也更有分量的事实前：他写痛，也继续写历史。作品的存在，不替施加给他的伤害辩护。

五、究天人之际

他把那部书写成了。

《史记》。按照《太史公自序》自己的总数，一百三十篇，五十二万六千五百字。十二本纪、十表、八书、三十世家、七十列传。不是一长列人物传记，而是几种观察历史的方法共同支撑起的建筑。

上起黄帝，下至汉武帝时代。古史传说，王朝兴亡，来到他自己所处的当下。所谓“三千年”的广阔尺度，在书里变成一篇接着一篇可以进入的文字。

他在《报任安书》中写出那个著名的愿望：“究天人之际，通古今之变，成一家之言。”

究天人之际——问天道与人事怎样相接。兴亡是天命，还是人的行动？《伯夷列传》里，善者饥饿早死，暴者却享寿，他问：“天道，是邪非邪？”提问并没有被一个漂亮的报应公式关闭。

通古今之变——把不同年代的变化连接起来。制度、战争、财富、人物选择，怎样共同改变局势？表让同时发生的事彼此对照，书追踪礼乐、历法、河渠、经济；传记把我们带到具体的人面前。

成一家之言——在继承和考证中形成自己的论述。不是因为出自自己就必然正确，而是不让材料只停在没有承担者的堆积里。

用本系列的词说，这三句各是一种“构”：哲学的问题，历史的组织，以及一个人愿意负责的判断。

孔子说“述而不作”，司马迁自己也说在“述故事”。继承与创新并不是两间互不相通的屋子。整理、取舍、编排，本身就让判断显出形状；许多篇章中的“太史公曰”，又使我们直接听见他的声音。

荷马史诗让我们想到口头叙事与文本的漫长关系；《史记》让我们看见档案、旧闻、访察和人物故事怎样交织。项羽有项羽的故事，刘邦有刘邦的故事，韩信有韩信的故事。名字重新长出一张面孔。

项羽输了，他的故事仍占据《本纪》；孔子没有封国，却有《世家》；陈涉的起义失败了，“王侯将相宁有种乎”仍被记住。实际政治支配、历史作用、文化传承，以不同的尺度为人物找到位置。

写项羽的悲剧，并不取消对其暴力与自负的批评；写游侠、货殖者，也不等于赞成他们的一切行为。人物可以值得被看见，同时仍然接受判断。

如果说“每个人都是目的”，这是本文借现代伦理语言表达的一种阅读愿望，不是宣布司马迁已经写出康德的原理。更贴近书本的说法是：一个人不应只剩胜败表中的一个符号。

六、他和苏格拉底

再把苏格拉底与司马迁并排放一次。

在柏拉图的《克里托》中，朋友提出出逃，苏格拉底拒绝；在《斐多》中，读者走进他最后一天的谈话与死亡。朋友的声音，把那场告别交给了后来的人。

司马迁的文字则把另一个问题送到我们面前：怎样在受过重刑之后继续活，怎样让未完成的工作仍有未来。他没有自由选择伤害；他努力选择伤害以后尚可继续的部分。

两个人都不肯让外在裁决说尽自己。一个走向最后的谈话，一个回到尚未写完的书。相似的坚持，落在很不一样的处境里。

苏格拉底的坚持也有漫长的日常，不是在喝药的几分钟里才突然成立。他死后，对话仍被人重写、争论；不能说他的目的从此全部完成，再不需要任何时间。

司马迁的工作尤其需要余下的时间。材料要整理，章节要写，声音要获得形状。父亲留下的事业，需要儿子的手继续做；后来读者与传抄者的手，还要接下去。

柏拉图使苏格拉底的形象继续说话，但不是他的透明替身。司马迁亲自撰述，也不意味着他不需要其他人的保存。一个人的判断不能简单委托给别人，一部书的存续又不可能只靠一个人。

苏格拉底的最后一天，司马迁没有记清年数的后半生：一个给我们集中的告别场景，一个让我们想到没有掌声的延续。不是短痛与长痛的比赛。

“苏格拉底死了一次，司马迁每天都在不死。”这句文学性的对照，想照亮的是另一种坚持：留下来，在未必有人理解的日子里，继续做。

七、被逼着构

这个系列写过许多受到逼迫、催促、限制的人。

传说中的尹喜请老子留下文字；华莱士的来信使达尔文长期积累的研究面临共同发表与优先权的问题；战乱改变杜甫的诗；耳聋改变贝多芬工作与交往的条件。

但请求不等于强迫，来信不等于凭空创造一部理论，杜甫在安史之乱前已经是诗人，贝多芬也不是因为患病才拥有音乐。

同样，宫刑没有把一个空白的人制造成史学家。司马迁在灾难到来以前就在写。他带进牢狱的，还有旅行、阅读、职守、父亲的遗命，以及已经开始形成的判断。

受刑改变了他理解自己写作的语言。信与自序中，那些受困而著书的前人，成了可以与他相伴的人。未完成的书也获得了更迫切的意义：里面还有他不愿随自己一同消失的东西。

读项羽在垓下四面楚歌、对虞姬悲歌的场景，读韩信被拘时说“狡兔死，良狗亨”的苦涩，今天的读者可能听见司马迁自身处境的回声。但相似不是创作因果的证明。这些篇章也来自材料、叙事技巧和对不同人物的判断，不能一律改写成作者的秘密自传。

所谓“载体被凿，余项进入作品”，若还有意义，就在这个比喻的限度里：遭遇不能被原来的解释完全安置，一个人尝试给尚未被说出的东西以形式。

杜甫把生活的裂缝带进诗，贝多芬在变化的条件中继续探索声音，司马迁把旧闻与自己的追问编成历史。他们都在回应，但不是伤害自动替他们写作。

没有理由因此说“没有苦难就没有深度”，更没有理由感谢那把刑刀。许多受伤者没有留下作品，他们的生命也不因此减轻。值得珍惜的是人的劳动，不是造成创伤的力量。

八、他不知道

《史记》留下来了。司马迁生命最后一段的记录，却没有同样清楚地留下。

他的卒年、地点和具体情形，现存材料不能确证。《汉书》的本传写了他的工作、受刑和后来的职位，并保存那封长信，却没有给出一份明确的死亡记录。

一个用那么多心力记录他人的人，自己的终点成了缺页。

他不能预先知道书的全部命运。《汉书》说，他死后著作“稍出”，到宣帝时，外孙杨恽传播，使之广为流布。从一家保存的书，到越来越多人的案头，还有一段由后来者走出的路。

传本中有缺失、补写与后来的编辑痕迹。班固等编成《汉书》，继承了纪传体的若干部件，却把通史改成断代史；后来的正史继续变化，最终形成以《史记》居首的“二十四史”序列。继承本身，也会重新构造。

杜甫不知道自己将怎样被世世代代称为诗圣，巴赫不知道他的音乐会走进怎样的未来，司马迁也不知道两千年后的读者怎样打开《史记》。不知道后世的荣耀，不等于他们都认为自己一生失败。

杜甫写过“无成涕作霖”，但一行诗容纳的是一个时刻，不是一生的最后判决。巴赫的音乐经家人与学生继续传下；司马迁在那封痛苦的信里，也已经把希望投向后来的人。作品里的失意，与作品所打开的未来，可以同时存在。

诗、赋格、史书都比创作者活得更久。不是时间自动奖赏天才，是家人、抄写者、学者、出版者、演奏者和读者，一次次让留下的东西重新发生。

《史记》仍可读。两千年不是替痛苦结账的赔偿，却是他当年所想的“来者”终于一次次到来的地方。

九、活着的人

在本系列想象的桥头，又来了一个人。

他很瘦，背微微弯着。苏格拉底站得轻，康德站得稳，秦始皇站得硬，华盛顿站得松，贝多芬握着拳头；老子似乎已经隐去。司马迁站在那里，像是还背负着什么。

一百三十篇。五十二万余字。从古史传说走向汉代的广阔时间。一份尚未交出的工作，在他背上留下了重量。

他的痛不能与杜甫的悲、贝多芬的激烈、耶稣的受难排出名次。每个人的处境都不能被另一个人的替换。在这里，我们尤其望着那个没有在一场壮烈告别中结束、而是继续过日子的人。

他曾对自己说，书还没写完。于是留下来。不是他的生命只能凭一本书取得生存许可，而是这本书给了他自己认出的、继续走的一条路。

若让桥上的两人说话，本文愿意这样想象：
苏格拉底说：我不愿以逃离背弃自己的理由。
司马迁说：我不愿让还没有说完的话随我死去。

两句话可以同时被听见。走向死亡的坚持，与留下来写作的坚持，在桥头给彼此留下了位置。

桥头没有“干净的死者”和“肮脏的生者”。有身体受过损伤、名誉被人裁判，却不因此失去作为人的位置的人。

书写成了。“究天人之际，通古今之变，成一家之言。”愿望进入文字，文字又离开了作者。

在这个想象的结尾里，他终于可以把书放下，向桥的另一端走去。

身影远去。史料没有告诉我们的终点，仍然留白。书却朝着我们打开。

父亲临终嘱托、三年后任太史令、太初改历以及书的结构与字数，参《史记·太史公自序》。家世叙述是文本所保存的自我定位，不能当作每一代连续任职都经独立验证的档案。太史令的职掌与后世专职史馆不同；司马迁出生年、受刑的具体时间、成书过程和卒年都有需要分辨的推算，本文不据其中一种建立精确年龄或“受刑后写十几年”的时间线。

项羽垓下悲歌与韩信被拘后的话分别参《史记》卷七、卷九十二。韩信引语保留原典“亨”字，通“烹”。读者可将这些场景同司马迁的经历相互参照，但不能证明每个人物都是他受刑后的自传投影，更不能把暴力视为伟大作品的必要条件。

苏格拉底拒绝出逃与最后一天分属柏拉图《克里托》《斐多》的叙事场景。本文比较的是不同的坚持，不计算谁更英雄或痛苦持续几分钟。宫刑所伴随的污名是应被说明的社会事实，不是作者认可的价值判断；人的生存资格不依赖于完成作品。

I. The Grand Historian

Sima Qian’s father, Sima Tan, was Grand Historian. He worked with astronomy and the calendar, inherited writings and old accounts. He watched how the stars moved and what people left behind.

His father had an unfinished undertaking: to arrange the old accounts into a history connecting past and present. In 110 BCE Emperor Wu performed the great imperial sacrifices. According to the Grand Historian’s Self-Preface, Sima Tan remained behind in the region of the Yellow and Luo rivers, unable to participate and approaching death in distress. His son, returning from an official mission, came to see him.

The father took his son’s hand and wept. He recalled their family’s connection with the historians of the Zhou and charged his son to continue the work after his death. What he had wanted to write must not be forgotten. An unfinished undertaking passed into his son’s hands.

Sima Qian bowed his head and wept. “Though I am not clever, I will fully discuss the old accounts you have arranged, and dare leave nothing out.”

Three years after his father’s death, Sima Qian became Grand Historian and gained access to the palace collections. Later he took part in reforming the calendar and continued his writing. What he had seen on his travels, what the archives held, what his father had arranged: gradually, these entered the book.

It began as an inherited obligation. But inheritance could not make his judgments for him. Which evidence to trust, whose lives to preserve, how to connect different ages: these decisions remained his to make. His father had left him work, not a finished book.

II. Li Ling

99 BCE. Sima Qian had been writing for several years.

Then something happened that changed the cost of his undertaking.

Li Ling led five thousand infantry against the Xiongnu. Han accounts describe prolonged fighting against superior forces, a fighting retreat, arrows exhausted and no relief arriving. After the attempt to break out failed, he surrendered.

The news reached court. In his later letter to Ren An, Sima Qian recalled that men who had raised cups to celebrate the earlier dispatches now seized on Li Ling’s defeat to attack him. The emperor was distressed and angry; his ministers were afraid.

When Emperor Wu called for his opinion, Sima Qian answered differently. He and Li Ling were not close friends. He thought the general had fought hard against overwhelming numbers, and that one defeat should not erase his earlier conduct. He also suggested that Li Ling had stayed alive in hope of another chance to serve the Han.

The advice was not received as advice. In Sima Qian’s account, the emperor heard an attack on General Li Guangli and special pleading for Li Ling. Li Guangli, elder brother of the emperor’s favored consort Lady Li, enjoyed imperial backing.

Sima Qian was handed over to the judicial officials and ultimately punished on a charge of misrepresenting matters to the throne. An answer given when summoned for his judgment entered the machinery of prison and punishment.

He had tried to preserve the complexity of a defeated general. Now his own life was being compressed into a criminal charge.

III. The Choice

In prison, the possible roads narrowed.

In the Letter to Ren An, Sima Qian says his family was poor and his resources insufficient to redeem his sentence. “None of my friends rescued me; those close at hand said not one word.”

Castration, the so-called palace punishment: brutal damage inflicted by the state. He underwent it and did not end his own life. Whenever we speak of his “choice,” the coercion that narrowed his possibilities must remain visible.

Why keep living?

His answer to Ren An was that the book was unfinished. This long letter is not only a declaration of purpose. It also answers a friend who had urged him to recommend worthy men: what could a person in his damaged social position still do?

“All people must die; some deaths weigh more than Mount Tai, others less than a wild goose’s feather. What they serve makes the difference.” Confronted with the death expected of him by the gentlemanly culture around him, he sought another way to explain remaining alive.

He imagined his execution counting for no more than “a single hair lost from nine oxen,” no different from an ant’s death. People would not necessarily honor him as a martyr to principle. They might merely say his crimes had caught up with him. Death would not automatically restore his authority to interpret his own life.

He endured because he grieved that something within him remained unexpressed, and that his writing might die with him, unknown to those who came later.

He traced a lineage of writing from frustrated purpose: King Wen confined and elaborating the Book of Changes; Confucius in distress and composing the Spring and Autumn Annals; Qu Yuan exiled and writing Encountering Sorrow; Zuo Qiuming blind and leaving the Discourses of the States; Sun Bin mutilated and discussing warfare; Lü Buwei ordered to Shu and his Annals transmitted; Han Fei imprisoned in Qin, associated with The Difficulties of Persuasion and Solitary Indignation.

“All these men had something pent up within them and could not make their way prevail; so they related the past and thought of those to come.” A road closed. They entrusted what they could not finish saying to the future.

He placed himself in that future too. His father’s charge remained, but the ambition to form an account of his own had become his own pursuit. He acknowledged that people love life, fear death and care for their families. Staying alive did not require proving himself free of those feelings.

He would not let the unfinished book vanish with him. For this person, remaining to undertake that work became a road harder than dying, and still a road he wished to take.

IV. Living Is Harder Than Dying

After the punishment, Sima Qian described an ordinary day in words that still hurt to read.

“Whenever I think of this disgrace, sweat breaks out on my back and soaks my clothes.”

“My bowels turn nine times in a single day.” The pain was not an event that occurred once and then became the past.

“At home I am dazed, as though I had lost something; outside, I do not know where I am going.” Here is no serene conqueror of every difficulty. Here is someone willing to tell a friend that the pain remains.

Bodily injury and social humiliation were entangled. He repeatedly described the contempt directed at a mutilated man. But being treated as less than human does not make someone less than a person. The shame belongs to the order that inflicts and stigmatizes the injury, not to an identity the victim deserves.

He later served as Director of the Palace Secretariat, still employed at court. Work continued, close to the power that had injured him. The letter lets us hear how far an outward position can stand from the experience within it.

In Plato’s telling, Socrates refused escape and went to his death. Sima Qian endured punishment and remained to write. One kind of persistence is often commemorated as noble; the other was once dismissed as clinging shamefully to life. The comparison exposes that unevenness of judgment.

It does not divide the dead into the “clean” and the living into the “dirty.” Nor need it crown Sima Qian a greater hero than Socrates. A humiliated person still possesses dignity. A life that must continue deserves understanding too.

A story of death often has a closing scene. A story of survival must include tomorrow, the day after and the next unfinished chapter. We do not know each of his days. We do know that he continued writing, and left a letter that refused to make pain look beautiful.

Beethoven composed after severe hearing loss; Sima Qian wrote history after mutilation. Bodily limitation did not stop all their work. These two kinds of sustained labor illuminate each other.

Nor are they identical cases. Disease differs from judicial violence. Yet neither person’s suffering can be separated into a purely physical half and a purely social half. Beethoven also experienced isolation; Sima Qian also suffered bodily injury.

Physical pain is not always something one can adapt to; social pain is not always something one cannot. A smaller and weightier fact is enough: he wrote about pain, and he kept writing history. The existence of the work does not justify the injury.

V. Between Heaven and Humanity

He brought the book into being.

The Records of the Grand Historian, or Shiji. The Self-Preface gives its total as 130 chapters and 526,500 characters: twelve Basic Annals, ten Tables, eight Treatises, thirty Hereditary Houses and seventy Biographies. Not merely a long row of lives, but a structure supported by different ways of examining history.

From the Yellow Emperor to the age of Emperor Wu: ancient traditions, the rise and fall of dynasties, and finally the historian’s own present. The immense reach conventionally called three thousand years becomes a succession of chapters a reader can enter.

In the Letter to Ren An he states the famous ambition: “To investigate the meeting of heaven and humanity, to comprehend the changes between past and present, and to form an account of my own.”

To investigate heaven and humanity: how do the order of the world and human action meet? Is a kingdom’s rise or fall heaven’s mandate or people’s doing? In the Biography of Bo Yi, good men starve or die young while a violent man enjoys a long life. “This so-called way of heaven: is it right, or is it not?” No elegant formula of deserved reward closes the question.

To comprehend change: how do institutions, wars, wealth and personal choices transform a situation together? Tables set simultaneous events alongside one another. Treatises follow ritual, music, calendars, waterways and economic affairs. Lives bring us close to particular people.

To form an account of his own: to develop a considered argument through inheritance and inquiry. A judgment is not correct merely because it is his. But the evidence should not remain a pile for which nobody takes responsibility.

In this series’ vocabulary, each clause is a kind of construction: a philosophical question, a historical arrangement, a judgment someone undertakes to answer for.

Confucius spoke of transmitting rather than creating; Sima Qian also described himself as relating old events. Inheritance and invention are not sealed rooms. Selection, arrangement and connection already give judgment a shape. In many chapters, “The Grand Historian remarks” then lets us hear his voice directly.

Homeric epic invites reflection on the long relationship between oral telling and written texts. The Shiji shows archives, inherited reports, inquiry and stories of people interweaving. Xiang Yu has his story, Liu Bang his, Han Xin his. A name grows a face again.

Xiang Yu lost, yet occupies a Basic Annal. Confucius had no hereditary realm, yet has a Hereditary House. Chen She’s uprising failed, but his challenge remains: “Are kings, nobles, generals and ministers born to their stations?” Political power, historical consequence and cultural transmission offer different measures by which to find someone a place.

Telling Xiang Yu’s tragedy does not erase criticism of his violence and self-conceit. Writing about wandering knights or merchants does not endorse everything they did. Someone can deserve to be seen and still be subject to judgment.

“Every person is an end” expresses this essay’s modern ethical aspiration as a reader, not a claim that Sima Qian had already formulated Kant’s principle. A claim closer to the book is enough: a person need not be reduced to a symbol on a table of winners and losers.

VI. Sima Qian and Socrates

Let us put Socrates and Sima Qian beside each other once more.

In Plato’s Crito a friend proposes escape and Socrates refuses. In the Phaedo we enter the conversations and death of his last day. A friend’s voice carries that farewell to those who come later.

Sima Qian’s writing puts a different question before us: how to continue after severe punishment, and how to give unfinished work a future. He did not freely choose to be injured. He tried to choose what might remain possible after the injury.

Neither man allows an external verdict to say everything about him. One moves toward a final conversation; the other returns to an unfinished book. A comparable persistence takes shape in very different circumstances.

Socrates’s commitment had its long, ordinary days too. It did not spring into existence in the few minutes of drinking poison. After his death the dialogues continued to be written, read and contested. We cannot say that his purpose was wholly completed and no longer needed time.

Sima Qian’s task needed time in a particularly tangible way. Materials had to be arranged, chapters written, a voice given shape. The father’s undertaking needed the son’s hands; later it would need readers and copyists to take it further.

Plato lets the figure of Socrates keep speaking, but is not his transparent substitute. Sima Qian wrote for himself, but still needed other people to preserve his work. One person’s judgment cannot simply be delegated; a book’s survival cannot depend on one person alone.

Socrates’s last day, and the later years of Sima Qian’s life whose exact duration we cannot fix: one gives us a concentrated farewell, the other an image of continuance without applause. This is not a competition between short pain and long pain.

“Socrates died once; Sima Qian went on living, day after day.” This literary contrast tries to illuminate another kind of persistence: remaining, and continuing the work through days in which understanding may never arrive.

VII. Under Pressure to Build

This series has followed many people who were pressed, constrained or urged onward.

In the traditional story, Yin Xi asks Laozi to leave a text. Wallace’s letter confronts Darwin’s accumulated research with questions of joint publication and priority. War changes Du Fu’s poetry. Deafness changes the conditions in which Beethoven works and meets other people.

But a request is not compulsion; a letter does not create a theory from nothing. Du Fu was a poet before the An Lushan Rebellion. Beethoven did not owe his possession of music to illness.

Likewise, castration did not manufacture a historian out of a blank person. Sima Qian was already writing. He brought travel, reading, official experience, his father’s charge and developing judgments into prison with him.

Punishment changed the language in which he understood his writing. In the letter and Self-Preface, earlier writers who had endured confinement became companions. The unfinished book took on a more urgent meaning: it held something he would not willingly let disappear with him.

Reading Xiang Yu surrounded by Chu songs at Gaixia, singing in grief to Lady Yu; reading the captive Han Xin’s bitterness that once the cunning hares are dead the good hunting dogs are cooked: a reader may hear echoes of the historian’s own situation. Resemblance does not establish the cause of composition. These chapters also depend on sources, narrative skill and judgments about different people. They cannot all be made into his secret autobiography.

If the phrase “the vessel is carved, and the remainder enters the work” retains a use, it is within these metaphorical limits: experience exceeds an old explanation, and someone tries to give the unspoken a form.

Du Fu brings the fractures of life into poetry. Beethoven keeps exploring sound under altered conditions. Sima Qian joins old accounts to his own questions and makes history. Each responds. The injury does not do the writing for any of them.

There is no warrant for concluding that depth requires suffering, still less for thanking the executioner’s knife. Many injured people leave no work; their lives do not therefore weigh less. What deserves our care is human labor, not the power that inflicted the wound.

VIII. What He Could Not Know

The Shiji survived. The final part of Sima Qian’s life did not leave an equally clear record.

The date, place and circumstances of his death cannot be securely established from the surviving evidence. The Book of Han describes his work, punishment and later office, and preserves his long letter, but supplies no definite death notice.

A man who spent so much care recording others has a missing page at the end of his own life.

He could not know the whole future of his book. The Book of Han says the work gradually emerged after his death, and that his grandson Yang Yun spread it more widely under Emperor Xuan. From a book preserved by a family to a book on more and more readers’ desks, there remained a road for others to travel.

The received text bears losses, supplements and later editorial traces. Ban Gu and others, in compiling the Book of Han, inherited elements of its annalistic-biographical structure but made a history of one dynasty rather than a comprehensive history. Later official histories changed it again, eventually forming the sequence known as the Twenty-Four Histories, with the Shiji first. Inheritance also rebuilds.

Du Fu did not know how future centuries would honor him as Poet Sage. Bach did not know the worlds his music would enter. Sima Qian did not know how readers two millennia later would open his book. Not knowing future fame does not mean that all three considered their entire lives failures.

Du Fu wrote of “nothing accomplished, tears falling like rain,” but a line can hold a moment without becoming a lifetime’s final verdict. Bach’s music passed onward through family and pupils. In his anguished letter, Sima Qian too was already placing hope in people still to come. A work can hold disappointment and open a future at the same time.

Poems, fugues and histories outlive their makers. Time does not automatically reward genius. Families, copyists, scholars, publishers, performers and readers repeatedly make what remains happen again.

The Shiji can still be read. Two thousand years are not compensation settling the debt of his pain. They are a space in which the future people he imagined keep arriving.

IX. The Living Man

Another person arrives at the bridgehead imagined by this series.

He is thin, his back slightly bent. Socrates stands lightly, Kant steadily, Qin Shi Huang stiffly, Washington loosely. Beethoven clenches a fist; Laozi seems already to have slipped away. Sima Qian stands as though still carrying something.

One hundred and thirty chapters. More than half a million characters. A reach of time extending from ancient traditions to the Han. Work not yet handed over has left its weight upon his back.

His pain cannot be ranked against Du Fu’s sorrow, Beethoven’s fierceness or Jesus’s suffering. No one’s situation is interchangeable with another’s. Here our eyes linger on the person whose story did not end in a magnificent farewell, but continued through ordinary days.

He once told himself that the book remained unfinished, and stayed. His life did not need a book to license its existence. The book gave him a path forward that he could recognize as his own.

If we let the two men speak on the bridge, let it be in this imagined exchange:
Socrates says: I would not abandon my reasons by escaping.
Sima Qian says: I would not let what remains unsaid die with me.

Both sentences can be heard. The commitment that goes toward death and the commitment that remains to write leave room for each other at the bridge.

There are no “clean dead” and “dirty living” at the bridgehead. There are people whose bodies have been injured and whose names have been judged, without their losing their place as persons.

The book was made. “To investigate the meeting of heaven and humanity, to comprehend the changes between past and present, and to form an account of my own.” The wish entered writing; the writing then left its author.

In this imagined ending he can finally set the book down and walk toward the far end of the bridge.

His figure recedes. The ending the evidence cannot give us remains blank. The book opens toward us.

Notes

The deathbed charge, appointment three years later, calendar reform, structure and stated size of the book are drawn from the Shiji’s Self-Preface. Its family genealogy is a self-presentation, not independently verified evidence of uninterrupted officeholding. The Han office was not a modern historical institute. Disputed reconstructions of birth, punishment, composition and death are not turned here into an exact age or a further decade of daily writing.

For Li Ling’s campaign and the court’s response, compare Book of Han 54 with the Letter to Ren An preserved in chapter 62. The letter is a participant’s account, not the complete legal file. It says his resources could not redeem him but specifies no price of 500,000 cash. Redemption, punishment and suicide must not be presented as a uniform, freely available menu for every condemned Han subject. The later execution of Li Ling’s family belongs to a separate stage of events.

The dating of the Letter to Ren An remains disputed. Chinese quotations principally follow the Book of Han text with modern punctuation; wording differs in other transmitted versions. The letter’s lineage of frustrated writers expresses Sima Qian’s argument, not an established chronology of composition. Compare Shiji 85, on Lü Buwei, and 63, on Laozi and Han Fei: the works mentioned cannot simply all be dated after exile or imprisonment.

The complete maxim about heaven and humanity, change and an account of one’s own appears in the Letter to Ren An. The Self-Preface separately speaks of forming an account and gives 130 chapters and 526,500 characters. That is the work’s reported extent, not a fresh character count of every surviving edition. Losses, additions and later material complicate any single moment of completion.

The questions in the Biography of Bo Yi, the criticism closing Xiang Yu’s Basic Annals and the placement of Confucius and Chen She indicate different measures of historical importance. The categories are not modern moral prizes. Persons as ends, carving, construction, remainder and the Homer comparison are this essay’s interpretive vocabulary, not a claim that Sima Qian held Kantian ethics.

The Gaixia scene and Han Xin’s words in captivity are in Shiji 7 and 92. The Chinese quotation preserves 亨, used for ‘cook.’ These episodes can resonate with the historian’s experience without proving that every character is an autobiographical projection written after punishment. Violence is not made a necessary condition of the achievement.

Socrates’s refusal of escape and final day belong to Plato’s Crito and Phaedo respectively. Their comparison with Sima Qian does not rank heroism or time suffering in minutes. The stigma of mutilation is a social fact to examine, not an ethical judgment endorsed here. A person need not complete a work to justify staying alive.

Book of Han 62 records Sima Qian’s later appointment and says his work gradually emerged after his death and was spread by Yang Yun. This does not establish complete prior disappearance or the author’s conviction that his life had failed. The Book of Han inherited annalistic-biographical structures within a single-dynasty scope. Physical appearances, postures and conversations at the bridge are literary inventions, not a reconstructed death scene. Du Fu’s line comes from Ill Aboard a Boat, Writing My Feelings from My Pillow in Thirty-Six Rhymes, Presented to Friends and Relatives in Hunan, in Complete Tang Poems, volume 233.
