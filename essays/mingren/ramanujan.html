一百零八种人生 · 066 / 108 · 生命之后留下的工作
Great Lives · 066 / 108 · Work That Outlives a Life

拉马努金，梦

Ramanujan, Dreams

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、请您看看这些结果

1913年1月16日，马德拉斯的一名港务局职员给剑桥的哈代写了一封信。信里先交代薪水、求学经历与处境，随后附上密密麻麻的数学结果。那些关系没有按照一篇成熟论文的样子，把来路逐段铺好。一个远方的人请求另一人，先认真看一看。

写信的是斯里尼瓦萨·拉马努金。他刚过二十五岁生日，信中却自报大约二十三岁。他没有取得大学学位，也没有走完通常的数学训练道路。纸上的工作，比履历上能够给人信心的部分多得多。该拿什么证明自己值得对方花时间，这是他首先遇到的困难。

哈代和利特尔伍德看出了这些材料不寻常。有的结果已经为人所知，有的错误，有的需要补上条件，还有些陌生得令人停下来。他们面对的不是一个等着盖章的完整答案，而是一片值得认真进入的地方。真正的判断，要能同时辨认错误与创造力。

后来人喜欢记住哈代对那些奇异公式的惊叹。更能改变拉马努金生活的，是他没有让惊叹停在一句赞美上。他回信，继续询问，要求证明，也参与为对方争取研究条件。信寄回去，远处便有人知道：我的工作已经进入了一场会有下文的谈话。

想象一封来信躺在桌上，并不难。难的是承认，收信人手上的选择有真实后果。缺少熟悉的身份、写法和推荐，容易让一个问题还没被看见就先被归档。哈代看进了公式；印度已经帮助过拉马努金的人，也终于多了一条能够接续的路。

到剑桥并不是下一封信就完成的事。资助、家人的顾虑、跨海的安排，都需要时间与他人的奔走。数学可以在纸上越过很远的距离，一个人却还需要买船票，吃饭，说明自己将要去哪里，并想办法让留在家中的生活继续。

因此，这封信最动人的地方，不只是天才终于被发现。它还是一次请求得到了回应。拉马努金把自己做的东西递过去；有人认真接住，没有要求他先把自己变成那间办公室最熟悉的模样。

二、纸页以前的路

拉马努金1887年12月22日出生于印度南部的埃罗德，在库姆巴科南长大。父亲在布店做账，家里并不宽裕。他受过学校教育，也曾进入学院求学。数学之外的课程与考试使他的学业屡次受阻，奖学金失去，学位未能完成，生活没有顺着成绩最好的那一科自动展开。

少年时接触到卡尔的《纯粹数学基本结果概要》，给了他一大片可供探索的材料。书里密集地列出结果，许多推导很简略。一个读者可以顺着它复算、猜测、变换，再自己尝试新的联系。后来笔记本那种浓缩的写法，也有真实的阅读经历与工作习惯作为背景。

称他自学，并不意味着他从未学习别人的东西。他读过书，做过大量计算，向人请教，也把成果送进印度数学学会的刊物。1911年，他关于伯努利数的论文已经发表。那封寄往英国的信不是数学生活的诞生，而是他替已经存在的工作寻找更大的空间。

拉马钱德拉·拉奥等人曾帮助他获得生活上的支持。港务局的纳拉亚纳·艾耶与他讨论数学；其他朋友为他介绍、推荐、寻找机会。这些人没有人人都能看懂笔记的深处，却有人愿意先把时间和信任放进去。一个人的工作能够留下来，往往在成名前就经过这样的接力。

贫困最具体的样子，有时是一种计算方式：先在可擦写的石板上试，再把值得留下的结果抄进纸本。石板可以擦掉重来，纸要省着用。我们无法从一条简短公式倒推出每一步当年都怎样发生，但能够看见，工作材料也在参与决定什么被保存、什么没有留下。

后来马德拉斯大学给予研究资助，他才有了更稳定的时间。对数学家而言，钱的意义在这里并不神秘。它可以买下不用处理账目的一个下午，让困在脑中的问题继续留在那里，而不是等到深夜才重新开始。研究自由有时首先是一份可以支付生活的安排。

这也让帝国中心与殖民地之间的距离变得具体。机会、图书、声望和决定谁值得被听见的渠道，并没有均匀分布。拉马努金在印度已有工作和伙伴，去剑桥又获得重要的新条件。两个事实可以并存；承认后来者的帮助，不需要抹去他先前走过的路。

三、梦有自己的语言

拉马努金的家庭奉祀纳玛吉丽女神。她与毗湿奴的那拉辛哈化身相联系，属于他的宗教生活所处的世界。关于公式在梦中出现、女神赐予启示的叙述，在回忆与传记中不断被讲起。那些叙述告诉我们，人也会用信仰的语言，理解一个念头怎样来到自己面前。

家人起初对跨海远行有顾虑，其中包含当时的种姓与宗教禁忌。传记记载，母亲后来以女神在梦中允诺的方式接受了他的出行。我们可以保留这个家庭讲述，而不把梦的内容当作外部能够核验的事件。对于一个家庭，允许离开的语言有时也必须来自他们熟悉的世界。

他在1914年3月启程，随后抵达英国。带过去的不只有笔记，还有饮食习惯、信仰、对家人的牵挂，以及那些让陌生地方成为陌生地方的小事。一个人在学术上获得邀请，并不意味着他已经知道那里的每顿饭、每一个冬日应当怎样度过。

梦值得被听见，不是因为它可以免除检验，而是因为它属于这个人如何生活、如何理解自己。一个结果是否成立，需要数学的理由；一个人为何珍惜某种经验，却不一定只剩一种允许他说出口的语言。两件事分开，交谈反而能够继续。

哈代的宗教立场与他不同，这并没有阻止共同研究。他们能够把一个公式放在两人之间，看条件、看计算、看哪里需要证明。共享数学的工作，不要求先把彼此的整个世界观整理成一样。相处的空间，有时来自知道哪些分歧暂时可以留在那里。

我们也可以暂时不替那些梦收尾。既不宣布女神替公式担保，也不急着用一个“直觉”把人说尽。读书、反复演算、记忆、感受与不知怎样忽然形成的联系，都在同一份生活中。一个名称帮助我们谈论经验，却未必已经交代了经验的全部来路。

四、两个人在桌前

1914年的剑桥，给拉马努金带来了能深入讨论问题的同行、图书与研究时间。他与哈代展开合作，也发表自己的论文。这里确实发生了生长。他带去已经形成的问题与方法，桌子另一边的人也因此改变。两个人各自已有的工作，开始有了共同的下一步。

他们共同研究整数的分拆。把一个整数写成若干正整数之和，不计排列顺序，有多少种办法？四可以是四，也可以是三加一、二加二、二加一加一、一加一加一加一。问题从手指能数的地方开始，很快长到无法靠逐项枚举轻松处理的规模。

哈代与拉马努金找到了描述分拆数增长的强大方法。这是一项共同创造的研究，不是一人交出神谕、另一人补齐格式。直觉帮助人猜到值得去的地方，估计、推导与证明让那里成为别人也能走到的地方。两个人的能力，在具体工作中互相改变。

证明也可以这样理解：你看见的关系，怎样让一个没有你的记忆、没有你的信仰、也没有当时灵感的人，仍然能够独立确认？把步骤交出来，不只是服从一种规范。它让下一位读者不用因为你是天才而相信你，也能在发现错误时指出是哪一步。

拉马努金本人会推导，也会证明。笔记里大量省略步骤，与一个人完全没有理由，是两回事。他留下的成果有的可以成立，有的要修正或限定。这样看待他，并不使惊人的部分减少，反而让他的工作与我们的工作处在同一个可以学习、可以犯错的世界。

战争很快改变了英国的生活。素食需求、自己做饭、供给困难与离家之远，都使日常更费力。到了1917年，他严重患病，随后长时间接受治疗。研究条件变好，并不等于一个人的身体、吃饭与孤独便已经安排妥当。桌前的合作之外，还有桌外需要照料的生活。

1918年，他当选皇家学会会员，也成为三一学院院士。荣誉改变了他的学术身份，病床上的日常仍然需要照料。一个机构会珍惜某人的成果，还需要继续学着怎样接待带着身体、习惯和脆弱走进门来的那个人。

五、一七二九，也有下一问

哈代后来回忆，去探望在普特尼养病的拉马努金时，他乘坐的出租车号码是1729。他觉得这个数有些乏味，随口提起。病床上的人却马上认出了其中有趣的关系。这一幕来自探访者的回忆，不妨先照它原有的亲近感来读。

1729是能够用两种不同方式写成两个正整数立方之和的最小正整数：1³加12³，也是9³加10³。一边是1与1728，另一边是729与1000。几下简单运算，读者就能检验两条路确实到了同一个地方；“最小”则还需要进一步排除更小的可能。

有意思的是，回忆没有停在这句漂亮回答。哈代接着问，若换成四次方呢？拉马努金想了想，说看不出明显的例子，估计第一个数会很大。那个会迅速看见关系的人，也有还不知道的地方。谈话仍然可以从那里往下走。

利特尔伍德曾用“私人朋友”形容正整数与他的关系，哈代转述了这个比喻。我们不必把数字真的变成人，便能体会它指向的熟悉。长期相处之后，别人扫一眼便略过的脸，在你眼里会有细小的区别；数字的组合与变化，也可以积累这样的密度。

亲近既有天分，也有劳动。反复试算、从例子寻找规律、变换猜想，都在哈代对他工作的记述里。那一瞬间的快，背后可能有很长时间的慢。说出答案的时候看不见全部过程，不表示过程从来不存在。

这个故事于是可以留下两种东西。一个是数字里未曾注意的关系，另一个是承认不知道也不会失去资格的谈话。假如我们只允许拉马努金每次都答对，便会把这位能继续提问的人，关进自己替他造好的神话里。

六、看见与说清之间

康托尔也使人感到，一个熟悉的世界忽然有了陌生的深处。他以严格论证区分无穷集合的大小，拉马努金则在级数、连分数、分拆与函数之间发现大量联系。两种工作没有必要排出谁看得更多；它们分别改变了人能够怎样提出数学问题。

康托尔的病，拉马努金的病，也不应被合并成“看见太多”的代价。思想的大胆并不提供医学上的因果解释。把患病写成天才命定的价格，很容易让本来应当追问的治疗、支持与生活条件，变成只需感叹的背景。

法拉第和麦克斯韦让我们看见过另一种接续。前者已有实验与理论，后者继续发展，并使关系获得新的表达。拉马努金与哈代同样不是无言的直觉遇到没有想象力的规则。两边都在思考，只是已有经验与擅长的工作方式有所不同。

同贞德并读时，也会碰到两种不同的问题：一个人怎样叙述自己听见或看见的东西，以及某个命题能否交给他人检验。尊重人的经验，可以与认真追问她或他的判断并存；既不必嘲笑，也不必撤销自己的判断。

王尔德与拉马努金都使读者看见帝国中心的门槛，却没有经历同一种命运。王尔德受到刑罚，拉马努金获得了重要合作与承认，同时遭遇战争、疾病和日常困难。相似处可以帮助提问，差别则提醒我们，人的一生不能被一幅已经画好的图吞掉。

“看见”与“说清”之间，常有值得共同工作的距离。它不是一道高低贵贱的分界。有人先发现方向，有人提出反例，同一个人也会在不同阶段承担不同工作。最好的相遇，不是替对方决定他只能是哪一种人，而是让彼此多出一些能做的事。

七、回去的人与留下的人

1919年，拉马努金回到印度。他仍然很虚弱，贾纳基参与照料他。关于疾病，当时与后来的解释并不一致：结核病曾被提出，后来的研究又讨论肝阿米巴病等可能。隔着百年的材料，我们能够确认严重的病痛，却不必假装已经握有一次完整的临床检查。

贾纳基在1909年与他结婚时，还是个孩子。那段婚姻不能只用“当时习俗”一句带过，更不能把她一生压缩成等候丈夫归来的一幕。她在这个故事里有自己的年龄、处境与漫长的后来。照顾一位数学家，也不会使照顾者的人生自动成为他的附属。

许多年后，研究者从她那里得到照片与回忆，帮助世人保存拉马努金的形象。那些记忆来自一个长期活着的人，不是传奇自己会保管的东西。我们今天能够看见谁，常常还依赖另一个人记得什么、保留了什么，以及愿意把什么交出来。

1920年1月，拉马努金仍写信给哈代，介绍自己称为“仿theta函数”的新对象。读者不需要先掌握这个名称，便能理解那个动作：他还在发现关系，还想让远方的人知道。疾病没有使这些问题突然失去吸引力，但继续工作也不是他必须完成的最后义务。

4月26日，他去世，三十二岁。留下来的纸页里还有别人尚未理解的东西。年纪与成果放在一起，令人难过；让人难过的首先还是那个人的生命停止了，而不只是数学世界少得了多少定理。他本来还可以有更多普通的日子。

回家没有让疾病退去，荣誉也没有替照料完成工作。后来者能做的，是保存、阅读、查证，也记得纸页之外的人。把公式继续向前带，并不要求把病床变成祭坛，或把另一个人的漫长余生放在故事结尾之外。

八、一本笔记的后半生

1976年，乔治·安德鲁斯在三一学院图书馆查阅资料时，认出了拉马努金晚年留下的一批散页。它们后来被称作“遗失的笔记本”。那不是一箱从来无人碰过的神秘遗物；材料经过其他数学家研究、保存、整理与移交，才来到能够被他重新读懂的地方。

沃森、兰金、惠特克等人属于这段传递的历史，露西·斯莱特的指引也在安德鲁斯的回忆里。发现不仅是一个人眼睛发亮的时刻，还包括有人没有扔掉纸，有人把它送进档案，有人知道该让谁看看。纸能够等到合适的读者，本身已有许多前提。

安德鲁斯、伯恩特及许多合作者，继续为笔记中的结果寻找证明、补足条件、辨认旧结果与新发现。关于仿theta函数的理解，也经过一代代研究，在二十一世纪初获得重要的新框架。这不是整个数学界等了一百年才一起醒来，而是长时间工作终于使一些线索连成更大的图景。

一个保留下来的式子，既是结果，也可能是问题。它在哪些条件下成立？相似的形式为何有的可行、有的不可行？能不能从这里找到更一般的关系？后人的劳动不是给遗物镀金，而是让纸上的内容重新进入可以争论、可以检验、可以生长的生活。

我们仍然不可能完整重现拉马努金每次发现的心路。石板擦掉了许多试算，简短的笔记省略了过程，回忆也各有视角。留下这种不知道，不妨碍证明一个具体结果。对来路保持谦逊，与对眼前的论证保持严格，可以发生在同一个人身上。

这或许是“梦”最值得保留的地方：一个人到达问题的全部路径，不能被最后的论文替代。但他也能把其中一些东西写出来，让没有做过那个梦的人接着想。别人不必进入他的内心，仍然能够与他留下的工作相遇。

九、这一次，先别擦掉

最后回到第五乐章虚构的桥头。拉马努金带着一块石板走过来。他走了很远，桌边有人替他挪出一个位置。先坐下，放下手里的东西。衣服、身体、一路的疲倦和心里还惦记的问题，都随着他一起来到这里。

法拉第的装置还在，麦克斯韦的纸上留着改动。拉马努金把石板靠在旁边，写下一条关系。有人看懂一部分，有人完全不懂。此刻不懂，并不妨碍把椅子让出来；愿意让他坐下，也不等于承诺他写的每个式子都对。

哈代来到桌边。虽然他不是这条人物长卷中单列的一篇，我们仍邀请他进入这个想象。他看了一会儿，指向一个步骤。拉马努金拿起粉笔，另写一行。这场交流里，两人都可以指出问题，也都可能从对方那里学到自己原来不知道的东西。

康托尔把自己的纸挪近一些。纸上有完整的论证，也有后来仍会被追问的问题。他不需要把无穷举得比别人的公式更高。桌面足够大，可以容下不同的问题，各自在需要的地方要求理由。

贞德可以讲自己的声音，拉马努金可以讲梦，王尔德可以保留那句尚未说出口的话。没有人被迫先交出生命中珍惜的语言，才能获得一个座位。谁若对某个结论不同意，也可以留下来继续说，不必把分歧变成对人的驱逐。

贾纳基也有一张椅子。这里不只请她来递水，或替别人的天才作证。她可以谈自己的生活，也可以安静一会儿。契诃夫先问谁累了，把讨论暂停，并不会使桌上的数学消失。

拉马努金习惯性地抬手，准备擦掉石板。旁边有人递来几张空白纸。先等等，把中间的几行也留下吧。不是每个念头都一定值得保存，但此刻至少不必因为纸张不够，让一个还没来得及解释的过程立刻消失。

他重新落笔。别人等着，没有催他把自己的全部来源说清。这里需要的只是眼前这一步，以及下一步可以从哪里开始。梦仍是他的梦，纸却能交给另一个人。

桥上的灯照着石板与纸。没有人必须先成为无所不知的天才，才值得拥有足够的光。拉马努金把这一行写完，停了一下。这一次，擦去之前，还有时间让别人读。

I. Please Look at These Results

On 16 January 1913, a clerk at the Madras Port Trust wrote to G. H. Hardy in Cambridge. He began with his salary, education and circumstances, then offered a dense collection of mathematical results. The pages did not provide the carefully prepared route a finished paper would normally give its reader. Someone far away was asking someone else to look seriously.

Srinivasa Ramanujan had recently turned twenty-five, although the letter described him as about twenty-three. He had no university degree and had not completed a conventional mathematical education. The work on the pages was much more substantial than the credentials with which he could introduce it. How do you show that you deserve another person's time before that person has read you?

Hardy and Littlewood recognized something extraordinary. Some results were familiar, some were mistaken, some needed qualifications, and others were profoundly surprising. This was not a complete answer awaiting an official stamp. It was a place worth entering. Sound judgment had to recognize errors without allowing those errors to conceal the originality surrounding them.

Hardy's subsequent expressions of wonder became famous. What changed Ramanujan's circumstances was that wonder turned into action. He replied, asked further questions and requested proofs; he also helped secure conditions in which the work could continue. A letter travelled back, allowing its recipient to know that his mathematics had entered a conversation with a future.

It is easy to imagine an unsolicited envelope resting on a desk. Harder to recognize the consequences of what happens next. Unfamiliar credentials or presentation can consign a question to a category before anyone examines it. Hardy looked into the mathematics. The people already supporting Ramanujan in India gained another possible connection through which their efforts could continue.

Getting to Cambridge took more than a return letter. Funding, family concerns and arrangements for crossing the sea required time and other people's work. Mathematics can travel a great distance on paper. A person must still buy a passage, eat, explain where he is going and find some way for the lives remaining at home to go on.

The letter's importance therefore includes something more ordinary than a genius being discovered. A request received a response. Ramanujan offered what he had been doing, and someone took it seriously without first requiring him to become the sort of applicant whose background made the Cambridge office entirely comfortable. A conversation began before all the differences had been settled.

II. Before the Pages Travelled

Ramanujan was born on 22 December 1887 in Erode, southern India, and grew up in Kumbakonam. His father worked for a cloth merchant, and money was limited. He attended school and went on to college. Difficulties with subjects outside mathematics disrupted his progress; he lost a scholarship and completed no degree. Excellence in one subject did not automatically organize a whole life.

As a teenager he encountered G. S. Carr's Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure Mathematics. Its densely collected results, often accompanied by little derivation, provided an enormous territory to explore. A reader could calculate, conjecture and transform, then try to build another connection. The concentrated form of his later notebooks had a history of reading and practice behind it.

Calling him self-taught should leave room for that history. He learned from books, performed extensive calculations, sought people out and contributed to the Journal of the Indian Mathematical Society. His paper on Bernoulli numbers appeared in 1911. The letter to England did not mark the birth of his mathematical life; it sought more room for work that already existed.

Ramachandra Rao and others offered practical support. Narayana Aiyar at the Port Trust discussed mathematics with him; friends introduced him, recommended him and searched for opportunities. Not everyone could follow every part of the notebooks. Some nevertheless committed time and confidence. The survival of a person's work often depends on such exchanges long before a famous name becomes attached to it.

Poverty can become visible in a method of calculation: work first on a reusable slate, then copy what seems worth keeping onto paper. A slate can be wiped and used again; paper has to be conserved. The final expression does not tell us everything that once happened around it. Materials help determine which parts of a thought survive for later readers.

A research scholarship from the University of Madras eventually gave him more dependable time. Money's significance here was straightforward. It could purchase an afternoon without account books, allowing a problem to remain present rather than be postponed until late at night. The freedom to investigate sometimes begins with an arrangement adequate to pay for ordinary living.

The distance between a colonial country and an imperial centre becomes concrete through these circumstances. Libraries, recognition and channels of access were unevenly distributed. Ramanujan had work and supporters in India, and Cambridge offered important additional opportunities. Both facts matter. Recognizing what a later encounter made possible does not require erasing the road already travelled to reach it.

III. A Dream Has Its Own Language

Ramanujan's family worshipped Namagiri, associated with Narasimha, an incarnation of Vishnu. Accounts of a goddess revealing mathematics and of formulas appearing in dreams recur in recollections and biographies. Such stories tell us something about the language in which a person and his community can understand the arrival of an idea. They belong to a lived religious world.

His family's early reservations about the voyage included caste and religious prohibitions on crossing the sea. Biographical accounts describe his mother accepting the journey after a dream of Namagiri granting permission. We can preserve that family account without treating the dream's contents as an independently witnessed event. Permission to depart may itself need to be expressed in the language a family knows.

He sailed in March 1914 and reached England the following month. Along with his notebooks went habits of eating, religious commitments, concern for people at home and the small uncertainties that make a foreign place foreign. An academic invitation does not tell its recipient how every meal, winter morning or lonely evening will actually be managed.

A dream deserves to be heard because it belongs to someone's experience, not because it exempts a statement from examination. Whether a mathematical result holds requires mathematical reasons. Why a person cherishes an experience need not be expressed in just one authorized vocabulary. Keeping those questions distinct can create more room for conversation than either dismissal or unquestioning assent.

Hardy's religious position differed from Ramanujan's. The difference did not prevent them from putting a formula between them and examining its conditions, calculations and proof. Sharing mathematical work required neither man to make the other's entire outlook identical with his own. Cooperation sometimes becomes possible by understanding which disagreements can remain while a particular question is investigated together.

We can leave some of the dreams unfinished. Neither divine certification nor the single word intuition provides a complete account of a life. Reading, repeated calculation, memory and relationships that suddenly become visible all belong to the same history. A name helps us speak about an experience; it need not exhaust everything that allowed the experience to occur.

IV. Two People at a Table

Cambridge in 1914 offered Ramanujan colleagues able to discuss his problems deeply, books and time for research. He collaborated with Hardy and published independently. Important development took place there. He had arrived with questions of his own, however, and those questions changed the work of the person across the table. The exchange had more than one direction.

Together they investigated partitions: how many ways can a positive integer be written as a sum of positive integers, disregarding their order? Four permits five possibilities: four; three plus one; two plus two; two plus one plus one; and four ones. A question small enough to count on fingers quickly grows beyond anything comfortably handled by listing each possibility.

Hardy and Ramanujan developed powerful methods for describing the growth of partition numbers. This was shared mathematical creation. Insight suggested promising directions; estimates, derivations and proofs made those directions accessible to someone else. Neither man could be reduced to a permanent job of delivering revelations or checking punctuation. Their abilities met within particular problems and changed what could be done.

A proof can be understood as an act directed towards a future reader. How can someone without your memory, faith or moment of inspiration independently establish what you have seen? Giving the steps means that this reader need not believe a statement merely because a genius announced it. It also gives disagreement a place to become specific: here is the step that fails.

Ramanujan himself derived results and supplied proofs. Omitted steps in a notebook do not establish that its writer had no reasoning. Some claims hold, others need correction or narrower conditions. This makes his extraordinary achievement part of a world in which people can learn, test and make mistakes. The brilliance becomes more substantial when it does not require infallibility.

War soon altered daily life in Britain. His vegetarian requirements, cooking, difficult supplies and distance from home all made ordinary living more demanding. He became seriously ill in 1917 and spent long periods under care. Better research opportunities did not automatically take care of his body, food or isolation. Beyond the collaborative table there was still a life needing practical attention.

In 1918 he was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society and a Fellow of Trinity College. Recognition changed his academic standing; everyday life in a sickroom still required care. An institution that values someone's results also has to learn how to welcome the person arriving with those results: a body, habits, needs and vulnerabilities rather than a disembodied supply of ideas.

V. 1729 Has a Next Question

Hardy recalled visiting Ramanujan while he was ill at Putney. His taxi had carried the number 1729, which he remarked seemed rather dull. Ramanujan immediately recognized an interesting property. The scene comes to us through the visitor's recollection. It can first be read as a moment of companionship, before being made to carry the whole mythology of genius.

1729 is the smallest positive integer expressible in two different ways as the sum of two positive integer cubes: 1³ plus 12³, and 9³ plus 10³. On one side stand 1 and 1728, on the other 729 and 1000. A reader can check the equal sums directly; establishing that this is the smallest requires ruling out smaller possibilities as well.

The recollection does not end with the brilliant answer. Hardy then asked about fourth powers. After thinking, Ramanujan said he could see no obvious example and supposed the first number would be very large. Someone capable of immediately recognizing an extraordinary relationship also had things he did not know. The conversation could continue from that place without anyone losing his standing.

It was Littlewood whom Hardy credited with the description of positive integers as Ramanujan's personal friends. The metaphor communicates intimacy without requiring numbers literally to become people. After long acquaintance, a face others pass without a second glance contains many distinctions for you. Repeated encounters with numerical combinations can give them a comparable density of recognizable possibilities.

That familiarity involves work as well as talent. Hardy described extensive calculation, induction from examples and the rapid modification of hypotheses. Behind an answer's speed may stand a long period of patient attention. When the process is invisible at the instant someone speaks, that does not mean there was never any process to make the answer available.

The taxi story can therefore leave us with two things: an unexpected numerical relationship and a conversation in which not knowing remains permissible. If Ramanujan must answer every question instantly to remain himself, we have trapped a working person inside a legend of our own construction. His next question deserves room alongside the answer everybody already knows.

VI. Between Seeing and Making Clear

Cantor also made a familiar world seem to acquire unfamiliar depths. He distinguished sizes of infinity through rigorous argument; Ramanujan found relationships among series, continued fractions, partitions and functions. There is no useful ranking of who saw more. Each changed the kinds of mathematical questions people could formulate, with different tools and different routes through difficult material.

Their illnesses should not be combined into the price of having seen too much. Intellectual daring is not a medical explanation. Calling suffering the destined cost of genius can turn questions about treatment, support and living conditions into scenery that merely invites a sigh. The work deserves to be understood, and the illness deserves attention on its own terms.

Faraday and Maxwell offered another example of continuation. Faraday already had experiments and theoretical ideas; Maxwell developed further relationships and new expressions. Ramanujan and Hardy likewise brought thinking to both sides of their table. It was not speechless intuition meeting unimaginative rules. Different experiences and working strengths helped determine what each person could notice and offer next.

Read beside Joan of Arc, two distinct questions emerge. How someone describes a voice or vision differs from how a claim can be examined by others. Respect for an experience can coexist with questions about judgment. Neither ridicule nor surrender of one's own judgment is necessary to make another person worth hearing. The conversation can keep both questions in view.

Wilde and Ramanujan both reveal thresholds within an imperial centre, but their histories cannot be folded into one fate. Wilde suffered criminal punishment. Ramanujan found consequential collaboration and recognition, alongside war, illness and practical hardship. Similarities help us ask questions; differences prevent the person from disappearing into a diagram prepared before we began to read his life.

Between seeing a direction and making it clear lies space for shared work, rather than a ranking of superior and inferior people. Someone proposes a pattern, another offers a counterexample, and the same person may perform both tasks at different times. A fruitful encounter enlarges what people can do; it does not permanently assign each person one permitted kind of mind.

VII. The Person Who Returned

Ramanujan returned to India in 1919, still seriously unwell. Janaki helped care for him. Contemporary and later accounts of his illness differ: tuberculosis was considered, and subsequent research has proposed hepatic amoebiasis. Across a century of surviving records, the suffering is clear even where the diagnosis remains uncertain. We do not possess the equivalent of a fresh clinical examination.

Janaki had been a child when they married in 1909. That marriage deserves more than a passing reassurance that it was customary. Nor should her whole life become one picture of a wife waiting for a husband to return. She had an age, circumstances and a long life afterwards. Caring for a mathematician did not make that life an appendage of his.

Years later, researchers obtained photographs and recollections from her that helped preserve Ramanujan's public image. Memory was carried by someone who continued living; the legend did not keep its own records. Whom we can still see often depends on what another person remembered, retained and agreed to pass on. Preservation has a human history, not merely a famous subject.

In January 1920 Ramanujan wrote to Hardy about new objects he called mock theta functions. A reader need not master the term to understand the action. He was still discovering relationships and wanted someone far away to know. Illness had not suddenly removed their fascination. Continuing to work was nevertheless something he did, not a final obligation his importance imposed on him.

He died on 26 April, aged thirty-two. The papers contained work that others did not yet understand. The age beside the achievement is painful, but the first loss is the person's life, not a calculation of how many additional theorems mathematics might have received. He could also have had more ordinary days, unrecorded and valuable without a result attached.

Returning home did not undo the illness; honours did not perform the labour of care. Later people could preserve, read and investigate while remembering the person beyond the pages. Carrying the mathematics further does not require making an altar of the sickbed, or placing another person's long remaining life outside the point at which the story ends.

VIII. The Later Life of a Notebook

In 1976, while consulting material at Trinity College Library, George Andrews recognized a collection of pages from Ramanujan's final period. They became known as the lost notebook. These were not mysterious relics untouched since their author's death. Other mathematicians had studied, kept, sorted and transferred material before it reached the place where Andrews could recognize its significance afresh.

Watson, Rankin and Whittaker belong to that chain; Lucy Slater's guidance also appears in Andrews's account. Discovery includes more than the moment somebody's eyes light up. Someone kept the paper, someone placed it in an archive, someone knew who might find it interesting. A document's ability to wait for a suitable reader already depends on many earlier actions.

Andrews, Berndt and numerous collaborators continued finding proofs, establishing conditions and distinguishing earlier knowledge from new discoveries. Understanding of mock theta functions also developed across generations, receiving important new frameworks in the early twenty-first century. Mathematics had not collectively slept for a hundred years. Sustained work gradually allowed previously separated clues to form a larger and more powerful picture.

A surviving expression can be both a result and a question. Under what conditions does it hold? Why do similar-looking expressions behave differently? Can a more general relationship be found? Later work does not merely gild an heirloom. It returns the contents of the page to a life in which they can be examined, challenged and made to grow.

We still cannot reconstruct every route by which Ramanujan arrived at an idea. Slate calculations were erased, compressed notes omitted steps, and recollections differ in perspective. Leaving some of that unknown does not prevent the proof of a particular result. Humility about the history of discovery and exactness about the argument before us can inhabit the same person.

Perhaps this is what the title Dreams can preserve. The full path by which someone arrives at a question cannot be replaced by the finished paper. Yet part of what they found can become communicable. Someone who never had that dream can think further, encountering the work without being required to enter and reproduce its author's entire inner life.

IX. Wait Before You Wipe It Away

Return to the explicitly imagined bridge of this fifth movement. Ramanujan arrives carrying a slate. He has travelled a long way, and someone makes room at the table. He may sit down and put his things beside him. His clothes, his body, the fatigue of the journey and the questions still occupying him have all arrived together.

Faraday's apparatus is still there; Maxwell's paper carries corrections. Ramanujan leans the slate nearby and writes a relationship. Some understand part of it; others understand nothing. Not understanding yet need not prevent them from offering a chair. Offering a chair does not, in turn, promise that every expression he writes will be true. Hospitality and inquiry can share the table.

Hardy approaches. Although he is not a separately numbered life in this sequence, we can invite him into its fiction. He studies the writing and points to a step. Ramanujan picks up the chalk and adds a line. Either may identify a problem, and either may learn something from the other that he did not know when he sat down.

Cantor moves his own paper nearer. It contains arguments and questions that will continue to attract further investigation. He need not hold infinity above everyone else's formulas. The tabletop is large enough for different questions, each requiring reasons at the point where reasons matter. Attention to one need not make the others disappear or wait for permission to exist.

Joan may speak of her voices, Ramanujan of dreams; Wilde may retain a sentence he has not chosen to say. Nobody must surrender a cherished language of experience before receiving a seat. Someone who disagrees with a conclusion may also remain and speak. Disagreement need not turn into the expulsion of the person whose conclusion is being questioned.

Janaki has a chair as well. She has not been invited solely to bring water or testify to another person's genius. She can speak about her own life, or remain quiet for a while. Chekhov first asks whether someone is tired. Pausing the discussion to attend to an actual person will not make the mathematics vanish from the table.

Ramanujan raises his hand out of habit to clear the slate. Someone offers several blank sheets. Wait a moment; perhaps the intermediate lines can stay too. Not every thought must be preserved, but at least this process need not disappear immediately because there is insufficient paper. A material condition can change what becomes available for a future conversation.

He begins to write again. The others wait without demanding a complete account of where everything in his mind comes from. What they need now is this step, and somewhere from which the next step might begin. The dream remains his. The paper can nevertheless pass into another person's hands, carrying something they can work on together.

The bridge's lamp lights paper and slate. Nobody has to become an all-knowing genius before deserving enough light to see by. Ramanujan finishes the line and pauses. This time, before it is wiped away, there is time for someone else to read.
