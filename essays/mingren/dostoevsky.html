陀思妥耶夫斯基，比弗洛伊德早了三十年

Dostoevsky, Thirty Years Before Freud

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、刑场

1849年12月22日，俄历。换成公历，已经是1850年1月3日。圣彼得堡，谢苗诺夫练兵场。

二十八岁的陀思妥耶夫斯基和同案者被带到这里。他们因参与彼得拉舍夫斯基小组的活动被捕；读书、讨论社会问题和传播被禁的文字，在沙皇眼里已经足够危险。

死刑宣告了。几个人被绑到刑柱上。他在等，知道再过一会儿就可能轮到自己。

后来，他在写给哥哥的信里说，那最后的时刻，他想到的是哥哥和家人。他同身边的人告别。接着，停止行刑的信号响起，他们听见了减刑的决定。

死亡的仪式是真实经历，处决却事先被决定不再执行。权力仍要让他们把那几分钟走完。一个人的生命，在别人手里被演成了戏；恐惧却不能跟着布景一起撤掉。

《白痴》中，梅什金谈到一个死刑犯怎样分配最后几分钟：告别，想自己，再看眼前的世界。陀思妥耶夫斯基把亲历带进小说，却没有把小说写成刑场记录。记忆到了人物身上，重新长出了声音。

这个系列里，杜甫经历流离，贝多芬承受失聪，司马迁受过刑罚。陀思妥耶夫斯基在这里碰到了生命可能立刻终止的边界。几种痛苦不必排出深浅，也没有哪一种是伟大作品应付的学费。

他活了下来。可此后，“还有五分钟”便不再只是一种说法。

二、地下室

随后是西伯利亚的四年苦役和几年的军役。癫痫也长期困扰着他。监狱、病痛、债务和写作交织在这段人生里，却不能合成一张天才的病因表。

1864年，《地下室手记》发表。一个四十岁、退职的小官僚，开始对着读者絮絮叨叨。

他没有名字。他先宣称自己有病、恶毒，接着又拆自己的话，抢在别人判断之前，先把自己骂得更狠。

他渴望有人靠近，又受不了靠近时显出的脆弱。年轻女子丽莎真的来找他，他便把受过的羞辱转手给她，最后试图用钱把这场相遇降成一笔交易。她把钱留下了。

桌上的那张钞票，比他的长篇辩论还难以打发。她不是他脑子里的听众，不肯照他写好的位置站下去。

地下室人要反驳一种漂亮的安排：只要算清利益，人就会自动选择对自己有利的生活。他偏要毁掉计算，哪怕连自己也伤到，仿佛只有这样才能证明他不是琴键。

可伤害别人并不因此变成自由的勋章。小说让他的抗议和他的卑怯一起出现；苏格拉底关于知与善的追问、社会环境对人的影响，也并没有被这个人物一举取消。

《地下室手记》比标为1900年的《梦的解析》早了三十六年。在精神分析形成以前，小说已经让我们听见自我解释中的裂声。

弗洛伊德后来画出心理冲突的图纸；本我、自我、超我的模型更要到1923年才提出。陀思妥耶夫斯基则让一个声音住进房间，把桌角的划痕、等人时的窘迫、侮辱出口后的空荡，一样一样给你看。

这是两种工作。小说不是诊断书，地下室人也不是作者的病历。但读完以后，那个地下室很难只属于书里的人。

三、杀人之后

1866年，《罪与罚》。贫穷的前大学生拉斯科尔尼科夫，想试一试自己是不是有权越过一般人的法律。

他拿拿破仑作比：有的人杀人却被历史称为伟大；如果自己除掉一个他认为有害的放债人，再拿钱做好事，是否也能站到那一边？

他杀了阿廖娜。她的妹妹丽扎韦塔意外回来，他又杀了她。理论里那个“只牺牲一个”的算式，连现场都没有容纳进去。

而抢来的东西没有变成他许诺的善业。他把它们藏起来。随后是发热、恐惧、骄傲和自我辩护的反复交战。

他回到凶案发生的房间，拉响门铃。那声音又响了一遍。读者跟着他的手，感到一件已经做过的事仍在拉扯现在。

后来的人可以从这里想到弗洛伊德的重复问题，或拉康讨论的无法顺利进入解释的东西。但人物不是两套理论提前送来的病例；门铃也不因为有了术语，就不再刺耳。

波尔菲里步步逼近，索尼娅听他说出真相，又劝他承担责任。疲惫、恐惧、爱、自负和良心都挤在自首的路上，不能用“他只是撑不住了”把它们扫掉。

到了西伯利亚，他也没有立刻把自己的理论全部放下。小说结尾才让另一种生活显出开始的可能。法律上的认罪，与心里慢慢承认另一个人，原来不是同一时刻。

这里让人想到尼采对旧道德的挑战，却不能把尼采的“超人”直接认成一个崇拜拿破仑的杀人者。相邻的问题，不等于相同的答案。

陀思妥耶夫斯基逼近的，是更具体的一步：当我把别人从“一个人”改写成“可以牺牲的材料”，那套说法到了门铃、血迹和另一个人的目光前，还能不能站住？

四、弑父

1880年完成的《卡拉马佐夫兄弟》，又把我们带进一个家庭。父亲费奥多尔·巴甫洛维奇死了。

德米特里曾扬言杀父，最终为这桩他没有实施的谋杀被判刑。伊万善于辩论，却越来越承受不了自己的话与家中灾难之间的联系。跟随佐西马长老的阿廖沙试着留在人与人之间。斯麦尔加科夫，一个被视为父亲私生子的仆人，向伊万承认自己动了手。

四个人并不是四个同等的刑事嫌疑人，更不是激情、理性、信仰、阴暗各自穿上一件外衣。他们会躲闪，会互相误解，也会把责任推到对方身上。

斯麦尔加科夫拿伊万的话为自己辩护。伊万不能靠“我没有亲手杀人”让全部问题消失；但这也不使说话者与行凶者的责任自动相等。

1928年，弗洛伊德在《陀思妥耶夫斯基与弑父》中，把这部小说放进俄狄浦斯的解释框架，也试图用弑父愿望与罪疚解释作家的癫痫。

那是弗洛伊德的解释，不是由小说证实的病因。一个人的疾病，不能因为适合某个故事就被故事接管。

真正耐读的地方，恰恰是人物总比解释多一点。德米特里的暴烈不等于他实施了这次谋杀；伊万的思想不等于他把所有后果想明白；阿廖沙的信仰也不能让疼痛自动停止。

小说先把这些彼此牵扯的生命放到我们面前。理论随后走来，可以照亮一角，却不能让整间屋子只剩自己的颜色。

五、如果没有上帝

人们常用“如果没有上帝，什么都是允许的”概括伊万的问题。它是流传已久的浓缩，并不是打开人物的万能原话。

真正难忘的伊万，不只在讨论道德需要什么基础。他还在说孩子：那些受了苦、等不到补偿的孩子。

假如未来的和谐必须用他们的眼泪来付账，他不肯接受。他宁可退掉那张进入和谐的票。你可以不同意他的结论，却很难说他只是对善恶毫不在乎。

这个拒绝，使伊万比一句无神论口号更大。他不肯拿具体的孩子填进宏大的好结果；后来，他又不得不面对自己的言语怎样被斯麦尔加科夫利用。

这里没有“无神论逻辑正确，所以必然杀人”的证明。有的是一个人发现：说出去的话进入了另一个人的怨恨、欲望和选择，已经不归自己独自掌管。

伊万后来的病乱与罪疚，是小说写出的个人危机，不是对所有不信神的人开出的命运判决。

尼采说“上帝死了”，也不只是在庆祝解放；旧价值失效后的虚无与危险同样在他的视野里。两位作者可以相互照亮，却不必被分派成光明与黑暗的两面。

思想一旦落到人身上，问题便多了一层重量。争论结束了，受伤的人还在。陀思妥耶夫斯基让我们留下来，看这一层。

六、宗教大法官

伊万还给阿廖沙讲了一个自己编的故事。十六世纪的塞维利亚，耶稣回来了。人群认出他；宗教大法官把他关进牢房。

夜里，近九十岁的老人来到囚徒面前。他的长篇独白，大意是：你给人的自由太重了，人要的是面包、奇迹和替他们作主的权威。

魔鬼的三个试探，被他重新解释成人类的三个需要：免于饥饿，不必怀疑，有人领路。你拒绝靠这些收买信仰；我们却接下了你放弃的办法。

让人自己选择，他们会痛苦。替他们安排一切，他们就会安稳。于是老人问出了最难受的问题：剥夺自由的人，能不能反过来声称自己更爱人？

耶稣没有同他辩论。他走过去，吻了老人的嘴唇。老人放他离开，却仍叫他不要回来。

一个吻没有完成制度改革，也没有让大法官立刻改信另一种生活。它只是使那个号称已把人类算清的人，短暂地颤动了一下。

想到秦始皇，我们会想到用统一压低异议的冲动；想到华盛顿的退让，我们会想到权力为别人腾出位置的可能。这只是局部的照面：前者不是小说人物，后者也不是耶稣，奴隶制尤其提醒我们，那份自由并未平等给到所有人。

故事是伊万讲的，大法官也不能代表全部教会。陀思妥耶夫斯基有自己的基督教信念，却让这位反对者的声音充分响起，没有用一句旁白把难题扫走。

讲完以后，阿廖沙也吻了伊万。故事里的动作，回到了讲故事的人之间。

这一次，没有谁赢了辩论。但有一个人仍愿意靠近另一个人，而不先要求他撤回全部疑问。

沉默留下的，不是一个已经证明的答案。是关系还没有被争论用尽。

七、他和这个系列

到了陀思妥耶夫斯基这里，小说坐到了哲学与精神分析旁边。

弗洛伊德描画内心冲突，拉康让我们重新听语言里那个不易掌握的自己；小说则让房间有了气味，让等候变长，让一句伤人的话说出口以后，再也收不回去。

你不必变成拉斯科尔尼科夫，才会在门铃响起时紧张。你也不必赞同地下室人，才会认出那种想靠近、又先把人推开的动作。

阅读可以让我们暂时离开自己的位置，却不夺走判断。读者可以停下、反驳，甚至拒绝被一个人物打动。

因此，小说的力量不在于把门锁上，强迫你与杀人者合成一个人。它在于你明明可以合上书，却愿意再留一会儿，听那个自己不会轻易结交的人说完。

哲学的论证、临床的倾听和小说的经验，各有能抵达的地方。这里不必宣布哪一种最深；有些东西，恰好是在它们相遇时显出来。

一套解释说：他就是这种人。小说又补上一句话、一张脸、一次迟疑。

于是那个“就是”，不那么容易出口了。

八、他走的时候

1881年2月9日，公历。陀思妥耶夫斯基在圣彼得堡去世，五十九岁。

此前一年，《卡拉马佐夫兄弟》完成；他在普希金纪念活动上的演说得到热烈回应。晚年的认可是真实的，却不能替我们判断他一生最幸福的时刻。

肺部疾病和最后的出血夺去了他的生命。许多人送葬。疾病没有替作品盖章，人群也没有替他写完余下的人生。

与杜甫晚年的困顿并读，这份生前的声望显出另一种命运。我们仍不必替两个人断定：一个自认失败，一个确信自己已经伟大。

陀思妥耶夫斯基还想继续写阿廖沙。他没有来得及。已经完成的小说，朝着没有写出的生活敞开。

巴赫的《赋格的艺术》留下未完成的最后赋格；达芬奇有未竟的计划，也有完成的作品；陀思妥耶夫斯基留下了尚未展开的阿廖沙。未完成有不同的原因，却都使后来的人站在一扇仍开着的门前。

在这个系列想象的桥头，又多了一个人。让他站在弗洛伊德旁边。一个低头看桥下的支撑，一个仿佛带来地下房间的脚步声。

他不会看穿每个路人的内心。小说也没有替他说尽一切。

但他知道，有时一个人已经把道理说得很完整，手却仍在发抖。

我们可以跟着那双手，再往下读。

注释

本文涉及《地下室手记》《罪与罚》《白痴》《卡拉马佐夫兄弟》的关键情节。刑场日期为俄历1849年12月22日／公历1850年1月3日；死亡日期为俄历1881年1月28日／公历2月9日。参见圣彼得堡陀思妥耶夫斯基博物馆年表。致兄长的刑场回忆参照《罪与罚》Garnett译本前言所引书信；《白痴》第一部第五章的死刑犯叙述是小说，不当作逐项同一的传记证词。

《地下室手记》第二部末章中，丽莎留下的是一张五卢布纸钞。正文沿人物的行为阅读，既不把叙述者等同作者，也不把他的自我伤害或伤人视为自由的充分证明。《罪与罚》参照第一部第七章、第二部第六章、第五部第四章、第六部末章及尾声，补足丽扎韦塔、索尼娅和悔改未在自首时完成的层次。

标题“三十年”是概数，不是优先权或学科胜负的判定：《地下室手记》1864年刊行，《梦的解析》1899年已出版、扉页标1900年，本我—自我—超我模型见1923年《自我与本我》。小说的地下室和反复返回的场景，是阅读中的比照，不为弗洛伊德或拉康的理论提供临床验证。尼采的超人不等于拉斯科尔尼科夫的拿破仑式越法者。

《卡拉马佐夫兄弟》第五卷第四、五章为伊万的“反叛”和“大法官”故事；第十一卷第六至十章及第十二卷提供斯麦尔加科夫的供认、伊万的危机与德米特里的审判。‘如果没有上帝，什么都是允许的’在本文作惯用概括，不伪装成某个固定中译句。大法官的长篇发言以转述呈现；故事末阿廖沙吻伊万的回声保留。人物的论证、作家的信念和本文的阅读不能互相替代。

弗洛伊德1928年的《陀思妥耶夫斯基与弑父》是精神分析解释史的材料，不是可靠的回溯病因诊断。本文不从癫痫推断性格、犯罪或天才，也不将疾病归因为流放与罪疚。博物馆年表记载最后的出血和当时医生判断；正文不据此延伸成现代诊断。

结尾的桥头是本系列的文学想象，不是人物会面记录。杜甫、巴赫、达芬奇等比较限于本文提出的问题；巴赫最后赋格的未完成、达芬奇未竟计划与陀思妥耶夫斯基未写成的续篇各有不同背景，不是‘所有作品皆未完成’。读者保有不同意、停止阅读和继续解释的空间。

I. The Firing Squad

December 22, 1849, in the Russian calendar: January 3, 1850, in the Gregorian. At the Semyonovsky parade ground in St. Petersburg, Fyodor Dostoevsky was waiting to die.

He was twenty-eight. He and his fellow prisoners had been arrested in connection with the Petrashevsky Circle. Reading forbidden texts and discussing a different social order had brought them within reach of the tsar's punishment.

The death sentences were announced. Men were bound to posts. Dostoevsky waited for his turn.

Writing to his brother afterward, he remembered thinking of him and his family, and saying goodbye to the men beside him. Then came the signal stopping the execution and the announcement that their lives had been spared.

The performance of death was real to the prisoners, although the decision not to execute them had already been made. Power required them to live through the final minutes anyway. A life had become somebody else's staged spectacle. The terror could not be packed away with the props.

In The Idiot, Prince Myshkin describes a condemned man dividing his last minutes between farewells, himself, and the world before him. Experience enters the novel, but it does not remain a transcript. Memory acquires another voice.

Elsewhere in this series, Du Fu endures displacement, Beethoven deafness, Sima Qian punishment. Dostoevsky encounters the possibility that life will end immediately. These ordeals need no ranking; none was a tuition fee that a great work ought to require.

He survived. But “five minutes left” could never again be merely a figure of speech.

II. The Underground

Four years of hard labor in Siberia followed, then military service. Epilepsy troubled him over many years. Imprisonment, illness, debts, and writing crossed his life, without adding up to a medical explanation of genius.

In 1864, Notes from Underground introduced a forty-year-old former minor official talking insistently to his readers.

He has no name. He declares himself sick and spiteful, then begins undoing his own declarations. Before anyone else can judge him, he will judge himself more savagely.

He longs for company but cannot bear the vulnerability of being met. When the young woman Liza actually comes to see him, he passes his humiliations on to her. Finally he tries to reduce their encounter to a transaction by giving her money. She leaves it behind.

That banknote on the table is harder to dispose of than an argument. Liza is not the audience in his head. She will not occupy the place he has assigned her.

He protests against a beautifully rational arrangement: calculate everyone's interests correctly, and people will automatically choose their advantage. He would rather wreck the calculation, injuring himself if necessary, than discover that he is only a piano key.

But cruelty does not thereby become a badge of freedom. The novel makes room for his protest and his cowardice together. It does not settle Socrates' questions about knowledge and goodness, or abolish the effects of social conditions, through one unruly speaker.

Notes from Underground preceded the 1900-dated Interpretation of Dreams by thirty-six years. Before psychoanalysis took shape, fiction was already letting us hear self-explanation crack.

Freud would draw maps of psychological conflict; his structural model of id, ego, and superego came later still, in 1923. Dostoevsky installed a voice in a room and showed us the scratches on the furniture, the embarrassment of waiting, the emptiness after an insult.

These are different enterprises. A novel is not a clinical report, nor is the Underground Man his author's medical history. Yet when we close the book, that room is difficult to leave entirely inside fiction.

III. After the Murder

In Crime and Punishment, published in 1866, the impoverished former student Raskolnikov tests whether he belongs among those entitled to step beyond ordinary law.

Napoleon supplies his example: some men kill and history calls them great. Could he remove a pawnbroker he considers harmful, put her money to good use, and establish himself as one of the exceptional men?

He kills Alyona. When her sister Lizaveta unexpectedly returns, he kills her too. The calculation that had allowed for a supposedly useful sacrifice cannot even contain the scene it has produced.

Nor do the stolen goods become the beneficent works he imagined. He hides them. Fever, fear, pride, and self-justification then struggle for possession of his days.

He returns to the room and rings the bell. The sound comes again. Following his hand, we feel how something already done continues to pull at the present.

Later readers may hear echoes of Freud's work on repetition or of Lacan's concern with what resists a settled explanation. But Raskolnikov is not a case history mailed forward to certify either theory. A name for the bell's disturbance does not silence it.

Porfiry closes in. Sonya hears his confession and urges him to take responsibility. Exhaustion, fear, love, conscience, and wounded pride crowd the road to surrender. “He simply could not stand it” clears away too much.

Even in Siberia he does not immediately abandon his old self-justifications. Only at the end does another life begin to appear possible. Confessing before the law and slowly acknowledging another person do not happen at the same instant.

Nietzsche's challenge to inherited morality belongs near this question, but his Übermensch is not interchangeable with a murderer modeling himself on Napoleon. Neighboring questions can lead in different directions.

Dostoevsky takes us to a particular threshold: once I have rewritten another person as expendable material, can my explanation survive the bell, the blood, and another person's eyes?

IV. Parricide

The Brothers Karamazov, completed in 1880, brings us into another household. Fyodor Pavlovich, the father, has been murdered.

Dmitri threatened to kill him and is convicted of a murder he did not commit. Ivan argues brilliantly but becomes unable to bear the connections between his words and the family's catastrophe. Alyosha, a disciple of the elder Zosima, tries to remain close to people. Smerdyakov, the servant believed to be the father's illegitimate son, tells Ivan that he committed the murder.

These are not four equally placed criminal suspects, or passion, reason, faith, and darkness dressed in coats. They evade one another, misunderstand one another, and shift burdens from one pair of shoulders to another.

Smerdyakov uses Ivan's words in his own defense. Ivan cannot make every question disappear by saying that he did not wield the weapon. But speaking and killing do not consequently become identical kinds of responsibility.

In Dostoevsky and Parricide, published in 1928, Freud placed the novel within an Oedipal framework. He also sought to interpret the writer's epilepsy through wishes against the father and guilt.

That was Freud's interpretation, not a cause established by the novel. An illness cannot be handed over to a story because the story finds it convenient.

What keeps the book alive is precisely what exceeds such arrangements. Dmitri's violence does not mean he committed this murder. Ivan's intelligence does not mean he foresaw every consequence. Alyosha's faith cannot make pain cease on demand.

The novel places these entangled lives before us. Theory arrives afterward and can illuminate a corner. It cannot require every light in the house to burn in its own color.

V. If There Is No God

“If there is no God, everything is permitted” is the familiar shorthand for Ivan's problem. It is a compressed formula, not a single quotation that unlocks the man.

The unforgettable Ivan is not only debating the foundations of morality. He is talking about children: children who suffer and will not receive their lives back.

If the harmony of the future must be purchased with their tears, he refuses it. He would rather return his ticket. We may reject his conclusion, but it is difficult to accuse him of simple indifference to good and evil.

This refusal makes him larger than an atheist slogan. He will not feed particular children into a magnificent final outcome. Later, he must face what Smerdyakov has made of his own words.

There is no proof here that atheism logically entails murder. There is a man discovering that words, once spoken, enter another person's resentment, desires, and decisions. They no longer belong to their speaker alone.

Ivan's subsequent illness and guilt form a particular crisis in a novel, not a sentence passed on everyone who does not believe in God.

Nor does Nietzsche's “God is dead” merely celebrate liberation. He also confronts the dangers and nihilism opened by the failure of inherited values. The two writers can illuminate each other without being cast as optimism and darkness.

When thought enters a life, the question acquires another weight. The argument ends; the injured person remains. Dostoevsky asks us to stay long enough to notice.

VI. The Grand Inquisitor

Ivan tells Alyosha a story he has invented. In sixteenth-century Seville, Jesus returns. The crowd recognizes him. The Grand Inquisitor has him imprisoned.

At night the old man, almost ninety, confronts the prisoner. His long speech amounts to this: the freedom you gave people is too heavy. They want bread, miracles, and an authority willing to decide for them.

He reinterprets the three temptations as three human needs: relief from hunger, relief from doubt, someone to follow. You would not purchase belief with these things; we have taken up the means you refused.

Leave people to choose and they suffer. Arrange their lives and they rest. The old man poses the unbearable question: can the person who takes freedom away claim to love humanity more?

Jesus does not debate him. He approaches and kisses his lips. The Inquisitor releases him, while insisting that he never return.

A kiss has not reformed an institution or instantly converted the old man. It has made someone who claims to have calculated humanity completely tremble for a moment.

Qin Shi Huang recalls the impulse to suppress dissent through unity. Washington's relinquishment of power recalls the possibility of leaving room for others. These are limited comparisons: neither history is reducible to the parable, and Washington is no Christ. Slavery especially reminds us how unequally that freedom was distributed.

This is Ivan's story, and the Inquisitor does not speak for every church. Dostoevsky held Christian convictions, yet allowed this adversary a formidable voice rather than dispatching the difficulty through an authorial verdict.

Afterward, Alyosha kisses Ivan. An action from the story returns to the people telling and hearing it.

No one has won the debate. Someone remains willing to approach another person without first demanding the withdrawal of every doubt.

The silence leaves no demonstrated solution. It leaves a relationship that the argument has not exhausted.

VII. His Place in This Series

With Dostoevsky, fiction takes a seat beside philosophy and psychoanalysis.

Freud describes inner conflict; Lacan asks us to listen again to the self we cannot quite command in language. Fiction gives the room a smell, makes waiting last, and lets a cruel remark pass the point where it can be called back.

We do not have to become Raskolnikov to tense when the bell rings. We do not have to endorse the Underground Man to recognize the gesture of wanting someone close and pushing them away first.

Reading can take us outside our usual position without confiscating judgment. We may pause, object, or refuse to be moved by a character.

The novel's power therefore does not lie in locking a door and forcing us to merge with a murderer. It lies in our ability to close the book, and our willingness to stay a little longer anyway, listening to someone we would not readily befriend.

Philosophical argument, clinical listening, and fictional experience reach different places. No ranking of depth is needed. Some things emerge precisely where their paths meet.

An explanation says: that is the kind of person he is. The novel adds another sentence, a face, a hesitation.

Suddenly, “that is” becomes harder to say.

VIII. When He Left

Dostoevsky died in St. Petersburg on February 9, 1881, in the Gregorian calendar. He was fifty-nine.

The previous year had brought the completion of The Brothers Karamazov and an enthusiastic reception for his Pushkin commemorative speech. Late recognition was real. It does not authorize us to identify the happiest years of his life.

Lung disease and a final hemorrhage ended that life. Many people attended his funeral. Illness did not authenticate the books, nor could a crowd finish the days he would never live.

Beside Du Fu's troubled last years, this public esteem suggests a different fate. We still need not assign either man a final judgment on himself: failure in one case, certainty of greatness in the other.

Dostoevsky intended to continue Alyosha's story. He ran out of time. A completed novel opened toward a life that remained unwritten.

Bach's Art of Fugue leaves a final fugue unfinished. Leonardo left unrealized projects as well as completed works. Dostoevsky left Alyosha's further life. Their incompletions have different causes, yet each leaves later readers or listeners before a door still open.

At this series' imagined bridgehead, let one more person stand beside Freud. One looks down toward the bridge's supports; the other seems to bring the sound of footsteps from an underground room.

He cannot read every passerby's mind. His novels have not said everything on his behalf.

But he knows that sometimes a person has explained everything with perfect fluency and still cannot stop his hands from shaking.

We can follow those hands, and read on.

Notes

This essay discusses major events in Notes from Underground, Crime and Punishment, The Idiot, and The Brothers Karamazov. The mock execution took place on December 22, 1849 Old Style / January 3, 1850 New Style; the death date is January 28 / February 9, 1881. See the St. Petersburg Dostoevsky Museum chronology. The letter to his brother is consulted as quoted in the introduction to the Garnett Crime and Punishment. The condemned man's account in The Idiot, Part I, chapter 5, remains fiction rather than an item-for-item biographical deposition.

In the final chapter of Part II of Notes from Underground, Liza leaves a single five-rouble banknote. The narrator is not equated with his author, nor are self-injury and cruelty treated as sufficient evidence of freedom. Crime and Punishment is read with particular attention to I.7, II.6, V.4, the end of Part VI, and the epilogue: Lizaveta, Sonya, and the distance between surrender and inward change matter.

The title's thirty years is approximate, not a claim of priority or victory between disciplines. Notes from Underground appeared in 1864; The Interpretation of Dreams was published in 1899 with a 1900 title-page date; the id–ego–superego model belongs to The Ego and the Id (1923). The basement and return to the scene offer literary comparisons, not clinical verification of Freud or Lacan. Nietzsche's Übermensch is not Raskolnikov's Napoleonic lawbreaker.

Books V.4–5 of The Brothers Karamazov contain Rebellion and The Grand Inquisitor; XI.6–10 and XII develop Smerdyakov's confession, Ivan's crisis, and Dmitri's trial. The familiar formula about God and permission is used as a summary, not attributed as a uniquely fixed sentence. The Inquisitor's speech is paraphrased; Alyosha's answering kiss is retained. Character, author, and essayist are distinct speaking positions.

Freud's Dostoevsky and Parricide (1928) belongs to the history of psychoanalytic interpretation, not to an established retrospective diagnosis. This essay derives neither character, criminality, nor genius from epilepsy, and does not attribute the illness to exile or guilt. The museum chronology records the final hemorrhage and the doctors' contemporary judgment; no modern diagnosis is inferred from it.

The bridgehead is an imagined scene, not a historical meeting. Comparisons with Du Fu, Bach, and Leonardo are limited to the questions raised here. Bach's unfinished last fugue, Leonardo's unrealized projects, and Dostoevsky's unwritten continuation have different histories; they do not imply that all these men's works were unfinished. Readers retain the freedom to disagree, stop, and interpret further.
