梦庄子

Dreaming Zhuangzi

Han Qin (秦汉) · March 2026

一、漆园吏

关于庄子，我们几乎什么都不知道。

司马迁在《史记·老子韩非列传》里给他留下短短两段。一个叫周的蒙人，曾为蒙漆园吏，与梁惠王、齐宣王同时；书有十余万言，大抵是寓言。这是汉代史家留下的轮廓，不是每一笔都经独立证实的履历。

就这些。

这两段没有给出出生年份、死亡年份、家世和求学过程。至于做官，只留下“漆园吏”这个名称。

漆园吏。通常被理解为管理漆园的小官。一个很小的名字，落在战国的官职之间。它让我们看见一个曾与制度相接的庄周，却不足以排出他的全部仕途，更不能换算成今天的行政级别。

接下来，文字把他带到一个拒绝的位置。

怎样离开漆园，史传没有交代。被辞退，自己辞职，还是任期结束？空白仍是空白。传记的重心不在离职手续，而在下一次邀请面前，他怎样回答。

不是没有机会回去。

《史记》说，楚威王听说庄周有才华，派使者带着厚礼来请他，许以相位。一个王国极高的职位。庄周笑了——史家特地留下这个“笑”字。他对使者说：

"千金，重利；卿相，尊位也。子独不见郊祭之犧牛乎？养食之数岁，衣以文繡，以入大庙。当是之时，虽欲为孤豚，岂可得乎？子亟去，无污我。我宁游戏污渎之中自快，无为有国者所羈，终身不仕，以快吾志焉。"

翻译一下：千金，是厚利。卿相，是尊位。但你没见过祭祀用的牛吗？养了好几年，穿上锦绣花衣，牵进太庙——到了那一步，它就算想做一头孤身的小猪，还能吗？你快走吧，别脏了我。我宁愿在污水沟里自在游戏，也不让有国有权的人拿绳子拴住我。不去做官，让我的志意痛快。

注意这个比喻。他不是说宰相的位置不好。他是说宰相的位置就像祭祀的牛——你以为你在享受最高的待遇，但你已经是一个被结构征用的物件了。你不再是你自己。你是结构的一部分。锦绣花衣越好看，你离"小猪"的自由就越远。

小吏与相位，在这幅传记图景里形成两端。无须说庄周亲历过所有层级，牺牛的比喻已经把问题逼到眼前：职位抬高了你，是否也把你交给了别人的用途？花衣的料子不同，绳索却可能相同。

这是本文读到的第一次“被推回来”。不是以一张完整的履历证明他看透一切，而是听见那个笑声：一个人可以拒绝让最高的奖赏决定自己是谁。

二、磨刀石

庄子有一个朋友。或者说，有一个对手。或者两者都是。

惠施。以辩说著称的战国思想家，后人常把他归入名家。《庄子》写他相梁，也写他与庄周来回交锋。一个在相位上，一个以拒绝相位的形象出现。书中的两人站在结构的两侧，话却总能送到对方面前。

他们吵的那些架，是中国哲学史上最漂亮的对话。

《秋水》有这样一个场景。惠施相梁，有人告诉他庄子来了，要取代他的位置。他害怕了，搜了三天三夜。庄子自己走到惠施面前，讲了一个故事：

南方有一种鸟叫鵷鶵，从南海飞往北海，非梧桐不歇，非竹实不食，非醴泉不饮。有一只猫头鹰捡到了一只死老鼠，看到鵷鶵飞过来，抬头怒吼："吓！"——生怕鵷鶵来抢它的死老鼠。

庄子说：你现在就是那只猫头鹰。你以为我要你那只死老鼠？

在这些对话里，惠施是庄子的磨刀石。辨名、析理、追问一句话的根据——他的锋刃不会轻易放过一个断言。“白马非马”更直接属于公孙龙的论题，不必移给惠施，才看得见这个辩论世界的锐利。本文把这样的区分叫作“凿”：凿到极致之后，剩下什么？

最清楚的一次，是濠梁之辩。

庄子和惠施在濠水的桥上散步。庄子看着水里的鱼说："鱼游得多从容啊，这就是鱼的快乐。"

惠施立刻反驳："你又不是鱼，你怎么知道鱼快乐？"

庄子回了一句："你又不是我，你怎么知道我不知道鱼快乐？"

惠施把论证收紧：“我不是你，当然不知道你。但你也不是鱼，所以你不知道鱼快乐，这就全了。”

庄子最后说了一句："请循其本。你刚才说'你怎么知道鱼快乐'——你说这话的时候，已经预设了我知道。你问的不是'你知不知道'，你问的是'你怎么知道'。我告诉你：我知道，是在这座桥上知道的。"

这段对话留下许多读法。“安知”可以牵动“怎么知道”与“在哪里知道”的转换，最后一句也未必是严格的逻辑证明。本文听见的是另一个重音：在断定彼此隔绝之前，两个人已在同一座桥上，看着同一条河。

逻辑是凿。“你不是鱼”是一刀。“所以你不知道鱼”是第二刀。前一句划出差异，后一句却还需要说明：差异为什么必然封死理解？这里质问的是一种推论，不是把历史上的惠施判为虚无主义者，也不是宣布所有逻辑都会把世界切碎。

庄子的最后一句把我们推回来。不是退进各自的碎片，而是重看切割尚未穷尽的相遇。人不是鱼，这个差别没有消失；同在一处的经验，也没有被差别自动取消。本文把这个未被区分收尽的部分叫作“切割之前”。

这是第二次被推回来。逻辑凿到极致，余项是"切割之前"。

三、鼓盆

《至乐》换了一个场景：庄子的妻子死了。

惠施来吊丧。他看到庄子坐在地上，岔开两腿，敲着一个瓦盆唱歌。

惠施受不了了："你和她一起生活，她为你养大了孩子，她老了，死了。你不哭也就算了，你还敲盆唱歌？这不是太过分了吗？"

庄子说：

"不是这样的。她刚死的时候，我怎么可能不难过？但我往回看——她最初本来就没有生命。不只是没有生命，本来就没有形体。不只是没有形体，本来就没有气。在那片恍恍惚惚的混沌之中，变化产生了气，气变成了形体，形体变成了生命。现在又变成了死亡。这不就是春夏秋冬四季的运行吗？她现在安安静静地躺在天地这间大屋子里，我还在旁边哇哇大哭——我觉得这是不通达命运的表现，所以我停了。"

乍听之下，好像生死既是自然变化，悲伤便不必存在。但故事并没有这样开始。

但表面之下有一个结构。

"是其始死也，我独何能无概然。"——他先是悲痛的。这一句很重要。他不是一开始就不悲伤。他悲伤了。悲伤是第一反应，是自然的，是人的。

然后他往回看。"察其始"——他开始追溯。本无生。本无形。本无气。杂乎芒芴之间。

他在做什么？他在从"死"这个终点往回走。死 → 生 → 形 → 气 → 混沌。每一步都是一次"退回"。每退一步，就退到更基本的层面。最后退到了"杂乎芒芴之间"——混沌。没有气，没有形，没有生，没有死。

悲伤是凿。失去妻子的痛是凿的余项——你和她之间的连接被死亡切断了，剩下的就是痛。但庄子没有停在痛上面。他被痛推着往回走，一路退，退到了痛之前，退到了生死之前，退到了形体之前，退到了气之前。

他退到了混沌。

在这番追溯里，生和死被放进更大的变化，而不是两个毫无关联的世界。四季的比喻不承诺同一个人会像春天那样回来，也不替任何丧亲者规定停止哭泣的期限。它改变的是故事中庄周观看死亡的位置。

他停止了哭泣。我们可以把这一刻读成：爱仍在，失去也仍在，但他不再只从终点看她的一生。所谓退到“爱”和“失去”之前，是本文的哲学比喻，不是取消这位妻子的独特生命。

这是第三次被推回来。生死凿到极致，余项是混沌。

四、梦蝶

现在我们可以讲那个最有名的故事了。

"昔者庄周梦为胡蝶，栩栩然胡蝶也，自喻适志与！不知周也。俄然觉，则蘧蘧然周也。不知周之梦为胡蝶与，胡蝶之梦为周与？周与胡蝶，则必有分矣。此之谓物化。"

庄周梦见自己变成了蝴蝶。翩翩然一只蝴蝶，快活得很，完全不知道自己是庄周。突然醒了，发现自己分明是庄周。不知道是庄周梦见了蝴蝶，还是蝴蝶梦见了庄周。庄周和蝴蝶之间一定有某种分别。这叫"物化"。

这段话可以引向真实与幻觉、梦与醒、身份与变化等不同问题。本文不替所有读法排座次，只跟随其中一条线：那个急着确定“谁”的人，能不能暂时松开自己的名字？

先看结构。

蝴蝶梦到了庄子。姑且叫它A。在本文的画面里，蝴蝶的“不知周也”让那个熟悉的名字暂时退场。不是说蝴蝶没有感官、没有分别，而是庄周的身份不再占满一切。我们可以把这一方向想成：未被这个名字固定的生命，梦出了一个有名字的人。

庄子梦到了蝴蝶。姑且叫它B。一个熟悉自己身份的人，在梦里成为蝴蝶。另一方向是：已经定形的“我”，又进入不受原来名字拘束的生活。

庄子问：不知周之梦为胡蝶与，胡蝶之梦为周与？

他没有替我们在A与B之间作最后裁决。

A与B不是形式逻辑里的一对命题与否定命题。非（非A）在经典逻辑中回到A，不能用来证明庄子走到了逻辑之外。这里的“否定之否定”只是本文对一种回转的命名：不肯被一个答案固定，也不急着用相反答案固定自己。他回到“不知”。

“不知”不是第三个已经证明的宇宙状态。它是阅读可以停留的位置：先不决定谁拥有谁的梦，先看梦怎样动摇“谁”的自明性。原文紧接着说“周与胡蝶，则必有分矣”。差别仍在；松动的是把差别永远钉死的冲动。

本文从“物化”读到的，是身份在变化中仍有差异，却不必成为牢笼。这不是穷尽词义的训诂结论。所谓回到两种说法都能出现的地方，是这篇文章的哲学延伸；那个位置的名字，暂且叫“不知”。

这和前面三次"被推回来"是同一个结构，但这一次他走得最远。

漆园吏和宰相：他被推回到社会结构之前。 濠梁之辩：他被推回到主客区分之前。 鼓盆而歌：他被推回到生死之前。 梦蝶：他被推回到"谁"之前。连"我是谁"这个问题都还没有产生的地方。

沿着这条解释的线，我们来到另一个寓言，另一个名字。

五、混沌

《庄子·应帝王》的最后一段：

南海之帝为儵，北海之帝为忽，中央之帝为混沌。儵与忽时相与遇于混沌之地，混沌待之甚善。儵与忽谋报混沌之德，曰："人皆有七窍以视听食息，此独无有，尝试凿之。"日凿一窍，七日而混沌死。

南海之帝叫儵，北海之帝叫忽，中央之帝叫混沌。儵和忽经常在混沌的地方见面，混沌对他们很好。儵和忽想报答混沌的恩德，说："人都有七窍用来看、听、吃、呼吸，只有混沌没有。我们来试着给他凿出来吧。"每天凿一窍，七天之后混沌死了。

这个寓言可以成为阅读庄子的一把钥匙。

凿。七窍是凿出来的。视觉、听觉、味觉、呼吸——这些都是区分的能力。有了眼睛，你就能区分光和暗。有了耳朵，你就能区分声和静。每一个窍都是一把刀，把连续的世界切成可以辨认的碎片。

儵和忽是善意的。他们不是要害混沌。他们觉得混沌没有七窍是一种缺陷——"人皆有七窍……此独无有"。他们在做好事。他们在帮混沌变得"正常"。

但混沌死了。

在本文的读法里，混沌是“尚未依照别人的尺度被凿过”的形象。七窍本来是能力；把这种能力认作人人必须具有的唯一形状，才让善意成了刀。儵和忽把自己的正常交给混沌，却没有为混沌自己的活法留下位置。

每凿一窍，混沌就少一点。七窍凿完，混沌就死了。

现在把这个寓言和前面所有的故事放在一起看。

漆园吏和宰相：社会结构是凿。它给你一个位置（吏，相），让你看得见自己在哪里——但你也被位置定义了。牺牛穿上锦绣就不再是小猪。

濠梁之辩：逻辑是凿。它给你主客区分（你/鱼），让你能做判断——但把区分当成绝对隔墙，判断也可能把尚待理解的相遇锁在外面。

鼓盆而歌：生死是凿。它给你时间的方向（生→死），让你能度量存在——但你也被方向困住了。你在终点哭泣，忘了终点之前还有"杂乎芒芴之间"。

梦蝶：身份是凿。它给你一个"我"（庄周/蝴蝶），让你知道自己是谁——但你也被"我"固定了。物化是流动的，"我"是凝固的。

每一次凿都有用。视觉有用，逻辑有用，身份有用，社会结构有用。儵和忽是善意的。但有用并不使它们免于追问：这一次获得，遮住了什么？“混沌的一部分死亡”是我们替这个代价画的像。

庄子的文本让这个代价可见。他也运用语言、区分与辩论，不能简单列入拒绝一切分别的一边。但读到这里，我们一再被推回来：构造留下的余项太大，必须重新看那未被构造收尽的部分。

混沌。

否定之否定。本文用这几个字连接两个动作：凿把某种连续划开；既成结构又把不能容纳的部分判为“无用”。被排除的价值于是重新要求我们回看起点。这是解释的路线，不是两个否定自动导出混沌的形式证明。无用之用。散木之寿。鼓盆之歌。梦蝶之化。

庄子是被推回去的人。每凿一次，回去一次。凿得越深，回得越远。最后他回到了最远的地方。

六、和前三个人的距离

现在把四篇文章放在一起。

在这组比较的画面里，尼采用锤子试探价值。“上帝死了”使继承来的根据震动，真理也被追问到隐喻和人的创造。有限的人仍然需要为行动赋义。“向死而生”是本系列的概括，不是他的专门术语；他的作品也不能被一句“从未转向他者”收尽。我们继续问的是：自我超越怎样通向不把他者仅当材料的承认？

康德用理性辨界：现象与物自身，先天条件与经验，绝对命令与假言命令。他不是把世界切成两件可以随手缝合的物体。《判断力批判》面对自然与自由之间的过渡，桥是一个有根据却有限度的比喻。限制知识，亦可为自由和道德行动留下位置。

王阳明的方向转向心。格竹的回忆、龙场的领悟，通向心即理、知行合一、致良知。拂去欲望、习气与偏见，如同考古学家拂去土。但他的工夫不是从世界撤回内室：良知要在实际事务中落实，行动仍须承担判断，战功也不等于哲学已获验证。

庄子不一样。

庄子的寓言把我们的目光移到受凿者身上。获得视觉，怎样改变看与不看的关系？形成知识，怎样安置未知？建立道德判断，怎样不把不能归类的生命先行抹掉？这不是宣告善恶无别，而是追问一种尺度在判断时付出的代价。

尼采知道凿有代价——"上帝死了"就是最大的代价。但他的回应是继续凿：既然上帝死了，那就在废墟上自己造意义。

康德知道凿有代价——物自体不可知就是代价。但他的回应是划界：告诉理性哪些地方不能去。

王阳明的格竹回忆也让求理的代价可见。但转向心并非不要外部事务，而是改变在事务中寻找与践履天理的方式。

庄子的回应不同。他不是继续凿，不是划界，不是换方向。他被推回去了。推回到了凿之前。他在那里看到了混沌——不是作为一种缺失（"还没有七窍"），而是作为一种完整（"不需要七窍"）。

这是本篇为庄子保留的位置：从余项那一侧看世界。它是一束选择性的光，不是说另三人从来没有想过限度、损失与不可化约之物。

尼采站在锤子这一侧。康德站在手术刀这一侧。王阳明站在考古铲这一侧。庄子站在被凿的那一侧——混沌那一侧。他从混沌的视角看凿，看到的不是建设，是损失。不是进步，是代价。不是七窍的获得，是混沌的死亡。

因此，本文从他那里听到的，不是简单的“不要凿”，而是：凿了之后，记得回看。回到混沌。回到无何有之乡。回到不能被你这一刀穷尽的地方。这是读者带走的提醒，不是冒充原文的语录。

因为那个地方不会消失。七窍凿完了，混沌死了——但"杂乎芒芴之间"仍然在。生死凿完了，悲伤来了——但"本无生、本无形、本无气"仍然在。逻辑凿完了，主客分开了——但"我知之濠上也"仍然在。

寓言里的混沌确实死了，不能替善意的凿者撤销这个后果。而在本文的哲学延伸里，“混沌”又成为另一层名字：世界并未被我们已经作出的区分穷尽。凿得越深，未尽之物退得越远；结构完成了，余项的问题仍未结束。

这些故事把我们推回来。回去看，才知道完成也会留下什么。

七、无何有之乡

惠施有一次嘲笑庄子。他说："我有一棵大树，人们叫它樗。它的树干臃肿弯曲，不合绳墨。它的小枝卷曲扭拐，不合规矩。立在路边，木匠看都不看一眼。你说的那些话，就像这棵树——大而无用，所有人都不要。"

庄子回答：

"你有一棵大树，嫌它没有用。为什么不把它种在无何有之乡，广莫之野？你可以在它旁边无所事事地闲逛，在它下面逍遥地睡觉。它不会被斧头砍倒，没有任何东西能伤害它。没有什么地方可以用它——可是又有什么能让它苦恼呢？"

无何有之乡。没有什么的地方。什么都没有的旷野。

可以把它读成逃离，也可以把它读成一种哲学位置。本文关心后者：当既有用途不再是唯一尺度，被判为多余的生命在哪里安放？

有用的树被砍。因为它有用，它被纳入了结构——变成房梁，变成家具，变成棺材。它的有用是它的死因。

无用的树活着。因为它无用，结构放过了它。它不合绳墨，不合规矩，木匠不看它一眼。它被结构排斥了。但正因为被排斥，它获得了结构内部不可能有的东西：不被砍伐的寿命。

无用之用。树逃过斧头，是寓言对用途尺度的反转，不是“凡被排斥者都安全”的现实保证。制度外的人也会受伤，不能把放逐浪漫化。这里被保住的，是生命不必证明自己能作木料，才有理由继续生长。

牺牛穿上锦绣进了太庙——有用，死了。散木立在路边无人问津——无用，活着。

在本篇的图景里，庄子选择散木。楚王派使者相邀的故事说明，拒绝不一定来自无能；大树的故事又说明，不合用途不等于没有生命的价值。这不能证明所有官职都必然是祭坛，却足以让一张征用万物的清单停顿一下。无何有之乡，给这停顿留下了地方。

八、四个人

现在回到本系列想象的桥。这里不是历史中的会面，而是四条思想道路的一幅画。桥头站了两个人——康德和王阳明。

尼采在桥的另一端，从否定出发，走向桥头。

庄子不在桥上。

庄子在桥下面。在水里。在濠水里。和鱼在一起。

他不需要站在桥头，因为桥是凿出来的。桥连接此岸和彼岸——但"此岸"和"彼岸"本身就是凿的产物。在凿之前，没有此岸和彼岸，只有水。

康德站在桥上看目的王国。王阳明站在桥上看此心光明。尼采在走向桥头的路上。庄子在桥下面看着他们三个，微笑。

他不反对桥。桥很好。桥让人过河。只是他的笑容给这幅画加上另一个问题：

桥是凿出来的。凿有代价。代价是混沌的一部分死亡。

同一与矛盾，时空与因果，生命与意识，有限生命的赋义与不疑——这串台阶属于本系列自己的构造，不是庄子的预言。早期论文曾用“十层”叙述它；旧层数也不能直接换成今天的DD地址。桥头的方向仍可说清：主体是目的，而不只是手段。non dubito是不动摇于这一结构事实，不是确信自己永远正确。

15DD自我立法主体能够不等待回报，承认他者作为目的的地位。16DD双向不疑则指向双方在共同生活的一切事务中持续相互承认的理想，不是一次合作便已完成。把它们放进这幅画，是今天的提问；没有哪个古人因此被追认为这套理论的作者。

但混沌还在。它退到了所有层的后面。它是凿构循环做完之后剩下的那个东西。它不消失。

庄子的古老寓言与这个新问题相遇。相遇不是先知式的证明，而是让我们再次回看。

四个人。尼采让价值的根据震动；康德辨认人的目的地位；王阳明把良知带进行动；庄子使我们低头看见桥下的水。每一幅侧影都有所选择，谁也没有替谁完成所有工作。

水没有数完台阶。它仍在那些台阶下面流动。

杂乎芒芴之间。

无何有之乡。

他不需要走到桥头。他本来就在水里。水不需要桥。[1][2][3]

I. The Lacquer Garden Clerk

We know almost nothing about Zhuangzi.

Sima Qian leaves two short paragraphs about him in the Records of the Grand Historian, among the biographies of Laozi and Han Fei. A man from Meng, named Zhou, once an official at its lacquer garden; a contemporary of King Hui of Liang and King Xuan of Qi; associated with a book of more than a hundred thousand words, largely fables. This is a Han historian's outline, not an independently verified record of every part of a life.

That is all.

Those paragraphs supply neither birth nor death year, neither a family history nor an education. For officeholding, they leave us one name: lacquer garden clerk.

A small office, usually understood as the management of a lacquer garden. The name places Zhou in contact with an institution. It does not chart his entire career or allow us to assign him a modern administrative grade.

The text then brings him to a refusal.

How he left the lacquer garden is not told. Dismissal, resignation, an appointment running its course? The blank remains a blank. The biography's interest lies elsewhere: when another invitation comes, how does he answer?

Not for lack of opportunity.

According to the Shiji, King Wei of Chu hears of Zhou's worth and sends emissaries with lavish gifts, offering a minister's office. One of the highest positions in a kingdom. Zhou laughs — the historian preserves that laugh — and answers:

Gold is a handsome reward; a minister's office is an honored place. But look at the sacrificial ox. Fed for years, dressed in embroidery, it is led into the great temple. Could it then choose to be a solitary piglet? Go away, he tells the envoys. I would rather play freely in a filthy ditch than let a ruler put a bridle on me. Let me follow my own inclination without office.

Notice the metaphor. He was not saying the prime ministership was bad. He was saying the prime ministership was like the sacrificial ox — you believe you are receiving the highest honor, but you have already become an object requisitioned by the structure. You are no longer yourself. You are part of the structure. The finer the embroidered silk, the farther you are from the piglet's freedom.

Small official and chief minister form the two ends of this biographical picture. Zhou need not have experienced every rank for the question to bite: when a position raises you up, does it also deliver you to someone else's purposes? The silk may differ while the tether remains.

This is the first movement of being “pushed back” that this essay follows. Not a complete career proving that he saw through everything. A laugh, and a refusal to let the highest reward decide who he must be.

II. The Whetstone

Zhuangzi had a friend. Or an opponent. Or both.

Hui Shi, or Huizi: a Warring States thinker renowned for argument, later associated with the School of Names. The Zhuangzi places him in office at Liang and stages his repeated encounters with Zhou. One holds high office; the other appears as a refuser of it. From opposite sides of the structure, they keep finding words for each other.

Their arguments are among the most beautiful dialogues in the history of Chinese philosophy.

Autumn Floods gives us this scene. Someone warns Huizi, minister of Liang, that Zhuangzi has come to replace him. Frightened, he searches for three days and nights. Zhuangzi comes to him of his own accord and tells a story:

In the south there is a bird called the yuanchu. It flies from the South Sea to the North Sea. It will rest on nothing but the parasol tree, eat nothing but bamboo seeds, drink nothing but sweet spring water. An owl had found a rotting rat. Seeing the yuanchu fly overhead, it looked up and screeched: "Shoo!" — terrified the yuanchu would steal its dead rat.

Zhuangzi said: you are that owl right now. You think I want your dead rat?

In these dialogues, Huizi is Zhuangzi's whetstone. He distinguishes terms, examines reasons, asks what supports an assertion. “A white horse is not a horse” belongs more directly to Gongsun Long; there is no need to transfer it to Huizi to feel this argumentative world's sharp edge. This essay calls the work of distinction carving. Once the carving has reached its limit, what remains?

The clearest instance was the debate at Hao Bridge.

Zhuangzi and Huizi were strolling on the bridge over the Hao River. Zhuangzi, looking at the fish in the water, said: "See how the minnows swim about at ease. This is the happiness of fish."

Huizi immediately countered: "You are not a fish. How do you know the fish are happy?"

Zhuangzi replied: "You are not me. How do you know I do not know the fish are happy?"

Huizi tightens the argument: “I am not you, so I do not know you. But you certainly are not a fish. Your not knowing the happiness of fish is established.”

Zhuangzi said one final thing: "Let us go back to the beginning. You asked, 'How do you know the fish are happy?' — but in asking how, you already presupposed that I know. You were not asking whether I know. You were asking how I know. I will tell you: I know it here, on this bridge."

The dialogue admits several readings. The expression an zhi allows a play between how one knows and where one knows; the last reply need not be a strict logical proof. This essay hears another emphasis. Before the speakers declare themselves sealed off, they are already standing on one bridge, looking at one river.

“You are not a fish” makes one cut. “Therefore you cannot know the fish” makes another. The first establishes difference. The second still owes us a reason why difference necessarily blocks understanding. That is a question about an inference, not a verdict that the historical Hui Shi was a nihilist or that all logic fragments the world.

Zhuangzi's final sentence pushes the reader back: not into a private fragment, but toward an encounter that the distinctions have not exhausted. A person is not a fish; the difference remains. Yet being here together is not automatically canceled by difference. The essay calls this unexhausted remainder “before the cut.”

This was the second time he was pushed back. Logic carved to its extreme; the remainder was "before the cut."

III. Drumming on the Basin

Perfect Enjoyment changes the scene. Zhuangzi's wife has died.

Huizi came to pay his respects. He found Zhuangzi sitting on the ground, legs splayed, beating on a clay basin and singing.

Huizi could not bear it: "You lived with her, she raised your children, she grew old and died. Not weeping would be enough — but drumming on a basin and singing? Is this not going too far?"

Zhuangzi said:

"It is not like that. When she first died, how could I not have been stricken? But I looked back to the very beginning — originally there was no life. Not only no life, but originally no form. Not only no form, but originally no vital breath. Mingled in the blurred and nebulous, a change occurred and there was vital breath. The vital breath changed and there was form. The form changed and there was life. Now there has been another change and she is dead. This is like the procession of the four seasons — spring, summer, autumn, winter. She now lies peacefully in the great chamber of heaven and earth, and I stand here sobbing beside her — I realized this was to fail to understand fate. So I stopped."

At first it can sound as though natural change makes grief unnecessary. But that is not where the story begins.

But beneath the surface is a structure.

"When she first died, how could I not have been stricken?" — he grieved first. This sentence matters. He was not ungrieving from the start. He grieved. Grief was the first response — natural, human.

Then he looked backward. "I examined the very beginning" — he began to trace back. Originally no life. Originally no form. Originally no vital breath. Mingled in the blurred and nebulous.

What was he doing? He was walking backward from the endpoint of "death." Death → life → form → vital breath → the nebulous. Each step was a retreat. With each retreat, he reached a more fundamental level. At the end he arrived at "mingled in the blurred and nebulous" — hundun. Chaos. No breath, no form, no life, no death.

Grief is carving. The pain of losing his wife was the remainder produced by carving — the bond between them severed by death, and what remained was pain. But Zhuangzi did not stop at the pain. The pain pushed him backward, retreating all the way: before pain, before life and death, before form, before vital breath.

He retreated to hundun.

The backward journey places life and death within a larger transformation instead of treating them as unrelated worlds. The seasons do not promise that the same person will return like spring. Nor do they set a deadline for anyone's mourning. What changes is the position from which the Zhou of this story looks at death.

He stops weeping. We can read that moment this way: love remains, loss remains, but the endpoint no longer supplies the only view of her life. Returning to “before love and loss” is the essay's philosophical metaphor, not the erasure of this particular woman's existence.

This was the third time he was pushed back. Life and death carved to their extreme; the remainder was hundun.

IV. The Butterfly Dream

Now we can tell the most famous story.

"Once Zhuang Zhou dreamed he was a butterfly — a butterfly flitting about, content with itself, not knowing it was Zhou. Suddenly he awoke, and there he was, solidly and unmistakably Zhou. He did not know whether he was Zhou who had dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming it was Zhou. Between Zhou and the butterfly there must be some distinction. This is called the transformation of things."

Zhuang Zhou dreamed he was a butterfly. Fluttering, carefree, a butterfly through and through, perfectly content, with no awareness of being Zhou. Then he suddenly woke and found himself unmistakably Zhou. He did not know whether Zhou had dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly was dreaming it was Zhou. Between Zhou and the butterfly there must be some distinction. This is called "the transformation of things."

This passage opens onto reality and illusion, waking and dreaming, identity and change. Without ranking all those readings, we can follow one thread: can the person so eager to establish “who” loosen his grip on a name?

Look at the structure.

The butterfly dreams Zhuangzi. Call that A. In this essay's picture, “not knowing Zhou” makes a familiar name fall away. It does not make the butterfly an animal without senses or distinctions. Rather, Zhou's identity no longer fills the whole field. Life not fixed by that name dreams a named person.

Zhuangzi dreams the butterfly. Call that B. A person familiar with his own identity becomes a butterfly in sleep. In this direction, an established self enters a life that its old name no longer governs.

Zhuangzi asked: he did not know whether Zhou had dreamed the butterfly, or the butterfly had dreamed Zhou.

He does not deliver a final verdict between A and B.

These are not a proposition and its formal negation. Classical double negation returns not-(not-A) to A; it cannot prove that Zhuangzi has stepped outside logic. “Negation of negation” here names an interpretive turning: refusing to be fixed by one answer without rushing to be fixed by its rival. He returns to not knowing.

Not knowing is not a third, demonstrated state of the universe. It is a place where reading can pause. Before deciding who owns whose dream, watch how dreaming unsettles the self-evidence of “who.” The text expressly insists that Zhou and the butterfly differ. What loosens is not difference, but the urge to nail it down forever.

In wuhua, the transformation of things, this essay reads identity changing without losing every distinction or becoming a prison. That does not exhaust the term's meanings. A return to the place where either account can arise is our philosophical extension; for now we call that place not knowing.

This is the same structure as all three previous instances of being pushed back, but this time he went the farthest.

The lacquer garden and the prime ministership: he was pushed back to before social structure. The Hao Bridge debate: he was pushed back to before the subject-object distinction. Drumming on the basin: he was pushed back to before life and death. The butterfly dream: he was pushed back to before "who." To the place where even the question "who am I" has not yet arisen.

Following that interpretive thread, we arrive at another fable, another name.

V. Hundun

The final passage of Responding to Emperors and Kings in the Zhuangzi:

The emperor of the South Sea was Shu. The emperor of the North Sea was Hu. The emperor of the center was Hundun. Shu and Hu from time to time met in the territory of Hundun, and Hundun treated them very generously. Shu and Hu discussed how to repay Hundun's kindness. They said: "All people have seven openings for seeing, hearing, eating, and breathing. Hundun alone has none. Let us try to bore them." Each day they bored one opening. On the seventh day, Hundun died.

The emperor of the South Sea was called Shu — "Swift." The emperor of the North Sea was called Hu — "Sudden." The emperor of the center was called Hundun — "Chaos." Shu and Hu often met in Hundun's territory, and Hundun treated them well. Shu and Hu wished to repay Hundun's kindness and said: "All people have seven openings for seeing, hearing, eating, and breathing. Hundun alone has none. Let us try to bore them for him." Each day they bored one opening. On the seventh day, Hundun died.

This fable can be a key to reading Zhuangzi.

Carving. The seven openings were bored out. Sight, hearing, taste, breathing — these are capacities for distinction. With eyes, you can distinguish light from dark. With ears, you can distinguish sound from silence. Each opening is a blade that cuts the continuous world into recognizable fragments.

Shu and Hu meant well. They were not trying to harm Hundun. They believed Hundun's lack of openings was a deficiency — "All people have seven openings … Hundun alone has none." They were doing a good deed. They were helping Hundun become "normal."

But Hundun died.

In this reading, Hundun is the figure of a life not yet carved to someone else's measure. Openings are capacities. The danger comes when a capacity becomes the single shape everyone must have. Shu and Hu give Hundun their normality without leaving room for Hundun's own way of living.

Each opening bored, Hundun diminished. When all seven were done, Hundun was dead.

Now place this fable alongside every story that came before.

The lacquer garden and the prime ministership: social structure is carving. It gives you a position (clerk, minister) and lets you see where you are — but you are also defined by that position. The ox draped in silk is no longer a piglet.

The Hao Bridge debate: logic is carving. Distinguishing person from fish makes judgment possible. Turning that distinction into an absolute wall can also exclude an encounter that remains to be understood.

Drumming on the basin: life and death are carving. They give you the direction of time (life → death) and let you measure existence — but you are also trapped in the direction. You weep at the endpoint, forgetting that before the endpoint there was "mingled in the blurred and nebulous."

The butterfly dream: identity is carving. It gives you a "self" (Zhou/butterfly) and lets you know who you are — but you are also fixed by the "self." The transformation of things is flowing; the "self" is frozen.

Sight is useful. So are logic, identity, social structures. Shu and Hu mean well. But usefulness grants no exemption from the question: what does this gain obscure? “A portion of Hundun's death” is the image this essay gives that cost.

Zhuangzi's texts make the cost visible. They too use language, distinction, argument: they cannot simply stand for the rejection of every difference. Yet the reader keeps being pushed back. The construction leaves too much out. We have to look again at what it has not taken in.

Hundun.

Negation of negation. The phrase joins two movements: a cut divides a continuity; the resulting structure dismisses what it cannot accommodate as useless. That excluded value asks us to reconsider the beginning. This is an interpretive route, not a formal proof that two negations yield Hundun. The use of uselessness. A useless tree's long life. A song on a basin. A butterfly's transformation.

Zhuangzi was the man who was pushed back. Each time he carved, he returned. The deeper the carving, the farther the return. In the end, he returned to the farthest place.

VI. The Distance from the Other Three

Now place all four essays together.

In this comparative picture, Nietzsche tests values with a hammer. God's death shakes inherited grounds; truth is questioned through metaphor and human creation. A finite person must still give reasons to act. “Living toward death” is this series' formulation, not his technical term. Nor can his work be exhausted by saying he never turned toward others. Our continuing question is how self-overcoming can lead to recognition without making another person merely its material.

Kant draws distinctions with reason: appearances and things in themselves, a priori conditions and experience, categorical and hypothetical imperatives. He does not cut the world into two objects that could simply be sewn together. The Critique of Judgment addresses a transition between nature and freedom; the bridge is an apt but limited metaphor. Limiting knowledge can also leave room for freedom and moral action.

Wang Yangming turns toward the heart-mind. His bamboo recollection and Longchang awakening lead toward mind as principle, knowing with acting, and the extension of innate knowing. Brushing away desire, habit, and prejudice resembles an archaeologist uncovering treasure. But cultivation does not withdraw from the world into an inner room. It must work in actual affairs; actions remain answerable to judgment, and military success does not verify a philosophy.

Zhuangzi was different.

Zhuangzi's fables turn our eyes toward the one being carved. How does gaining sight change the relation between seeing and not seeing? Where does knowledge put the unknown? How can moral judgment avoid erasing an unclassifiable life in advance? This does not declare good and evil indistinguishable. It asks what a standard costs in the act of judging.

Nietzsche knew carving had a cost — "God is dead" was the greatest cost of all. But his response was to keep carving: since God is dead, build meaning from the rubble.

Kant knew carving had a cost — the unknowability of the thing-in-itself was the cost. But his response was to draw boundaries: tell reason where it cannot go.

Wang's recollection of investigating bamboo also makes the cost of seeking principle visible. Yet turning toward the heart-mind does not abolish external affairs. It changes how principle is sought and enacted within them.

Zhuangzi's response was different. He did not keep carving, did not draw boundaries, did not change direction. He was pushed back. Back to before the carving. There he saw Hundun — not as a deficiency ("lacking seven openings") but as a completeness ("not needing seven openings").

This essay reserves that position for Zhuangzi: looking from the remainder's side. It is a selective light, not a claim that the other three never considered limits, losses, or what resists reduction.

Nietzsche stood on the side of the hammer. Kant stood on the side of the scalpel. Wang Yangming stood on the side of the archaeologist's trowel. Zhuangzi stood on the side of what was being carved — the side of Hundun. From Hundun's perspective, carving looked not like construction but like loss. Not like progress but like cost. Not like the gaining of seven openings but like the death of Hundun.

What this reading takes away is therefore not simply “do not carve,” but remember to look back after carving. Back to Hundun. Back to the village of nothing-at-all. Back to what your cut cannot exhaust. This is the reader's reminder, not a quotation masquerading as Zhuangzi's words.

Because that place does not disappear. The seven openings are bored and Hundun dies — but "mingled in the blurred and nebulous" remains. Life and death are carved and grief arrives — but "originally no life, originally no form, originally no vital breath" remains. Logic is carved and subject and object separate — but "I know it here on this bridge" remains.

In the fable, Hundun really dies. We cannot pardon the well-meaning carvers by canceling that consequence. In the essay's philosophical extension, however, Hundun also names something else: our established distinctions do not exhaust the world. The deeper the cut, the farther the unexhausted retreats. The structure is complete; the question of the remainder is not.

The stories push us back. Looking again, we discover what completion leaves behind.

VII. The Village of Nothing-at-All

Huizi once mocked Zhuangzi. He said: "I have a great tree that people call the ailanthus. Its trunk is so gnarled and swollen that it defies the plumb line. Its branches are so twisted and crooked that they defy the compass and square. It stands by the road and no carpenter gives it a glance. Your words are just like this tree — grand and useless, rejected by everyone."

Zhuangzi replied:

"You have a great tree and are troubled that it is useless. Why not plant it in the village of nothing-at-all, in the vast and open wild? You could wander idly by its side, doing nothing, and lie down to sleep beneath it in perfect ease. No axe will cut it short. Nothing can harm it. It has no use — and so what could ever cause it distress?"

The village of nothing-at-all. A place where there is nothing. A vast and empty wilderness.

One can read this as escape, or as a philosophical position. This essay follows the second possibility: once established usefulness is no longer the only measure, where may a life judged superfluous find room?

The useful tree is felled. Because it is useful, it is absorbed into the structure — turned into a beam, into furniture, into a coffin. Its usefulness is its death sentence.

The useless tree lives. Because it is useless, the structure passes it by. It defies the plumb line, defies the compass, and the carpenter does not glance at it. It has been rejected by the structure. But precisely because it was rejected, it gained something impossible within the structure: a life not cut short.

The use of uselessness. A tree escaping the axe reverses usefulness within a fable; it does not guarantee that every excluded person is safe. Those outside institutions can also be harmed. Exile should not be romanticized. What the image preserves is a life's right to keep growing without proving that it would make good timber.

The sacrificial ox is draped in silk and led into the temple — useful, dead. The tree of no use to the carpenter stands by the road, ignored — useless, alive.

In this essay's picture, Zhuangzi chooses useless timber. The king's invitation through envoys shows that refusal need not mean inability. The great tree shows that unfitness for a purpose need not mean a worthless life. Neither proves that every office must be an altar. Together they make a list for the requisitioning of everything pause. The village of nothing-at-all gives that pause a place.

VIII. Four Men

Return now to the bridge imagined by this series. It is not a historical meeting, but a picture of four philosophical paths. Two men stand at the bridgehead: Kant and Wang Yangming.

Nietzsche is at the other end of the bridge, walking from negation toward the bridgehead.

Zhuangzi is not on the bridge.

Zhuangzi is beneath the bridge. In the water. In the Hao River. With the fish.

He does not need to stand at the bridgehead, because the bridge was carved. A bridge connects this shore to the far shore — but "this shore" and "the far shore" are themselves products of carving. Before the carving, there was no this shore and no far shore. There was only water.

Kant stands on the bridge looking toward the kingdom of ends. Wang Yangming stands on the bridge looking toward the luminous mind. Nietzsche is walking toward the bridgehead. Zhuangzi is beneath the bridge, looking up at the three of them, smiling.

He is not against the bridge. The bridge is good. It lets people cross. His smile simply introduces another question into the picture:

The bridge was carved. Carving has a cost. The cost is the partial death of Hundun.

Identity and contradiction, space-time and causality, life and consciousness, meaning in finite life and non-doubt: these steps belong to this series' construction, not to Zhuangzi's prophecy. Earlier papers described ten layers; those historical numbers cannot simply be converted into today's DD addresses. The bridgehead's direction remains clear: a subject is an end, not merely a means. Non dubito is not wavering on that structural fact, not certainty that one is always right.

The 15DD self-legislating subject can recognize another's standing as an end without waiting for recognition in return. The 16DD ideal of mutual non dubito is sustained mutual recognition throughout the affairs of a shared life, not a single successful cooperation. Bringing these terms into the picture asks a present question; it does not conscript an ancient thinker as the author of our theory.

But Hundun is still there. It has retreated behind all the layers. It is the thing remaining after all chisel-construct cycles are complete. It does not vanish.

Zhuangzi's ancient fables meet this new question. The meeting is not prophetic proof. It makes us look again.

Four men. Nietzsche shakes the grounds of value; Kant articulates a person's standing as an end; Wang takes innate knowing into action; Zhuangzi makes us look down at the water beneath the bridge. Every silhouette is selective. None has completed all the others' work.

The water has not counted the steps. It is still flowing underneath them.

Mingled in the blurred and nebulous.

The village of nothing-at-all.

He does not need to walk to the bridgehead. He was already in the water. Water does not need a bridge.[1][2][3]
