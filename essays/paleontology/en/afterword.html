A Ladder Is Not an Elevator: Three Billion Years Revisited through the DD Sequence

The main essays are enough on their own. Read on if you want to revisit them through the author's DD framework—a philosophical reading, not a biological ranking.

1 Two Kinds of Order

This series has twenty-three essays, arranged in time. The first stands on a rock more than three billion years old and asks whether what happened there counts as life. The twenty-second stops in the forests of the Late Miocene, home to several kinds of ape, most of which would leave no descendants. In between, the essays work their way roughly upward through the strata: membranes, oxygen, eukaryotic cells, sex, multicellularity, the Cambrian, the move onto land, coal, and the world emptied and filled again, over and over.

That is one kind of order: the order of time. This afterword will try another, rearranging the same twenty-three essays.

The new order comes from the skeleton of the framework behind the series. It is called the DD sequence. There are sixteen levels, preceded by a 0DD called chaos, where no distinctions have yet been established. Beginning with 1DD, each level establishes one more law, draws one more line: First, a caution: this is the author's philosophical framework, not an accepted biological sequence or a ranking of species. The main essays stand on their own. This optional afterword brings their background thinking into view to ask what it illuminates—and what it does not.

The first round, 1DD to 4DD: identity, contradiction, interval, causality. The second, 5DD to 8DD: replication, self-maintenance, differentiation, reproduction. The third, 9DD to 12DD: selection, perception, memory, prediction. The fourth round begins with the self-subject at 13DD; 14DD is purpose. This afterword will not go any higher.

Every round is three plus one. Each of the first three levels establishes a law; the fourth brings the three together. Causality combines identity, contradiction, and interval into a world with direction. Reproduction combines replication, self-maintenance, and differentiation into a life that can grow all over again. Prediction combines selection, perception, and memory into a mind that can infer the future from the past.

This ladder was not originally built out of biology. Its starting point is as spare as could be. It assumes no laws and does just one thing: negates. “This is not that.” Then, in the gap the statement leaves, it says “This is not that” again, growing upward one level at a time. Only after it had grown to sixteen levels was it found to be four times four. No one drew the grid first and filled it in afterward.

Nor was the paleontology series written to illustrate the ladder. Not once did DD appear in the body of the twenty-three essays as they were being written. Now that both have taken shape, we can set them side by side and see where they match and where they do not.

The conclusion can come first. There are many matches: almost every one of the eight levels in the second and third rounds has a place in this series. The first round is not in the rocks; it is in the hands of the people who read them. The series stops at the threshold of the fourth.

The mismatches matter just as much, and one runs through the whole afterword: the ladder orders structures, not events in time, and the ladder has no motor. Some of the finest essays in this series demonstrate precisely that.

2 The First Round: Four Lines Brought by the Readers of Rocks

1DD is identity: this is this, not that.

In the first essay, someone had actually counted: there were 123 collected definitions of life. Every one of them performs the same act. It draws a line through the continuous world of chemistry and says that this side counts as life and that side does not. The first essay called this act chiseling: “Usually, when we speak of chiseling, someone is holding the chisel. For this cut, no one is.”

The law of identity is the form of that first cut. It says nothing about where the blade should fall, only that once it falls, the two sides are no longer the same thing. The disagreements among those 123 definitions are not a failure of the law of identity. The law supplies a blade, not a place to put it. Grounds for the placement come only at later levels, and something is always standing on the line: viruses, prions, the little cone at the opening of the first essay. Look from another direction, and it no longer qualifies. The remainder of the law of identity is whatever stands on the blade's edge.

2DD is contradiction: a thing cannot be both A and not-A at the same time.

The Ediacaran organisms in the ninth essay show this level's remainder most clearly. Was Dickinsonia an animal or a lichen? Was Charniodiscus a relative of corals? Over the decades, each was put into several different boxes, then taken out again. A classification table requires every kind of organism to occupy one box only. That is exactly what the law of non-contradiction demands. But Ediacaran bodies did not grow to fit the table.

Essay 9 contrasted boxes and trees. Boxes sort selected traits; trees follow an ancestor and its descendants. These do not obey different laws of noncontradiction: they classify on different grounds. An organism can be an early member of a branch yet fit none of our familiar trait boxes. Logical consistency does not guarantee that a classification exhausts the world.

3DD is interval: there is a distance between here and there.

The golden spikes in Essay 23 make interval tangible. Geologists choose a reference point so boundaries can be correlated across the world. More than a hundred positions need definition, and approval is required. The point itself has no thickness. Layers on both sides belong to named intervals, but a shared name cannot contain everything that happened in them.

The framework places interval before causality as a structural order, not a claim that the universe made space before time. Paleontology often starts with stacked strata: in a sedimentary sequence not overturned or otherwise disturbed, lower layers generally formed first. Reading above and below as before and after is a beginning, not yet a clock. Thickness needs sedimentation rates, isotopic dates, or other calibration to yield years; molecular differences need rate models too. The disagreements of Essay 19 arise within these conversions and gaps.

4DD is causality: what comes before constrains what comes after, and there is no going back.

The Great Oxidation in the fourth essay is the prototype for this level. Oxygen released by cyanobacteria, originally the waste product of photosynthesis, accumulated in the oceans and atmosphere and rewrote the chemistry of the whole planet. There was no going back: oxidized iron sank to the seafloor and became layers of banded iron ore. They are still there today. The coal in the thirteenth essay tells the same story. A forest's carbon was buried underground, to be dug up by people hundreds of millions of years later. What cannot be replayed backward is the whole history, not the chemistry: iron can be reduced again, and oxygen levels can fall.

Causality brings the first three levels together. With identity, difference, and distance established, direction must be added before the world becomes one in which “before” constrains “after.” But the eighteenth essay names the remainder at this level. At the end of the Cretaceous, an asteroid struck the Gulf of Mexico, while vast expanses of basalt were erupting in India. Both events happened, both were large enough, and they occurred very close together in time. The law of causality guarantees that the extinction had causes. It does not guarantee that we can disentangle them. The direction can be clear while contributions remain uncertain. New records and models can narrow the possibilities; lacking a second Earth does not make attribution forever unknowable.

Look at these four levels together, and a common feature appears. In this series, none of them is in the rocks. They are in the hands of the people reading the rocks. A stone does not say “this is life,” sort itself into a box, drive a spike into itself, or declare “it was the impact, not the volcanoes.”

The twenty-third essay put it this way: “A construct made by a chiseler without intentions is described by a group of chiselers who have intentions.” The first round consists of the four tools these people carry with them. They are a priori, which does not mean they cannot be wrong. It means they were not learned from fossils. They were already in the reader's hands before the fossil was picked up.

3 The Second Round: A Line Drawn on the Self for the First Time

The second round's four levels, replication, self-maintenance, differentiation, and reproduction, are the bones of the first half of this series. In the first round, readers drew the lines. From this round onward, life begins to draw its own.

5DD is replication: this pattern does not disappear.

The last universal common ancestor in the second essay gives this level its shape. All life on Earth today, bacteria, archaea, oaks, humans, uses a nearly universal genetic code with some variants, the same energy currency, the same kind of ribosome. These common features point back to a common ancestor. Yet that ancestor was reconstructed out of absence. It left not a single fossil of itself. People worked backward from what all its descendants share and inferred an outline.

The law of replication says that the pattern does not disappear. It does not promise that every copy will be identical. In the second essay, genes passing sideways between bacteria turned the root of the tree of life into a web. The early forms of life that left no descendants, meanwhile, never even got the chance to be inferred. The remainder of replication is everything that was not copied onward.

6DD is self-maintenance: actively resisting decay.

The membrane in the third essay is the first line drawn by nature. A layer of lipids encloses a little cluster of chemicals. Concentrations inside can differ from those outside and, more than that, can stay different. This is self-maintenance at its smallest: preserving an unevenness in a world where everything tends toward uniformity. When this series defined a “construct,” it expressly included the words “the line may be drawn by nature, as with a membrane.” This is the level they refer to.

The experiment in Essay 1 brings the question close. In 2016, cycles of design, synthesis, and reduction produced a cell with 473 genes that grew under specified culture conditions. This was not the absolute minimum for all life. At publication, 149 genes lacked specific functional assignments, and later work continued investigating them. Building a working reduced system did not mean understanding every part it needed.

Setting replication and self-maintenance side by side also unpicks an old argument from the first essay. At the origin of life, which came first: replication or metabolism? Molecules that could copy themselves, or chemical cycles that could sustain themselves? The first essay set out seven routes. Each had people following it. The last line of every route was blank.

The ladder puts replication at 5DD and self-maintenance at 6DD. But the ladder's order is structural. It says that a complete life must have both; without either one, it does not count. It does not say which appeared first in history. One reason this argument never ends is that both sides want the order of structures to testify on behalf of the order of events.

7DD is differentiation: what was alike becomes unlike.

Multicellularity in Essay 8 brings the issue into focus. A broad count reaches at least forty-five origins, but cells gathering together is not always a division into reproductive and other roles. Many lineages persist as filaments or clusters, not failed halfway products. Complex specialization is concentrated in fewer branches, including animals, land plants, and some large algae and fungi.

The hive in Essay 16 suggests differentiation at another scale. Queens and workers share a genetic toolkit, not necessarily identical DNA; larval nutrition and other factors influence developmental routes. The queen normally does most egg-laying and worker reproduction is suppressed, but workers can lay eggs under some conditions. The strong division of labor makes a colony body-like in some respects, not literally a body of cellular bees.

The twenty-first essay took this level in reverse. Parasitic barnacles shed almost their entire bodies, retaining reproductive structures and a rootlike nutrient-absorbing network; cave fish lost their eyes. Differentiation does not have to keep adding things.

8DD is reproduction: one generation hands itself on anew to the next.

This level combines the previous three. A fertilized egg has to replicate anew, maintain itself anew, and differentiate anew into an entire body. Every generation grows from the beginning again. Sexual reproduction adds something else to this process: information from two different individuals is recombined. The sixth essay called it “institutionalizing chance.” Every generation has a twofold bill to pay. For a female that reproduces only asexually, all her offspring are fertile daughters; half the offspring of sexual reproduction are male. Asexual lineages have never disappeared, yet among complex forms of life, recombination is almost everywhere. The twofold figure belongs to a particular comparison: a roughly equal sex ratio, no added paternal care, and otherwise comparable conditions. It is not a fixed bill paid by every sexual organism.

Here the ladder runs straight into a mismatch with time. Differentiation occupies 7DD, reproduction 8DD. In history, though, sexual reproduction in eukaryotes appeared before complex multicellular bodies. This series likewise dealt with sex in the sixth essay before turning to multicellularity in the eighth.

A mismatch is not a mistake. The ladder asks what has to be in place for something to constitute reproduction in full. A body capable of differentiation grows all over again in each generation: that is what reproduction looks like once it has incorporated differentiation. Which happened first on Earth is a question for time. The ladder has nothing to say about it. What comes earlier in a structure need not have come earlier in history.

Something else happens in the second round that did not happen in the first. In the fifth essay, a bacterium was swallowed by another cell, escaped digestion, and became a mitochondrion. In the eighth, cells gathered into a body that reproduced together. In the sixteenth, worker bees formed a colony selected as a whole. Three times, the unit being selected moved to a different scale. This series calls such an event a phase transition. This concerns highly integrated systems in which the larger whole becomes an important unit of selection. Not every social insect has made the same transition, nor does lower-level selection disappear.

A phase transition is not a climb up one rung. It occurs within the same level. The “self” that draws the line changes from a cell to a larger cell, from a cell to a body, from a body to a colony. The ladder has not changed. What stands on it has.

4 The Third Round: From Being Sifted to Seeing

The third round is selection, perception, memory, and prediction. Fossil outcomes and organs offer more direct ways to discuss the first two; the processes of the latter two rarely preserve directly, so this afterword turns to researchers remembering and predicting. That is a difference in our evidential window, not a claim that ancient animals lacked those capacities.

9DD is selection: some descendants remain, and others do not.

Three whole essays in this series concern selection in its bluntest form. The fourteenth treats the end of the Permian, where the best-known figure has 96 percent of marine species going extinct; the essay examines how that number was calculated. The eighteenth treats the end of the Cretaceous, through which only the bird branch of the dinosaurs made it. The twentieth treats the Cenozoic, when mammals filled the ecological niches left empty.

The law of selection is a level this series has had to defend again and again, because it is the easiest to misrepresent. The fourteenth essay quoted the title of Raup's little book: “Extinction: Bad Genes or Bad Luck?” Survivors need not be the ones that ordinarily do best. The twentieth essay ended by saying that no one was waiting for a place, and no place was waiting for anyone. The sieve has no eyes. What it sifts out and what it keeps depend on conditions at that moment. Once the moment has passed, the conditions change again.

10DD is perception: actively sensing the environment and responding to it.

The Cambrian in the tenth essay is this level's brightest moment in the fossil record. Every tiny lens in a trilobite's compound eye was a transparent grain of calcite, its crystal's optical axis aligned with the incoming light. These were animals that saw the world through stone. One explanation links the Cambrian explosion to the appearance of eyes. Once there were hunters that could see, being seen became a matter of life and death. Shells, spines, hiding, camouflage: all at once they had a use. The tenth essay offered this as one explanation among several, never as the answer.

Dinosaur colors, in the seventeenth essay, turn perception in another direction. The significance of color often lies in being seen by other eyes: courtship, warning, concealment. To reconstruct a dinosaur's color today, researchers rely not on their own eyes but on the shapes of melanosomes under a microscope. The seventeenth essay asked readers to read the conclusion twice. On the second reading, ask: if bright yellow had been there, would this method see it? The researchers' perception is on one level, the animals' perception on another. Between them lie a hundred million years and a proxy mainly for melanin-related colors, not the whole palette.

11DD is memory: retaining past perceptions.

Here, for the first time, particular care is needed. The eleventh essay said that the fossil record is a sieve, not an inventory. A deer that dies in a forest will almost certainly leave nothing. Strata retain certain things, but strata remember nothing. The law of memory concerns a living thing retaining its own past perceptions for its own future use. There is no such “self” in the rock.

Strata retain traces rather than remembering as organisms do. Readers lend the rocks their own memory. Coal and reefs need conditions that permit accumulation and preservation. Disturbance can interrupt or erode a record without making all later accumulation impossible. Essay 15 asks why some large, sustained systems recovered slowly; small reefs and local deposits still count. Read its claim about missing continuity within that scope.

12DD is prediction: inferring the future from past patterns.

This level brings the third round together. With selection, perception, and memory, a mind can infer what will happen from what has happened. Paleontology as a whole uses this level in reverse: it infers the past from present patterns.

The molecular clock in the nineteenth essay is this backward inference in its purest form. Take the difference between two species' DNA, apply a roughly steady rate of change, and estimate when they diverged. The twelfth essay recorded a case in which backward inference went awry. According to the tree of life, transitional fossils between fish and tetrapods ought to have come first, tetrapod footprints later. Then footprints older than those transitional fossils turned up in a Polish quarry. The inference had not gone wrong in its arithmetic. It had gone wrong by assuming that the order of the record was the order of events.

The title of the first essay's last section names the remainder at this level: “A sample size of one, and tools made to fit that one.” We have seen only one kind of life. Every tool we use to recognize life was made to fit that kind. How far prediction can go depends on how many patterns the past has supplied. For life, it has supplied only one.

Together, this round reveals a shift in narrative viewpoint: from selected groups, eyes, and colors to researchers retaining fragmentary traces and making predictions. It is the perspective chosen by this essay, not a fossil-established cognitive boundary between animals and humans.

The second round mostly examined life's organization; the latter half of the third brings its readers into view. Life did not wait until this point to remember. Rather, we can no longer pretend that the people reading the picture stand outside it.

5 At the Threshold of the Fourth Round

The fourth round begins at 13DD, the self-subject: not just prediction happening, but recognizing the experience as mine—“I am predicting.”

The twenty-third essay turned the lens on the series itself. It said that, at the outset, this series wanted to take the human out of the role of chiseler. Natural selection divides up the space of possible forms; it has neither purpose nor prejudice, and its constructs still cannot close. That holds at the first level. At the second level, the chiselers have been the same people all along: the people classifying, voting, drawing diagrams, arranging these twenty-three essays.

A vote on a golden spike is a very small scene of 13DD. A group of people faces a rock wall. Where, on the same piece of stone, should the spike go? They have to vote by a show of hands. At that moment they are not merely making predictions about strata. They know that they themselves are making the predictions and that they themselves are drawing this line. That is why they have to vote, why they have to record their reasons. For the first time, the line at 13DD draws the person drawing it into the picture.

Essay 22 stops with Miocene apes without unfolding the later human branch. In this afterword's order of reading, that leaves us at the question of selfhood. This is a narrative boundary, not a fossil date for the origin of self or a denial of related capacities in other animals. Asking “How do I become myself?” brings the reader even more plainly into the picture.

14DD is the subject as its own end: recognizing not only “I,” but a direction and value of one's own to take responsibility for. Asking why to act within a finite life is one way into this question, not an examination for subjecthood. Inability to articulate it does not cancel a person's standing as an end.

Looking back, this explains a danger the essays resist: projecting a later destination into the past. Worker reproduction need not be explained as moral sacrifice; changes in fish limbs were not made for a future terrestrial endpoint. Costs and functions can be discussed without treating outcomes as proof of what an organism—or an entire lineage—intended.

Purpose here means a subject taking its life as its own responsibility. It does not cover every biological function or goal-directed behavior. Hindsight readily overlays human ends on evolution, making every change appear to prepare for us. In this framework's language, that is the colonization to resist: our story swallowing possibilities that remained open to those lives.

Species extinction and individual death are not merely larger and smaller versions of the same event. One ends a lineage; the other ends a life. Reflecting on death is another layer of experience, but inability to reflect does not make death weightless. This series mainly traces extinction and persistence, leaving a subject's encounter with its own finitude for another inquiry.

This afterword will not go beyond 14DD. Only one thing can be said: from beginning to end, this series has had a subject that never appeared in any of its essays.

6 The Ladder Has No Motor

Now we return to the point set down at the beginning: a ladder is not an elevator.

Things arranged one level above another are easily read as a process of moving upward, level by level. From replication to self-maintenance, from differentiation to reproduction, from selection to perception, then memory, then prediction, then the animal that can say “it is I.” The procession dismantled in the twenty-third essay, an ape straightening up step by step until it becomes a human, is that reading turned into a picture.

Several essays in this series stand squarely in the way of such a reading.

Essay 7, the Boring Billion. Coarse geochemical curves and sparse fossils can make the interval look quiet; that does not mean eukaryotes stopped innovating. At low resolution, much change disappears from view. If the ladder metaphor helps, it must first allow a history that does not display an ascent at every turn.

Essay 8: multicellularity arose repeatedly, with very different degrees of specialization. Filaments and clusters can persist for immense spans. They are not stuck below a rung they were obliged to climb. Continuing that way is a complete history too.

The twenty-first: simplification. An entire essay about lineages going down. Parasites shed their digestive tracts, cave fish lost their eyes, and some animals dwindled from a complex body to just a few cells.

Essay 15, the gap. For millions of years after extinction, many studied regions lack widespread coal and large, complex reef systems—not every trace of coal or every small reef worldwide. Life persisted. Some modes of accumulation and organization requiring longer stable windows recovered much more slowly.

Together, these four essays establish a structural property of the ladder: the remainder at each level makes room for the next. It does not push anything up into it.

When this framework discusses the move from 3DD to 4DD, it uses a phrase: the remainder of interval opens up causality. Distances in space cannot close the account; they leave the question of before and after, and causality establishes itself there. Read the later levels in the same way. Replication cannot close the account either. Copies can go wrong; the environment can wear them away. Self-maintenance now has somewhere to stand.

But making room is not supplying motive force. A framework's allowance for complexity does not require history to produce it; once produced, it may simplify or disappear. These three billion years constrain the metaphor: a ladder may organize dependencies, but cannot install a motor in evolution. This is a philosophical reading answerable to the evidence, not a fossil proof of the entire DD theory.

That is both a constraint on the ladder and a protection for it. If the ladder had a motor, the purpose of 14DD would be read as something destined to arrive ever since 5DD. The whole ladder would become a work of teleology. Yet this framework began by doing nothing but negating. Every level on it grew into being. None was summoned.

7 This Series Is a Line Too

Finally, put the series itself back on the ladder.

What it does belongs to the first round: drawing lines, excluding, measuring intervals, identifying causes. What it describes belongs to the second and third: replication, self-maintenance, differentiation, reproduction, selection, perception, and the tiny fraction of things that fossils have retained for us. Its act of writing belongs to the last level of the third round: inferring the past itself from the patterns of the past. The essay in which it looks back at itself, the twenty-third, is already standing at 13DD.

Each level leaves further questions. Identity has its edge cases; classification meets lives outside old boxes; named intervals cannot contain the rocks; causal attribution still needs testing. Replication leaves things untransmitted, self-maintenance genes not yet understood, differentiation enduring simplicity, reproduction costs that depend on conditions. Selection includes contingency, perception uses proxies, preservation filters traces, prediction works with limited evidence. Remainder is not a universal “we don't know,” but an opening for the next specific question.

This is not the series' failure. A central judgment of the framework is that having a remainder is not a defect in a construct. A construct cannot close because the very act of drawing a line leaves something on each side. Every new level of the ladder draws another line and leaves another remainder. It is still so after sixteen levels.

The twenty-third essay ended by saying that this series was written from outside the ladder; the next, on ancient humans, cannot stand outside it. We can now make that statement a little more precise. This series concerns the second and third rounds, while both its writer and its readers are already above the third. The next series will ask how a kind of animal reached the point where it could read this one. It will come to 13DD. It will come to 14DD. It will encounter a chiseler who knows that it is drawing a line.

Then the level left unwritten in this afterword may appear there in another form. Before that happens, however, anyone who has read this far has already done something possible only in the fourth round: recognized these twenty-three essays as a construct, recognized that its lines were drawn by people, and decided to keep reading.

You need not memorize every rung. Return to a rock, a feather, a tooth. Ask what it lets us see and what it does not yet reveal. The next question is yours to choose.
