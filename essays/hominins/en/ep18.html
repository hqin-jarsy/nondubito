A Question You Were Not Allowed to Ask

1. Article Two

On March 8, 1866, the statutes of the Linguistic Society of Paris were approved. Article Two was a single sentence: “The Society will accept no submission concerning either the origin of language or the creation of a universal language.”

A committee drafted the statutes. They were discussed publicly, voted on and sent to the ministry for approval. Article Two governed what papers the society would accept. It put two topics side by side and accepted neither: where language came from, and how to make a language everyone could use. The society’s own history page still calls it the famous Article Two.

Language does not leave fossils. Say something and the sound scatters into the air. It is gone in a second or two. Bones buried in the ground can stay there for hundreds of thousands of years. When people returned to the question about a century later, what they had in their hands was mostly bone.

The bones came from several places: a Neanderthal skeleton in a small cave in south-central France, a skeleton about 60,000 years old from a cave at Mount Carmel in Israel, and fossils more than 400,000 years old from a bone pit in northern Spain. Another piece of evidence was not bone at all. It was a gene in people alive today.

The question took a different form. Not where language came from, but whether this body could make the sounds of speech, and whether it could hear them clearly.

2. A Wastepaper Basket in London

A wastepaper basket full of speculation about the origins of all languages was a London society president’s joke. On May 16, 1873, Alexander Ellis gave his second annual address as president of the Philological Society in London. One section was called “The Origin of Language.”

Discussing some of the theories current at the time, he said questions of this sort did not belong to philological research in the strict sense. Some later histories of linguistics set the remark beside Article Two in Paris. A few went so far as to say that the London society had banned the topic around 1872 too.

One historian of linguistics says Ellis declared the question outside philology proper. In the text from the time, it is one president’s judgment in a speech, not a clause in the rules.

3. A Skeleton’s Throat

Can you reconstruct a throat from a skeleton? In 1971, Philip Lieberman and Edmund Crelin used the skull of the La Chapelle Neanderthal from France to reconstruct its vocal tract, the passage sound takes from the larynx to the lips. Their paper began by calling language the most important thing distinguishing humans from other animals.

They measured cross sections every half centimeter in the reconstructed tract and in a newborn’s vocal tract, then used an acoustic model to work out what vowels it could make. They concluded that Neanderthals lacked the anatomical prerequisites for the full range of human speech. In their reconstruction and calculations, the tract could not produce several English vowels and consonants.

The same paper said the brain might have developed enough to support a language, and that some form of language had existed. The authors acknowledged that they could say little about how the larynx produced sound or how it was controlled.

In 1975, a researcher working from the comparative anatomy of human and chimpanzee throats criticized Crelin’s reconstruction. Place the larynx as he had, and even swallowing would be a problem. The verdict that Neanderthals could not make certain sounds, the critic concluded, remained unproven because the larynx had been reconstructed in a questionable way.

Later, a team used another model of the vocal tract and calculated a larger vowel space. The title said that a Neanderthal’s potential vowel space was as large as a modern human’s. The authors made clear that they had studied only shape and acoustics. In a later reply they added that their results did not prove Neanderthals could speak.

Lieberman replied that it was wrong to reduce the 1971 paper to “a species that could not speak.” He had said Neanderthals were not as well equipped for language as living humans. Two models of the vocal tract. One says several vowels are beyond it. The other gives it a potential vowel space as large as ours.

4. A Small Bone Inside a Jaw

The hyoid is a small horseshoe-shaped bone above the throat, where muscles of the tongue attach. Kebara Cave on Mount Carmel in Israel yielded a Neanderthal skeleton in 1983, numbered Kebara 2. It was about 60,000 years old. Its bones were still in place relative to one another.

The hyoid of Kebara 2 was not recognized in the cave. It turned up during cleaning in the lab, within the area enclosed by the jaw and still in its anatomical position.

In 1989 a group published it in Nature under the title “A Middle Palaeolithic Human Hyoid Bone.” The abstract said it was almost identical to the hyoid of modern humans in size and shape. The morphological basis for human speech appeared to have been fully developed.

In 2013, another team scanned it, looked at the structure inside, modeled the forces on it and compared it with human hyoids today. They began with a caution: similarity in overall shape does not necessarily show that a hyoid was used the same way. Their findings, they concluded, were consistent with Neanderthals being capable of speech.

5. An Opening for a Nerve

Can a skull show how well a tongue could move? The nerve that controls the tongue’s movements passes through a small channel at the skull base called the hypoglossal canal. In 1998, three researchers proposed that a wider canal might carry more fibers for controlling movement. They measured a set of skulls. Chimpanzees averaged about ten square millimeters. Living humans averaged about nineteen.

Two fossil Neanderthals averaged about sixteen, and the Kabwe skull from Zambia gave about nineteen. The reasoning rested on an if: if, as the researchers supposed, the size of the canal reflected the number of motor nerve fibers.

On that basis they suggested that human-like speech might have appeared in some people several hundred thousand years ago. The paper also said that the nerve was not the only thing in the canal. There was a bundle of veins too.

In 1999 three Berkeley researchers replied in the same journal. The abstract said they had falsified the hypothesis. The Berkeley release explained that they had checked seventy-five primate skulls, including monkeys and apes. Whether they took the absolute or relative size of the canal, the same forty fell in the modern human range.

They also dissected five bodies. There was no clear connection between the size of the canal and the size of the nerve or the number of its fibers. In one person the canal on one side was about twice the size of the other. Their conclusion was that the size of the hypoglossal canal was not a reliable sign of speech ability.

David DeGusta, one of the authors, told the university that if the hypothesis were right, human hypoglossal canals should be larger than monkeys’, because monkeys do not speak. The university’s headline said graduate students had overturned the theory that Neanderthals could talk. Their paper had overturned one indicator: the size of the canal.

6. Four Thousand Hertz

A telephone line needs to carry frequencies only up to around four thousand hertz for speech to be clear. In 2004, a paper used this as an example. The human ear is particularly sensitive between two and four thousand hertz, where a great deal of the information in speech lies.

The researchers studied five fossils more than 400,000 years old from the Pit of Bones in northern Spain. A whole ear does not survive. They measured dimensions in the bony ear canal and middle ear, then fed the numbers into a model of how sound travels through them.

In the model, the human-like pattern of sound transmission extended to around five thousand hertz in the fossils, setting them apart from chimpanzees. In relating this to communication, the authors spoke of what the results suggested and what was possible to imagine.

In 2021, a group compared fossils from the pit with Neanderthals and people today. They calculated a measure called occupied bandwidth, roughly the width of the frequency range where the ear is most sensitive. Neanderthals came out close to us; the range from the bone pit was narrower. The title described similar hearing and speech abilities in Neanderthals and Homo sapiens.

An American university put it more firmly in a release: Neanderthals could perceive and produce human speech. A coauthor told the public that the results were solid and clearly showed that ability. The abstract said “implied”; the coauthor speaking in the release said “clearly showed.”

7. One Gene

Several members of one family had a severe speech and language disorder. A person unrelated to them had similar symptoms. On October 4, 2001, Nature published a paper identifying a change in the same gene in these people. The title said the gene was mutated in a severe speech and language disorder.

Headlines went further. The Washington Post reported the discovery of a language gene. Wired called it the first language gene. A Chinese site reposting a Xinhua report gave it a more guarded title: a gene associated with language ability had been found. Language gene became a familiar label in Chinese reporting as well.

Faraneh Vargha-Khadem, a coauthor, told a reporter that she did not think “language or grammar gene” was accurate. The main difficulty lay in speech and articulation more than in the most complex forms of language. Another author called it the first gene implicated in a speech and language disorder.

In 2002, a team compared the gene in humans, chimpanzees, gorillas, orangutans, rhesus macaques and mice. In the human lineage the protein it encoded had changed in two places. The changes and the pattern of variation strongly suggested recent selection, the authors said. The data fit a model in which the two changes spread through the population within about the last 200,000 years. The family’s mutation was not at either site.

In 2007, a team read the gene in Neanderthal DNA, checking specifically for contamination from modern humans. The two key changes in the human lineage were in Neanderthals too.

The team proposed that the two changes and the related selection might have preceded the split. In the paper’s estimate, the common ancestor lived about 300,000 to 400,000 years ago. That did not fit the later date of the 2002 paper.

Late in 2012, researchers used DNA from a Neanderthal who lived around 49,000 years ago to compare a long stretch containing the gene with that of living people. They found several changes whose new versions are carried by nearly everyone today but were absent or not always found in Neanderthals. One lay at a place involved in turning activity on or off. In an experiment the new version did not activate the switch as strongly as the old one. In 2018 another group recalculated using data from more populations and found no evidence of that kind of recent selection in Homo sapiens. What researchers first found in that family was a change linked to a speech disorder. It cannot put a postmark on the beginning of speech.

8. The Eighth Row

A table, eight rows. The last shows a move from primate societies to human societies, with “language” alongside. The table appeared in a review titled “The Major Evolutionary Transitions,” published in Nature on March 16, 1995, by Eörs Szathmáry and John Maynard Smith.

In a 1999 book, the two set human language beside DNA. They described it as the other known system of unlimited heredity: a finite number of sounds can be arranged to produce an unlimited number of meanings.

A later line of work in biology asks when a new individual emerges. Think of cells and a body: in a multicellular body, most cells leave the production of offspring to the body as a whole. That approach boils down to three questions.

First, did the units that formerly produced descendants of their own lose the ability to do so independently? Second, did a new unit emerge that reproduces as a whole, with gains and losses in fitness reckoned at that level? Third, is there a mechanism that consistently keeps conflicts among the lower-level units in check? On this definition, if even one condition is missing, there is no new individual.

Put language to the test, starting at the first question. People who speak do not hand over the production of offspring. Children are born to parents, not to a group that speaks the same language. Move somewhere else and change languages, and you can still have children. On the three-question test, language stops at the first question.

Szathmáry and Maynard Smith also listed several recurring features. One was already close to that first question: units that once replicated independently can, after some transitions, replicate only as part of a larger whole. Another was a change in the way information is stored and transmitted, and they explicitly included human language as such a change. The features recurred in many transitions, they wrote. They did not all have to appear in every one.

In discussing when a group could count as a whole, they listed a condition: the members’ gains and losses in fitness come into line. They themselves said it could not hold at the very moment the transition began. Their list did not require every feature. The three-question test requires all three.

The 1971 vocal-tract paper ended with the claim that the ability to speak that way is what makes us human. In 2020 a Chinese university introducing a linguistics course called language an important mark of being human.

A 2026 report from China’s Xinhua News Agency on preserving language resources took another approach. It spoke only of what language does: a tool for exchanging ideas, a carrier of civilization, a bridge to understanding.

A hyoid, a hypoglossal canal, an ear, a vocal tract, a gene. What can be measured is whether certain sounds could be made, whether frequencies could be heard clearly, and whether certain letters changed in a stretch of DNA. The bones and a stretch of DNA survived. Words scatter as soon as they are spoken. Not one sentence survived.

The line was arranged, not walked.
