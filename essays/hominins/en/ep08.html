Filling a Skull with Peppercorns

1. A Can and a Big Bottle

People often say the brain is what separates humans from apes: the bigger the brain, the smarter and the more human. The Smithsonian’s site on human evolution says on its brain page that brain size tripled over the course of human evolution.

A can of soda holds around 330 milliliters. Lucy lived about 3.2 million years ago. One modern compilation puts the cavity inside her skull at 387 milliliters, a little more than a can of soda. The average for humans today is around 1,330 milliliters, a little less than a big one-and-a-half-liter bottle of water.

The Smithsonian tells the public that the brains of Lucy’s species were under 500 cubic centimeters, about a third the size of ours. What is measured is the empty space inside the skull, called cranial capacity. A cubic centimeter is a milliliter. The brain itself is long gone. What gets measured is the box that once held it.

In 1948, the British anatomist Arthur Keith drew a line through this series of numbers: 750 milliliters. He likened it to the Rubicon, the river Julius Caesar crossed in ancient Rome and from which he could not turn back. In Keith’s scheme, apes were on one side, humans on the other.

Where the line went, how it was drawn, and how it was redrawn all start with how to measure the empty space inside the skull. The method itself changed several times.

2. A Tin Cylinder

In nineteenth-century Philadelphia, the physician Samuel Morton described his method in detail. In his 1839 book Crania Americana, he wrote that he plugged the smaller holes in the base of the skull with cotton. Then he poured in white peppercorns through the foramen magnum, packing them down with his fingers until the skull could hold no more.

Once a skull was full, he poured the peppercorns into a tall, narrow tin cylinder, shook them down, and read the scale on a measuring rod. He had marked the scale using water, adding a mark for each five cubic inches. The water calibrated the scale. Peppercorns filled the skull. A cubic inch is just over sixteen milliliters.

A few years later Morton switched to lead shot. Many later accounts say he had used mustard seed in his early work, and researchers found that Morton himself later referred to white mustard seed. In 2018, anthropologist Paul Mitchell found Morton’s handwritten measurements from 1840 and noted that some skulls in the collection still had peppercorns lodged in their cracks.

Few people pour seeds into skulls now. One way to measure the cavity is to cast its inner surface, making what is called an endocast. More often, the kind of CT scan used in hospitals records the skull slice by slice. On a computer, the researcher traces the cavity, closes the openings and calculates the volume. In 2008 a Japanese team had five people measure two modern human skulls by both methods. The technical measurement error was about twelve milliliters with millet and about 1.4 milliliters with CT.

3. One Skull, Several Numbers

A scan can make measurement error small. It cannot do the same for problems with the skull. When the little one-meter-tall hominin from the Indonesian island of Flores was published in 2004, its cranial capacity was put at about 380 milliliters. A year later another team made a virtual endocast from CT scans and got 417.

The numbers differed by thirty-seven. The group attributed the gap to the way openings in the skull were closed and to measurement error introduced in reconstruction. In 2013 a more detailed scan gave 426.

Skull 1470, the Kenyan find known by its accession number, was put at around 800 milliliters in the 1970s press. A 2017 compilation gave 752. The Smithsonian’s public page gives 775 and calls the large capacity one of its truly distinguishing features.

The skull of Little Foot in South Africa was crushed and distorted in the cave. The researchers who measured 408 milliliters expressly called that a minimum estimate. For a child’s skull there is also the step of projecting to adulthood. In 1948 Keith used the then-current estimate of 500 milliliters for the Taung Child. His proportions for childhood development gave an adult estimate around 650. One skull, several numbers. It matters how you close the holes, fit the pieces, fill in crushed areas and decide whether to turn a child into an adult.

4. The Rubicon

Keith’s book was called A New Theory of Human Evolution. The section about the line was titled “Crossing the Rubicon Between Ape and Man.” He asked first whether the brain could draw a boundary that teeth and posture had failed to make clear.

He listed two sets of numbers. Another anatomist had measured skulls from Indigenous Australians; the lowest result among women was 855 milliliters. In gorillas, the highest result among males was 650.

On the evidence then available to him, Keith first put the boundary somewhere between 700 and 800, then set it at 750. This fell between the largest gorilla skull in his figures and the smallest skull he listed from an Indigenous Australian woman.

He then placed fossils on either side of the line. He required a group mean of 750 or more; the individual specimens did not all have to cross it. Two Javan skulls that he then took to be female and male gave 750 and 950 respectively. Their average was 850, on the human side.

For several South African specimens he gave 650, 560 and 435, all below the line. Elsewhere the book gave 775 for a Javan specimen from 1938, just over the boundary. In the early skulls from Java the cavity had been filled by something as hard as cement. It had to be cleared before an endocast could be made.

5. Six Hundred

In 1964, Louis Leakey, Phillip Tobias and John Napier named Homo habilis in Nature. The species name means able or skillful. The paper opened by reviewing three earlier dividing lines, 700, 750 and 800 milliliters, proposed by three researchers, one of them Keith.

Then they gave a new definition of Homo. The pelvis and legs were adapted to habitual upright posture and a two-legged gait. The forelimbs were shorter than the hindlimbs. The thumb could fully oppose the fingers, and besides a strong grasp, the hand had at least a simple precision grip.

Cranial capacity varied considerably, the paper said. Its average was higher than in australopiths, but the low end overlapped with the high end in australopiths. The range went from about 600 in the early forms to more than 1,600.

A common later account says that the threshold for the brain size of Homo was lowered to 600 to make room for habilis. A 1999 history of the debate said the capacity range for Homo had to come down to 600. The Smithsonian’s current page also says that the lower limit on brain size was reduced.

The type specimen of habilis, a juvenile found by Jonathan Leakey at Olduvai Gorge in 1960, is nicknamed “Johnny’s Child” on the Smithsonian site. The 2017 compilation gives a cranial capacity of 729, at the low end of the 729–824 milliliter range estimated in its 2015 reconstruction. The same table gives a mean of 616 for habilis. The new definition was challenged as soon as it appeared. In 1965 a scientist asked in Nature whether habilis and the australopiths could be told apart. The following year Tobias replied under the title “The Distinctiveness of Homo habilis.”

6. Out of Order

Set several numbers from the 2017 compilation side by side. One skull in Australopithecus africanus has a capacity of 510 milliliters. One in Homo habilis has 509. One counts as an australopith, the other as Homo. The estimates differ by a milliliter.

At Dmanisi, Georgia, five skulls come from the same site and period. The smallest is 546, the largest 730. If the bar had been lowered to 600, four are above it and one below. Across the whole species Homo erectus, the table goes from a little over 540 to a little over 1,250. The difference is more than 700 milliliters, more than two cans of soda.

Two skulls of Homo naledi from South Africa’s Dinaledi Chamber held 465 and 560 milliliters, not far from australopiths. They date to about 335,000 to 236,000 years ago. A skull from another chamber held 610 and had not been dated directly when it was published in 2017.

Australopithecus sediba is around two million years old, with a capacity of 420. Homo floresiensis is around 100,000 to 60,000 years old, also around 420. Make a clock of brain size and it does not run in order. Across the long run, brain size in hominins has generally increased. But ranking skulls one at a time from earlier to later with that measure is something else.

7. Neanderthals

People often say Neanderthals had bigger brains than we do. The Smithsonian site says their brains were as large as ours and often larger. The same sentence goes on to say that their bodies were stockier too. In a widely cited compilation, the ranges for Neanderthals and Homo sapiens overlap substantially. Both run from around 1,100 to over 1,700 or 1,800 milliliters.

In 2020, three researchers compared cranial capacities for eleven Neanderthals and ninety-four skulls from recent humans. The Neanderthal values were significantly larger only in the comparison with the women in that sample. The researchers placed Neanderthal brain size at the upper end of variation in recent humans. By brain size, Neanderthals do not go behind people today, but they do not necessarily go ahead either.

Another team compared 163 individuals of the genus Homo from the Pleistocene, from about 2.6 million years ago until a little over ten thousand years ago. Their average body weight was about a tenth greater than ours. Absolute brain size and brain size relative to body weight are two different numbers.

Hence a relative measure, often called the encephalization quotient. Divide actual brain size by what an animal of that weight would be expected to have. What counts as expected depends on which group of animals goes into the formula. In 2007 a study of cognition in nonhuman primates found that absolute brain size was a better predictor than the quotient. In 2021 another group simply titled its paper “Farewell to the Encephalization Quotient.”

8. The Same Measure

In 1839, Morton divided his skulls into the five “races” of his day and averaged their capacities. The “Caucasian” group, at eighty-seven cubic inches, came out highest. The “Ethiopian” group, which in that classification meant people from sub-Saharan Africa, came out lowest at seventy-eight.

The entry for the Americas was printed as eighty-two. Morton corrected it by hand to eighty in some copies. Recalculate from the individual cases and the result is 80.2. At eighty-two, the Americas ranked above the “Malay” group. At eighty, they ranked below it. A single misprinted number could switch two entries around.

In 1978, the paleontologist Stephen Jay Gould published an article in Science titled “Morton’s Ranking of Races by Cranial Capacity: Unconscious Manipulation of Data May Be a Scientific Norm.” He did not measure the skulls again. He recalculated from Morton’s published figures.

By Gould’s reckoning, the change from seeds to lead shot added 5.4 cubic inches to the mean for Morton’s “Black” group and only 1.8 to the “Caucasian” group. Gould strongly suspected a systematic bias that led to underestimates for Black skulls.

In 2011, a team returned to Morton’s collection and measured again the skulls it could identify, about half the original collection. The researchers found that Morton’s lead-shot measurements were largely accurate. They concluded that his errors showed no consistent direction by group.

The argument then moved to a different question. As one philosopher of science pointed out, accuracy with lead shot did not dispose of Gould’s criticisms of how the seeds were packed or how the samples were chosen. Another group distinguished several questions: Are the individual numbers accurate? Can those numbers answer the question Morton meant to ask? Can we infer his intentions from the results?

In 2018, Mitchell brought out Morton’s seed measurements from 1840. The data offered no evidence of packing bias in the direction Gould had suggested. At the same time Mitchell made clear that Morton’s prior beliefs about race could still have shaped how he grouped, sampled and interpreted the skulls.

Cranial capacity can be measured again and again. Keith’s 750 and the later “600” each cut a line through a series of milliliters. There are always skulls the line cannot sort neatly. The table gives two estimates one milliliter apart for fossils in different genera. Homo floresiensis, from tens of thousands of years ago, falls into the same box as Australopithecus sediba from two million years ago. The things a line cannot place cleanly are the remainder.

Put skulls in a row from smallest to largest by milliliters. People did that with fossils. In 1839 they did it with the five “races” and their averages as well.

The line was arranged, not walked.
