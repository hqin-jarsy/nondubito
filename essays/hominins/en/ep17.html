1. A Hat

In 1939, the American anthropologist Carleton Coon published The Races of Europe. Turn to page twenty-four. Figure 1 shows a Neanderthal with hair added, wearing a hat and a set of modern clothes. The caption begins: “Neanderthal man in modern dress.”

The caption goes on to say that someone else had reconstructed the figure from the La Chapelle skeleton. The artist added the hat, hair and clothes. The Neanderthal Museum in Germany describes the picture as a Neanderthal in a hat and suit.

La Chapelle-aux-Saints is a village in south-central France. The skeleton was unearthed at the bottom of a small cave there on August 3, 1908. Two priests from the Bouyssonie family and their companion Bardon had been digging there since 1905.

A Smithsonian account from 1909 says another Bouyssonie helped. Local French materials credit the three Bouyssonie brothers with the discovery. That day they came upon a shallow pit as they were nearly finished clearing a layer of soil left by human activity in the cave.

The pit was roughly rectangular, about a meter and a half long, and not deep. The person lay on his back, his head toward the west, with stones beside it and thirty to forty centimeters of undisturbed soil above. The skull from that skeleton was the basis of the reconstruction in Coon’s book.

The year after the skeleton was found, a French illustrated magazine ran a picture of a Neanderthal. He was hairy all over and looked apelike, and he held a club. The picture circulated widely.

Did Marcellin Boule, the French paleontologist who studied the skeleton, take part in making the picture? Later sources differ. An article on the history of reconstructions says it was made from information Boule supplied. A researcher at an American science library reports finding no archival record that places him directly in charge of it.

Neanderthals lived in Europe and western Asia. Most of the things people have used to measure them are small: engraved lines, beads, eagle talons, broken stalagmites, hand stencils, and a few skeletons.

Some come from places Neanderthals occupied: caves in France, Croatia and Spain, and a site in northern Iraq. Some come from the southern coast of South Africa, the East African Rift or an Indonesian island, and are usually attributed to Homo sapiens. A child’s skull came from a bone pit in northern Spain. Most of the material dates from a little over 170,000 to 50,000 years ago. The child in the pit is much earlier, at about 430,000 years.

More than 110 years have passed since the skeleton came out of the ground in 1908. Count twenty-five years to a generation and what they measured lies thousands or tens of thousands of generations back. The people measuring it span only four or five.

2. A Cultural Big Bang

High above the western shore of Lake Naivasha in the East African Rift is Enkapune Ya Muto, a rock shelter. More than three meters down, excavation yielded obsidian, a dark volcanic glass that flakes to an edge sharp as a scalpel. There were ostrich eggshells at the site as well. Thirteen fragments had been ground into little disk-shaped beads roughly six millimeters across.

In 2002, the American paleoanthropologist Richard Klein and a coauthor wrote a book for the public that opens at that shelter. Stanford’s author page describes it as a bold new theory of what triggered the human cultural big bang.

On Klein’s reading, a decisive change in human behavior took place in Africa about 50,000 years ago. He set the later material against what came before and counted item by item: more efficient hunting, fishing and eating aquatic foods, new forms of tools in stone and bone, denser populations, longer social ties, personal adornment and symbols.

The book connects the symbols on the list with being human. Once symbols appear in the archaeological record, the authors say, we know we are dealing with people like us. They also say no one knows whether the beads were deliberately made to be given as gifts. What follows is conditional: if symbolic meaning had been attached to the beads.

Why then? In a 2004 biographical interview for the U.S. National Academy of Sciences, Klein said the simplest explanation was a genetic change that brought African people to a modern level of intelligence beyond that of Neanderthals. A paper of his in 2008 still put that change around 50,000 years ago.

3. The Revolution That Wasn’t

Did the things on the list all arrive together? In 2000, the archaeologists Sally McBrearty and Alison Brooks published a paper in a journal of human evolution. It ran over 110 pages and was called “The Revolution That Wasn’t.”

The model they criticized was known as the human revolution. As their abstract described it, the model had modern behaviors appearing suddenly around 40,000 to 50,000 years ago. They laid out earlier evidence from Africa item by item: long thin blades and small stone tools, bone tools, wider ranges, specialized hunting, aquatic food, long-distance exchange, systematic processing and use of pigments, art and adornment.

Their conclusion was that the items did not appear suddenly as a package. They occur at different times and places, suggesting that they came together piecemeal. McBrearty and Brooks saw a deep Eurocentric bias in the idea of a revolution. Klein’s book, for its part, ends by saying one thing is more certain: that dawn did not take place in Europe.

McBrearty and Brooks pushed the earliest traces of these behaviors in Africa back to between 250,000 and 300,000 years ago. To make that the start of Homo sapiens was conditional on placing a kind of fossil from the period within our species. In the same African material, Klein saw a change about 50,000 years ago. McBrearty and Brooks saw things coming together one at a time over hundreds of thousands of years.

In 2002, a team published two pieces of red ocher from Blombos Cave on the southern coast of South Africa. Ocher is an iron-bearing material that can be ground into pigment. Abstract designs had been engraved into these two pieces, found in place in 1999 and 2000. The layer containing the engravings averaged about 77,000 years old. The paper’s title spoke of the emergence of modern human behavior.

The same paper said ocher from the early Paleolithic might have had practical uses only, or might have been used as pigment for symbolic purposes. Before 40,000 years ago, evidence of modern behavior was relatively rare and often ambiguous.

4. A Hash Mark

Someone used a red ocher “crayon” to draw crossing lines on a smooth flake of stone. It looked like a hash mark, Reuters reported. The flake came from a layer about 73,000 years old at Blombos Cave in South Africa and was published in 2018. The paper said it was at least 30,000 years older than other known abstract or figurative drawings.

The syndicated headline on the Reuters story called it the oldest known human drawing, found in a South African cave. In the very same report, the lead author said he would be very hesitant to call it art.

That year another team published red markings in three Spanish caves in Science: a ladder-shaped sign on a wall, a hand stencil, and a stalagmite colored red. A carbonate crust had grown over the pigment. The researchers dated the crust by measuring the decay of uranium into thorium. Because the crust lies above the pigment, the result is a minimum age for the image.

The markings from all three sites were older than 64,800 years. By the dates the authors used, Homo sapiens would not arrive in Europe for at least another 20,000 years. They said this implied Neanderthal authorship. They also made clear that it required an unambiguous stratigraphic relation between the sample and the pigment.

The same year, four researchers challenged the oldest dates in the same journal. On their reckoning, another 25,000 years separated these marks from the next known cave-wall art. Uranium might also have moved in or out of the crust. The original authors replied that these criticisms were unfounded.

In 2026, a hand stencil from a cave on Indonesia’s Muna island was given a minimum age of 67,800 years, about 1,100 years older than the minimum for the Spanish hand stencil. National Geographic called it the oldest rock art yet attributed to modern humans. The Spanish study attributed its markings to Neanderthals; the Muna authors said the stencil alone could not tell them which hominin had made it.

Across these dates and headlines, the label “earliest art” has moved back and forth between Homo sapiens and Neanderthals. Each move depends on the latest dates, and twice those dates were minimums. It also depends on what counts as art: crossing red lines on a flake, a red ladder on a cave wall, or a hand stencil.

5. Eagle Talons and Rings of Stalagmites

Eight talons from white-tailed eagles, from at least three birds. The three largest have a notch in roughly the same place on the underside. They came from layers around 130,000 years old at Krapina, Croatia, and were dug up more than a century ago. Four researchers looked closely at them again in 2015.

Four talons had several cuts with rounded edges. All eight showed polished or abraded surfaces. A toe bone that fits one of the talons also had several cut marks.

The paper was titled “Evidence for Neandertal Jewelry.” The authors said the traces suggested that the talons had made up a set of jewelry, then went a step further and proposed some symbolic purpose.

In Bruniquel Cave in southwestern France, 336 meters from the entrance, are two rings of broken stalagmites. The larger ring is more than five meters across, with four smaller piles inside and outside it. All six arrangements show traces of burning. It is a little farther in than the length of three football pitches laid end to end.

The 2016 paper treated the geometry, the way the stalagmites were broken and the fire traces as evidence that people had arranged them. Uranium–thorium dating of the calcite that later grew on stalagmites and burned bone, as well as tips of stalagmites placed in the rings, gave a date of about 176,000 years ago, in the time of early Neanderthals.

The Nature editor’s summary called them ancient rings built by Neanderthals. What the rings were for is still a matter of conjecture. The paper’s abstract ended by calling the ability to go so far underground something that could be seen as a major step in human modernity. In one case, the talons suggest jewelry. In the other, going deep underground is read as a step toward modernity. Both are being fitted to boxes on the list.

6. A Half-Bent Knee

Could a Neanderthal straighten a knee? Between 1911 and 1913, Boule published his study of the La Chapelle skeleton, later collected as a monograph. Of the knee he wrote that full extension did not appear to have been normal; the joint was normally held half bent.

He concluded that an ordinary Neanderthal gait must therefore still have been quite different from ours. Writing for the public in a French newspaper in 1913, a naturalist described a figure with its head forward, back arched and legs half bent.

In 1957, the anatomists William Straus and A. J. E. Cave published “Pathology and the Posture of Neanderthal Man.” They connected abnormalities in this individual’s bones to disease, particularly clear degeneration of the joints. They argued against making the posture of a single patient the norm for all Neanderthals. The Smithsonian site later summarized the condition as severe, deforming osteoarthritis.

In the same paper, the two imagined bringing him back to life and putting him on the New York subway. Given a bath, a shave and modern clothes, he might attract no more attention than some other passengers. Boule judged how far the man’s walk lay from ours. Straus and Cave judged whether he would look out of place on the subway.

The American paleoanthropologist Erik Trinkaus later reexamined the question. One skeleton. Boule read half-bent knees. Straus and Cave read an individual with a disease. Trinkaus said disease of the joints could not account for everything in Boule’s reconstruction of the posture.

7. Injuries in Bone

Several plates of bone make up a skull. The seams gradually close as the brain grows. The Pit of Bones at Atapuerca in northern Spain yielded a child’s skull numbered 14. In 2009, a paper diagnosed the child with sutures that had closed too early. Both braincase and brain changed shape as a result; the bones of the face were likely asymmetric too.

The authors said it was the oldest case of craniosynostosis known at the time. The child had lived for more than five years.

Shanidar Cave in northern Iraq yielded several Neanderthal skeletons. Shanidar 1 was an adult estimated to have been between thirty-five and fifty at death. In 1982, Trinkaus and a colleague recorded the injuries on the skeleton one by one.

Two injuries to the head; injuries to the right arm and right foot. The collarbone, shoulder blade and upper arm bone on the right were small or had wasted away. The collarbone showed a past infection. Several joints in the right leg and foot showed wear, and the left shinbone showed remodeling. The paper identified injuries sustained during life, with healing and remodeling in the bones. The person lived on after being injured. Bone records some of that time.

8. The Same Ruler

France’s National Museum of Natural History has a page for the La Chapelle skeleton. It introduces it as the first well-preserved specimen to have been studied where it was found. Below are a few lines. One says spoken language was “acquired.” One says “burial.”

Boule studied the skeleton at that very museum. In discussing its gait he used a word that translates as “still.” The entry uses a word that translates as “acquired.” One word pushes them back; the other pulls them forward. Both take people alive today as the point of comparison.

Pulling forward are the hat and suit of 1939, the subway of 1957, and “acquired” on the museum page. Pushing back are the hairy figure with a club from 1909, the arched back in the 1913 newspaper and the simplest explanation offered in 2004: a genetic change raised African intelligence beyond that of Neanderthals.

The entries on the list are called “modern human behavior” in both paper titles. The cuts on eagle talons, the rings of broken stalagmites deep in a cave, the red ladder on a cave wall, the bend or straightness of a skeleton’s knees: none of these fit neatly in a box or in a place farther forward or back. They are the remainder.

The artist put the hat, hair and clothes on the figure in Coon’s book. Straus and Cave said that Boule’s half-bent knees could not make one patient’s bones a rule for the posture of everyone. There is one skeleton. What it wears and how it stands were given to it by the people who drew it and the people who read it.

The line was arranged, not walked.
