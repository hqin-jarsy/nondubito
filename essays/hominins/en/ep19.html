Where Did the Other Hominins Go?

1. A Thighbone on the Riverbank

In 2008, on the left bank of the Irtysh north of Ust’-Ishim in western Siberia, a craftsman named Nikolai Peristov was looking for mammoth tusks to carve. That is how the Russian Geographical Society and Science told the story in 2014. He was an ivory carver from Omsk.

In those accounts, Peristov picked up a piece of bone on the bank and took it to his workshop. A local paleontologist, Alexei Bondarev, recognized it as a human thighbone. The paper that later read it says it had washed out of the sands and gravels of the bank. The exact place was unclear.

The bone was radiocarbon-dated twice. The two results, calibrated together, put it at about 45,000 years ago. More than one kind of hominin lived across Eurasia then. Hominins are all the members on our side after the split from the chimpanzee branch.

Neanderthals were in Europe, western Asia and Central Asia. Denisovans were in Asia. Farther southeast, on Flores, stone tools attributable to the hominins who stood a little over a meter tall date to as recently as around 50,000 years ago. Another kind lived on Luzon. Its fossils have only minimum ages, and we do not know how long it survived.

Homo sapiens came out of Africa. Some reached the banks of a Siberian river; others reached a lake in southeastern Australia. The 38,000 years from about 50,000 to about 12,000 years ago fit into a one-year calendar at about a century a day. The man by the Irtysh falls in the middle of February.

In a model for Europe as a whole, the last Neanderthal record falls between late March and mid-April on this calendar. In the original team’s dating, human footprints by a lake in New Mexico fall between mid-September and early October. As the pages turn, Neanderthals, Denisovans and several branches of Homo sapiens drop out of the record. Where did they go?

2. Seven Thousand Years

When did Neanderthal and Homo sapiens DNA mix? Populations outside Africa generally carry pieces of Neanderthal DNA today. The lengths and distribution of the pieces keep a record of how long ago the mixing took place.

In 2024, a team reported in Science that it had analyzed those stretches in more than three hundred ancient and present-day genomes. The result was a period, from about 50,500 to 43,500 years ago.

The authors described one shared, extended period of gene flow, accounting for the vast majority of Neanderthal gene flow. They said the evidence strongly supported it.

The paper summarizes that span as about seven thousand years. The Science editor’s summary on the same page calls it about six thousand.

In December 2024 another group published genomes from several people in a cave at Ranis in Germany, online in Nature. One person’s bone was dated directly to a range from a little over 46,000 to a little over 43,000 years ago, close to the time of mixing.

From the Neanderthal stretches that person carried, the authors dated the shared mixing to about 45,000 to 49,000 years ago. They attributed the narrow range to how close the person was to the event and how precisely the bone was dated. It was one shared event in the model, but the authors said it might have lasted several generations.

One paper assembled a mixing period of about seven thousand years from a large set of genomes. The other used a person who lived close to the mixing to date a shared event, within a range about four thousand years wide.

3. The Last Page in Each Place

In 2014, Tom Higham and colleagues made a model for Europe as a whole. They brought together the end dates for the stone tool tradition associated with Neanderthals across different places. The result was between about 41,000 and a little over 39,000 years ago.

In the model, Neanderthals overlapped the earliest known Homo sapiens in Europe by a little over two thousand to more than five thousand years. Disappearance happened at different times in different regions, the paper said.

Northwestern Europe was one of those regions. Spy Cave in Belgium yielded Neanderthal bones; some of their older radiocarbon results looked too recent. In 2021 a team redated a particular compound in the bone. It wrote that contamination might have made some of the old Spy estimates up to ten thousand years too young.

The team modeled the new dates from Spy along with those from two other Belgian caves. It placed the disappearance of Neanderthals from northwestern Europe between about 44,200 and 40,600 years ago.

In 2014, Higham told The Guardian he thought Neanderthals had eventually lost out in competition. The article also quoted a caution from archaeologist Wil Roebroeks. Many of the dates Higham used came from stone tools, not directly from human bones. It is not always possible to tell which group used a particular toolkit.

4. A Rib

A photograph shows a small light brown fragment of rib. The scale beside it spans just one centimeter. It came from Baishiya Karst Cave at Xiahe, Gansu, on the northeastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau. A local monk found the jaw from the cave in the 1980s.

A 2024 release from the Chinese Academy of Sciences said the deposits with traces of human activity were about three meters thick. From a large set of bone fragments whose species could not be recognized, the researchers used proteins to pick out this human rib. From the proteins they also attributed it to the Denisovans.

The rib itself was not directly dated. Layer 3, where it was found, accumulated between around 48,000 and 32,000 years ago. The rib’s age can only be placed within the span of that layer, more than ten thousand years. The younger end of the layer is 32,000 years ago.

A paper in Nature that year said earlier evidence supported Denisovans being there until about 60,000 years ago, and possibly as late as 45,000. On the paper’s reading, the rib shows that Denisovans were still in the cave at some point in that long span represented by the layer. We do not know where or when Denisovans dropped out of the record.

5. Five Explanations

A 2011 release from Arizona State University quoted a researcher on the long-standing account that Neanderthals had been outcompeted by better adapted modern people. He cited it in order to criticize it.

In 2017, The Washington Post asked why people found it easy to read human history as a fight between species. It brought up the phrase “survival of the fittest.” One researcher said the Neanderthals were not simply beaten. Why did they disappear from the record, or, put another way, why was only one kind left? There are at least five explanations: competition, a volcanic eruption, assimilation, small populations and disease.

In 2008, a team combined archaeological and ancient climate data in a model estimating what environments Neanderthals could live in. It argued that the retreat of their range southward in southwestern Europe could not be explained by climate alone and accompanied the expansion of Homo sapiens. The term in its title was “competitive exclusion.”

In 2014, an archaeologist and Roebroeks compared the archaeological record point by point. They found no evidence for explanations based on a supposed behavioral superiority of modern humans. Interbreeding and assimilation might matter more, they argued.

There was a huge eruption in the volcanic area near Naples, in Campania, southern Italy. A study in 2017 dated it to nearly 40,000 years ago. Another team modeled the sulfur dioxide thrown out by the eruption. Using two different amounts of emissions, it found that the strongest cooling and acid deposition lasted about one to two years.

The researchers said the eruption alone was not enough to explain the Neanderthals’ disappearance. They left open the possibility that it put added pressure on populations already vulnerable.

Assimilation is another explanation. In 2007, the American paleoanthropologist Erik Trinkaus drew on features of early Homo sapiens skeletons from Europe. The simplest explanation, he argued, was that Neanderthals had joined Homo sapiens populations in a limited degree of assimilation.

On that reading they only partly disappeared. His evidence was in the bones. The pieces of Neanderthal DNA that people outside Africa carry today are a different line of evidence.

Small numbers are another explanation. In 2014, a high-quality genome of a Neanderthal woman from the Altai showed that her parents had been as closely related as half siblings.

A team in 2019 modeled close inbreeding, the difficulty of keeping very small populations going and the effects of chance. The three might have been enough for Neanderthals to disappear, the authors wrote, even without competition from Homo sapiens.

Disease is the fifth explanation. In 2010, two researchers proposed a disease model. Homo sapiens might have brought pathogens from Africa that were new to Neanderthals, they suggested, with a herpesvirus as one possible example.

A 2017 assessment said Neanderthals might have been relatively more susceptible to some new pathogens, but the predictions of such models did not hold everywhere. Researchers have tested evidence against all five explanations. Each comes with objections or limits in the published work.

6. Branches We Cannot Detect

In 1950, a fairly complete skull and other bones from an adult woman were found in the Koněprusy cave system in what is now the Czech Republic. The findspot is called Zlatý kůň. An early radiocarbon result put her at a little over 12,000 years old. Later work concluded that a binding material such as animal glue had contaminated the sample.

The cave at Ranis that yielded the person who lived close to the mixing event held bones from at least six people, three male and three female. Among them were a mother and daughter and two infants. The 2024 Nature paper found that the woman from Zlatý kůň and the people from Ranis came from the same group. The lead author told the public that she was related to two people there, about as closely as distant cousins.

The Max Planck Institute’s release described a small population of closely related people. The paper called it the deepest branch yet known of the lineage that left Africa. In its analysis, the ancestry from this branch is not detectable in living people.

The paper also lists the roughly 40,000-year-old jaw from Romania’s Cave with Bones and the man by the Irtysh as early branches without detectable ancestry in later populations. The paper that read the jaw DNA said the group to which its owner belonged made no significant genetic contribution to later Europeans.

The 2014 paper that read the riverbank man’s genome said his group split off before the eastern and western Eurasian groups separated, or around the same time. A 2021 paper on Zlatý kůň said his group had no detectable genetic continuity with later Eurasians. There was a man by the Irtysh in Siberia about 45,000 years ago. We cannot detect ancestry from his population in people alive today.

At Mal’ta in southern Siberia a little boy was buried about 24,000 years ago. His genome, published in 2014, indicated that about fourteen to thirty-eight percent of the ancestry of Indigenous Americans may come from a population related to his. Both are from Siberia. We cannot detect the riverbank man’s group in people alive today; we can read the ancestry of the Mal’ta boy’s group in Indigenous Americans.

7. Broken Bones on a Dune

July 1968, the Willandra Lakes in western New South Wales, southeastern Australia. Jim Bowler, a graduate student in geomorphology at the Australian National University, was surveying the land. Lake Mungo had long since dried up. Downwind, the wind had piled up a crescent-shaped dune. Bowler saw pieces of exposed bone where the dune had eroded.

In March 1969 an archaeological team formally recovered the remains. An early paper by Bowler and colleagues described burned bones with a carbonate crust.

The National Museum of Australia later explained that the body had first been cremated, the bones then crushed and burned again, and the remains finally buried in the dune. She became known as Mungo Lady. In 2003, Bowler and colleagues called it the earliest recorded cremation in Nature. New South Wales’s World Heritage management documents call it the world’s oldest cremation site.

The national museum calls the two sets of remains from the site among the oldest human remains known from Australia. The other was the skeleton of a man that Bowler found nearby in February 1974. He lay on his back with hands folded near his abdomen and red ocher sprinkled over him. The age of this skeleton has been disputed.

A group in 2000 dated it to about 62,000 years ago. Bowler and colleagues objected quickly. They said the date did not agree with the deposits or with results from other dating methods. In 2003, Bowler’s team rebuilt the chronology by measuring when the sand grains last saw sunlight. Bowler told the Australian Broadcasting Corporation that year that there was now complete agreement.

The chronology placed Mungo Lady and the man’s skeleton at about 40,000 years ago. People had begun to leave evidence by Lake Mungo around 50,000 to 46,000 years ago, within a few thousand years of the man by the Irtysh. In those few thousand years, one branch of Homo sapiens left a thighbone on a Siberian riverbank, and other people reached Lake Mungo.

8. Prints on a Lake Bed

At a site in White Sands National Park in New Mexico, trails of human footprints cross several surfaces. In 2021, a team described them in Science as human footprints preserved in place.

The prints were not dated directly. The researchers dated seeds from water plants in layers above, below and at the same level as the prints. The calibrated ages came out at about 23,000 to 21,000 years ago. In those millennia nearly all the ice sheets were close to their greatest extent, a period called the Last Glacial Maximum.

In 2022 another team challenged the dates. Aquatic plants take in carbon from the water. If the water carries older carbon, the seeds can look older than they are. The original team said it had allowed for this and stood by the dates. In 2023, it dated pollen from land plants in the same layers and measured when quartz in the deposits last saw light. In its reading, both supported the original dates.

In 2024 another group wrote that the dates were still unresolved. Carbon in the seeds might be skewed. The quartz might not have been fully exposed to light. The conifer pollen itself might be old, washed out of an earlier layer and then deposited again. In 2025, a different team identified lake and wetland deposits that could be traced to the footprint-bearing beds. Two laboratories produced twenty-six new radiocarbon dates that also placed the prints in the Last Glacial Maximum. In 2026, members of the original team and collaborators widened the excavation and reported twenty surfaces with in-place human footprints, animal tracks or drag marks. Seeds, pollen, quartz and lake deposits have all given dates for the prints beside that North American lake. None is the footprint itself.

Monte Verde in Chile was an open-air camp on the bank of a stream. Peat later covered it. Wood and plants did not rot in the waterlogged deposits, where little oxygen reached them. An expert group visited the materials and the site in 1997 and agreed that the younger layer was an archaeological site. Its uncalibrated radiocarbon age was around 12,500 years. The older layer, around 33,000 years, was unresolved at the time. The younger layer later came to be dated at about 14,500 years ago after calibration. In 2026, a team compared new exposures with a layer of volcanic ash it placed below them, arguing that the site should date between 8,200 and 4,200 years ago. The original excavator, Tom Dillehay, and colleagues replied that the new study had not examined the original archaeological layer directly. Nor, they said, did it explain where artifacts and features lay within that layer. They stood by human activity around 14,500 years ago. On the banks of the same stream, the two groups had not measured the same bit of earth.

The group the Mal’ta boy belonged to can still be read in the genomes of Indigenous Americans. White Sands and Monte Verde left footprints and a campsite. Ask where Homo sapiens went and the answers, too, come one place at a time. Some reached Australia and the Americas. Several branches are not detectable in living people.

Neanderthals drop out of the record at different times in different places. We do not know when the Denisovan record ends, and the riverbank man’s group is not detectable in people alive today. None of the five explanations has ruled out the others. What “why was only one kind left?” cannot fit into that question is part of the remainder.

From the Irtysh bank to the White Sands lake bed, what those 38,000 years have left is a thighbone, a piece of rib, a body burned twice, a few layers of prints and stretches of different lengths in people alive today.

The line was arranged, not walked.
