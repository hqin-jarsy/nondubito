Severe COVID and a Piece of Ancient DNA

1. A Stretch of Chromosome 3

It was 2020, the first year of the COVID pandemic. In early data collected by a collaboration pooling genetic information from patients in different places, a stretch of chromosome 3 was associated with whether patients were hospitalized. Hugo Zeberg and Svante Pääbo compared that stretch with Neanderthal genomes.

One copy of the human genome is about three billion letters long. The core of this stretch is only about fifty thousand letters. Its variants nearly always pass down together in a block.

That block lies within a much longer string resembling Neanderthal sequences, around 330,000 letters at its longest. Resemblance alone was not enough. Some older stretches of DNA, handed down by ancestors that lived before the two groups split, might still be found on both sides.

Their model gave a probability of less than one in a thousand that the core would still be so intact if it had been handed down from that common ancestor. The probability for the longer string was far lower. They concluded that the stretch had entered our lineage from Neanderthals. A stretch of inherited DNA associated with a higher risk of severe COVID came from Neanderthals.

Carriers are unevenly distributed. In the data, about half of people in South Asia carried at least one copy, compared with about one in six in Europe. In discussing disease burden, the paper said this stretch might make an important contribution to COVID risk in some populations. The model assumed that the mixing took place about 50,000 years ago. The stretch had been handed down ever since.

The next year the two researchers found a segment on chromosome 12 that also came from Neanderthals, but the association went the other way. In their analysis, each copy was associated with a relative risk of needing intensive care about a fifth lower. The statistics alone could not tell the authors which position in the segment mattered. Both stretches came from Neanderthals. The one on chromosome 3 was associated with a higher risk of severe illness, the one on chromosome 12 with a lower risk.

2. One or Two Letters in a Hundred

How much Neanderthal DNA does a person outside Africa carry? A group in 2010 said its data suggested that one to four percent of the genomes of Eurasian populations came from Neanderthals. The same passage said the amount of interbreeding may have been very limited and that contact may have taken place in the Middle East.

Later research used different methods and reference samples. It gave figures between one percent and a little over two, with East Asian populations slightly above Europeans. A 2014 study cautioned that within a population, people usually differ by only about a tenth of a percentage point.

According to the authors, commercial Neanderthal ancestry calculators that claim to measure individual differences of about a percentage point mostly pick up noise. But the percentages were put to other uses too.

A 2025 historical study followed conversations on a genetic testing company’s forum. After the company recalculated how many Neanderthal variants its customers carried in 2020, one customer wrote that they no longer knew whether they counted as white. Others linked a higher Neanderthal count with being white in some Germanic, Celtic or Scandinavian sense.

In an open letter in 2019, ancient-DNA researcher David Reich wrote that no human population is pure or unmixed.

Some earlier methods took Africans as the reference group with no Neanderthal ancestry. In 2020, another team used a method without that assumption. In African samples from the 1000 Genomes Project, it found on average about seventeen million letters per person in Neanderthal-like sequences, roughly one-third of the amount in people outside Africa.

The team proposed at least two sources. People with Eurasian ancestry migrated back into Africa. And farther back, a group of Homo sapiens that had left Africa passed DNA into Neanderthals. The researchers called them apparent Neanderthal sequences. On their reading, DNA passed between Homo sapiens and Neanderthals in both directions.

3. Working Backward

In the tropics, it is still hard to recover ancient DNA. In 2021, researchers collected genetic data for many populations in the Philippines, twenty-five of them groups that identify as Negrito. The researchers also obtained whole genomes from five Ayta Magbukon people.

The result went into the title. This population had the highest level of Denisovan ancestry in the world, the authors wrote. Their estimate was thirty to forty percent higher than in Aboriginal Australians and Papuans. The lead author told the public that Philippine Negrito groups had mixed relatively recently with East Asian-related groups, reducing the proportion of Denisovan ancestry.

For now, the authors wrote, working backward from the genomes of living people appeared to be the only viable alternative in the tropics. It yielded possibilities: there may have been several Denisovan populations across the islands of the Philippines.

4. A Gene on the Plateau

On the Tibetan Plateau above four thousand meters, a breath of air contains roughly forty percent less oxygen than the same volume of air at sea level. In 2010, a team at the Kunming Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, published research identifying two genes that had undergone strong natural selection in Tibetans. One regulates the body’s response to a lack of oxygen. By the researchers’ estimate, selection on it went back at least about 18,000 years.

In 2014, a team read a stretch around this gene at high coverage in forty Tibetans and forty Han Chinese. The form common among Tibetans was marked by five variants found together. In the 1000 Genomes Project data, only one Southern Han Chinese participant and one Han Chinese participant from Beijing carried the same set.

The abstract said the unusual pattern could be convincingly explained only by DNA entering our lineage from Denisovans or related individuals. The title called it “Denisovan-like DNA.” The paper also considered whether the form had been present in a common ancestor and handed down on both sides. The authors thought this highly unlikely. The same year China Science Daily reported that DNA from Denisovans might have helped Tibetans adapt faster to low oxygen at high altitude.

In 2015 a Shanghai team found a short deletion near the same gene, much more common among Tibetans living on the plateau but absent from the Denisovan genome. In its coverage the Shanghai branch of the Chinese Academy of Sciences said the result raised questions about the earlier conclusion. The deletion and the five linked variants were not the same finding. In 2021 another team compared the proposed sources and still found the most support for a Denisovan origin.

In 2019, when China Science Daily covered a jaw from Xiahe, Gansu, identified as Denisovan, it called it the last piece in the puzzle of where the gene had come from. That gene had not been sequenced from the jaw. The paper’s title said “Denisovan-like DNA.” A news story five years later said “the last piece of the puzzle.”

5. The Cave with Bones

In the Carpathian Mountains of southwestern Romania, there is a cave with a name that means “Cave with Bones.” Three cavers, Adrian Bîlgăr, Laurențiu Sarcină and Ștefan Milota, found the chamber while exploring.

On February 16, 2002, a human jaw was found on the old floor near the current entrance to the chamber. The paper noted that it might already have been moved from its original position. A history of exploration at the site, published in 2013, says the cavers contacted a research institution after the find.

On March 25, 2002, a researcher emailed a specialist that she knew they should not touch or move the objects. A few days later she and the cavers went back, took photographs to document the jaw, then carried it out to Cluj-Napoca.

Getting in was difficult. In 2004 a reporter for The Washington Post went into the cave with the team and spent around nine hours underground. The entrance was an icy mountain stream. Inside it was cold and wet. Farther in, they had to swim on their backs, then dive through a submerged passage more than twenty meters long. Milota, who led the dive, warned the newcomers that in twenty or thirty seconds they could be gone.

Direct dating put the jaw at 37,000 to 42,000 years ago. In 2015, Qiaomei Fu and colleagues read DNA from it. About six to nine percent of the person’s genome came from Neanderthals. Three stretches in particular were long.

In each generation the two chromosome copies carried by a parent can swap and rejoin pieces before one copy is passed on. Long stretches from one ancestor become shorter. The longer the stretches, the closer that ancestor is likely to have been. Their length led the team to put the person’s most recent Neanderthal ancestor four to six generations back. About 40,000 years ago, a person in the Carpathians had a Neanderthal ancestor four to six generations earlier.

Four generations back means the parents of your great-grandparents. Fu said publicly that the jaw showed mixing had taken place in Europe as well as in the Middle East.

6. A Population Without Bones

Can a group leave no bones and yet leave a mark on other people’s genomes? In 2020, two researchers analyzed genomes from four West African populations, including Yoruba and Mende people. In their model, two to nineteen percent of the ancestry of these populations came from an archaic population that split off before Neanderthals and Homo sapiens separated.

The paper’s title called it a ghost. Here that means there is a population in the model but no ancient genome from the group to compare with. The paper did not identify a fossil belonging to it either. With no confirmed fossil there is no finder, no date of discovery, no color and no weight.

Ancient DNA from Africa is scarce. The researchers used a method that did not require an archaic genome as a reference. The first sentence of the abstract said little was known about the contribution of archaic humans to genetic variation in Africans today. The group accounts for two to nineteen percent in the model, with not a single bone confirmed to belong to it.

7. A Braided River

In 2015, Lee Berger told National Geographic he preferred a braided river to a family tree whose branches never meet again. The river divides into separate channels that come together downstream. In 2023, researchers sequenced new genomes from forty-four Nama people, a Khoisan group in southern Africa.

Together with samples from southern, eastern and western Africa and from Eurasia, they put 290 genomes into models for comparison. The abstract said they inferred a reticulated history for African populations, a history more like a network.

In their model, the earliest detectable split among populations living today took place 120,000 to 135,000 years ago. Before it there were two or more populations that were not very different genetically, connected for hundreds of thousands of years by the DNA flowing between them. The authors said the model could explain some variants previously attributed to mixing with an archaic population, without assuming such mixing.

One author said in a university release that earlier, more complex models had proposed a contribution from archaic humans, but this model pointed to something different. Several of its more recent dates and proportions were set in advance. The split between Eurasian and East African populations, for example, was set at 50,000 years ago.

The authors said comparing a range of models was essential to making reliable inferences about the deep past. Their network model has no single, clearly defined earliest split.

A 2023 commentary on these metaphors argued that a tree is one special kind of network. Trees and networks coexist; they are not opposites. Putting a braided river in place of a tree poses a false choice. In an interview in 2026, Chris Stringer said that if it is a braided river, then it is one that flows out of Africa. To tell the story of the same part of our past, some people draw a tree and some draw a braided river.

8. A Tree Only Branches

On a tree, the tips still living today are all in the crown. Lower down are branches that broke off partway up. Biological classification borrows the picture.

Trace the living tips down to their most recent common ancestor. That ancestor and all its descendants make up the crown group. Below it are the broken branches that are closer to the crown group than to other surviving branches. They are the stem group. In 2002, Stringer applied this distinction to humans in his own way.

People living today are in the crown. In his account, archaic Homo sapiens are on the stem of our species. Neanderthals are on a separate branch beside ours. A tree only branches; its branches do not merge. Those stretches in the genome came in where branches met.

The stretch on chromosome 3 is now carried by members of the crown. In Stringer’s picture it came from a branch next to ours. The question changes shape: not just who descended from whom, but how much of one person’s DNA came from where.

One model reads similar signals in West African genomes as mixing with an archaic population with no confirmed fossils. Another reads them as a network connected for hundreds of thousands of years. Where the two readings fail to line up is part of the remainder.

The two COVID segments, the form of a gene common on the plateau, the sequences from African samples: all were read in the genomes of people alive today. The researchers who measured them are people alive today too. The segments they read are in people living now.

The title of the 2020 paper was “The major genetic risk factor for severe COVID-19 is inherited from Neanderthals.”

The line was arranged, not walked.
