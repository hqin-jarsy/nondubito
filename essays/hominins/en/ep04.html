A Trail That Looked Like a Bear’s

1. Elephant Dung

The words you hear most often about how early humans walked are “walking upright.” They are often followed by a little verdict: this fossil or that one was “not yet fully upright.” To see how that verdict is arrived at, start with a practical joke.

Laetoli, northern Tanzania, 1976. Mary Leakey’s team was looking for fossils there. In recollections and retellings from years later, a few people near the dry-season camp were amusing themselves by throwing dried elephant dung at one another.

In one version, the visiting paleontologist Andrew Hill ducked low to dodge a throw. There, on an exposed surface of volcanic ash rock, he saw tracks made up of little hollows.

There are several versions. Accounts differ on who threw first, and on whether Hill fell, lay flat or crouched to look for ammunition. They agree on a few things: 1976, dried elephant dung, and Hill down low, noticing animal tracks no one had paid attention to before.

The exposed rock was a layer of volcanic ash about 3.66 million years old. While the ash was still damp and soft, animals passed over it and left footprints. Later deposits covered them. The version told in museums is more vivid: rain wet the ash, the sun hardened the trampled surface, and the next fall of ash sealed it.

Researchers later counted more than 18,000 animal prints on a patch of ground smaller than 500 square meters. Hominins, all the members on our side after the split from chimpanzees, had left footprints in the ash too. Five odd prints made by something on two feet were found that same year. Two years later an entire trail turned up at another spot.

2. The Length of a Basketball Court

In 1978, the chemist Paul Abell joined Mary Leakey’s expedition and found a trail of hominin prints at what became Site G. The Smithsonian’s page today credits both Leakey and Abell with its discovery.

The trail is nearly twenty-seven meters long, about the length of a basketball court, with some seventy prints. Leakey and a geologist published the formal report in Nature in 1979.

More than one individual left those prints. One trail stands clear on its own. In another, the smaller feet seem to have stepped into the larger prints, making the two hard to tell apart. A 2010 paper counted at least two walkers, perhaps three. One widely used method estimates their heights from footprint length. It puts the walker on the separate trail at about 1.1 meters, and the two on the overlapping trail at around 1.3 meters to a little over 1.4.

In 1979, writing in a magazine for the general public, Leakey said it was hard to overstate the importance of walking on two legs in hominin development. The scientific paper’s abstract said many of the prints could be matched with local fossil animals.

The excavators covered the prints again when they finished in 1979, to keep weather and feet off them. Later conservation accounts describe trees growing on the cover, some with roots that reached the track surface. In the 1990s, an American conservation organization and Tanzanian heritage officials reopened part of the trail. They cleaned and recorded it, removed roots and buried it again. Today most people see the Laetoli prints in photographs, models and three-dimensional records.

3. The Same Trail, Two Experiments

The Smithsonian explains that the big toe points the same way as the other toes in the Site G prints. The heel landed first, then the toes pushed off. The prints are close together: the steps were not long. Footprints cannot talk, so researchers got people today to walk.

In 2010, a team asked people to walk barefoot across a track of sand in two ways. First they walked normally, with fairly straight legs. Then they bent their knees and hips on purpose, moving forward in a half crouch. The researchers measured how deeply the heels, balls of the feet and toes sank in the sand in both gaits. Then they compared the prints with Laetoli.

They concluded that the transfer of weight in the Laetoli prints came closest to the economical, extended-leg bipedal gait of humans. The title called the prints the earliest direct evidence for human-like bipedal biomechanics.

In 2016, another team did it differently. They compared prints from more people, added experimental chimpanzee prints, and picked fourteen areas on three-dimensional models to compare depth.

Their results suggested that, when a foot landed at Laetoli, the walker may have bent the knee and hip to a degree between the humans and chimpanzees they tested. The title described a two-legged gait different from that of both living humans and chimpanzees.

One trail, two experiments, two titles. The differences were in what the walkers walked on, how many there were, whether the researchers compared relative depths at a few spots such as toes and heels or depths at fourteen locations on a 3-D model, and how they worked back from depth to the bend of a joint.

4. Bears

In 1976, on the Laetoli surface full of animal tracks, expedition members Peter Jones and Philip Leakey found five consecutive two-footed prints at what became Site A. Unlike those at Site G, they were short, broad and oddly shaped.

Mary Leakey described the gait as shuffling, probably slow. The paper she wrote with the geologist called it somewhat staggering and noted that one foot crossed in front of the other.

Who left the tracks? An American anthropologist later left several possibilities open: the ground might have deformed and produced an illusion, a young bear might have walked upright, or another kind of hominin might have left them. In 1987, two other researchers said the question could not be settled without cleaning more of Site A and following the tracks around it.

In June 2019, the Tanzanian paleoanthropologist Charles Musiba led a team that located the five prints again and cleared away the cover. To clean one print, they used a wooden tongue depressor to pick away the tuff packed into the toe marks. They did not clean another all the way down. A neater outline was not worth damaging the original print.

They put the bear question to bears. In more than fifty hours of video of wild black bears, the bears stood or walked unsupported on two legs for just 0.09 percent of the time. Only once did one take four consecutive steps.

They also brought in four young black bears of about twenty kilograms each. They used applesauce and maple syrup to coax them across mud and measured forty-six bear prints. The bear prints were on average about 1.6 centimeters shorter than the Site A prints. When the bears walked on two legs, their feet fell farther apart side to side.

Could the Site A walker have been the same kind of creature as the Site G walkers, taking an awkward step? People today can cross one foot over the other. The researchers asked ten adults to walk on sediment like that at Laetoli. Each walked normally five times and crossed one foot over the other on purpose five times.

The lead author, Ellison McNutt, explained to the public that a person trying to regain balance might make this move. But in the team’s comparisons, even deliberate crossover steps by modern people could not account for all the differences between Site A and Site G.

The researchers published their findings in Nature in 2021 under the title “Footprint evidence of early hominin locomotor diversity at Laetoli, Tanzania.” They attributed the Site A prints to a hominin walking on two legs, about a meter tall, with a differently shaped foot from those at Site G and a slightly separated big toe. They did not name a species. In their reading, there were at least two kinds of feet on the Laetoli ash.

5. Size and Sex

Beginning in 2015, prints came to light at another spot at Laetoli. It was named Site S and published in 2016. One of its walkers was large. By the same estimation method, this one stood more than 1.6 meters and weighed over forty kilograms. Another was more than 1.4 meters tall.

The paper noted a detail: the ash layer containing the prints can be divided into roughly ten smaller layers. The same layer of ash does not mean the same afternoon.

Using the differences in print size and a model of body size, the authors considered one possible reconstruction. The largest walker might have been male, the smaller ones female or not yet grown. That led to a discussion of differences in size between the sexes and of how the group might have been made up. Similar reasoning has been used for much later prints. Another site in Tanzania has more than four hundred. A 2020 paper estimated that one group moving in the same direction consisted of fourteen adult women, two adult men and one adolescent. It suggested the prints might record a cooperative foraging trip with a division of labor.

6. The Knee

The traces of walking are in bones as well as prints. In November 1973, the year before Lucy was found, Donald Johanson came across a fragment from the top of a shinbone at Hadar, Ethiopia. Later accounts say he took it for a monkey’s at first. Nearby he found the lower end of a thighbone. Fit them together and there was a knee joint, often called “Johanson’s knee.”

What mattered was an angle. Rest the lower end of a modern human thighbone flat on a table and the shaft does not point straight up. It angles outward from knee to hip. This is called a valgus angle. It brings the knee under the body’s center of mass so the weight bears down on it when one leg supports the body. The pronounced angle sets the human knee apart from those of many living primates. Other apes can have a much smaller angle.

Johanson’s knee had the angle. Its owner was assigned to Lucy’s species, Australopithecus afarensis. Lucy’s pelvis was short and broad, and her thighbone sloped in the same way. In 2011, three researchers used a complete bone from the foot of an afarensis individual to argue it had an arch, the upward curve along the sole.

Most researchers accept that afarensis walked regularly on two legs. They differ over how much time it spent in trees and how much it walked like modern humans. Lucy’s pelvis was already crushed and fragmented when it was found. Different reconstructions give it different shapes.

An arch is not simply something a foot has or does not have. In a skeleton you are reading the shape of a foot bone. In a footprint you are reading how far the middle of the foot sank into mud. The 2016 experiment put the Laetoli midfoot depths between those of modern humans and chimpanzees.

7. The Big Toe

In the usual reconstruction, the big toe of afarensis pointed in much the same direction as the other toes. Not all feet from before or around the same time were like that.

Ardi is a skeleton of Ardipithecus ramidus from about 4.4 million years ago. The 2009 paper on her foot took as its subject the way one foot combined grasping with pushing off. The big toe stuck well out to the side, like a thumb, and could grasp. That is called an abducted toe. But the outer side and middle of the foot were stiff and could support the body on the push off the ground.

The Ethiopian paleoanthropologist Yohannes Haile-Selassie, who worked on the skeleton, recalled in 2025 what a surprise it had been to find an abducted big toe.

The Burtele foot, about 3.4 million years old, had one too. It was found in Afar, Ethiopia, in 2009 and published in 2012. The big toe could grasp, and other bones had features associated with bearing weight while walking.

Haile-Selassie remembered knowing then that this was different from Lucy’s species. Ardi lived 4.4 million years ago; the Burtele foot came a million years later. Both had an abducted big toe.

In 2025 the authors assigned the foot to Australopithecus deyiremeda, the close-relative species, as the more parsimonious explanation. News releases and reports said the researchers thought it pushed off more from the side of the second toe, rather than relying mainly on the big toe as we do. In reviewing earlier studies, the paper spoke of diverse ways that feet can be adapted to walking on two legs.

8. A Ruler

The chimpanzee, often placed at the opposite end of the scale from “walking upright,” can walk on two legs too. In 2007, a team had adult chimpanzees walk on a treadmill on four legs and on two while measuring the energy they used. On average, the chimpanzees used about the same amount of energy either way. Modern people use considerably less. There was also a good deal of variation among the chimpanzees, depending on the size of their steps and how straight they kept their hips and knees.

Researchers use a familiar set of signs to assess two-legged walking: heel first, big toe in line with the others, a foot arch, an inward-set knee, left and right prints close to each other, and prints in a continuous track.

Take any one sign on its own and it will only get you so far. A bear puts its whole foot down, heel and all. A modern human can cross one foot over the other. Ardi and the Burtele foot have big toes that stick out and are still discussed as feet for two-legged walking. How far the middle of the foot sinks depends on how wet the mud is and how fast the creature is walking.

The same signs are used much farther afield. On the Greek island of Crete are prints about five to six million years old. The original paper in 2017 listed such features as two-footed walking, five toes and a whole sole contacting the ground, known as plantigrade locomotion. Its title ended with a question mark: “Possible hominin footprints from the late Miocene (c. 5.7 Ma) of Crete?”

The journal published a commentary supporting the original paper in the same issue. A dissenting commentary followed the next year. A fresh look at the date in 2021 moved the prints from about 5.7 to about 6.05 million years ago. The date moved; who left them is still unsettled. The thighbone from Chad poses a similar problem. A 2020 paper and a 2022 paper reached opposite conclusions from the same bone.

In many popular accounts, “walking upright” is like a ruler. At one end is a modern human and at the other a chimpanzee. Every fossil goes somewhere in the middle, bent to straight, all in a row. A judgment follows: this one was “not yet fully upright.” That is a reading on the ruler, not something visible in a footprint or on a bone.

There is just one specimen at the “fully upright” end: us. Ardi’s foot that could grasp and push, the Burtele foot with its splayed big toe, and the crossover step at Laetoli’s Site A do not each occupy a slot on this line. They are different combinations. Speaking to the public about several australopiths, Haile-Selassie talked about each one’s range of ways of getting around.

What can be measured is the length and depth of a print, the angle and shape of a bone. What can be inferred is a way of walking. Put it on the ruler and only then do you read “not yet fully upright.” As the story goes, the ash first caught somebody’s eye during a game of throwing elephant dung. In the reading of the 2021 paper, both kinds of prints at Sites A and G were left in the footprint-bearing deposits about 3.66 million years ago. That does not let us put one kind of foot before the other in evolution.

The line was arranged, not walked.
