金银铜铁

Gold, Silver, Bronze, Iron

Han Qin (秦汉)

卷三快到头的时候，护卫者的教育总算讲完了。学什么曲子，练什么身体，听什么故事，不听什么故事，一样一样都交代过了。管事的那一小拨人也已经挑出来：从小看到大，一层一层地试，在难处里试，在好处里试，看谁不动摇。苏格拉底说，这就像在火里试金子一样。

挑完了，教育也办完了，他说还差一件。

他说，得让全城的人信一个故事。一个编出来的故事。

格劳孔问，什么故事。

苏格拉底就吞吞吐吐起来。

格劳孔说，你这话怎么老在嘴边上打转。

苏格拉底说，等你听完就不奇怪了。

格劳孔说，讲吧，别怕。

苏格拉底说，我实在不知道该拿什么脸看你，也不知道该用什么话把它说出来。

格劳孔说，难怪你不好意思。

这一段值得原样抄下来，因为为难的样子写得太具体了。不是一句我不好意思就带过，是四五个来回。

然后他讲了。

他说，这座城里所有的人，其实都是从地底下生出来的。他们受的那些教育，长的那些年月，都是一场梦；他们连身上的武器和家伙，都是在地底下打好的。地是他们的母亲，也是他们的奶娘。所以他们得护着这块地，像护着自己的母亲；城里别的人他们得当兄弟，因为大家是同一个地母生的。

接下来是那几样金属。

他说，神在造他们的时候，往每个人身上掺了一样东西。将来要管事的那些，掺的是金；给他们做帮手的那些，掺的是银；种地的和做手艺的那些，掺的是铜和铁。

这个故事还留了一条活门，而且留得很显眼。

他说，血统不算数。金的父母可能生出银的孩子，银的父母也可能生出金的孩子。所以神给统治者下了头一道命令：仔细看孩子身上掺的是哪一样。金父银父的孩子身上要是掺了铜铁，一点情面也不许留，得把他降到种地做手艺的那一层去；反过来，铜父铁父的孩子里要是出了金的银的，得把他提上来。

最后是一句神谕：什么时候铜的人或者铁的人当上了这座城的护卫，这座城就完了。

讲完，苏格拉底问了一句：有没有什么办法能让这城里的人信这个。

格劳孔答得很平静。他说，这一代人是不行了。他们的儿子也许行，儿子的儿子也许行，再往后的人也许行。

到这里，先说两边合得上的那一层。

柏拉图不掩饰这是编的。这一点了不起，可了不起的地方不在他承认，在他承认的方式。他不是编完之后加了一句免责的话，他是一边编一边把编的过程摊在桌上：先说这是个谎，再说自己不好意思说，说完还问了一句多久才有人肯信，然后把那个答案原样写进了书里。讲共同故事的人多半是站在故事里面讲的，他是站在外面讲的，而且拉着读故事的人一起站到外面看了一眼。

SAE 这边也不反对编故事。这一点常被读反，所以得说明白。人要过日子，就得靠一大堆造出来的说法：钱，国家，公司，星期三。这里头没有哪一样是从地里长出来的，可它们真的管用，管得住事，办得成活。造说法不是毛病，是本事。

两边在这一层上是一个意思：一座城需要一个共同的故事，而这个故事是人做出来的。

然后是分开的地方，而分开的那一处很窄，窄到两页纸。

往前翻两页。挑管事的人那一段，苏格拉底用的话是：一层一层地试他们，像在火里试金子一样。

这句话里的金子是个比方。它管的是一道手续：我们拿什么办法把人挑出来。比方是可以查的。你这个试法准不准，试漏了怎么办，试的人自己靠得住吗。这些话都问得出口，因为那是一道人定的手续，人定的手续总有一个定它的人站在那儿。

隔了两页，金子从火里挪到了血里。

同一样东西，先是我们试他用的一个说法，后来成了他生下来就带着的一样成分。挑出来的那个人，从我们试过他，变成了他生来就是这个成色。

前一句管的是手续，后一句管的是出身。手续可以查，出身不能查。你没法问一个人凭什么是金的，就像你没法问他凭什么是他爹妈生的。

换掉的不是内容，是这句话后面还能不能再接一句。

一样造出来的东西，被说成本来就摆在那儿的。这个动作跟好坏没有关系。金银铜铁之所以是标本，标本到几乎像插图，正因为它连动机都是干净的。编它的人不图私利。他图的是城里的人别打起来，图的是每个人心里安稳，有个自己的位置，不必天天惦记别人那一份。他是真心的。可真心不改变那个动作的形状。

这一点在小地方看得更清楚。

一家公司讲自己怎么起来的那个故事，多半是真事：哪两个人在什么地方熬了几年，哪一年最难的时候谁没有走。是真事，也是选出来的真事。选的时候就已经在编了，只是编得不着痕迹。这个故事管用，新来的人听了会多待两年，这没什么不好。它变成另一样东西，是在有人拿它挡住一句话的那一刻：这事我们不这么干，因为我们不是那样的公司。到这一句为止，一件往事变成了一样天生的东西，而对着天生的东西是没有下一句可说的。

每家人也都有几条说不清来路的规矩。为什么这个菜过年一定得做，为什么这件事一定得让谁去办。你问一句，多半得到的回答是我们家一直这样。

这句话本身没有毛病。多数时候它就是一句实话，说的是这条规矩确实传了很久。它换性质，也是在被拿来结束一句话的时候：你再问下去，它就成了回答，而且是最后一句回答。到那一刻，一条传下来的规矩变成了一件天生的事，往下就没有话了。

说到这里得跟一条纪律，不跟就容易读成审判。

判的是那个动作的形状，不是编故事的那个人。这不只是客气话。把一个诊断办成定罪，最直接的后果是往后没有人肯把编的过程摊开了，都藏起来；而藏起来的那一种，才是真正看不见的那一种。柏拉图恰好是没有藏的那一个。

那个故事本身好不好，也是另一个问题。这里只问一件事：它出示的那份凭据，是不是它自己挣来的。

回到那个故事，最见功夫的一处，其实是那条活门。

血统不算数，金父可以生铜子，铜父可以生金子，统治者必须照着实情把人升上去降下来。这一条看着是开放的，甚至有点动人：不认门第，只认成色。

可是一条能升能降的规矩，比一条只能待着的规矩，更需要有人来判。谁判。统治者判。判的凭据是什么。是一样看不见的东西。

两句摆在一起看就清楚了：这条活门没有松开谁，它把判断权收得更紧了。一个只认门第的城，门第好歹是明摆着的，谁都能数得出你爷爷是谁；一个认成色的城，成色在哪儿，只有看的人知道。

那么这边主张怎么办，得说清楚，因为这里最容易被读成拆台。

不是把那个故事删掉。删不掉，也不该删。要动的只有一处：把它放回它挣来的那一格。它靠什么挣的，就管到哪儿为止；它没挣到的那一段，标出来，写上这是我们做的，往下还可以再商量。故事照旧管用，只是不再当地基使。

他编完那个故事，问了一句话。那句话到这里才该答。

他问的是：有没有办法让这城里的人信这个。

于是他会问：你说不必删，只要在下面补一行字，写上这是我们做的。好。那一行字补上去以后，这个故事还剩下什么。

你别忘了它挣来的是什么。它挣来的不是一个说法，是这城里的人肯把彼此当兄弟，肯为脚下这块地去死。你要把它退回它挣来的那一格，那一格在哪儿。一个下面标着我们做的的兄弟，还是兄弟吗。

第二句更实。我连它要多久才生根都替你算过了：这一代人不行，得等他们的儿子，儿子的儿子。一个从头一天起就标着做的的故事，第一代就不肯信，你上哪儿找第二代去。

第三句最短。拆一样东西，比它长起来快得多。你把它标出来是一天的事，它当初长成那个样子用了几代人。中间那一段，这座城靠什么站着。

这一问他问得理直气壮，因为他不是没想过，他是想过之后决定不让人知道。

最后回到那个说不出口的人。

他不知道该拿什么脸看格劳孔，不知道该用什么话讲。他还是讲了。讲完他问了一句，这能不能让人信。

这个故事从头到尾，唯一一个明确不信它的人，是编它的那一个。

而他把这一层写进了书里，写在那个故事的正下方，一句也没有删。后来的人要拆这个故事，头一件趁手的工具，就在他自己写的这几行里。

Near the end of Book III, the guardians’ education has finally been set out. Which music to learn, how to train the body, which stories to hear and which not to hear: each has been explained. The small group who will govern has also been chosen. They have been watched from childhood onward and tested at every stage, in hardship and in good fortune, to see who remains steady. Socrates says it is like testing gold in fire.

The selection and education are complete. One thing is still missing, he says.

The whole city must be brought to believe a story. A made-up story.

What story, Glaucon asks.

Socrates begins to stumble over his words.

Why do you keep hovering on the edge of saying it, Glaucon asks.

You will not wonder when you have heard it, Socrates says.

Tell us, Glaucon says. Do not be afraid.

Socrates says he truly does not know how to look Glaucon in the face, or what words he could use to tell it.

No wonder you are embarrassed, Glaucon says.

This passage deserves to be copied as it stands, because the embarrassment is rendered so concretely. It is not dispatched with a single “I am embarrassed.” It takes four or five exchanges.

Then he tells it.

Everyone in this city, he says, was actually born beneath the earth. Their education and the years they spent growing up were all a dream. Even their weapons and equipment were fashioned underground. The earth is their mother and their nurse. They must therefore protect this land as they would their own mother, and treat the other citizens as brothers, because all were born of the same earth-mother.

Then come the metals.

When the god made them, he says, he mixed something into each person. Into those who would govern, gold; into their helpers, silver; into farmers and craftspeople, bronze and iron.

The story also leaves a door open, and makes the door conspicuous.

Ancestry does not settle the matter, he says. Golden parents may have a silver child, and silver parents a golden child. So the god’s first command to the rulers is to look carefully at what has been mixed into the children. If a child of a golden or silver father contains bronze or iron, no sentiment is permitted: the child must be moved down to the farmers and craftspeople. Conversely, if a child of a bronze or iron father contains gold or silver, that child must be brought up.

Last comes an oracle: whenever someone of bronze or iron becomes the city’s guardian, the city will perish.

When he has finished, Socrates asks: is there any way to make the people of the city believe this?

Glaucon answers calmly. Not this generation, he says. Perhaps their sons, their sons’ sons, and those who come later.

Here, first, is the level on which the two sides agree.

Plato does not conceal that this is made up. That is remarkable, but what is remarkable is not his admission itself. It is how he makes it. He does not invent the story and then add a disclaimer. He lays the process of invention on the table as he goes: first calling it a lie, then saying he is embarrassed to tell it, then asking how long it will take before anyone believes it, and finally writing the answer into the book unchanged. People telling a common story usually speak from within it. He speaks from outside and takes the reader outside with him for a look.

SAE does not object to making up stories either. This is often read backward, so it needs to be stated clearly. To live, people depend on a great many accounts they have made: money, countries, companies, Wednesday. None of these grew out of the ground. Yet they really work, organize affairs, and get things done. Making accounts is not a defect. It is an ability.

At this level, the two sides mean the same thing: a city needs a shared story, and people make that story.

Then comes the separation, and the place where they separate is narrow, only two pages wide.

Turn back two pages. In the passage about choosing rulers, Socrates says to test them at every stage, like gold in fire.

Gold in that sentence is a metaphor. It concerns a procedure: how we choose people. The metaphor can be checked. Are your tests accurate? What if they miss something? Can the testers themselves be trusted? These questions can all be asked, because this is a procedure established by people, and behind such a procedure there is always someone who established it.

Two pages later, the gold moves from the fire into the blood.

The same thing is first a way of describing how we test a person, then an ingredient the person has carried from birth. The person selected moves from “we have tested him” to “he was born with this quality.”

The first sentence concerns procedure; the second concerns birth. A procedure can be checked; birth cannot. You cannot ask on what grounds someone is golden, any more than you can ask on what grounds that person was born to those parents.

What has been replaced is not the content, but whether another sentence can follow it.

Something made is presented as something that was already there. This move is not a matter of good or bad. Gold, silver, bronze, and iron make such a clear specimen, almost an illustration, precisely because even the motive is clean. The storyteller seeks no private gain. He wants the citizens not to fight, wants everyone to feel settled and have a place of their own, without longing every day for someone else’s share. He is sincere. But sincerity does not change the shape of the move.

This is easier to see on a smaller scale.

A company’s story of how it began is usually true: which two people struggled for years in which place, who did not leave in the hardest year. It is true, and it is selected truth. The telling was already being composed in the selection, only without visible seams. The story works. New employees hear it and stay two years longer. There is nothing wrong with that. It becomes something else at the moment someone uses it to block a sentence: we do not do things that way, because we are not that kind of company. At that point, something that happened in the past becomes something inherent. Faced with what is inherent, there is no next sentence to say.

Every family, too, has rules whose origins nobody can quite explain. Why must this dish be made at New Year? Why must this particular person handle that matter? Ask, and the answer will usually be: our family has always done it this way.

There is nothing wrong with that sentence itself. Most of the time it is simply true: the rule really has been passed down for a long time. It changes its nature when it is used to end a discussion. Ask further, and it becomes the answer, the final answer. At that moment, an inherited rule becomes something inherent, and there is nothing more to say.

At this point a discipline must be observed, or the account can easily be read as a trial.

What is judged is the shape of the move, not the storyteller. This is more than politeness. Turn a diagnosis into a conviction, and the most immediate result is that nobody will be willing to lay the making of stories open anymore. Everyone will hide it. The hidden kind is the kind that truly cannot be seen. Plato is precisely the one who did not hide.

Whether the story itself is good is another question as well. Here there is only one question: has it earned the warrant it presents?

Return to the story. Its most skillful feature is actually that open door.

Ancestry does not settle the matter. A golden father can have a bronze son, a bronze father a golden son. Rulers must promote and demote according to what is actually there. The rule looks open, even moving: not family standing, but the quality of the metal.

Yet a rule allowing people to move up and down needs someone to judge more than a rule requiring them simply to stay put. Who judges? The rulers. On what evidence? Something invisible.

Put those two sentences together and the matter becomes clear: this open door releases no one. It concentrates the power of judgment more tightly. In a city that recognizes only ancestry, ancestry is at least in plain sight. Anyone can trace who your grandfather was. In a city that recognizes the metal’s quality, only the person looking knows where that quality lies.

What, then, does this side propose? It needs to be made clear, because this is the point most easily mistaken for an attempt to bring the whole thing down.

It does not propose deleting the story. The story cannot and should not be deleted. Only one thing needs to change: put it back in the place it has earned. Whatever earned its standing sets the limit of its authority. Mark the part it has not earned and write: we made this, and there is room for further discussion. The story still works. It simply no longer serves as the foundation.

After inventing the story, he asked a question. Only now is it time to answer.

His question was: is there a way to make the people of this city believe it?

So he will ask: you say there is no need to delete it, only to add a line underneath saying that we made this. Very well. Once that line has been added, what is left of the story?

Do not forget what it has earned. What it has earned is not an account. It is the willingness of the people in this city to treat one another as brothers and to die for the land beneath their feet. You want to return it to the place it has earned. Where is that place? Is a brother with “we made this” written underneath still a brother?

The second question is more practical. I have even worked out for you how long it takes to put down roots: not this generation, but their sons and their sons’ sons. If a story is marked as made from its first day, and the first generation will not believe it, where will you find a second?

The third is shortest. Taking something apart is much quicker than letting it grow. Labeling it takes you a day. Growing into that form took generations. What keeps the city standing in between?

He asks with good reason, because it is not that he failed to think about this. He thought about it and decided not to let people know.

Finally, return to the man who could not get the words out.

He did not know how to look Glaucon in the face or what words to use. He told it anyway. When he had finished, he asked whether anyone could be made to believe it.

Throughout this story, the only person who explicitly does not believe it is the person who made it up.

And he writes that into the book, directly beneath the story, without deleting a sentence. For those who later wish to take the story apart, the first useful tool lies in those lines he wrote himself.
