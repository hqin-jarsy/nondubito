各司其职，和人是目的

Each Doing Their Own Work, and the Person as an End

Han Qin (秦汉)

造城那一段，起头很实在。

格劳孔要听正义本身好在哪里，苏格拉底说个人身上那几个字写得太小，眼神不好的人认不出来，不如先去看写得大的那一份。他没有说两份字一模一样，他说的是先读大的，读完了再回头对小的，看对不对得上。于是这场谈话动手造了一座城。

城是从缺口上起来的。一个人要是什么都不缺，他就不必跟谁来往；正因为谁也不能自足，人才凑到一块儿。凑到一块儿就得分活。分活的道理他讲得很朴素：人跟人天生不一样，这个适合做这个，那个适合做那个；一个人专做一件，东西出得又多又好，还省事。

那条规矩就是这么放进去的：一人一事，按天性。

放进去的时候，它只是一条管出活的规矩。讲的是鞋怎么做得好，地怎么种得好。谁也没有说它是正义。

然后隔了两卷。

到卷四，城造完了，四样德性一样一样找过去。找到最后一样的时候，苏格拉底说了一句：这就是我们一开头造城时立下的那一条，做属于自己的那一件事，不去插手别人的。

要紧的不是这个来回，是那条规矩在这一路上换了性质。进去的时候它管活儿，出来的时候它管人。进去的时候它说的是这样做东西做得好，出来的时候它说的是这样的人才是正义的人。

紧接着他把话说到了另一头。三个等级各做各的，这是正义；反过来，做鞋的想去打仗，打仗的想去管事，等级之间互相插手互相顶替，那是对这座城最大的害，而这个害的名字就叫不正义。

这句话里有一处值得记住。他说互相插手是对这座城最大的害。害是有收款人的，他把它记在了城的名下。你越了位，亏的是城。至于你越位那一天心里在想什么，你原来那个位置合不合你的手，在这个句子里都没有位置。

到这里，两边碰上了，碰在一个字上面。

那个字是自己的。

柏拉图这个定义有一处很硬，硬在它不往外找。它没有说正义是守法，没有说正义是让神满意，也没有说正义是让多数人过得去。它说的是：有一样东西是属于你的，正义就是你做那一样，不去动别人那一样。

SAE 这边也有一个自己的，押的分量一样重。人是目的，不可以被降成手段；说到底是这么一句：一个人身上有一样东西是他自己的法，别人可以用他，可以求他，可以指挥他，可是不能把那一样降成一个由别人调的数。

两边都把最重的东西押在这个词上，都认为一个人身上有一处是属于他自己的，动了那一处，事情就不对了。

可是这两个自己的，来路正好相反。

柏拉图那个自己的，是城给的。城看你天性适合什么，把你安在那个位置上，那件事从此就属于你了。SAE 那个自己的，是本人立的，谁也安不上去。

同一个词，两条相反的来路。这个比两边说了同一句话更值得看。说同一句话有可能只是碰巧；一个词被两边同时押上重注，来路却相反，那说明这个位置本身很要紧，要紧到谁也绕不过去。

上班的人对这个分别熟得很。公司给你排的那个岗，和你自己认下来的那件事，有时候是同一件。合上的那一阵，日子是顺的，人也踏实，那种踏实很像柏拉图说的正义：你在做属于你的那一件，不必东张西望。合不上的时候呢，你照样把活干完，干得还挺像样，可你心里清楚那不是你的。而合不合，多数时候不由你定。

那么问题就很干脆了：属于自己的那一件事，由谁定。

在理想国里由城定。

这不是替他说的，是他自己写的整套流程。孩子生下来，看资质，分路子，各受各的教育，一层一层地试，试到最后各归各位。你适合做什么，不由你说，由看的人说；而看的人是替城看的。

他把这件事说得最明白的地方在卷五。他说，最好的城应当尽量像一个人。一个人的指头被砸了一下，不是指头一个在疼，是整个人跟着疼，整个人一起去照管那根指头。城要办到这个地步，才算办好了。

这个比方漂亮，也可怕。漂亮在它讲的是关切：一处疼，处处疼，没有谁被丢下。可怕在它讲的是身份：指头是身体的指头。指头没有它自己要办的事，指头的好就是身体的好，两者之间不留一条缝。

他不是没有被人当面顶过。阿德曼图斯就顶过一次，顶得很实在。他说，你这些护卫者过的是什么日子。城是他们的，他们却什么也捞不着：没有自己的钱，没有房子地产，不能出门散心，连给相好的人花一笔都不能。你把他们弄得像一队驻扎在城里的雇佣兵。

苏格拉底的回答很有名，而且他一点没有绕。他说，我们造这座城，本来就不是为了让哪一个等级特别快活，是为了整座城。他还打了个比方：我们在给一尊塑像上色，你跑过来说，眼睛是全身最好看的地方，你怎么给它上黑的，眼睛该上紫的才对。可我们要的不是眼睛好看，是整尊像好看。

这个回答是诚实的，诚实得让人不好受。他知道有人在这里吃亏，他把这一层摆在明处，然后说它就该这么算。

SAE 这边不这么算。

不是说他算错了，是说这不该这么算。人是目的这句话的意思是，一个人不是任何别的东西的部件，其中也包括不是一座好城的部件。一座城办得好不好，不能拿来当作一个人为什么该待在那儿的理由。

分歧其实落在一个很小的地方，就落在那尊塑像上。

塑像的眼睛没有它自己的事要办。你把它上黑还是上紫，它不会有意见，因为它压根不是一样会有意见的东西。所以为了整尊像好看而委屈眼睛，这句话本身是通的，通得没有一点毛病。

可人不是眼睛。一个被安置在他位置上的人，同时还在过他自己的那一辈子。他在那个位置上老下去，他的孩子在那个位置边上长大。整座城好不好，和他这一辈子好不好，是两件要分开算的事。那尊塑像的比方，恰恰是把这两件并成了一件。

而并成一件的那一下，用的是天性两个字。你天生就适合这个，所以这个位置就是属于你的；既然是属于你的，你待在这儿就既是城的好，也是你的好。两头在这里合了拢，合得严丝合缝，看不出接缝在哪儿。

一个组赶活儿那一阵，谁都听过这句话：这一仗打下来，大家都好。这句话不假，真打下来了确实大家都好。可它有两处从来不说：万一没打下来呢，万一打下来了而你那一份没到呢。它把整体的好和你的好并成了一句，并的时候你听不出接缝，因为那个接缝本来就落在你听不见的地方。

这一刀下得重，重到得补一句公道话。

柏拉图不是不管人。他整本书都在论证正义的人比不正义的人过得好，而且是在没有一个人看着的情况下也过得好。他真心认为待在属于自己的位置上，对那个人自己就是最好的。分歧不在他管不管人，分歧在于那个位置到底是谁认下来的。他那边是看的人认下来的，认得准不准，是一个手艺问题；SAE 这边是本人认下来的，认得准不准照旧是问题，只是认的那一个换了人。

可是饭总得有人做。

这句话不是抬杠，它就是柏拉图这座城的起点。城是因为谁也不能自足才起来的。有人种地，有人打铁，有人今天晚上得站在城墙上不睡觉。

于是他会问：你说每一个人都是目的，不许把人降成手段。好。今晚站在城墙上的那个人呢。他站在那儿，是为了城里睡着的那些人。

你多半会说，不许的是把人锁死在那个位置上，不是不许用人。那就问得再细一点。守夜这件事，得有人今晚在，明晚也在，年年都在。要它年年都在，就得有人一辈子干这个。一个人一辈子干一件事，和被锁在那件事上，你打算怎么分。你说他随时可以不干；那这座城就得随时准备着有人不干。一座随时准备着有人不干的城，是一座什么样的城。

代价我是认的。我写过，造这座城不是为了让哪一个等级特别快活；护卫者手里什么也没有，我知道，我照旧那么办，还画了一尊塑像给你看。这一层我摆在明处了。你那边不许人被锁死，可饭还是得有人天天做。不许，和办得成，是两件事。

这一问不是从外面来的。它是从他自己那座城的地基上长出来的：城起于缺，缺就得有人去补。

最后回到那根指头。

那个比方里，指头被砸了一下，整个人跟着疼，整个人一起去照管它。柏拉图拿这个说城，说的是关切，说的是没有谁被丢下。

可是把这句话倒过来念一遍，它就换了个样子。整个人会疼，是因为那根指头是它的。它照管那根指头，跟它照管自己是同一件事，中间没有第二个人。

这个比方后来被用过很多次，用的多半是它好的那一面：你不是孤零零的一个，你是一个更大的东西的一部分，那个东西会疼你。这句话是真的。真的东西才拢得住人。

只是一根指头没法问一句，我为什么非得是这只手上的。

The building of the city begins in a very practical place.

Glaucon wants to hear what is good about justice itself. Socrates says that the letters written on the individual are too small for weak eyes to make out. Better to look first at the copy written larger. He does not say that the two texts are identical. He says to read the larger first, then return to the smaller and see whether they match. So the conversation sets about building a city.

The city rises from a lack. If a person lacked nothing, there would be no need to deal with anyone else. People come together precisely because none can be self-sufficient. Once together, they must divide the work. His reason for dividing it is plain: people differ by nature. One is suited to this, another to that. When each specializes in one thing, more and better things are produced, with less trouble.

That is how the rule enters: one person, one task, according to nature.

When it enters, it is only a rule for getting work done. It concerns how to make good shoes and farm well. Nobody has said it is justice.

Then two books pass.

In Book IV, the city is complete, and they look for the four virtues one by one. When they reach the last, Socrates says: this is the very rule we laid down when we began building the city, doing what belongs to oneself and not interfering in the work of others.

What matters is not the journey back and forth, but the change in the rule’s nature along the way. It went in governing work and came out governing people. It went in saying that this way of doing things makes good products. It came out saying that only a person who lives this way is just.

He immediately takes the point to its other end. Each of the three classes doing its own work is justice. Conversely, when a shoemaker wants to fight, a fighter wants to govern, and the classes interfere with and replace one another, that is the greatest harm to the city. The name of that harm is injustice.

There is something in this sentence worth remembering. He says that interference is the greatest harm to the city. The loss has an account to which it is charged, and he enters it under the city’s name. When you step out of your place, the city loses. What you were thinking that day, or whether your original place suited you, has no place in this sentence.

Here the two sides meet, at a single expression.

The expression is one’s own.

There is something firm in Plato’s definition: it does not look outside. It does not say that justice is obeying the law, pleasing the gods, or making life tolerable for the majority. It says: something belongs to you, and justice is doing that thing without touching what belongs to someone else.

SAE, too, has a “one’s own,” and it bears the same weight. A person is an end and cannot be reduced to a means. Ultimately, this comes down to a sentence: a person has something that is their own law. Others may use their services, ask things of them, or direct them, but may not reduce that law to a number someone else adjusts.

Both sides place their greatest weight on this expression. Both hold that there is a place in a person that belongs to that person, and that interfering with it makes things go wrong.

Yet these two versions of one’s own come from opposite directions.

For Plato, what is one’s own is given by the city. The city sees what your nature suits you for and places you there. From then on, that task belongs to you. For SAE, what is one’s own is established by the person. Nobody else can install it.

The same expression, two opposite origins. This deserves more attention than two sides saying the same thing. Saying the same thing might be a coincidence. When both stake so much on one expression, yet reach it from opposite directions, the place itself must matter, so much that neither can get around it.

Anyone with a job knows this distinction well. The post your company assigns you and the work you recognize as your own are sometimes the same. While they coincide, life runs smoothly and you feel settled. That feeling resembles Plato’s justice: you are doing what belongs to you, with no need to look about elsewhere. What happens when they do not coincide? You still finish the work, perhaps quite well, but you know inwardly that it is not yours. Most of the time, whether they coincide is not yours to decide.

The question, then, is straightforward: who decides which task belongs to a person?

In the Republic, the city decides.

This is not something said on Plato’s behalf. He writes out the entire procedure himself. Children are born, their aptitudes assessed, their paths divided. Each receives the appropriate education and undergoes test after test until everyone arrives at their place. What you are suited to is not for you to say. It is for the observers to say, and the observers look on the city’s behalf.

He states the matter most clearly in Book V. The best city, he says, should resemble a single person as closely as possible. When a finger is struck, it is not the finger alone that hurts. The whole person hurts with it and joins in caring for it. Only when a city achieves that has it been well made.

The image is beautiful and frightening. Beautiful because it speaks of care: when one place hurts, every place hurts, and nobody is left behind. Frightening because it speaks of identity: a finger is the body’s finger. It has no business of its own. The finger’s good is the body’s good, with no gap left between them.

It is not as though nobody has challenged him to his face. Adeimantus does so once, in very practical terms. What sort of life do your guardians lead, he asks? The city is theirs, yet they get nothing from it: no money of their own, no houses or land, no trips away for pleasure, not even money to spend on a lover. You have made them like a troop of mercenaries stationed in the city.

Socrates’ reply is famous, and he does not evade the issue. We are building this city, he says, not to make any one class particularly happy, but for the whole city. He offers another image. We are painting a statue, and you come along and say: the eyes are the most beautiful part of the body, so why paint them black? They ought to be purple. But what we want is not beautiful eyes. It is a beautiful statue.

The answer is honest, so honest that it is uncomfortable. He knows that some people lose out here. He puts that in plain sight, then says this is how the account ought to be reckoned.

SAE does not reckon it that way.

It is not saying that he has miscalculated, but that the calculation should not be made this way. Saying that a person is an end means that a person is not a component of anything else, including a good city. How well a city is run cannot serve as the reason why a person should remain in a particular place.

The disagreement falls in a very small place: on that statue.

The statue’s eyes have no business of their own. Whether you paint them black or purple, they will have no opinion, because they are not the kind of thing that can have an opinion at all. So the claim that the eyes may be shortchanged for the sake of the whole statue’s beauty makes perfect sense. There is nothing wrong with it.

But people are not eyes. A person installed in a position is also living their own life. They grow old there; their children grow up beside it. Whether the whole city fares well and whether that person’s life fares well are two accounts that must be kept separate. The statue image combines precisely those two accounts into one.

The words that join them are by nature. You are naturally suited to this, so this position belongs to you. Since it belongs to you, staying here is both the city’s good and your own. The two sides close together so neatly that the seam disappears.

Anyone who has worked in a team rushing to finish a job has heard this: once we win this battle, everyone will benefit. It is not false. If the battle really is won, everyone really does benefit. But two things are never mentioned: what if it is not won, and what if it is won but your share never arrives? The whole’s good and your good have been joined in a single sentence. You cannot hear the seam as they are joined, because the seam was placed where you cannot hear it.

That cut is sharp enough to require a word of fairness.

Plato is not indifferent to people. His entire book argues that the just person lives better than the unjust, even when nobody is watching. He sincerely believes that remaining in the place that belongs to a person is best for that person. The disagreement is not about whether he cares for people. It is about who recognizes the place as theirs. On his side, the observers recognize it, and the accuracy of their recognition is a question of craft. On SAE’s side, the person recognizes it. Whether that recognition is accurate remains a question. Only the person doing the recognizing has changed.

But someone still has to cook.

This is not a quibble. It is the starting point of Plato’s city. The city arose because nobody could be self-sufficient. Someone farms, someone works iron, and someone has to stand awake on the wall tonight.

So he will ask: you say that every person is an end, that nobody may be reduced to a means. Very well. What about the person standing on the wall tonight? That person stands there for the people asleep inside the city.

You will probably say that what is forbidden is locking people into that position, not making use of them. Let us ask more closely, then. Someone must keep watch tonight, tomorrow night, year after year. For it to continue year after year, someone must do it for a lifetime. How will you distinguish doing one thing all one’s life from being locked into it? You say that the person can stop at any time. Then the city must be ready at any time for someone to stop. What sort of city is a city perpetually ready for someone to stop?

I acknowledge the cost. I wrote that the city is not built to make any one class especially happy. I know the guardians have nothing in their hands. I proceed anyway, and I paint you a statue to show it. I have put this in plain sight. Your side forbids locking people into place, but someone must still cook every day. Forbidding something and making things work are two different matters.

The question does not come from outside. It grows from the foundations of his own city: the city begins in lack, and someone has to fill the lack.

Finally, return to that finger.

In the image, the finger is struck, the whole person hurts with it, and the whole person joins in caring for it. Plato uses this to speak of the city, of care, of nobody being left behind.

But read the sentence backward and its shape changes. The whole person hurts because the finger is theirs. Caring for that finger is the same act as caring for themselves. There is no second person in between.

The image has been used many times since, usually for its good side: you are not alone. You are part of something larger, and that larger thing will care for you. This is true. It takes something true to draw people together.

Only, a finger cannot ask why it has to belong to this particular hand.
