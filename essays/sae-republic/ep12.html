责任在选择者

Responsibility Belongs to the Chooser

Han Qin (秦汉)

十卷谈完，天快亮了。苏格拉底最后讲了一个故事。

有个人叫厄尔，打仗死了。十天以后收尸，别人的尸首都烂了，他的还好好的。抬回家，第十二天放上柴堆要烧，他活了过来，然后讲了他这十二天看见的事。

他说他的魂到了一个地方，那儿有两个口子朝上，两个口子朝下，中间坐着判官。做过的事一样一样算，好的往上，坏的往下，来回一千年。有个僭主想爬上来，口子发出一声吼，几个火一样的人把他捆了拖走。

轮到要投生了。所有的魂集合起来，有个先知抱着一堆东西站到高处，说了一段话。

这段话是全书的落点。

他说：朝生暮死的魂啊，你们又要开始一轮了。

不是有个神来给你们抽签定命。是你们自己挑。抽到第一号的先挑，挑中哪一个，就得跟它过完这一辈子。

德性这样东西没有主人。你看重它，你就多得一点；你不当回事，你就少得一点。

最后一句：责任在选的那个人，神没有责任。

这一句结结实实地立在那儿。整本书绕了十卷，最后落在这里。

然后开始挑。

摆出来的是一堆命，比在场的魂多得多，什么样的都有，人的，也有牲口的。抽签只定谁先谁后，挑什么完全由你。

有一处得说清楚，不然会读岔：挑的是整个一包。你挑不了性子。你挑了哪一种命，那种命自带的性子就跟着来了，跑不掉。

第一个抽到号的最惨。他从天上那一路下来的，二话不说抓了最大的一个僭主。抓完他才慢慢看清楚，这一包里还有一条：他要吃自己的孩子。

苏格拉底顺手说了一条规律，说得很冷。从天上那一路下来的人，多半挑得糟，因为他们没吃过苦；从地下那一路上来的，自己受过也见过别人受，挑的时候反而不着急。

那个人上一辈子是在一座规矩不错的城里过的，人也算好人。可他那个好是习惯出来的，没有想明白过。

接下来一个一个挑，柏拉图挑了几个大家都认识的写。俄耳甫斯挑了天鹅，他被女人害死过，不想再从女人肚子里出来。埃阿斯挑了狮子，他还记着当年那副铠甲判给了别人。阿伽门农挑了鹰，他受够了人。忒耳西忒斯挑了猴。

最后一号是奥德修斯。

他前面那一辈子跑得太累了，累到把想出人头地这件事治好了。他找了很久，找的是一个平常人的日子，不操什么心的那一种。这样的命扔在一边没人要，因为谁都看不上。他找见了，很高兴地拿了。

他说了一句：这一号要是排在第一个，我也挑这个。

挑完以后的事很短。命运三姊妹一个接一个：一个把你挑的那个交给你，一个把它转一圈定下来，最后一个把它拧死，从此改不了。然后所有人从必然的座位底下过去。

再往后是一条河，河的名字叫无心。这条河的水，什么器皿也盛不住。

每个魂都得喝，喝一份定量。可那些心里没数的，喝得比定量多。

喝完就都忘了。半夜里一声雷，地一震，魂就往各处投生去了，像天上划过的流星。

厄尔没有喝那口水。所以他记得。

到这里，两边是碰得上的，而且这一处碰得几乎没有缝。

责任在选的那个人，神没有责任。这一句这边照单全收，一个字不改。

而且他把这一句放的位置，比这一句本身更值得看。他没有把它写成一条道理讲出来，他把它写成一个人在你耳边说的话，在你排队等着挑的那一刻说的。听完你就得动手。

德性没有主人这一句也一样。没有主人的意思是没人管得着它，也没人替你保管它。它不归神管，不归城管，也不归你父母管。

然后是分开的地方，而这一处分了三层。

第一层是那一堆命。

它们是事先摆好的。数目再多，也是有数的；样子再全，也是别人做好的。你挑，你只能从摆着的那些里挑。

这一边不这么看。摆在人面前的不是一堆做好的东西。认识一件事也好，过一段日子也好，说到底是划一道界，把这一边和那一边分开，本系列一直把这个动作叫作落刀。一刀落下去立住一块，同时留下这一刀没有收进去的那一部分。而下一刀能落在哪儿，是从这一部分里长出来的，不是从一张单子上挑出来的。

所以这边不给包裹。给的是一个还能下手的地方。

十八岁填志愿的时候，谁都尝过前一种。摆在你面前的是一张表，一行一个名字。你不知道那一行后面跟着什么样的一群同学，什么样的作息，什么样的几种活法。你挑的时候，看见的多半只是排在前头的那几个字。

而人到三十岁再回头看，事情就是另一种样子了。那时候你早已不是在一张表上挑，你是从上一件事留下的那个口子里往下走。

第二层是那三姊妹。

挑完之后，交给你，定下来，拧死。柏拉图把这一步写得很清楚：从此改不了。

那一选是一次性的，选完就锁上了。整个一辈子的分量，全压在排队时的那一下。

这一边没有那样一步。没有哪一刀是最后一刀。上一刀留下的地方还在那儿，下一刀还能落。

可这一层有个后果，不见得轻松。

正因为没有哪一次算到不能改，肯不肯接着来，就成了每一次都得重新答一遍的事。柏拉图那个人只要在排队的时候用一次心；这边这个人，得一直用着。

第三层是那口水。

所有人都得喝。心里没数的多喝一点，可谁也不能不喝。

这一边不给这口水。忘不忘是另一回事，忘了不等于那件事没发生。它照旧在那儿，只是没有人再提起它。

而且忘这件事在日常里是有分寸的。一个人回头看自己二十岁做过的一个决定，多半说不清当时到底怎么想的。不是记性差。是当时那些理由本来就零零碎碎，隔了很久，剩下的只有一个结果，和一句事后编得挺顺的说法。

那句事后编得挺顺的说法，最像喝了一口那样的水。

这三层合起来，这边落下的是一句话：那个永远差一点的我。

差一点，说的是每一刀取走的是实得，不是接近。人确实在往下走，一刀比一刀多拿到一点东西，可前头没有一个走到了在那儿等着。所以永远差一点，不是说他没走到，是说没有那个可以走到的地方。

这句话听着挺稳。可把书往回翻几页，就会碰上一尊像。

就在厄尔之前，苏格拉底在讲魂不死。他说，我们现在看见的魂，不是它本来的样子，它跟身子搅在一起，弄坏了。

他打了个比方，用的是海里那位格劳科斯。那尊像在水底泡了太久，有的地方磕断了，有的地方被浪磨平了，身上又长满了贝壳海藻和碎石头，看上去更像一头怪物，不像它原先那样东西。

然后他说了半句。他说，你要是把它捞出来，把身上那些东西敲掉，那时候才看得见它真正的样子，是一样呢还是好几样。

这半句他说得很小心，小心到值得抄下来看。他用的是那种如果怎样就会怎样的说法，不是说我看见了。他还在后面又加了一句：或者说，不管它究竟是个什么样子。

也就是说，他没有说那些贝壳敲得掉，也没有说底下真有一个干净的样子等着。他只留下了一个问法。

于是他会问。

你说沉在下面的那些东西不是脏，它们本来就是这个人的一部分，敲不掉，也不该敲。你说能做的只有把它们重新摆一摆，看清楚哪一样是从哪儿来的。

好。那我再问一句：你那句永远差一点的话，差的是什么。

差得离哪儿远。你要说不差谁，那这句话是在说什么。你要说差着一个本来的样子，那你就得有一尊我这样的像；可你刚说了你没有。

我那半句是留了一个问法。你连那个问法都取消了。

这一问就搁在这里。

最后回到最后一号。

奥德修斯挑得最晚。轮到他的时候，好的都被人挑走了，剩下的是别人不要的。

可他找了很久。他不是随手在剩下的里头捡了一个，他是找，一样一样地看过去，找那一个平常人的日子。它扔在角落里，谁都看不上，所以还在。

然后他说了那一句：这一号要是排在第一个，我也挑这个。

排在第一个的人抓了一个僭主，排在最后的人挑了一个平常人的日子，而后者说他早排也会这么挑。

柏拉图把这句话给了一个走了很远才回家的人。他没有解释为什么给他。

Ten books of conversation are over. Dawn is near. Socrates tells one final story.

A man named Er dies in battle. Ten days later, when the bodies are gathered, the others have decomposed but his remains intact. He is carried home. On the twelfth day, placed on the funeral pyre, he comes back to life and describes what he has seen during those twelve days.

His soul, he says, reached a place with two openings upward and two downward, judges seated between them. Deeds were reckoned one by one: good upward, bad downward, a thousand-year journey there and back. A tyrant tried to climb out. The opening roared, and men like fire bound him and dragged him away.

Then comes the time for rebirth. All the souls gather. A prophet takes a pile of things to a high place and speaks.

This speech is where the whole book lands.

Souls of a day, he says, you are about to begin another round.

No god will draw lots to decide your fate. You will choose for yourselves. Whoever draws the first lot chooses first. Whichever life you select, you must live it through.

Virtue has no master. Honor it and you will have more of it. Disregard it and you will have less.

The final sentence: the responsibility belongs to the chooser; the god bears no responsibility.

That sentence stands firmly there. The whole book has traveled through ten books to arrive at this.

Then the choosing begins.

A heap of lives is laid out, far more than there are souls, of every kind, human and animal. The lots determine only who goes first and who goes later. What you choose is entirely up to you.

One thing must be clear, or the passage can be misread: what you choose is a whole package. You cannot choose the character separately. Once you choose a kind of life, the character that comes with it follows, inescapably.

The first chooser fares worst. He has come down by the heavenly route and immediately grabs the greatest tyranny. Only afterward does he gradually see another clause in the package: he will eat his own children.

Socrates adds a chilling general observation. Those coming from heaven often choose badly because they have not suffered. Those coming up from below have suffered themselves and seen others suffer, so they are less hasty in choosing.

That first man had spent his previous life in a well-ordered city and was a good enough person. But his goodness came from habit. He had never thought it through.

The choosing continues, and Plato singles out familiar names. Orpheus chooses a swan. Killed by women, he does not want to emerge from a woman’s womb again. Ajax chooses a lion, still remembering the armor awarded to someone else. Agamemnon chooses an eagle, having had enough of human beings. Thersites chooses a monkey.

The last lot belongs to Odysseus.

His previous life exhausted him so thoroughly that it cured his desire to rise above others. He searches for a long time, looking for the life of an ordinary person, one free of troubles. Such a life has been tossed aside, unwanted because everyone looks down on it. He finds it and takes it gladly.

He says: even if my lot had been first, I would have chosen this.

What follows the choosing is brief. The three sisters of Fate act in succession. One hands over what you chose, another turns it to fix it, and the last fastens it tight, beyond alteration. Then everyone passes beneath the seat of Necessity.

Further on is a river called Unmindfulness. No vessel can hold its water.

Every soul must drink a set measure. Those who lack sense drink more than the measure.

After drinking, all forget. At midnight thunder sounds, the earth shakes, and the souls shoot away to be born in different places, like stars streaking across the sky.

Er does not drink that water. That is why he remembers.

Here the two sides can meet, almost without a gap.

The responsibility belongs to the chooser; the god bears no responsibility. This side accepts the sentence in full, without changing a word.

And where he places it is even more worth noticing than the sentence itself. He does not present it as a principle to be explained. He makes it something someone says in your ear while you queue to choose. Once you have heard it, you must act.

The same applies to “virtue has no master.” Having no master means nobody has jurisdiction over it and nobody keeps it for you. It belongs neither to a god, nor to the city, nor to your parents.

Then comes the separation, in three layers.

The first is that heap of lives.

They are laid out in advance. However many there are, their number is finite. However varied, someone else has made them. You choose, but you can choose only among what is laid out.

This side sees it differently. What stands before a person is not a heap of finished things. Knowing something or living through a stretch of life comes down to drawing a boundary, separating this side from that. This series has called that act making a cut. A cut establishes a portion and simultaneously leaves what it has not taken in. Where the next cut can be made grows from that remainder. It is not chosen from a list.

So this side offers no packages. It offers somewhere further work remains possible.

Anyone filling in university choices at eighteen has tasted the first arrangement. A form lies before you, one name per row. You do not know what classmates, daily routines, and ways of living trail behind each row. When you choose, you usually see only the few words at its head.

Looking back at thirty, things are different. By then you have long since stopped choosing from a form. You are proceeding through the opening left by the previous event.

The second layer is the three sisters.

After you choose, it is handed over, fixed, and fastened tight. Plato makes this step explicit: from then on it cannot be changed.

The choice happens once and is locked afterward. The weight of an entire life rests on that moment in the queue.

This side has no such step. No cut is the last cut. The place left by the previous one remains, and another cut can still be made.

But this has a consequence that is not necessarily comforting.

Precisely because no occasion settles things beyond change, the willingness to continue must be answered anew each time. Plato’s person need attend carefully only once, while queuing. The person on this side must keep attending.

The third layer is the water.

Everyone must drink. Those who lack sense drink a little more, but nobody may abstain.

This side offers no such drink. Whether you forget is another matter. Forgetting does not mean the event never happened. It remains where it was, though nobody mentions it again.

And forgetting takes particular forms in everyday life. Looking back on a decision made at twenty, people usually cannot say exactly what they were thinking. This is not poor memory. The reasons were fragmentary in the first place. Much later, only a result remains, along with a smooth account composed afterward.

That smoothly composed account is the closest thing to a drink of such water.

Together, these three layers leave this side with a phrase: the self that is always a little short.

“A little short” means that each cut takes away an actual gain, not an approximation. The person really does proceed, gaining a little more with each cut. But no point of arrival waits ahead. Being always a little short does not mean failing to arrive. It means there is no such place to arrive at.

The sentence sounds steady enough. Turn back a few pages, though, and you meet a statue.

Just before Er, Socrates discusses the soul’s immortality. The soul we see now, he says, is not in its original state. Entangled with the body, it has been damaged.

He compares it to Glaucus in the sea. The statue has lain underwater too long. Parts are broken, others worn smooth by waves. Shells, seaweed, and pebbles cover it until it looks more like a monster than what it originally was.

Then he adds half a sentence. If you brought it up and knocked those things away, he says, only then would you see its true form: whether it is one thing or several.

He says this with a care worth copying down. His phrasing is conditional: if this were done, that would appear. He does not say he has seen it. Then he adds: or whatever its nature may be.

In other words, he does not say the shells can be knocked off, or that a clean form really waits beneath. He leaves only a way of asking.

So he would ask.

You say the things settled underneath are not dirt. They belong to this person, cannot be knocked off, and should not be. You say all that can be done is to rearrange them and see clearly where each came from.

Very well. Then let me ask: in your phrase “always a little short,” what is missing?

How far short of where? If you say it falls short of nothing, what does the sentence mean? If you say it falls short of an original form, you need a statue like mine. But you just said you have none.

My half-sentence leaves a way of asking. You have canceled even that question.

Let the question rest here.

Finally, return to the last lot.

Odysseus chooses last. By his turn, the good lives have been taken. What remains is what others did not want.

But he searches for a long time. He does not casually pick from the leftovers. He searches, examining them one by one for that ordinary person’s life. It has been thrown into a corner, despised by everyone, and so it is still there.

Then he says it: even if my lot had been first, I would have chosen this.

The first chooser grabs a tyranny. The last chooses an ordinary life and says he would have chosen it even earlier.

Plato gives that sentence to a man who traveled a long way before coming home. He does not explain why he gives it to him.
