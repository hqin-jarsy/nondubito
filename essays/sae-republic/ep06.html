统治这门手艺为谁做

Whom Is the Craft of Ruling For?

Han Qin (秦汉)

卷一谈到一半，来了一个不客气的人。

他叫色拉叙马霍斯，坐在旁边听了半天，几次想插话都被按住了。等苏格拉底和玻勒马霍斯把正义是什么绕来绕去说到没词，他再也忍不住，像一头野兽一样扑上来，说你们这是在互相客气，谁也不肯说实话。

他说的实话就一句：正义就是强者的利益。

这句话的意思不是拳头大的说了算。他讲得比这具体。他说，每一种政体都按自己的利益立法，民主政体立民主的法，僭主立僭主的法，立完就宣布这叫正义，谁不守就治谁的罪。所以所谓正义，从来就是上面那一伙人的好处，只是换了个好听的名字。

而且他把话说到了底。他说，真正干净的不正义比正义有力量，也更自由，更像个主人。你看小偷小摸的被抓住要受罚，可要是有人一口气把整座城连人带钱都拿了去，人家管他叫有福气的人。人们骂不正义，不是怕做不正义的事，是怕自己挨。

他还算了一笔很实的账。合伙做生意，散伙的时候正义的那个总是拿得少；交税，同样的家底他交得多；当了官，自家的事顾不上，还落一身埋怨。

苏格拉底先抓了一个小口子。他说，管事的人也会出错，出错的时候他定的法就不合他自己的利益了，那么老实守法的人反倒是在做对强者不利的事。

色拉叙马霍斯不吃这一套。他说，你要抠字眼，那就抠到底：一个统治者，就其为统治者而言，是不会出错的；他一出错，那一刻他就不是在统治。

这一步他退得很硬气。可他没想到，他把一件很趁手的东西递到了对面手里。

苏格拉底接过来的，就是这个就其为什么而言。

好，他说，那照这个说法看看别的行当。医术自己有毛病吗，需要谁来照顾它吗。不需要，医术就是医术，它没有缺。医术照顾的是身体。它谋求的不是医术的好处，是身体的好处。

御马的那门手艺也一样。它谋求的是马的好处，不是它自己的。

一路数下去，结论是同一句：凡一门手艺，就其为手艺而言，谋求的都不是强的那一边的好处，是它所照管那一边的好处。

所以统治，就其为统治而言，谋求的是被治者的好处。

色拉叙马霍斯当场反扑，而且这一扑很有分量。他说你未免太天真。牧羊人喂羊照顾羊，难道是为了羊。他是为了主人，为了自己。管事的人看治下的人，就跟看羊一样，白天黑夜盘算的都是自己那一份。

这一击很实，今天读也还实。

苏格拉底的回答是把两样东西拆开。他说，牧羊人就其为牧羊人而言，管的是羊养得好不好。至于他靠这个挣钱，那是另一门手艺，叫挣钱的手艺；它常常跟着别的手艺一起来，可它不是别的手艺本身。医生治病是一门，收诊费是另一门。一个人两门都在做，不等于两门是一门。

顺着这个说法，还长出一个结论，他自己也认下来了。既然每一门手艺都不为施行的人自己谋利，那么行手艺的人凭什么肯干。得给报酬。而报酬是从挣钱那一门来的，不是从这一门来的。

所以在他那一套里，管事这件事，对管事的人来说是亏的。要有人肯管，就得给他点什么。他数了三样：钱，名声，还有一样是罚，就是不肯出来管事的人要受的那个罚。而那个罚就是被一个比自己差的人管着。好人肯出来管事，多半是为了躲开第三样。

所以在这场论证所取的那个严格意思里，剥削性的那一种根本不叫统治。那是别的东西穿着统治的衣裳。

到这里，两边碰上了，而且碰得很正。

柏拉图做的动作是：把一个构成条件写进概念本身。不为被治者谋利的，不构成统治。

SAE 做的是同一个动作：把所治对象剥成一样东西的，那个东西不构成权力。

这不是两个碰巧相似的主张，是同一种手法。两边都没有去说那种东西坏。说坏是评价，评价可以被顶回来，对方大可以说我不在乎你怎么评价。两边说的是它压根不是那种东西，连这个词你都用不上。

连收尾的样子都像。柏拉图说，剥削性的那一种不叫统治，那是别的东西穿着统治的衣裳。SAE 这边说，把一个人剥到干干净净，剥的那一方并没有因此拿到更大的权力，他是从权力这件事里退了出去；剩下的那个关系不再是人对人，是人对东西。那也是别的东西穿着权力的衣裳。

这个手法分量很重，重在它改的是词的边界，不是词的褒贬。一个词一旦这么定下来，很多架就不用打了，因为对方站的那块地已经不在这张图上。

然后是分开的地方，而分开的地方不在刚才那一句上。刚才那一句两边是对得上的。

分歧在它之上：过了这一句，还有没有下文。

柏拉图那里没有。为被治者谋利，就是统治；不为，就不是。过了这道关，剩下的是办得好不好，那是本事问题了。

这边过了那一句才刚开头。还得再问一句：被治的那一边手上还剩多少。而剩得最要紧的那一样，是能喊停，也就是这件事往下走，中间有没有一处是他按一下就走不动的。有，那一处在他手上；没有，这件事从头到尾就只在上面那一边手里。

这一层之差，把一个人分开了。

一位仁慈的君主，完全满足柏拉图那个条件，而在柏拉图那里，满足了也就到头了。他真心为百姓打算，判断多半还对，他确实比百姓自己更清楚该先修哪条路。

往上那一问他答不出来。百姓不是没有位置。他们有位置，有活计，有说话的地方，也有人认真听。他们没有的是那一处按一下就走不动的地方。想往哪儿走都行，只是每一条路的尽头都回到他那里。剩多少不由他们定，全看他今天心里怎么想。

这一层落到看病上最清楚，而且这个例子是柏拉图自己起的头。

一个好医生当然为病人打算，这一点不必怀疑。可你去看病，手上还有别的东西。你可以再问一个医生，可以要一份病历，可以在那张同意书上不签字。不签字就是那一下能喊停。这些东西不是为了防着医生坏，是因为不能只靠医生好。

柏拉图那个条件说的是医生朝哪边使劲，这边这个条件说的是病人手上有没有那几样。两句话对着的是同一件事，可它们要的不是同一样东西。

而理想国那座城，正是那位仁慈的君主。

管事的那一小拨人是为整座城打算的，这一点柏拉图写得很清楚，也很认真，一点没有藏私。城里别的人得着好处，得着安顿，得着一份合自己的活计。他们唯一没有的，是那一处按一下就走不动的地方。管事的人定下来的事，一路走到底，中间没有一道要下面点头才过得去的关口。剩下多少，是上面那一小拨人替他们定的。

还有一层更细的分别，藏在那个手艺论证里面。

柏拉图那个条件不是一件心里的事。医术该照管身体，这是明面上的道理，苏格拉底正是拿它当公道话讲给对面听的。可这套论证从头到尾没配一个核对的办法。手艺自己有规矩，懂这门手艺的人就懂这条规矩；至于某一天某一个医生是不是真在照着办，除了他自己，没有第二个位置站在那儿。条件是硬的，施行它的只有一头。

SAE 那个条件不一样。那样东西必须在对方手上，握着它的人不是掌权的那一方。这不是因为信不过掌权的人，是因为一个条件如果只由被审的那一方自己领会，那就是自己审自己。

当过家长的人对这个分别熟得很。

你为孩子好，这是真的，而且他小的时候，你的判断确实比他准，准得多。可他长到某个岁数，事情就变了：他得有一处能说不。这个能说不，不是因为你不再爱他，也不是因为你判断错了，是因为再往下，你的爱和你的判断都不能替他做那一件事。

多数人家最难过的一段，就在把那一处交出去的时候。

两个条件的难处也不一样。利益的朝向说得清，可说得清不等于有人在核，而这套论证里没安排谁去核。那一处按一下就走不动的地方不一样，它不问谁心里怎么想，只问这件事走到中间有没有一道要下面点头的关口，而这件事外面的人问得出来。

所以前一个容易成立，也容易走样；后一个难成立得多，可一旦成立，就不太靠谁的心思吃饭。

还有一处两边同病，顺带提一句。

把剥削从统治这个词里逐出去以后，正在被剥削的那些人身上发生的事，总得有个说法。

柏拉图那边其实没给。他从手艺里分出去的只是报酬那一门；至于靠着管事把别人往下削这一整件事，卷一里始终没落到一个名字上。它被赶出了统治这个词，然后就那么站着，没人再管它叫什么。

SAE 这边给了名字，把剥到干净的那一种叫作人对东西的处置。可讲权力的那套话到这里也就不往下说了，因为它自己声明过不评价。

一边没有名字，一边有名字而不开口。最坏的那一格，两边都没有话。

可是那一样能喊停的东西，究竟是怎么到人手上去的。

这一问 SAE 这边有答话，而且答得挺硬。它说那样东西不是谁给的。你是一个人，你就有；不是因为谁承认了才有，是先有了它，承认才有得承认。掌权的那一方没有一个发放的动作可做，他能做的只是尊重，或者压住。而且压也压不掉，那样东西一直在往上顶，压的人得一直使着劲，一停手它就回来了。

不过这套说法自己留了一句实话：不是给的，只在人被当作完整的一个人来对待的那一层上才这么说；那一层底下，它承认那样东西确实是拿别的东西换来的，也确实收得回去。至于谁在哪一层，从底下怎么上去，它没有再往下说。

回头看一眼刚才那个家长。多数人家最难过的一段，就在把那一处交出去的时候。

于是他会问：交出去，那不是给是什么。

你说不是给的，那是在讲它的来路。来路我不跟你争。可到了一户人家，一间病房，一座城，它照旧要等上面那一边松一次手，而松不松，什么时候松，是上面那一边说了算。你把给这个字取消了，你没有把那个动作取消。

那两层之间呢。一个人怎么知道自己站在哪一层，从底下上到上面又是谁动的手。这两句你留在外面了，可要紧的正是这两句：底下那一层既然收得回去，那一层上的人手里握着的那一样，跟没有差在哪儿。

而且这一比，我那个条件反倒是当下就立得住的。医生今天朝身体使劲，这门手艺今天就成立，不用等谁先动一下。你那个要等。等得到，你那句话就成立；等不到，它就一直是一句将来的话。而这中间的一段，是有人在过的。

这一问落在很硬的地方。

最后回到那个不客气的人。

他说完那一大套，就想走。柏拉图这一笔写得好玩，说他像澡堂子里的伙计，端起一桶话哗地浇在众人耳朵上，浇完抬脚就要走。

在座的人不放他走。他们把他按住，让他留下，把话说完，说清楚。

一个人把最难听的话说完就想走，而听的人偏不放他走。权力这个题目，是被一个想走而走不掉的人逼出来的。

Halfway through the discussion in Book I, someone arrives who is not inclined to be polite.

His name is Thrasymachus. He has been sitting nearby listening for a long time and has been stopped several times when trying to interrupt. Once Socrates and Polemarchus have talked themselves in circles about justice and run out of things to say, he can bear it no longer. He springs at them like a wild beast and says they are merely being polite to each other. Neither will tell the truth.

His truth comes down to one sentence: justice is the advantage of the stronger.

He does not simply mean that the biggest fist gets its way. His claim is more specific. Every regime, he says, makes laws in its own interest. A democracy makes democratic laws; a tyranny makes tyrannical laws. Once made, the laws are declared just, and anyone who disobeys is punished. So what passes for justice has always been the benefit of the people at the top, given a nicer name.

And he takes the argument all the way. Thoroughgoing injustice, he says, is more powerful than justice, freer, more masterful. A petty thief is caught and punished. But someone who takes an entire city at a stroke, people and money together, is called fortunate. People condemn injustice not because they fear committing it, but because they fear suffering it.

He also does some very practical accounting. When a business partnership breaks up, the just partner always takes less. With the same wealth, that partner pays more tax. In public office, the just person neglects private affairs and earns a heap of complaints.

Socrates first finds a small opening. Rulers make mistakes, he says. When they do, the laws they make will not serve their own interests. Someone who faithfully obeys the law will then be doing what harms the stronger.

Thrasymachus will have none of this. If you want to split hairs, he says, split them all the way: a ruler, insofar as he is a ruler, does not make mistakes. The moment he errs, he is not ruling.

He yields that step with considerable defiance. What he does not realize is that he has handed his opponent a very useful instrument.

What Socrates takes up is precisely this qualification: insofar as something is what it is.

Very well, he says. Let us look at other occupations on those terms. Is there something wrong with medicine itself? Does it need someone to care for it? No. Medicine is medicine. It lacks nothing. What medicine cares for is the body. It seeks the body’s good, not its own.

The craft of horsemanship is the same. It seeks the good of the horse, not its own.

Go through the crafts one by one, and the conclusion is the same: every craft, insofar as it is a craft, seeks the good not of the stronger side, but of the side in its care.

So ruling, insofar as it is ruling, seeks the good of the ruled.

Thrasymachus strikes back immediately, and the blow has weight. You are much too innocent, he says. Does a shepherd feed and tend sheep for the sheep’s sake? He does it for his master and himself. Rulers see those they govern exactly as they see sheep, calculating their own share day and night.

That is a solid blow. It still lands when we read it today.

Socrates answers by separating two things. A shepherd, insofar as he is a shepherd, concerns himself with whether the sheep are well tended. Earning money from it is another craft, the craft of money-making. It often accompanies other crafts, but it is not those crafts themselves. Treating a patient is one craft; collecting the fee is another. That one person practices both does not make the two one.

A further conclusion grows out of this account, and he accepts it himself. If no craft seeks the practitioner’s own benefit, why should anyone agree to practice it? There must be a reward. And that reward comes from the money-making craft, not from the craft being practiced.

So in his account, governing costs the person who governs. If someone is to agree to do it, something must be offered. He lists three things: money, honor, and a third, a penalty for those unwilling to govern. That penalty is being governed by someone worse than oneself. Good people mostly agree to govern in order to avoid the third.

In the strict sense adopted by this argument, then, the exploitative kind is not ruling at all. It is something else dressed in ruling’s clothes.

Here the two sides meet, and meet squarely.

Plato’s move is to write a constitutive condition into the concept itself. What does not seek the good of the ruled does not constitute ruling.

SAE makes the same move: what strips the governed person down to a thing does not constitute power.

These are not two claims that happen to resemble each other. They use the same method. Neither side says that such a thing is bad. Calling it bad is a judgment, and a judgment can be thrown back at you: your opponent can simply say that your opinion does not matter. Both sides say that it is not that kind of thing at all. You do not even get to use the word.

Even the way they finish is alike. Plato says that the exploitative kind is not ruling; it is something else dressed in ruling’s clothes. SAE says that stripping a person bare does not give the person doing the stripping more power. That person has stepped out of power altogether. The relation left behind is no longer person to person, but person to thing. That, too, is something else dressed in power’s clothes.

The method carries great weight because it changes a word’s boundary, not its favorable or unfavorable coloring. Once a word is defined this way, many arguments no longer need to be fought, because the ground on which the other side stands is no longer on the map.

Then comes the separation, and it does not lie in the sentence just stated. On that sentence, the two sides agree.

The disagreement comes beyond it: after that sentence, is there anything more to say?

For Plato there is not. Seeking the good of the ruled is ruling; failing to do so is not. Once that threshold has been crossed, what remains is how well it is done, a question of ability.

On this side, that sentence is only the beginning. Another question remains: how much is left in the hands of those governed? The most important thing left is the ability to call a halt. As the process goes forward, is there a point where their pressing once can stop it? If there is, that point is in their hands. If there is not, the entire process belongs to the people above them, from beginning to end.

That one further level divides the verdict on a person.

A benevolent monarch fully satisfies Plato’s condition, and for Plato, satisfying it is the end of the matter. The monarch sincerely thinks of the people’s interests, is usually right, and really does know better than the people themselves which road should be built first.

The further question is one he cannot answer. It is not that the people have no place. They have places, work, opportunities to speak, and people who listen seriously. What they lack is the point where pressing once stops the process. They can go whichever way they like, but every road leads back to him. How much they retain is not theirs to decide. It all depends on what is in his mind that day.

The difference becomes clearest in medical care, and Plato himself introduced that example.

A good doctor certainly thinks of the patient’s interests. There is no need to doubt that. But when you seek treatment, you have other things in your hands. You can consult another doctor, request a copy of your medical records, or decline to sign the consent form. Not signing is the act that can call a halt. These things are not there to guard against a bad doctor. They are there because the doctor’s goodness cannot be all you rely on.

Plato’s condition concerns the direction in which the doctor works. This side’s condition concerns whether the patient has those things in hand. The two sentences address the same situation, but they do not ask for the same thing.

And the city of the Republic is precisely that benevolent monarch.

The small group in charge acts for the whole city. Plato writes this clearly and earnestly, with no concealed private interest. The other people in the city receive benefits, a settled place, and work suited to them. The one thing they lack is the point where pressing once stops the process. A decision made by the rulers goes all the way through. Nowhere along the way is there a checkpoint that requires agreement from those below. How much they retain is decided for them by the small group above.

There is a finer distinction still, hidden within the craft argument.

Plato’s condition is not a matter of inward feeling. That medicine should care for the body is a publicly intelligible principle. Socrates presents it to his opponent precisely as a claim to which both can appeal. Yet the argument supplies no way to check it. A craft has its own rule, and someone who understands the craft understands that rule. As to whether a particular doctor is actually following it on a particular day, however, no second position stands there beside the doctor’s own. The condition is firm; its application rests with one side alone.

SAE’s condition is different. The thing in question must be in the other person’s hands. The person holding it is not the one exercising power. This is not because the powerful cannot be trusted. It is because a condition understood only by the party under examination amounts to that party examining itself.

Anyone who has been a parent knows this distinction well.

You want what is good for your child. That is true, and when the child is small, your judgment really is better, much better. But at a certain age, something changes: the child needs a point at which they can say no. This ability to say no does not arise because you no longer love them or because your judgment is wrong. It arises because, beyond this point, neither your love nor your judgment can do that one thing for them.

For most families, the hardest stretch comes when that point has to be handed over.

The two conditions are difficult in different ways. The direction of benefit can be stated clearly, but being clear does not mean anyone is checking, and this argument appoints no one to check. The point where pressing once stops the process is different. It does not ask what anyone has in mind. It asks whether the process contains a checkpoint that requires agreement from below. Someone outside can ask that question.

So the first condition is easier to establish and easier to distort. The second is much harder to establish, but once established, it depends far less on what someone happens to be thinking.

There is another trouble the two sides share, worth mentioning in passing.

Once exploitation has been expelled from the word ruling, there still has to be some account of what is happening to those being exploited.

Plato does not really provide one. What he separates from the craft is only the craft of earning payment. The whole business of using one’s position in charge to strip other people down never receives a name in Book I. It is driven out of the word ruling and left standing there, with nobody bothering to say what it should be called.

SAE does give it a name. It calls the fully stripped-down relation a person’s handling of a thing. But its account of power goes no further at that point either, because it has declared that it does not pass judgment.

One side has no name; the other has a name but does not speak. For the worst case, neither side has anything to say.

But how does the thing that can call a halt get into a person’s hands in the first place?

SAE has an answer here, and a firm one. It says that this thing is not given by anyone. You have it because you are a person. You do not have it because someone recognizes it. You have it first, and only then is there something for recognition to recognize. The powerful have no act of distribution to perform. They can only respect it or suppress it. And suppression cannot make it disappear. It keeps pushing upward; the person holding it down must keep exerting force. Stop, and it returns.

Yet this account leaves one candid qualification in place: it calls the thing not given only at the level where a person is treated as a whole person. Below that level, it acknowledges that the thing really is obtained in exchange for something else and really can be taken back. As to who is at which level, or how someone moves up from below, it says no more.

Look back at the parent just mentioned. For most families, the hardest stretch comes when that point has to be handed over.

Then he will ask: if you hand it over, what is that if not giving it?

When you say it is not given, you are talking about where it comes from. I will not dispute its origin. But in a household, a hospital room, or a city, it still has to wait for the upper side to loosen its grip. Whether to loosen it, and when, remain for that side to decide. You have abolished the word giving. You have not abolished the act.

And what about the space between those two levels? How does a person know which level they occupy, and whose hand moves them from the lower to the upper? You have left those two questions outside, yet they are precisely what matters. If the thing can be taken back at the lower level, how does holding it there differ from not having it?

Besides, in this comparison my condition can stand here and now. If a doctor works for the body’s good today, the craft exists today. It need not wait for someone to make a move first. Yours must wait. If that move comes, your sentence holds. If it does not, the sentence remains about the future. And someone is living through the time in between.

That question lands somewhere hard.

Finally, return to the impolite man.

After delivering his whole argument, he wants to leave. Plato writes the moment amusingly: like an attendant in a bathhouse, he has lifted a bucket of words and poured it over everyone’s ears, then made to walk away.

Those present will not let him go. They hold him there and make him stay, finish what he has to say, and make it clear.

A man has said the most unwelcome things and wants to leave, but his listeners refuse to release him. The question of power is forced into the open by someone who wants to go and cannot.
