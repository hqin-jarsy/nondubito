为什么要强迫哲人回去

Why Compel the Philosopher to Return?

Han Qin (秦汉)

洞穴讲完，苏格拉底自己解释了一遍，说教育不是把知识装进人里去，是把整个人转过来。

紧接着他说了一段很少被单独拎出来的话。

他说，那些又聪明又坏的人，你别以为他们眼力不好。他们眼力好得很，只是那点锐气被扭到了别的方向上去。他打了个比方：有些人身上像挂着铅坠子，是从小吃喝玩乐一样一样挂上去的，把他们的眼光一直往下拽。要是这些坠子从小就被敲掉，同样这几个人，眼光会朝上。

这句话很重。他说的是，看得见看不见，多半不是天分的事。

然后他把话转到自己头上。他说，那么我们这些造城的人，手上就有一件活儿要干：把最好的那些天性逼上去，逼他们一直走到看见那个善。

到这儿都还顺。逼人上坡这件事，前面已经逼过一次了。

可他接着说了半句，这半句才是这一篇的题目。

他说，等他们上去了，看够了，我们不能再由着他们。

由着他们做什么呢。由着他们待在上面不下来。

格劳孔当场就顶了回去。他问得很直：那我们岂不是在亏待他们。他们本来可以过一种更好的日子，我们偏要让他们过差的。

这一问问得好，好在它是替对方问的。格劳孔自己不是哲人，他为一群不在场的人喊了一声不公平。

苏格拉底的回答有四条，一条一条来。

第一条，法律要顾的不是哪一个等级特别好，是整座城。这句话他在讲护卫者没有家产的时候已经用过一遍了。同一句话第二次拿出来，分量却不一样：上一次那些人交出去的是钱财和自由，这一次交出去的是他刚刚看见的那样东西。

第二条最要紧，也最容易读快过去。

他说，别的城里也有哲人，那些人是自己长出来的，而且是逆着当地的政府长出来的，没人管他们。这样长起来的人不欠谁的养育。可你们不一样。你们是我们生的。

他用的词就是生。他还打了个比方，说我们把你们养出来，是要你们做这个蜂群里的头，像蜂王一样。

所以你们下去。

第三条讲本事。他说你们下去以后，看那些影子会比洞里的人看得清一万倍。你们认得出每一个影子是什么的影子，因为你们见过真的东西。

第四条讲一个更冷的道理，而且这一条不靠前面任何一条。

他说，一座城里，将要管事的那些人越是不想管，这座城就治得越好；反过来，抢着管的人越多，城就越糟。因为抢着管的都是些爱这个位子的人，爱同一个位子的人碰上了，就得打。所以要管事的位子上坐着不爱这个位子的人。

说完这一条，格劳孔的回答只有三个字：不可能。他的意思是，不可能不干，因为我们是拿公道的事去要求公道的人。苏格拉底顺着补了一句：他们每一个都会去管事，可去的样子是去做一件不得不做的事。

不图荣誉，不图别的，就是不得不。

到这里，两边是碰得上的。

那个人上去了，看见了，然后他不留在上面。这件事本身，两边都认。

SAE 这边最高的那一步不是自己再往前走一格，是给别人的路留出走得动的地方。一个人走到某一处以后能做的最要紧的事，是让后面的人走得动，而不是把自己走到的那一格收起来。

所以两边都不把上去这件事算成一份私产。上去了就得回来，回来不是慷慨，是这件事本身的下半截。

这一点比看上去要紧。一个人费那么大劲上去，见着了，然后这件事还没完，还剩下他得下来这一半。等于说，上去这件事在两边都不归他一个人。柏拉图那边它归城，这一边它归后面还没走到的那些人。归给谁不一样，可两边都没归给他自己。

而且两边都没把回来说成好事。柏拉图写得很实：去的样子是去做一件不得不做的事，不图荣誉，就是不得不。这一边也一样，那不是发善心。

还有一件事，得看着原文才发现。

上去和下来，他用的是同一个词。逼最好的那些人上去看善，是这个词；上去之后不许他们留在上面，还是这个词。他没有为下来另找一个说法，就用了上去那一个。

第一篇里留过一句话，说被迫这个词后面还会回来一次，方向正相反。它回来了，而且回来的时候连样子都没换。

然后是分开的地方，而这一处分得很干净。

分歧不在要不要回去，在怎么让他回去。

前面那次逼人上坡，那个逼是有话说的。上面确实有一样他没见过的东西；他现在看不见，正因为看不见他才不肯走。你拖他上去，拖的是他自己将来会认下来的那件事。这个结构是站得住的，第一篇里落过一次。

这一次不行。这一次那个人已经看见了。他不肯下去，不是因为他不知道下面有什么，恰恰是因为他知道。下面没有一样他还没见过的东西在等他。所以上一次那条理由，这一次一个字也用不上。

柏拉图自己清楚这一点，清楚到他换了整套说法。你去数一数他那四条：法律顾整座城，你们是我们生的，你们看得清一万倍，不想干的才该干。里头没有一条是说下面有什么好东西等着你。

四条里最硬的是第二条，而它硬在一个很朴素的地方：你是我们造的。

这条理由在中国的村子里也活了很多年。一个村里第一个考出去的人，全村凑的钱，他自己心里也有数。他该不该回去，回去到什么份上，这件事争过多少回了。而村里人给的理由，跟苏格拉底给的几乎是同一条：你是我们供出来的。

这条理由的力气不小。它不需要那个人愿意，不需要他觉得下面有什么好，甚至不需要他还认这个村子。只要他是被养出来的，这笔就算数。

也正因为这样，SAE 这边一条也搬不过来。

第二条搬不过来，因为这一边不认那个前提。一个人不是任何人造出来的东西，也不是哪一座城造出来的。前提不认，从前提上长出来的那份亏欠也就跟着没了。

第三条搬不过来。看得清一万倍，说的是他有这个本事；有本事和该去做，中间还差一步，而这一步柏拉图是靠前两条垫上的。

第四条更搬不过来。不想干的才该干，这句话要成立，得先有一个位子，还得有一套能把人安上去的办法。

第一条也搬不过来。整座城比一个等级要紧，这句话的分量全在把人算进城里；这一边不这么算。

数下来一条不剩。剩的只有一句：他自己转过身来。

这件事在日常里是有的，而且不算少见。

会一样东西的人肯教。教跟做不一样：做，你自己会就够了；教，你得把当年自己怎么绕过那些弯路重新想一遍，还得忍着看别人在你早就过去的地方卡住。费的劲不比自己干少，落到手里的东西少得多。

可一个行当就是这么传下来的。而且没有一条规矩管得住它。你没法立一条法说会的人必须教，立了也没用，因为逼出来的教是教不成的，一逼就变成走过场。

而柏拉图那四条绳子，要拴的正是这样一件事。

他会把那四条一条一条数给你听，然后问你。

你说不能逼。好，不逼。可我那四条，你一条也不要，你手上还剩什么。剩一句他自己转过身来。那我问你：他为什么会转过身来。

再往里一层。你说这件事只能在人跟人之间发生，得有人先认下另一个人是个人，路才走得动。

那么在一个还没有人肯这么认的地方，头一个这么认的人是从哪儿来的。他认的时候，没有人认过他。他是跟谁学的。

你别拿他自己会转过身来搪塞我。我问的正是这个转。

我这边好歹造了一座城，城里有人从头教他，教完他还欠着这座城。你连城都不肯要。

这一问就搁在这里。

最后回到那个下来的人。

他从亮的地方进到暗的地方，眼睛里灌满了黑。洞里的人看着他磕磕绊绊的样子，笑了。在这些影子上头，他刚从真东西那儿回来，本该是全场最认得的一个；可就在这一刻，他是全场最看不见的一个。

眼睛要过一阵才缓过来。柏拉图特意写了这一句：得让他们习惯了才行。

他没有说这一阵有多久，也没有说这中间那个人是什么滋味。他只说，下去。

After telling the cave story, Socrates explains it himself. Education, he says, does not put knowledge into a person. It turns the whole person around.

Immediately afterward comes a passage rarely singled out on its own.

Do not suppose, he says, that clever but wicked people have poor sight. Their sight is excellent. Its sharpness has simply been twisted in another direction. He offers an image: some people seem to have lead weights hanging from them, attached one by one from childhood through eating, drinking, and pleasure, continually dragging their gaze downward. If those weights had been knocked off when they were young, those very same people would look upward.

This is a weighty sentence. He is saying that whether people can see is usually not a matter of talent.

Then he turns the argument toward himself. We who are building this city, he says, therefore have a task: to compel the best natures upward, to make them go all the way until they see the Good.

So far, the argument runs smoothly. People have already been forced uphill once.

But he adds half a sentence, and that half-sentence is the subject of this essay.

Once they have gone up and seen enough, he says, we can no longer let them do as they please.

Let them do what? Stay above and refuse to come down.

Glaucon objects at once. His question is direct: would that not be treating them unjustly? They could live a better life, and we insist on making them live a worse one.

It is a good question because it is asked on someone else’s behalf. Glaucon is not himself a philosopher. He cries unfairness for a group who are not there.

Socrates gives four answers. Take them one by one.

First, the law must care for the whole city, not the particular advantage of one class. He has already used this sentence when explaining why the guardians have no property. The same sentence appears a second time, but its weight is different. Before, those people surrendered wealth and freedom. This time, they surrender what they have just seen.

The second is the most important, and the easiest to read too quickly.

Other cities have philosophers too, he says. Those people grow up on their own, against their local governments, with nobody caring for them. People who grow that way owe nobody a debt for raising them. But you are different. We gave birth to you.

Birth is the word he uses. He also offers an image: we raised you to be leaders in this hive, like queen bees.

So go down.

The third answer concerns ability. Once you go down, he says, you will see the shadows ten thousand times more clearly than the people in the cave. You will recognize what each shadow is a shadow of, because you have seen the real things.

The fourth offers a colder argument, and it does not depend on any of the preceding three.

The less the people destined to govern a city want to govern, he says, the better the city will be governed. Conversely, the more people compete to govern, the worse it will be. Those who compete love the position, and when people who love the same position meet, they will fight. So the seat of government must be occupied by someone who does not love it.

After this, Glaucon’s answer is just one word: impossible. He means that refusal is impossible, because we are asking just things of just people. Socrates adds: each will govern, but will go to it as something they must do.

Not for honor or anything else. Simply because they must.

Here the two sides can meet.

The person has gone up and seen, and then does not remain above. Both sides accept the act itself.

For SAE, the highest step is not another step forward for oneself. It is leaving room for another person’s path to move. Once someone has reached a certain place, the most important thing they can do is make it possible for those behind to move, rather than keep the place they have reached to themselves.

So neither side treats ascent as private property. Going up requires coming back. Return is not generosity. It is the second half of the undertaking itself.

This matters more than it first appears. Someone makes an enormous effort to go up, sees, and still the undertaking is unfinished. The half in which they must come down remains. In other words, ascent belongs to more than the person who ascends on both accounts. For Plato, it belongs to the city. On this side, it belongs to those behind who have not yet arrived. The recipient differs, but neither side assigns it to the ascending person alone.

Nor does either side describe returning as a good deed. Plato is concrete: they go as to something they must do, not for honor, but simply out of necessity. This side says the same. It is no act of charitable feeling.

There is something else that becomes apparent only by looking at the text.

He uses the same word for going up and coming down. Compelling the best people upward to see the Good takes that word. Refusing to let them remain above once they have ascended takes the same word. He does not find a different expression for descent. He uses the one he used for ascent.

The first essay left a sentence waiting: the word forced would return, in exactly the opposite direction. It has returned, without even changing its form.

Then comes the separation, and here it is a clean one.

The disagreement is not about whether the person should return, but about how to make the person return.

The earlier compulsion uphill had something to be said for it. There really was something above that the person had never seen. They could not see it then, and precisely because they could not see it, they refused to go. In dragging them up, you were dragging them toward something they would later recognize for themselves. That structure holds, as the first essay established.

It does not hold this time. This person has already seen. They refuse to descend not because they do not know what is below, but precisely because they do. Nothing they have never seen is waiting for them there. Not one word of the earlier reason can therefore be used this time.

Plato knows this himself, so clearly that he replaces the entire argument. Count his four reasons: the law cares for the whole city; we gave birth to you; you see ten thousand times more clearly; those who do not want to govern are the ones who should. Not one says that something good is waiting for you below.

Of the four, the second is the firmest, and its firmness rests on something very plain: we made you.

This reason has lived for many years in Chinese villages too. The first person from a village to pass the examinations and leave has been supported by money the whole village contributed, and knows it. How many times have people argued over whether that person should return, and how much returning is owed? The villagers’ reason is almost the same as Socrates’: we paid your way.

The reason has considerable force. It does not require the person’s willingness, a belief that anything good awaits below, or even a continuing acknowledgment of the village as their own. So long as the person was raised and supported, the account stands.

And precisely for that reason, SAE cannot take over a single one of the four.

It cannot take over the second, because this side does not accept the premise. A person is not something made by anyone else, or by any city. Once the premise is rejected, the debt that grows from it disappears with it.

It cannot take over the third. Seeing ten thousand times more clearly establishes an ability. Between having an ability and being obliged to use it, a step is missing. Plato bridges that step with his first two reasons.

Still less can it take over the fourth. For “those who do not want to govern are the ones who should” to hold, there must already be a position and a procedure capable of placing someone in it.

The first cannot be taken over either. The claim that the whole city matters more than one class derives all its weight from counting people as part of the city. This side does not count them that way.

Count through them, and not one remains. Only a sentence is left: the person turns around of their own accord.

This does happen in everyday life, and it is not especially rare.

Someone who knows a skill is willing to teach. Teaching is different from doing. To do it, knowing how is enough. To teach it, you must think back through how you once found your way around those difficulties. You must also bear watching others get stuck where you passed through long ago. It takes no less effort than doing the work yourself and leaves much less in your hands.

Yet this is how a craft is passed down. And no rule can secure it. You cannot legislate that everyone who knows must teach. Even if you did, it would be useless, because teaching forced out of someone does not succeed as teaching. Once compelled, it becomes going through the motions.

And this is precisely the kind of thing Plato’s four ropes are meant to bind.

He will count out the four, one by one, and then ask you.

You say it cannot be compelled. Very well, no compulsion. But you reject every one of my four reasons. What is left in your hands? A sentence: the person turns around of their own accord. Then let me ask: why would they turn?

Go one level deeper. You say this can happen only between people. Someone must first recognize another person as a person before the path can move forward.

Then in a place where nobody is yet willing to recognize anyone in this way, where does the first person who does so come from? When that person recognizes another, nobody has recognized them. From whom did they learn?

Do not put me off by saying that they will turn around of their own accord. It is precisely that turn I am asking about.

On my side, at least I have built a city. Someone there teaches the person from the start, and when the teaching is finished, the person still owes the city. You will not even accept the city.

The question is left here.

Finally, return to the person coming down.

He passes from light into darkness, his eyes flooded with black. The people in the cave watch him stumble and laugh. He has just returned from the real things and ought to recognize these shadows better than anyone present. Yet at this very moment, he sees less than anyone present.

His eyes need time to recover. Plato takes care to write this sentence: they must be allowed to become accustomed.

He does not say how long that takes, or what it feels like for the person in the meantime. He says only: go down.
