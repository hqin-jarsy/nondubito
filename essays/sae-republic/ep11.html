退化不是堕落，是复利

Decline Is Not Moral Decay but Compound Interest

Han Qin (秦汉)

城造好了，正义也找到了，按理这本书可以收了。

苏格拉底偏不。他说，好城只有一种，坏城有四种，我们把那四种也演一遍。

于是从卷八起，他做了一件很少有人做的事：他让自己刚造好的那座城，一级一级地坏下去。

坏的第一步，多数人记不住，因为它跟大家预期的不一样。

他说，凡是生出来的东西总要散，就算你这座城也一样。那么它从哪儿开始散呢。

不是从有人贪，不是从有人懒。是从一次算错。

他说，生育这件事有它的时候，时候合不合有一个数管着。而这个数，凭管事那些人全部的智慧和全部的教育，也算不到。他把话说得很死：这件事会从他们眼皮底下溜过去。

算错了会怎么样呢。婚配就配在了不该配的时候，生下来的孩子就不那么好。这一代护卫者比上一代差一点，差得还看不出来。再往后，他们开始不那么把音乐当回事了。

于是有一天，城里的金银和铜铁开始往两边拽。铜铁那边想着挣钱置产，金银那边还想着从前那一套。两边拽不过，就折中了。

折中的结果是这样：把地和房子分了，各归各家；而原先被他们当作自由人和朋友护着的那些人，从此成了他们手底下的仆役。

这一句要慢慢读。同一批人，同一座城，中间没有谁作恶，也没有一场政变。只是两拨人各让一步，谈成了一个都能接受的方案。而方案落定的那一刻，一批自由人变成了仆役。

后面几级他演得同样细。

金银那一套还剩个荣誉，管事的人爱面子，爱打仗。可家里的钱越积越多，攒钱这件事越来越像正事。他说，私人手里的金子攒起来，就是这一级完蛋的原因。

然后来了一条法律。这条法律定一个财产的数目，够这个数的才能做官，不够的靠边站。

一条法律。就这么一条。

他接着说了一句极冷的话：这样的城已经不是一座城了，是两座，一座穷人的，一座富人的，住在同一块地方，日日夜夜互相算计。

再往下，穷人翻身。翻身的由头也很实在：打仗的时候站在一块儿了。一个晒得黑瘦的穷人，旁边站着一个白白胖胖没吃过苦的富人，喘得比谁都急。穷人回来一想，这帮人也没什么了不起。

于是民主来了。

这一段他写得最长，也最热闹，而且读起来他自己也知道它热闹。

他说，这是最好看的一种政体。像一件花衣裳，什么颜色都有，谁看了都觉得漂亮。他还说，这地方像一间铺子，各种政体的样子都摆在那儿，你想要哪一种，进去挑就是了。

官是抽签抽出来的。人人有说话的份，人人爱怎么活怎么活。你有本事管事，没人逼你去管；你不想被人管，也没人非管你不可；打仗的时候别人都上，你不想上，你就不上。

他一句好话也没落下，可他说这些的时候，语气是笑着的。

然后他下刀。

他说，毁掉民主的，正是它最看重的那一样：自由。人要自由要到没够的时候，别的什么都顾不上了。

他把这个过程写得很具体，具体到今天读还觉得眼熟。

当爹的开始学着跟儿子平起平坐，还有点怕他。当老师的怕学生，见了学生要陪着笑。年纪大的人怕被人说古板，就学着年轻人说话行事。

他还说了一句，说完自己也乐了：到了这地步，连买来的驴和马都在街上大摇大摆地走，谁挡道就往谁身上撞。

然后是那一句最狠的：自由过了头，换来的多半就是奴役过了头。这句话他说的是城，也说的是人。

再往下就是僭主。一个替穷人出头的人站出来，护着大家，很得人心。他打了个比方，说阿卡狄亚有座庙，人说凡是尝过一口混在祭品里的人肉的，就非变成狼不可。护着大家的那个人也一样：他手上一沾同城人的血，这条路就回不去了。

最后一句他写得像叹气。他说，这些人本来是想躲开一点烟的，那点烟是给自由人当下手；躲着躲着，掉进了火里，那火是给奴才当奴才。

到这里，两边碰上了，而且碰得很实。

他没有把这一路下滑写成一连串意外。每一级他都给了一个说得出的由头：一次算错，一次折中，一条法律，一场仗里看见的事，一次要自由要过了头。这是一条机制，不是一串故事。

而更要紧的是那第一跌。

他大可以写成护卫者变坏了。他没有。他写的是他们算错了一个数，而且他明说，凭他们全部的智慧和教育也算不到。

下滑的第一步，在他自己笔下，是一次知道不了，不是一次不肯好。

然后是分开的地方，而分开的地方在读法上。

他的机制给得很足，可他每一级都配了一个人：爱面子的人，爱钱的人，什么都想要一点的人，被欲望管着的人。城的每一级配一种人的每一级，配得严丝合缝。

配上以后，整条路就有了一个总的说法：一代不如一代。

而这个总的说法，会把他自己给的那些机制盖住。你记住的是人心不古，记不住的是那一次算错和那一条法律。

这一边的读法是另一样。

不是人变坏了。是每一级都比上一级少一道复核。

上一级没有对完的那一笔，下一级接手的时候，是当作已经对完了接的。它不再被拿出来看，因为它已经是地面了。于是下一级的人做事，脚下比上一级多了一层没有查过的地面；他们在这层地面上做的事，又成了再下一级的地面。

所以往下走这件事不是每一级各坏一次，是每一级都省掉一道手续，而省掉的那些是叠着算的。

那次折中就是最好的例子。分地分房，把原先护着的人变成仆役，这件事当时是有说法的：两边都退了一步，城保住了。说法在那一刻是成立的。可它从此没有再被人问过一次。到了下一级，地是私产这件事已经不用讲了，它是地面。到了再下一级，连穷人富人分成两座城都不用讲了。

一代不如一代这句话，说的是人；每一级少一道复核这句话，说的是手续。前一句让人叹气，后一句让人知道该看哪儿。

在自家过日子的人对这个分别熟。

一个屋子不是哪一天忽然乱掉的。是有一回东西没归位，你太累了，说明天再说。第二回就顺手多了，因为已经有一样东西在那儿了，多一样看不出来。等到你有一天真想收拾，你会发现最难的不是活多，是你已经不知道哪一样是该在那儿的，哪一样是那天没归位的。

乱的不是屋子。是你已经分不出地面和堆在地面上的东西了。

按这个读法回头看民主那一段，它的位置就变了。

在他那儿民主排倒数第二，紧挨着僭主，这个排法是他的判决。可换到这个读法上，民主是五种里唯一一种把复核写进制度的。抽签，说话，谁都能问一句凭什么，这些不是它的装饰，是它那道手续本身。

它的病和它的药是同一样东西。它容得下所有人来问，所以它也容得下有人把这件事做成表演，做成买卖，做到没有人再当真。而复核这道手续一旦只剩样子，剩下的就只是一间什么都摆着的铺子，谁进来都能拿走点什么。

柏拉图看见的那个滑坡是真的。只是滑的不是人心。滑的是那道手续被当真的程度。

所以两边开的方子也不一样。

他要的是一部不再改的宪法，加上一批从小养出来的人，养到会照着它办。他要把那道手续钉死。

这边要的是另一样：一个不休庭的法庭。不是一部不许动的法，是没有哪一笔可以被当作已经结清了。

而正是在这儿，他会问回来。

法庭是要有人开的。

他会说：你那个不休庭的法庭，谁来开庭，谁来传人，谁来管那些不肯来的。一座城正是在它最不想再被查的时候，最需要有人把庭开起来；而那个时候，凭什么会有人去开。

我那边至少有两样东西：一部写死的法，还有一批被养到会照着它办的人。你笑我把人养成器官，好，那你告诉我，你那个法庭靠什么开下去。靠每个人自己想着要开。

那不是制度，那是运气。

而且我数给你听：你自己那条路上，每一级少的都是同一道手续。也就是说，最先松掉的就是它。等到该开庭的时候，最先没了的正是开庭这件事。你那个方子，是在它最不可能有的地方要它。

这一问落在很实的地方。

最后回到那点烟。

他说，这些人本来只是想躲开一点烟。烟不是火，烟只是呛，只是不舒服，只是让人觉得这日子过得憋屈。

没有人是为了跳进火里才动身的。他们只是想往没那么呛的地方挪一挪。

而他那一整套五级，从头到尾，也没有一处是有人打算要坏。

The city has been built and justice found. In principle, the book could close.

Socrates refuses. There is only one kind of good city, he says, but four bad kinds. Let us act out those four as well.

So, beginning in Book VIII, he does something few people do: he lets the city he has just built deteriorate, one level at a time.

Most people do not remember the first step, because it is not what they expect.

Everything that comes into being must eventually come apart, he says. Even your city. Where, then, does its dissolution begin?

Not with somebody becoming greedy or lazy. With a miscalculation.

Reproduction, he says, has its proper times, and a number governs whether the timing is right. Yet the rulers, with all their wisdom and education, cannot work this number out. He is categorical: it will slip past them under their very eyes.

What happens when they calculate wrongly? Marriages take place at the wrong times, and the children born are not as good. This generation of guardians is a little worse than the last, at first too little to notice. Later they begin to take music less seriously.

One day, the city’s gold and silver start pulling one way, its bronze and iron the other. The bronze and iron want money and property. The gold and silver still want the old arrangement. Neither can pull the other over, so they compromise.

The compromise divides land and houses into private holdings. Those they formerly protected as free people and friends become servants under their hands.

Read that sentence slowly. The same people, the same city, with no one committing an evil act in between and no coup. Two groups merely yield a little and reach a plan both can accept. The moment the plan is settled, a group of free people become servants.

He stages the later levels with equal care.

The gold-and-silver arrangement retains a remnant of honor. The rulers care about prestige and love war. Yet money accumulates in their homes, and saving money increasingly looks like serious business. The accumulation of privately held gold, he says, is what destroys this level.

Then comes a law. It fixes a property threshold: those who meet it may hold office; those who do not must stand aside.

One law. Just one.

Then he says something chilling. Such a city is no longer one city but two, one of the poor and one of the rich, occupying the same place and plotting against each other day and night.

Further down, the poor rise. Their reason is concrete too: they have stood together in battle. A lean, sunburned poor man stands beside a pale, plump rich man unused to hardship and panting harder than anyone. Back home, the poor man thinks: there is nothing so special about these people.

So democracy arrives.

He writes at greatest length and with greatest liveliness here, and as you read you can tell he knows how lively it is.

It is the most beautiful constitution, he says, like a multicolored garment whose every color catches the eye. He also compares it to a shop displaying every kind of constitution. Whatever kind you want, you can go in and choose.

Offices are filled by lot. Everyone has a voice; everyone lives as they please. If you are capable of governing, nobody forces you to govern. If you do not want to be governed, nobody insists on governing you. When everyone else goes to war, you can decline to go.

He leaves out none of its attractions, but says all this with a smile in his voice.

Then he makes the cut.

What destroys democracy, he says, is precisely what it values most: freedom. Once people cannot get enough of it, nothing else receives attention.

He describes the process so concretely that it still looks familiar today.

Fathers learn to meet their sons as equals and even fear them a little. Teachers fear students and greet them with ingratiating smiles. Older people, afraid of seeming old-fashioned, imitate the speech and conduct of the young.

He adds a remark that amuses even him: by this point, even purchased donkeys and horses swagger through the streets and charge into anyone who blocks their path.

Then comes the harshest sentence: an excess of freedom tends to bring an excess of slavery. He says this of cities and of people.

Next comes the tyrant. Someone stands up for the poor, protects everyone, and becomes popular. Socrates offers a comparison: at a temple in Arcadia, people say that anyone who tastes human flesh mixed into the sacrificial offerings must become a wolf. The people’s protector is the same. Once his hands are stained with a fellow citizen’s blood, there is no turning back.

The last sentence sounds like a sigh. These people originally wanted to escape a little smoke, the smoke of serving free people. In escaping, they fell into the fire: being slaves to slaves.

Here the two sides meet, and meet firmly.

He has not made the decline a sequence of accidents. Every level has an identifiable cause: a miscalculation, a compromise, a law, something seen in battle, a demand for freedom taken too far. This is a mechanism, not a string of stories.

More important still is the first fall.

He could easily have written that the guardians became bad. He did not. He wrote that they miscalculated a number, and explicitly said that all their wisdom and education could not make the calculation possible.

In his own account, the first step downward is a failure to be able to know, not a refusal to be good.

Then comes the separation, and it lies in how the sequence is read.

His mechanisms are ample, but he also pairs each level with a person: one who loves honor, one who loves money, one who wants a little of everything, one ruled by desire. Each level of the city corresponds exactly to a level of human character.

Once paired, the whole road acquires a general description: each generation is worse than the last.

That general description can conceal the mechanisms he himself supplied. What you remember is that people are no longer what they were. What you forget is the miscalculation and the law.

This side reads it differently.

It is not that people have become bad. Each level has one fewer check than the level before.

An account left unverified at one level is taken over at the next as though verification were complete. It is no longer brought out for inspection because it has become the ground. The next level acts on one more layer of unexamined ground than the previous one, and what it does there becomes ground for the level after that.

So descending does not mean each level separately goes bad. It means each level dispenses with a procedure, and those omissions compound.

The compromise is the clearest example. Dividing land and houses and turning the formerly protected into servants had a justification at the time: both sides yielded and the city survived. At that moment the account held. But it was never questioned again. At the next level, land being private property needed no explanation. It was the ground. At the level after that, even the separation into a city of the poor and a city of the rich needed no explanation.

“Each generation is worse than the last” is a statement about people. “Each level has one fewer check” is a statement about procedure. The first makes you sigh. The second tells you where to look.

Anyone who keeps a household knows the difference.

A room does not become untidy all at once. Once, something was not put away. You were too tired and said: tomorrow. The next time was easier, because one thing was already there and another would scarcely show. When you finally decide to tidy up, the hardest part is not the amount of work. It is that you no longer know which things belong there and which were simply not put away that day.

It is not the room that is confused. You can no longer distinguish the floor from what has been piled on it.

Read the passage on democracy this way, and its position changes.

For him, democracy ranks second from the bottom, next to tyranny. That ranking is his verdict. In this reading, however, democracy is the only one of the five that writes reexamination into its institutions. Drawing lots, speaking, anyone being able to ask “On what grounds?”: these are not its decorations. They are the procedure itself.

Its disease and its medicine are the same thing. Because it permits everyone to question, it also permits people to turn questioning into performance and commerce until nobody takes it seriously. Once the procedure of reexamination is only an appearance, what remains is a shop displaying everything, where anyone can come in and carry something away.

The slide Plato sees is real. Only, what slides is not the human heart. It is how seriously that procedure is taken.

So the two sides prescribe different remedies.

He wants a constitution that will no longer change and people raised from childhood to follow it. He wants to nail the procedure down.

This side wants something else: a court that never adjourns. Not an untouchable law, but no account that may be treated as finally settled.

And precisely here he will question it in return.

Someone has to open the court.

He would say: in your court that never adjourns, who convenes the hearing, who summons people, and who deals with those who refuse to come? A city most needs someone to open the court precisely when it least wants to be examined again. Why would anyone open it at that moment?

On my side there are at least two things: a fixed law and people trained to follow it. You mock me for turning people into organs. Very well. Tell me what keeps your court running. Everyone remembering on their own that they want to open it?

That is not an institution. It is luck.

And let me count it out for you. On your own account, what each level loses is the very same procedure. In other words, it is the first thing to loosen. When the court needs to open, opening the court is exactly what has disappeared first. Your remedy demands it precisely where it is least likely to be found.

The question lands somewhere solid.

Finally, return to that little smoke.

These people, he says, originally wanted only to escape a little smoke. Smoke is not fire. It merely chokes, discomforts, makes life feel constricted.

Nobody set out in order to jump into the fire. They merely wanted to move somewhere less choking.

And throughout his entire sequence of five levels, nobody at any point intended things to go bad.
