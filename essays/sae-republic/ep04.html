Essay 04 of 13

隐身的人不被追问

The Invisible Person Is Not Questioned

Han Qin (秦汉)

卷一收尾的时候，苏格拉底把色拉叙马霍斯驳退了，自己也觉得可以散了。卷二一开头，格劳孔把他按了回去。

格劳孔做的事很少有人肯做。他说，你驳倒的那个人说得不好，我来替他说一遍，说到最强。他还先声明，这些话不是我信的，我恰恰想听你把正义夸一夸，所以我得先把对面那一套摆到它最硬的样子，不然你的回答不算数。

一个人替他不同意的立场出全力，这在两千四百年里也不常见。这一篇要讲的东西，就是他出全力做出来的。

他先讲正义是从哪儿来的。他说，做坏事而不受报，这是最好的；挨了坏事又还不了手，这是最坏的。人受够了后一种，又摸不着前一种，就彼此讲和，说好谁也别做谁也别挨，把这份约定写下来叫作法，管它叫正义。所以正义是一个折中，是够不着最好又躲不开最坏的人才要的东西。谁也不是心甘情愿守它，是没有本事不守。

这个说法要成立，得有一个前提：只要有本事不守，人就不守了。

于是他讲了那个戒指的故事，用来把这个前提立稳。

故事里的人是个牧羊人，替吕底亚的国王放羊。后来那枚戒指有了名字，人们叫它吉格斯的戒指，柏拉图写的其实是吉格斯的一位祖上，不过这不要紧。

有一天下大雨，又地震，他放羊的地方裂开一道口子。他下去看，看见一匹空心的铜马，马身上开着门；门里躺着一具尸首，个头比人大，身上什么也没有，只有一枚金戒指。他把戒指摘下来，上去了。

后来牧羊人按月聚会，向国王报羊的事。他坐在那里，手里无意识地转那枚戒指，把戒面转向了掌心。就在那一下，旁边的人开始当着他的面说他，好像他不在。他吓了一跳，把戒面转回去，人又出现了。他反复试了几次，确认了：转向里面就看不见，转向外面就看得见。

接下来的事很短。他想办法进了去见国王的队伍，勾了王后，跟她合谋杀了国王，坐了那个位子。

讲完他就下推论。他说，假如有两枚这样的戒指，一枚给正义的人戴，一枚给不正义的人戴，没有哪个人硬得住。他会从市场上随手拿东西，进任何一户人家，想跟谁睡跟谁睡，想杀谁杀谁，想放谁放谁，在人间像个神一样行事。两个人会走成同一个样子。所以，没有人是自愿正义的，都是被逼的。

他还嫌不够狠，又加了一层。他说，要比就比干净的。把正义的那个人身上所有的好名声都剥掉，让他一辈子被人当作最坏的人，挨鞭子，上刑，眼睛被烧瞎，最后被钉桩处死；另一边那个不正义的人则一辈子名声极好，有口才，有朋友，有钱，谁也想不到他。现在你再来说，哪一个过得好。

格劳孔说完，他弟弟阿德曼图斯接着补了一刀，补得同样狠。他说，你听听大人从小是怎么教孩子的。他们叫孩子要正义，可给的理由从来是正义带来的那些东西：名声好，神喜欢，办事顺，儿孙有福。没有哪一个大人跟孩子说，这件事本身就值得做。孩子听着听着，学会的是另一件事，真正要紧的不是正义，是被人看成正义。

兄弟俩合起来把话说完了。哥哥说，把看着的人拿走，正义就没了。弟弟说，我们从小学的本来就是怎么伺候那些看着的人。

这个挑战之所以难缠，不在它狠，在它干净。他把别的变量全按住了，只动一样：有没有人看着。

到这里，SAE 这边不接他那个题目。

他问的是正义本身好不好。这一篇要问的是另一件事：那枚戒指到底取消了什么。

常见的读法说，取消的是惩罚。这个读法不算错，可它太浅。惩罚只是后果里的一种，而且是最粗的一种。戒指取消的东西比惩罚早得多，也小得多。

它取消的是被追问。

一个人做一件事，心里总要给自己一个说法。这个说法说得通说不通，往往不取决于它本身，取决于有没有人对着它说一句你凭什么。有人问，说法就得站住；站不住，你要么补，要么承认这里欠着。这件事很日常，日常到不像一件事。

没有人问的时候，说法不会当场垮。它会沉下去。

沉下去这个词是照字面用的。就像一件东西放在桌上，天天看得见；哪天你把它塞进抽屉，它还在，只是从此不在你眼前了，你做别的事的时候不会再绕开它。

沉下去以后，它就不再是一个说法了，它成了下一件事的地面。下一次你不必再想理由，因为上一次那个已经过去了，而且过得挺顺。第二笔比第一笔容易，第三笔连想都不用想。

所以真正在滚的不是坏事的件数，是门槛。每一笔没有被结过的账，都把下一笔的门槛往下压一格。所以这里说的滚，不是件数翻倍，是一次比一次省事。

还有一层容易看反。往下掉的那个过程，摸上去不像欠债，像长本事。你处理得越来越利落，犹豫越来越少，别人还在那里纠结的事你一步就过去了。这个感觉不是假的，所以人不会把它当成滑坡，会把它当成成熟。它不但看不见，还挺受用。

开车的人都懂。那个没有人的路口，第一次闯灯，心里咯噔一下；第二次就顺手了；到第三次，它已经不算一件事了。变的不是那个路口，是你不再问自己了。

而最难办的一条在这里：这个过程，本人不容易看见。

戒指没有拿走一个人自问的能力。他照样可以问自己。可他问的时候，手上只有自己那一套，而门槛正是用那一套量的。自己回头看，看见的是一串顺理成章的往事，一件比一件顺，怎么看怎么没问题。顺，恰恰是它在往下掉的样子。

所以自问不是不管用，是它担保不了。担保不了的那一块，得有人从外面碰一下才现形，而戒指拿走的正是外面那个人。

这一段听着像在讲道德，其实不是。同一件事在别处一样发生，跟好坏没有关系。

一个人自己在家练一门手艺，没有师傅，也没有同行看。头几个月长得很快，后来就停在一个地方，而且自己觉得挺好。停下来的不是他的手。是没有人再对他说这一处不对。他所有的毛病都被自己越练越熟地重复着，越熟越深，最后连改都改不动了，因为那已经是他的手感。他不是不努力，他是一直在练，只是练的东西没有人碰过。

戴上戒指的人，就是那个再也没有师傅的人。

这里要说清楚一件事，免得读歪。上面这些话是账单，不是命令。

它说的是，一个人停止被追问之后，他手上那本账会照着这个样子走。它没有说你应当去被追问。当不当接受追问，是各人给自己立的法，不是这里能替谁立的。有人愿意付这个价，那是他的事，账单如实开出来就够了。

可是开账单的人，总得说得出这笔账在哪儿。

他要求把外表剥干净了再比。好，剥干净。柏拉图没有写那个牧羊人后来过得怎么样，也不必写，因为他的论证本来就不靠结局：只要没有人看得见，正义的人和不正义的人会走成同一个样子。那就设这么一个人，一辈子没有被谁问过一次，自己从头到尾觉得挺好。

于是他会问：你说这个人的账在烂。烂在哪里，指给我看。他自己感觉不到，你也说他感觉不到；旁人看不见，因为没有人看得见他。那么这个在烂，是谁看见的。

他还有第二句，更难躲。你这套话对那个牧羊人一句也用不上，他听不见，也不会来听。可对一个本来就肯让人问的人，你根本不必说，他自己每天都在做。也就是说，它派得上用场的地方，恰好是最不需要它的地方。

第三句。一笔没有债主，没有到期日，当事人也感觉不到的债，和一句神会看着，究竟差在哪里。你连债主都不给，还说账在滚。

这一句他问得起。他自己到全书末尾是给了一个债主的，那是另一篇的事。

这一问不轻。它问的不是这套说法对不对，是这套说法在他描述的那个人身上，有没有一处落得下来。

最后再回到那个动作本身。

牧羊人是无意中转的。他坐在一群同行中间，手里闲着，把戒面转向了掌心。就这么一下。

那个动作小得几乎不算一个动作。没有决心，没有盘算，甚至没有留意。可是从那一下起，他做的每一件事都不再有人对着说一句你凭什么，而他自己也就一路顺到了王座上。

真正要命的东西，往往就是这个尺寸。不是有人递给你一枚戒指，是你某天顺手把手转了一下，从此没有人再问你。

At the end of Book I, Socrates has refuted Thrasymachus and thinks they can go home. At the opening of Book II, Glaucon makes him sit back down.

Glaucon does something few people are willing to do. The person you defeated, he says, did not put his case well. I will put it for him, at its strongest. First he makes a disclaimer: these are not my beliefs. Precisely because I want to hear you praise justice, I must first put the other side in its strongest form. Otherwise your answer will not count.

A person putting all their strength behind a position they disagree with has not been a common sight in twenty-four centuries. What this essay concerns is what he produced by doing so.

He begins with the origin of justice. Doing wrong without suffering for it, he says, is best. Suffering wrong without being able to retaliate is worst. People have had enough of the latter but cannot reach the former, so they make peace with one another. They agree that nobody will do wrong and nobody will suffer it. They write the agreement down as law and call it justice. Justice is thus a compromise, something wanted by those who cannot reach the best or escape the worst. Nobody willingly observes it. They lack the power not to.

For this account to work, one premise must hold: whenever people have the power not to comply, they will not comply.

So he tells the story of the ring to establish that premise.

The person in the story is a shepherd tending the king of Lydia’s sheep. The ring later acquired a name: the Ring of Gyges. Plato actually writes of an ancestor of Gyges, but that does not matter here.

One day, heavy rain and an earthquake open a chasm where he grazes his sheep. He climbs down and finds a hollow bronze horse with doors in its sides. Inside lies a corpse larger than a man, with nothing on it but a gold ring. He removes the ring and climbs out.

Later the shepherds gather for their monthly report to the king about the flocks. Sitting among them, he absentmindedly turns the ring, bringing its setting toward his palm. At that instant, the people beside him begin talking about him in front of his face as though he were absent. Startled, he turns the setting back, and reappears. After several trials he is certain: inward makes him invisible; outward makes him visible.

What follows is brief. He gets himself included in the delegation to the king, seduces the queen, conspires with her to kill the king, and takes his place.

The story finished, Glaucon draws the inference. Suppose there were two such rings, one worn by a just person and one by an unjust person. Nobody could hold firm. The wearer would take whatever they pleased from the market, enter any home, sleep with anyone they wished, kill or release whomever they wanted, and act like a god among human beings. The two people would end up behaving alike. Therefore nobody is just voluntarily. Everyone is forced.

He thinks even this is not harsh enough and adds another layer. If we are to compare, he says, make it a clean comparison. Strip the just person of every good reputation. Let everyone regard him as the worst of men throughout his life. Let him be whipped, tortured, have his eyes burned out, and finally be executed on a stake. Meanwhile, let the unjust person enjoy a superb reputation all his life, with eloquence, friends, money, and no suspicion falling on him. Now tell me which one lives well.

When Glaucon finishes, his brother Adeimantus adds a cut just as sharp. Listen, he says, to how adults teach children from an early age. They tell them to be just, but their reasons are always the things justice brings: a good name, favor with the gods, smooth dealings, blessings for their descendants. No adult tells a child that the act itself is worth doing. As children listen, they learn something else: what really matters is not justice but being seen as just.

Between them, the brothers complete the case. The elder says: remove those watching and justice disappears. The younger says: what we have learned since childhood is how to cater to those watching.

The challenge is difficult not because it is harsh but because it is clean. He holds all other variables still and changes only one: whether somebody is watching.

Here SAE does not take up his question.

He asks whether justice is good in itself. This essay asks something else: what, exactly, does the ring remove?

The usual reading says punishment. That is not wrong, but it is too shallow. Punishment is only one consequence, and the crudest. What the ring removes comes much earlier and is much smaller.

It removes being questioned.

When people act, they always give themselves some account of what they are doing. Whether that account holds up often depends less on the account itself than on whether someone faces it and asks: what gives you grounds for that? Once someone asks, the account must stand. If it cannot, you must either supply what is missing or acknowledge the debt. This is so ordinary that it hardly seems to be an event.

When nobody asks, the account does not immediately collapse. It sinks.

“Sink” is meant literally. An object lies on your desk, visible every day. One day you push it into a drawer. It is still there, but no longer before your eyes. You no longer have to work around it as you do other things.

Once it has sunk, it is no longer an account. It becomes the ground for the next action. Next time, you need not think through the reason again. The previous occasion has passed, and passed quite smoothly. The second entry is easier than the first. By the third, no thought is needed.

So what is really rolling onward is not the number of bad acts but the threshold. Every unsettled account presses the threshold for the next one a notch lower. “Rolling” here does not mean the number of acts doubles. It means each becomes less trouble than the last.

There is another layer that is easy to read backward. The descent does not feel like running into debt. It feels like gaining competence. You handle things more efficiently, hesitate less, and step straight past matters that still trouble others. The feeling is real, so you do not read it as a slide. You read it as maturity. It is not merely invisible. It feels good.

Drivers know the experience. At an empty intersection, the first time you run the red light your heart gives a jolt. The second time it comes easily. By the third, it scarcely counts as an event. The intersection has not changed. You have stopped questioning yourself.

The hardest part is this: the person involved does not easily see the process.

The ring has not removed the ability to question oneself. The wearer can still do that. But when asking, they have only their own apparatus to hand, and that same apparatus measures the threshold. Looking back, they see a sequence of perfectly reasonable events, each smoother than the last. Whichever way they look, nothing seems wrong. Smoothness is precisely what the descent looks like.

So self-questioning is not useless. It cannot provide a guarantee. The part beyond that guarantee becomes visible only when someone touches it from outside, and the ring removes exactly that outside person.

This sounds like a discussion of morality, but it is not. The same thing happens elsewhere, with no relation to good and bad.

Someone practices a craft alone at home, with no teacher and no peers watching. Progress is rapid for the first few months, then stops at a point where the person feels quite satisfied. Their hands have not stopped. What has stopped is anyone saying, “This part is wrong.” Every fault is repeated with increasing fluency, sinking deeper as familiarity grows, until it can scarcely be changed: it has become the person’s feel for the craft. The problem is not a lack of effort. They practice all the time. It is simply that nobody has touched what they are practicing.

The person wearing the ring is the person who no longer has a teacher.

One thing must be made clear to prevent a misreading. All this is a bill, not a command.

It says that once a person ceases to be questioned, the ledger in their hands proceeds in this way. It does not say that you ought to submit to questioning. Whether to accept questioning is a law each person lays down for themselves, not one this account can impose on anyone. If someone is willing to pay the price, that is their affair. An accurate bill is enough.

But whoever issues the bill must be able to say where the debt lies.

He demands a comparison stripped of appearances. Very well: strip them away. Plato does not tell us how the shepherd’s later life goes, nor does he need to. His argument never depended on the ending: as long as nobody can see them, the just and unjust will come to act alike. So imagine a person never questioned once in a lifetime, who feels entirely fine from beginning to end.

He would then ask: you say this person’s accounts are rotting. Where? Show me. The person cannot feel it; you say so yourself. Others cannot see it, because nobody can see the person. Who, then, has seen this rot?

He has a second question, harder to evade. Not one word of your account is of use to that shepherd. He cannot hear it and will not come to listen. Yet to someone already willing to be questioned, you need say nothing: they do it every day. In other words, the place where your account is useful is exactly where it is least needed.

Third: a debt with no creditor, no due date, and no sensation of debt for the debtor. How, exactly, does that differ from saying “a god will be watching”? You supply not even a creditor, yet say the account keeps rolling onward.

He is entitled to ask. By the end of the book, he himself does supply a creditor. That belongs to another essay.

This is no light question. It asks not whether the account is right, but whether any part of it can find a foothold in the person he has described.

Finally, return to the movement itself.

The shepherd turns the ring by accident. Sitting among his fellow shepherds with an idle hand, he turns the setting toward his palm. Just that.

The movement is so small it scarcely counts as a movement. No resolve, no calculation, not even attention. Yet from that instant onward, nobody confronts anything he does with “What gives you grounds for that?” And he himself proceeds smoothly all the way to the throne.

What is truly fatal often comes at this scale. Not somebody handing you a ring, but a casual turn of your hand one day, after which nobody questions you again.
