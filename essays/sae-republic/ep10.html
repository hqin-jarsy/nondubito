被逐的诗人带走了什么

What Does the Banished Poet Take Away?

Han Qin (秦汉)

全书快完了，苏格拉底忽然把一桩旧案又翻了出来。

前面造城的时候，他把模仿的那一路诗人赶了出去。现在灵魂分成几块这件事已经讲明白了，他说，我更觉得当初赶得对。

不过他紧接着说了一句私心话，而且说得很不好意思。

他说，我从小就对荷马有一份敬重，甚至可以说是一份爱，这份东西到现在还在。这话说出来我有点难为情。可人不能比真理更值得敬重。

于是他动手。

第一刀是三张床。

有一张床是神做的，就一张。为什么只有一张，他给的理由很利落：神要是做了两张，那两张后面又会冒出第三张来，因为那两张总得有个共同的样子。木匠做的是第二张，他照着神那一张做。画画的人做的是第三张，他照着木匠那张画。

所以画画的人离真的那一头隔着三层。他手上那个东西，是一个东西的影子的影子。

他把这个数目说得很具体，隔三层，不是隔得远。数得出来的距离，比一句离得远难反驳。

第二刀是一串问题，问的是荷马。

他说，荷马啊，你要是真懂你写的那些事，你早该去做那些事，不是去仿那些事。那么我问你：你替哪一座城立过法。哪一仗是靠你带兵或者出主意打赢的。你有没有发明过什么让人过日子方便的东西，像泰勒斯那样。有没有一群人跟着你，从你这儿学出一套过日子的路数来，像毕达哥拉斯那样。

一样也数不出来。

第三刀最见功夫，他把一门手艺拆成了三份。

用的人，做的人，仿的人。吹笛子的人最懂一支笛子好不好，因为他天天在用；他会告诉做笛子的人，这儿该怎么改。所以用的人手上有的是知道，做的人手上有的是照着话来的把握，而仿的人两样都没有，他只见过样子。

第四刀落在人身上。

他说，量一量，数一数，称一称，这是人身上最靠得住的几样本事。可同一根棍子插在水里看着是弯的，拿出来是直的；同一样东西，离得近显得大，离得远显得小。看着像什么，跟量出来是什么，常常对不上。而模仿这件事，做的正是看着像什么这一头。

刀落到这里都还在讲道理。真正重的一刀在后面，而且那一刀他讲得极准。

他说，你去看戏。台上那个人遭了大难，捶胸顿足地哭。你在底下也跟着难受，而且你难受得越厉害，越觉得这戏好，出来还要夸那个诗人。

可换了你自己家里出事呢。你会撑着。你会觉得一个男人当众哭哭啼啼不像样，得忍住。

他说，这就是问题所在。你自己那点事上被你按住的那一样东西，到了别人的事上被放开了，还被喂饱了。而放开过之后，回到自己身上就不那么好按了。

笑也一样。台上那些话，让你自己说你会觉得丢人，可你在底下笑得很痛快；笑着笑着，回家你就说出来了。

所以他给诗定的罪名是这个：它在浇灌那些本该弄干的东西。

弄干这个词是他自己用的。

判之前他还说了一句，这一句很少有人提。

他说，哲学跟诗吵架，这是古已有之的事，不是我今天挑起来的。他还顺手举了几句诗人骂哲学的话：说哲学是一条对着主人叫的狗，说它在一群傻子的空话里称王。

也就是说，他知道自己是这场架里的一方，不是站在架外头判的。

然后，你以为该判了。可他没有判死。

他留了一扇门，而且门开得很正式。他说，喜欢诗又不是诗人的那些人，可以替诗说话，用散文说，别用诗说。他们只要能说清楚一件事，诗不光让人快活，它对城邦和过日子还有用，那么我们很乐意把她请回来。

不是让人快活就行。要有用。

到这里，两边是碰得上的，而且碰在两处。

头一处是他那份难为情。他没有装作自己不爱荷马。他把爱说出来，再说人不能比真理更值得敬重，然后照着后一句办。一个人拿自己最喜欢的东西开刀，还当众交代自己下不去手，这不常见。

第二处更要紧。他留了那扇门。

他没有说诗是坏东西，他说的是我还没看出它有什么用；你要是能说清楚，我们就请回来。这是一个可以被推翻的判决。整本《理想国》里，这样的判决不多。

然后是分开的地方。

这一边的说法是这样：认识一样东西，说到底是划一道界，把这一边和那一边分开，本系列一直把这个动作叫作落刀。一刀下去立住一块，同时也留下这一刀没有收进去的那一部分。那一部分不会因为没人提它就没有了。

而诗做的事，多半就是让那一部分留着声音。

一个人心里堵得慌，听见一首唱到点子上的歌，那一下不是被谁劝住了。是有个人替他把那件说不出来的事说出来了。说出来它并没有变小，也没有被解决，可它从此不再是只有他一个人知道的东西。

带过孩子的人也见过另一种。孩子睡前要听同一个故事，一遍一遍，讲错一个字都不行。那个故事里多半有一处正对着他自己的什么，而他说不出来是哪一处，你也看不出来。故事没有解决那件事，也没有教会他什么。它只是每天晚上把那一处摆在那儿，让它有个地方待着。

歌没有替他办成任何事。它只是不让那件事变哑。

所以柏拉图那个罪名，这边不否认，只换一句话来说。他说诗在浇那些本该弄干的东西。这边说：弄干不等于没有。你不浇它，它只是不出声。

而一个不出声的东西，最容易被当成不存在。

这一层落回他自己那扇门上，事情就清楚了。

那扇门上写着的那一条是有用。诗要证明自己对城邦和过日子有用，才请得回来。

可诗替人留着的那一部分，恰恰是还没派上用场的那一部分。一样东西要是有用，它早就被收进去了，也就不用谁来替它留着了。留着它的必要，正来自它眼下什么也办不了。

所以这扇门开得再正，诗也进不来。不是因为它没资格，是因为门口那一条量的不是它这个东西。

再往下还有一层，是他自己也走不掉的。

他逐诗人，用的却是一整套诗人的手艺。洞穴是一幅画，太阳是一个比方，线段是一张图。而就在这一卷判完诗人之后没几页，他写了全书最后一个故事，一个人死了又活过来，回来讲他看见的事。那个故事的头一句还套着荷马的调子。

柏拉图没有对这件事说过一句话。他只是这么做了。

而正是这一处，他会问回来。

他会说：你说诗替那一部分留着声音，好。可我给过你一个机制，你回头看一眼。人在别人的事上哭，哭过之后自己那道闸就松了。这个机制我说得很清楚，你也没有反对。

那么我问你：同一只手，怎么分得出它是在替那一部分留声音，还是在拿那一部分做文章。

一首歌把你心里那件事说出来了，和一支曲子把你煽起来去干一件你本来不会干的事，用的是同一条路子，走的是同一道闸。你说前一种是留着，后一种是操弄。分是好分，可你拿什么去分。

我逐诗人，正是因为我分不出来。我宁可把整只手都拿开。

你不拿开，那你手上得有一把尺。你那把尺在哪儿。

这一问不好躲，因为它不是问诗好不好，是问那个替诗说话的人，凭什么担保自己不是在被诗办。

最后回到那份难为情。

他说那句话的时候是认真的。他确实爱荷马，从小就爱，写这一段的时候还爱着。他把这个说出来，然后照着相反的方向办完了事。

一个人对着自己最舍不得的东西下手，还先把舍不得说出口，这已经是很老实的做法了。

只是他没有说，那把刀落下去以后，被切下来的那一块去了哪里。

他把她请出了城门，还在门外留了一句话：什么时候你说得清自己有用，就回来。

她没有回来。她也不是靠有用活着的。

With the book almost over, Socrates suddenly reopens an old case.

Earlier, while building the city, he expelled the poets who practice imitation. Now that the soul’s division into parts has been explained, he says he is even more convinced that expelling them was right.

But he immediately adds a personal confession, with considerable embarrassment.

Since childhood, he says, I have felt reverence for Homer, even love, and that feeling remains. I am a little ashamed to say this. But a person cannot deserve more reverence than truth.

Then he gets to work.

The first cut is three beds.

One bed is made by the god, and there is only one. His reason for there being only one is neat: if the god made two, a third would emerge behind them, because the two would have to share a common form. The carpenter makes the second bed, following the god’s. The painter makes the third, painting from the carpenter’s.

So the painter stands three levels away from what is real. What he holds is the shadow of a thing’s shadow.

He makes the number specific: three levels away, not merely far away. A distance that can be counted is harder to dispute than a statement that something is distant.

The second cut is a series of questions addressed to Homer.

Homer, he says, if you truly understood what you wrote about, you would long ago have gone and done those things instead of imitating them. So tell me: for which city have you made laws? Which war was won because you led its armies or advised them? Have you invented anything that makes everyday life easier, as Thales did? Has a group of people followed you and learned a way of living from you, as they did from Pythagoras?

Not one example can be counted.

The third cut is especially skillful. He divides a craft into three.

The user, the maker, the imitator. The flute player understands best whether a flute is good, because he uses it every day. He tells the maker what needs changing and how. So the user has knowledge, the maker has an assurance grounded in what he is told, and the imitator has neither. He has seen only the appearance.

The fourth cut falls on the person.

Measuring, counting, weighing: these, he says, are among a person’s most reliable abilities. Yet the same stick looks bent in water and straight when taken out. The same thing looks large nearby and small at a distance. What something looks like often fails to match what measurement finds. And imitation works precisely on the side of what things look like.

Up to here, the cuts still concern reasoning. The truly weighty cut comes next, and he describes it with extraordinary precision.

You go to the theater, he says. Someone onstage has suffered a terrible calamity and weeps, beating his chest and stamping his feet. You in the audience suffer with him. The more intensely you suffer, the better you think the play is, and afterward you praise the poet.

But what if trouble strikes your own household? You hold yourself together. You think it unseemly for a man to weep in public. You must restrain yourself.

That, he says, is the problem. What you hold down in your own affairs is released in someone else’s, and fed until it is full. Once released, it is harder to hold down when it returns to your own life.

Laughter works the same way. You would be ashamed to say the things said onstage, yet in the audience you laugh heartily. After enough laughing, you go home and say them yourself.

So this is his charge against poetry: it waters what ought to be dried out.

Dried out is his own expression.

Before passing judgment, he adds something seldom mentioned.

The quarrel between philosophy and poetry, he says, is ancient. I did not start it today. He even supplies a few poets’ insults against philosophy: a dog barking at its master, a ruler among the empty talk of fools.

In other words, he knows he is one side in the quarrel, not someone judging it from outside.

Now, you think, the verdict must come. But he does not close the case for good.

He leaves a door open, and opens it formally. Those who love poetry but are not poets may speak on its behalf, he says. In prose, not verse. If they can make one thing clear, that poetry not only gives pleasure but also benefits cities and everyday life, we will gladly invite her back.

Giving pleasure alone is not enough. It must be useful.

Here the two sides can meet, in two places.

The first is his embarrassment. He does not pretend that he does not love Homer. He declares the love, then says that a person cannot deserve more reverence than truth, and acts on the latter sentence. It is uncommon for someone to take a knife to what they love best while publicly acknowledging how hard they find it.

The second matters more. He leaves the door open.

He does not say poetry is bad. He says he has not yet seen what use it is. If you can make that clear, we will invite it back. This is a verdict that can be overturned. There are not many such verdicts in the Republic.

Then comes the separation.

This side’s account goes like this: knowing something ultimately means drawing a boundary, dividing this side from that. Throughout this series, this act has been called making a cut. A cut establishes something and at the same time leaves a part it did not take in. That part does not disappear because nobody mentions it.

What poetry does, more often than not, is let that part keep its voice.

A person feels something painfully blocked inside and hears a song that reaches exactly the right place. In that moment, nobody has talked them out of anything. Someone has put into words what they could not say. Saying it has not made it smaller or solved it. But from then on, it is no longer something only that person knows.

Anyone who has cared for children has seen another version. Before bed, a child wants the same story again and again. Not a single word may be wrong. There is probably a place in the story that speaks directly to something in the child, but the child cannot say where, and you cannot see it either. The story has not solved the matter or taught the child anything. It simply sets that place before them each night and gives it somewhere to stay.

The song has accomplished nothing for the person. It simply refuses to let that thing become mute.

So this side does not deny Plato’s charge. It merely states it differently. He says poetry waters what ought to be dried out. This side says: dried out does not mean gone. Stop watering it, and it merely stops speaking.

And something that does not speak is very easily taken not to exist.

Bring this back to his own open door, and the matter becomes clear.

The condition written on the door is usefulness. Poetry must prove that it is useful to cities and everyday life before it can be invited back.

Yet the part poetry preserves for people is precisely the part that has not yet been put to use. If it were useful, it would already have been taken in, and nobody would need to preserve it. The need to preserve it comes precisely from its inability to accomplish anything at present.

So however properly the door is opened, poetry still cannot enter. Not because it is unqualified, but because the condition at the entrance measures something other than what it is.

There is a further level from which he himself cannot escape.

He expels the poets using an entire set of the poet’s crafts. The cave is a picture, the sun a comparison, the divided line a diagram. And only a few pages after passing judgment on the poets in this very book, he writes the work’s final story: a man dies, comes back to life, and returns to tell what he saw. Even the story’s opening sentence borrows Homer’s cadence.

Plato says not a word about this. He simply does it.

And this is precisely where he will question the other side in return.

You say poetry keeps a voice for that part, he will say. Very well. But I have given you a mechanism. Look back at it. People weep over someone else’s affairs, and after weeping, their own gate loosens. I have explained this mechanism clearly, and you have not disputed it.

Then let me ask: with the same hand at work, how do you tell whether it is keeping a voice for that part or exploiting it?

A song that says what is in your heart and a tune that rouses you to do something you would not otherwise do use the same route and pass through the same gate. You call the first preservation and the second manipulation. Drawing the distinction is easy enough. But what will you use to make it?

I expel the poets precisely because I cannot tell the difference. I would rather remove the whole hand.

If you do not remove it, you must have a measure in your own hand. Where is that measure?

The question is hard to evade. It does not ask whether poetry is good. It asks what entitles the person defending poetry to guarantee that poetry is not using them.

Finally, return to the embarrassment.

He means what he says. He really does love Homer, has loved him since childhood, and still loves him while writing this passage. He says so, then carries the matter through in the opposite direction.

To act against what one can least bear to lose, first admitting that one cannot bear to lose it, is already a very honest way to proceed.

Only, he does not say where the severed piece goes after the knife falls.

He escorts her out through the city gate and leaves a sentence outside: when you can explain what use you are, come back.

She does not return. Nor is usefulness what keeps her alive.
