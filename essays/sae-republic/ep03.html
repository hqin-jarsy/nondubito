线段不是梯子，是账

The Line Is a Ledger, Not a Ladder

Han Qin (秦汉)

太阳那个比方讲完，格劳孔还没缓过来，苏格拉底就接着往下画了。他说，你再拿一条线来。

他前面拿太阳抵过一回，说本钱今天付不出，先给点利息。这条线是第二笔利息。本钱还是没还，他还的又是一样看得见的东西，好让人顺着它去想那样看不见的。

这条线在洞穴的前一页。顺序值得留意：先有太阳，再有这条线，最后才是洞穴。三样东西是一口气讲下来的，讲的是同一件事的三个面。

线怎么画，他说得很具体。把一条线切成不相等的两段，一段归看得见的那一头，一段归想得到的那一头；然后每一段再照原来的比例切一次。这样一共四段。

四段各是什么。最下面那一段是影子和倒影，水面上的，光滑东西上映出来的。往上一段是投下这些影子的东西本身，活物，草木，人做出来的器具。再往上一段是数学那一路，几何学家画一个方出来，讲的却不是这个画出来的方，是方本身。最上面那一段，他说的是另一种走法，不借任何图像，从一个念头走到另一个念头。

第三段那里有个细节值得停一下。他们画出来的那个方，属于第二段，是看得见的东西；可他们嘴里讲的不是这个方。也就是说，第三段的人一边用着第二段的东西，一边说我讲的不是它。这个又用又不认的姿势，柏拉图写得很准。

到这里，一般的读法就说，你看，四层楼，越往上越真。这个读法不算错，他自己后来也是这么排的。可是四段之间最要紧的那一处分别，不在这个排法里。

最要紧的一处在第三段和第四段之间，而且它跟对象没有关系。

他说，几何学家拿奇数偶数，拿各种图形，拿三类角，当作已知的东西。这些东西他们不再交代，不管是对别人还是对自己，都不再交代一遍为什么。他用的话很不客气：他们不屑于给出交代。拿这些当出发点，一路往下推，推到他们本来要证的那个结论为止。

另一路不这样。另一路也从假设出发，可它把假设当踏板，不当地基。它踩着假设往上走，去问那个假设自己凭什么。

所以第三段和第四段的分别，不在于你研究的是图形还是理念，在于你拿自己的起点怎么办。当作已知，不再交代，就是那一段；当作还可以再问的东西，往上追，就是这一段。

这条分界线，SAE 这边整个认下来。

SAE 那边一路在说的一件事就是，你承担的那些前提得一直摊在受审的位置上，不许供起来。前提当然要有，不设前提连话都开不了口；可是设了之后不许贴上免检的条子。一贴条子，那句话就从可以被问的东西变成了不能被问的东西，而那正是麻烦开始的地方。

柏拉图那句不屑于给出交代，说的就是贴条子这个动作。他自己看得极清楚，而且他把这个看清楚的东西当成了分界线。这一处不是我们替他找的对接，是他亲手划的那一刀。

可是接下来他做了另一件事。

他把这条关于做法的分界线，折算成了一把梯子。

他不是偷偷折算的，他说得很明白。要紧的是它明说得有多早。

回头看画线的第一句指令：切成不相等的两段。高低在这一句里就已经露面了，比任何论证都早。哪一段长哪一段短，比例定下来的那一刻，清楚和不清楚已经分了先后，后面的话都在这条线上说。

得说准一点：排序不是从这三个字里长出来的。他在别处另有明说，四种状态按各自分到多少真理，就分到多少清楚。所以把线改成等分，排序不会跟着消失，消失的只是它被画进图形的那一次露面。不相等这三个字要紧，不在于它是排序的来源，在于它是排序最早的一次露面，而且早到人不会把它当成一个主张。

折算之后发生了什么，值得说慢一点。

原来那条分界线问的是：你对你自己的起点交代过没有。这是一个可以对账的问题。有人问你凭什么，你答得出或者答不出，答不出就补，补不上就承认这里欠着。整件事在同一个平面上进行。

折算成梯子之后，问题变成：你在第几层。这就不能对账了。你在第二层，那是你的位置，不是你的欠账。补不了，只能往上爬。而爬这件事有没有路，路怎么走，谁说了算，全是另外的问题。

我这一刀漏了什么，和你站得低，是两句完全不同的话。前一句可以一笔一笔对，对不上就补，补不上就写在那里；这不等于每一笔都能在原来那本账里还清，有些还不清，可它至少还在账上。后一句连账都没有，只能认。

而且梯子一立起来，就会长出一样东西，位次。上班的人对这个过程都不陌生。本来问的是这件事你想清楚了没有，问着问着就变成了你是几级。那个级别一开始是拿来标记想清楚到什么程度的，标着标着，级别自己成了要紧的东西，原来那个问题反倒没有人再问了。梯子不是不好用，是它一好用，就容易把它本来要衡量的那件事替换掉。

SAE 那边也有四格。

先把话接上。认识一样东西，说到底是划界，把这一边和那一边分开，本系列一直把这个动作叫作落刀。四格说的就是这一刀。

那四格是横着排的，不是竖着排的。它们不是四层高低，是同一刀可能欠下的四种账。

你划一条线，把红划出来。这一刀之后，可能欠下的账有四种。

第一种，收进来的这一边没有收干净。红这一侧还有你没有收进去的东西，亮度，浓淡，此刻它落在谁的眼睛里。这一种最普通，也最躲不掉。

第二种，划错了边。本该在这边的被划出去了，或者本不该在的被收了进来。这一种事后能纠。

第三种，两边都够得着。同一样东西，按这个条件算红，按那个条件算不红。这不是它模糊，是你没说清按哪个条件，条件一补上，两属就散了。

第四种，这把尺子根本管不着。有些东西落在这套判据的场子外面，你说它红或者不红都不对，因为这个问题在这里不予结算。它不是假的，是没有资格进这本账。

这四样在 SAE 那边各有名字，收纳之亏，错置之亏，两属，判外。名字用不用都行，要紧的是它们是四种问法，不是四个高度。

家里换季整理衣柜，四样一次全齐。你把衣服分成夏天穿的和冬天穿的。分完之后，夏天穿的那一堆里，还有一大堆这个分法照顾不到的事，哪件配哪件，哪件领子已经磨了，这是第一种。有件外套你顺手放错了堆，这是第二种。有件薄外套春秋都穿，两边都想要它，其实是你没说清按气温分还是按场合分，这是第三种。角落里那把伞，既不是夏天穿的也不是冬天穿的，它根本不是衣服，这个分法管不着它，这是第四种。

四种里没有哪一种更真。它们记的不是东西的高低，是你这一刀的亏欠。而且这四种不称穷尽，也不互相排斥，同一刀可以同时欠好几种。

再换个说法。同一件事，你可以说我大概知道，可以说我算过，可以说我亲手做过一次。这三样常常被读成一样比一样扎实。可仔细看，它们是三种不同的知道法，各有各的漏。大概知道的人抓得住整个形状，说不出细节；算过的人数目对得上，可能从没碰过实物；亲手做过一次的人手上有实感，却容易把那一次的偶然当成普遍。你要把它们排个高低，总能排出来，可是排完你就看不见各自漏的是什么了。

这里得说一句公道话。往上一层这个感受是真的，不是幻觉。学会一件难事，确实像是上了一格，站的地方不一样了，回头看以前的自己看得清清楚楚。这个经验 SAE 不否认，也不该否认。分歧只在于这份感受该记在哪一栏。记在实得那一栏，它是真的，你确实取走了东西；记在高度那一栏，它就开始替你回答一些它其实回答不了的问题。

拆掉梯子的人，得回答梯子原来管的那件事。

他划的那条分界线，你刚刚整个认下来了，而且认得很痛快。可是我那条线，本来就是用来分高低的。不给自己起点交代的那一路，和肯往上追问的那一路，在他那里不是并排的两种做法，是一低一高。他说得明明白白：清楚的程度，按它们分到多少真理来定。

于是他会问：那条线你认，那条线分出来的高低你不认。你把线拿走，又把线分出来的东西丢掉，这是想两头都要。

他还有第二句。我画这条线，是画给一群将要管事的年轻人看的。他们要知道该往哪儿使劲，先学什么后学什么，什么时候算是可以往前一步了。梯子做的就是这件事。你那本账，一条一条记着这一刀漏了什么那一刀漏了什么，一个想学点东西的人拿在手上，该怎么办。

还有一件他可以顺手指出来，而这件事是 SAE 自己说的：那四种问法不称穷尽，欠账的种类本身没有底。

于是他问：你那本账，一共几笔。梯子上的人至少知道自己在第几级。

这一问落得很实。梯子告诉你脚往哪儿放，账本只告诉你还欠着什么。要一个人真动起来，多半还是梯子更管用。

最后回到那条线本身。

如果当初那第一句指令不是切成不相等的两段，而是切成相等的两段，会少掉什么。

排序不会少。他在别处照样说得出，谁分到的真理多，谁就分到的清楚多。四种分辨方式也照样是四种，几何学家照样不交代他的起点，另一路照样往上追问。

少掉的只有一样：那把梯子就不再是画出来的了，它得靠一句一句讲出来。一件要靠讲的事，是可以被问的；一件已经画在那里的事，通常没有人问。

Glaucon has barely recovered from the comparison with the sun when Socrates keeps drawing. Now, he says, take a line.

Earlier he offered the sun in lieu of payment: he could not pay the principal today, so he would pay some interest. This line is a second installment of interest. The principal remains unpaid. Once again he offers something visible, so that people can follow it in thinking about what cannot be seen.

The line comes on the page before the cave. The order is worth noticing: first the sun, then the line, and finally the cave. All three are told in one stretch, as three sides of the same matter.

His instructions for drawing the line are specific. Divide it into two unequal parts, one for the visible side and one for the intelligible side. Then divide each part again in the original ratio. That makes four segments.

What are the four? At the bottom are shadows and reflections, in water or on smooth surfaces. Above them are the things that cast those shadows: living creatures, plants, and human artifacts. Above those comes the mathematical approach. A geometer draws a square, but speaks not of the drawn square but of the square itself. At the top is another way of proceeding, using no images, passing from one thought to another.

A detail in the third segment deserves a pause. The square they draw belongs to the second segment: it is visible. Yet the square they speak of is not this one. So those in the third segment use things from the second while saying, “That is not what I mean.” Plato catches this posture, using something while disavowing it, exactly.

The usual reading now says: look, four floors, each truer than the one below. This is not wrong; he later ranks them that way himself. But the most important distinction among the four segments is not contained in that ranking.

It lies between the third and fourth segments, and it has nothing to do with the objects.

Geometers, he says, take odd and even, the various figures, and the three kinds of angle as known. They give no further account of why, either to others or to themselves. His phrase is blunt: they disdain to give an account. Taking these things as starting points, they reason downward until they reach the conclusion they originally meant to prove.

The other route works differently. It too begins with hypotheses, but treats them as stepping-stones, not foundations. It steps upward on a hypothesis to ask what the hypothesis itself rests on.

So the difference between the third and fourth segments is not whether you study figures or Forms. It is what you do with your own starting point. Treat it as known and give no further account: you are in that segment. Treat it as still open to questioning and pursue the question upward: you are in this one.

SAE accepts that dividing line in full.

One thing SAE has been saying all along is that the premises you undertake must remain laid out for examination. They must not be enshrined. Of course premises are needed: without them, you cannot even begin to speak. But once set, they must not receive an “exempt from inspection” sticker. The moment you attach one, the statement changes from something that can be questioned to something that cannot. That is where the trouble starts.

Plato’s phrase “disdain to give an account” names exactly this act of attaching the sticker. He sees it with great clarity and makes what he has seen into a dividing line. We did not find this point of contact on his behalf. It is the cut he made himself.

But next he does something else.

He converts this dividing line about ways of proceeding into a ladder.

The conversion is not concealed. He states it quite openly. What matters is how early he states it.

Look back at the first instruction: divide the line into two unequal parts. Higher and lower have already appeared, before any argument. The moment the proportion fixes which part is longer and which shorter, clarity and obscurity have already been ordered. Everything that follows is said on that line.

We must be precise: the ranking does not grow out of the word unequal. Elsewhere he explicitly says that the four states receive clarity in proportion to the truth allotted to them. Divide the line equally instead, and the ranking would not disappear. Only this instance of its being drawn into the figure would disappear. “Unequal” matters not because it originates the ranking but because it is the ranking’s earliest appearance, so early that people do not notice it as a claim.

What happens after the conversion deserves to be told slowly.

The original dividing line asks: have you accounted for your own starting point? That is a question whose accounts can be checked. Someone asks what grounds you have. You can answer or you cannot. If you cannot, you supply what is missing; if you cannot supply it, you acknowledge the debt. The entire affair takes place on one plane.

Once converted into a ladder, the question becomes: which level are you on? Now there is no account to check. Being on the second level is your position, not your debt. It cannot be made good; you can only climb. Whether a route exists, where it goes, and who gets to decide are all different questions.

“What did this cut of mine leave out?” and “You stand lower” are entirely different statements. The first can be checked entry by entry. What does not add up can be made good; what cannot be made good can remain written down. This does not mean every debt can be settled in the original ledger. Some cannot, but at least they remain on the books. The second statement offers no ledger at all. You can only accept it.

And once a ladder is erected, something grows from it: rank. Anyone with a job knows the process. The question begins as “Have you thought this through?” and turns, in the asking, into “What grade are you?” The grade initially marks how far the thinking has been done. Eventually the grade itself becomes what matters, and nobody asks the original question anymore. It is not that a ladder is useless. It is that, once useful, it can easily replace the very thing it was supposed to measure.

SAE too has four slots.

First, reconnect the thread. Knowing something comes down to drawing a boundary, separating this side from that. Throughout this series, that act has been called making a cut. The four slots concern that cut.

They are laid out horizontally, not vertically. They are not four heights but four kinds of debt the same cut may incur.

You draw a line to distinguish red. After this cut, four kinds of account may remain unsettled.

First, the included side has not been fully collected. On the red side there are still things you have not taken in: brightness, intensity, whose eyes it meets at this moment. This is the most ordinary kind, and the hardest to avoid.

Second, something has been placed on the wrong side. What belongs inside has been excluded, or what does not belong has been included. This can be corrected afterward.

Third, something reaches both sides. The same thing counts as red under one condition and not red under another. It is not blurry. You have failed to specify the governing condition. Supply that condition and the dual belonging dissolves.

Fourth, this ruler has no jurisdiction. Some things fall outside the field of these criteria. Calling them either red or not red is wrong, because the question is not adjudicated here. The thing is not false; it is ineligible for entry in this ledger.

SAE has names for these four: deficit of inclusion, deficit of placement, dual belonging, and outside adjudication. Use the names or not. What matters is that these are four ways of asking, not four heights.

A seasonal wardrobe clear-out brings all four together. You divide clothes into summer wear and winter wear. Afterward, much in the summer pile remains beyond the division’s reach: what goes with what, which collar has worn thin. That is the first kind. You have casually put a coat in the wrong pile: the second. A light jacket worn in spring and autumn is claimed by both piles. Really, you have not specified whether you are sorting by temperature or occasion: the third. The umbrella in the corner is neither summer wear nor winter wear. It is not clothing at all, so the division has no jurisdiction over it: the fourth.

None of the four is truer than another. They record not the heights of things but the liabilities of your cut. Nor are they claimed to be exhaustive or mutually exclusive. The same cut can incur several kinds at once.

Put it another way. About the same matter, you might say “I have a rough idea,” “I have calculated it,” or “I have done it once myself.” These are often read as successively firmer kinds of knowledge. Look closely, though, and they are different ways of knowing, each with its own omissions. Someone with a rough idea grasps the overall shape but cannot supply details. Someone who has calculated it can make the numbers add up but may never have touched the object. Someone who has done it once has a feel for it but can easily mistake the accidents of that one occasion for a universal rule. You can always rank them. Once ranked, however, you can no longer see what each leaves out.

One thing must be said in fairness. The feeling of rising a level is real, not an illusion. Learning something difficult really does feel like moving up a step. You stand somewhere different and can look back at your former self with perfect clarity. SAE does not deny that experience and should not. The disagreement is only about the column in which it belongs. Enter it under actual gain and it is true: you have genuinely acquired something. Enter it under height and it starts answering questions it cannot actually answer.

Whoever takes down the ladder must answer for the job it used to do.

You have just accepted his dividing line completely and readily. But his line was meant to distinguish higher from lower. The route that gives no account of its starting point and the route willing to pursue the question upward are not, for him, two methods standing side by side. One is lower, the other higher. He says so plainly: degrees of clarity are determined by how much truth each receives.

So he would ask: you accept the line but reject the heights it separates. You take the line and throw away what the line distinguishes. You want it both ways.

He has a second question. I drew this line for young people who are going to govern. They need to know where to direct their effort, what to learn first and afterward, and when they are ready to move forward. That is what the ladder does. Your ledger records, entry by entry, what this cut missed and what that cut missed. What is someone who wants to learn supposed to do with it?

There is another point he can readily make, and SAE itself supplies it: those four ways of asking make no claim to exhaustiveness. The kinds of debt themselves have no bottom.

So he asks: how many entries are in your ledger? At least the person on the ladder knows which rung they occupy.

The question lands firmly. A ladder tells you where to put your foot. A ledger tells you only what you still owe. For getting a person actually moving, the ladder probably works better.

Finally, return to the line itself.

If that first instruction had been to divide it into equal rather than unequal parts, what would be lost?

Not the ranking. Elsewhere he could still say that whoever receives more truth receives more clarity. The four ways of distinguishing would still be four. The geometer would still give no account of his starting point, and the other route would still pursue the question upward.

Only one thing would be lost: the ladder would no longer be drawn. It would have to be explained sentence by sentence. Something that must be explained can be questioned. Something already drawn there usually goes unquestioned.
