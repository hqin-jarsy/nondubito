三分的灵魂与发生的链

The Three-Part Soul and the Chain of Becoming

Han Qin (秦汉)

城造完了。造它的分工，造它的护卫者，造它怎么挑人怎么教育，一直造到谁来管事。造完苏格拉底说，这座城既然是照着完善造的，那么智慧，勇敢，节制，正义这四样就都该在里面，我们一样一样找找看。

前三样各有各的落处。智慧在管事的那一小拨人身上，勇敢在护卫者身上。讲到勇敢他打了个比方，说这就像染毛线，要先把毛处理好，颜色才咬得住，洗多少遍都掉不了；护卫者受的那套教育就是这个处理，为的是让他该怕什么不该怕什么这件事，遇上快活和恐惧也冲不掉。

到节制这一样，情形有点不同，而且这个不同他自己也说了。前两样各待在各的等级里，节制却不待在任何一格，它穿过三个等级，像一支曲子里几个音合上了，是全城上下对谁该管事这件事的一致。

也就是说，他那张表上有一项，从一开始就不肯好好待在格子里。

找到第四样的时候，他说了一句很实在的话。他说我们真可笑，这东西从一开头就在我们脚边滚来滚去，我们却像手里攥着东西还满地找的人。

第四样是各司其职，每个人做属于自己的那一件事，不去插手别人的。这个定义要紧，要紧到后面还得单说。这一篇要看的是他接下来做的那个动作。

他说，城里分三个等级。要是一个人身上也有对得上的三样，那么正义在人身上就是同一个形状。于是他从城走回人。

走回去之前，他先立了一条规矩，立得很小心。他说，同一样东西，在同一个方面，对着同一个对象，在同一个时候，不会又做又不做。

有人当场抬杠：一个陀螺立在原地转，它是动还是不动。他答得很稳。陀螺的轴不动，圆周在动，那是两个方面，不算同一样东西在同一个方面又动又不动。

规矩一立，他就拿它来拆人。

一个人渴。渴这件事，就其为渴而言，要的就是喝。可有时候一个人渴得难受，却不肯喝。想喝的和拦着的，不可能是同一样东西的同一个方面，那就是两样。他把要喝的那一样叫欲望，把拦着的那一样叫讲道理的那一部分。

第三样要靠一个故事才立得起来，而那个故事是全书里最忘不掉的一段。

有个人叫勒昂提乌斯，从港口那边上来，走在北墙外面。他看见刑吏脚边躺着几具尸首。他想看，同时又觉得恶心，就把脸转开了。他跟自己较了一阵劲，抬手把眼睛捂上；最后还是没顶住，他把手拿开，眼睛睁得大大的，一边往那边跑一边冲自己的眼睛骂：看吧，你们这些不争气的东西，把这好景致看个够。

苏格拉底拿这一段说，那股冲着自己发的火，既不是那个想看的，也不是那个讲道理的，它是第三样。而且要紧的是，这一次它站在讲道理那一边，跟欲望作对。

前两样他是拿规矩推出来的，第三样他拿了一个故事。这个换手值得留意。想喝而不肯喝，靠讲道理就分得开；可一个人对自己发火，这件事讲道理讲不出来，得让人看见一回才认。

这一刀了不起，隔这么久读还是了不起。

他说的不是人有时候会犹豫。犹豫谁都说得出。他说的是人里面有好几样东西，各有各的要求，会真的打起来，打起来还要分胜负。勒昂提乌斯那一天输了，而且他自己知道自己输了，所以才骂。

当过家长的人对这个画面熟。孩子说好只吃一块糖，第二块摆在那儿，他站着不动，眼泪在眼眶里打转。想吃的是他，忍着的是他，气自己没忍住的还是他。三样都是他，可它们确实不是一回事。这一点小孩比大人明白，因为他还没学会把这场架藏起来。

还有一处值得记一笔。动手拆之前，苏格拉底自己先声明了一句：照我们现在用的这个办法，得不出精确的答案，要得准，得走一条更长的路。他把自己的办法标成了近路，而且标在动手之前，不是被人问住之后。

然后是分开的地方。

他给出来的是一张表。三样东西，各有各的名字，各有各的位置，谁该管谁也标好了。

表这样东西有它的脾性。表上的项可以打乱，打乱了还是那张表；可以单看一项，不必先看别的；可以添一项减一项，别的项照旧站着。他在城里配那四种德性，用的就是这个办法。

表好用，好用在可以查。你不必从头读，翻到哪一行看哪一行，看完就走。一样东西只要做成了表，它就随时能被人拿来对着自己的情况比一比，这是它两千年不掉队的原因。

SAE 那边给的不是表，是一条链。

这里得把话接上。认识一样东西，说到底是划界，把这一边和那一边分开，本系列一直把这个动作叫作落刀。一刀下去，立住一块，同时也留下这一刀没有收进去的那一部分。

那条链就是这么一级一级下来的。每一级都不是新添上去的一项，是从上一级留下的那个缺口下手，再落一刀切出来的。所以它跟表最不一样的地方在这里：链的次序本身就是内容。你把第九级和第三级对调，那不是换了个排法，是这条链根本就不成立了，因为第九级本来就是从第八级那个缺口里出来的。

有一个说法要先按住，因为它顺口，但是站不住。有人会说，表是有限的，链是无穷的。不对。那条链就是十六级，数得出来，一级不多一级不少。

分别不在多少，在来路。表上的项是列出来的，链上的级是一刀一刀切出来的。一张表，一个人坐下来把想到的都写上就成了；一条链不行，你只能从这一级留下的缺口出发，看下一刀能落在哪儿。列出来的东西经得起打乱，一刀一刀切出来的经不起。

认识一个人也是这两种法子。

你可以给他列一张表：高个子，爱下棋，脾气急，念旧。这张表挺管用，见面之前看一眼，心里有个数。

你也可以听一条线：他小时候家里出过一件什么事，后来在哪一年碰上了谁，从那以后他就不太肯把话说满了。

两样都是真的，可它们不能互换。表打乱了还是那张表，线打乱了就听不成了，因为线上每一段都是从上一段留下的那个口子里长出来的。

而且有意思的是，真要跟一个人打交道，多数人还是要听那条线。表是拿来认人的，线是拿来懂人的。

还有一处更细的分别，容易滑过去。

柏拉图那里，讲道理的那一部分是统治另外两个的，愤怒是它的帮手。统治这个词他用得很实，就是管着，压着，不许它乱来。

SAE 这边，高的那一层对低的那一层说的不是你不许送，是我不收。这两句话差得远。饿了想吃第三碗，那个想还在，它没有被取消，也没有被按住，只是上面那一层不把它当成一个可以照办的提案。它照旧饿着，照旧出声，只是没被采纳。

一边是赢的把输的压住，一边只是不收。

分别落在账怎么记。压住，是把那样东西算成对头，压着它就得一直使着劲；不收，是把它算成一份没有被通过的提案，它还在原处，下次还可以再送上来，而这一次没有照办。

一张表还有一个好处，是它把人扣在一起。

这一点柏拉图后来说得很明白。他说正义的人做的是把身上那几样调理好，像调三个音，最低的，最高的，中间的，还有它们中间那些，一起系住，让自己从许多变成一个，然后才谈得上去做事。

从许多变成一个。那是他给的答案，也是他要的结果。

SAE 这边的说法是另一套：那一刀是可以不落的，落不落是那个人自己的事，他随时可以停。

于是他会问：我那三样是同时在的，谁管谁是它们之间的事，怎么吵，吵的都是同一屋子里的人。你那条链一刀接一刀往下走，前一刀和后一刀凭什么算同一个人。停了很久以后再落的那一刀，落刀的还是先前那一个吗。从许多变成一个这件事，你交给谁去做。

这一问不好答，因为它问的不是链上哪一级出了毛病，是那条链凭什么算一条。表是一张纸，纸把上面的项圈在一起；链没有那张纸，它靠的是一级扣一级。可万一中间空过一段呢。

最后回到勒昂提乌斯。

他那天没有赢。他骂完自己，还是跑过去看了。柏拉图把这个人留在书里，没有替他描补，也没有交代他后来怎么样了。

写三分那一套的人，手边放着的就是这样一个例子。他见过那场架打起来是什么样子。

一个人站在北墙外面，手捂着眼睛，跟自己较劲，最后自己输给自己。隔了这么久，这个画面一点没旧。

The city has been built: its division of labor, its guardians, its methods of selection and education, all the way to who will govern. Once it is finished, Socrates says that since it has been built to be complete, wisdom, courage, moderation, and justice must all be inside. Let us look for them one by one.

The first three each find a place. Wisdom belongs to the small group who govern, courage to the guardians. To explain courage, he compares it to dyeing wool. The wool must first be prepared so that the color takes and will not wash out, however often it is washed. The guardians’ education is that preparation. What they ought and ought not to fear must not be washed away by pleasure or terror.

With moderation, things are different, and he says so himself. The first two remain in their respective classes. Moderation stays in no single slot. It runs through all three classes like notes sounding together in a tune: the agreement of the whole city, high and low, about who should rule.

In other words, from the very beginning one entry in his table refuses to stay neatly in its box.

When they find the fourth, he says something quite down-to-earth. How ridiculous we are: this thing has been rolling around at our feet from the start, while we have searched everywhere like people holding the very thing they are looking for.

The fourth is each doing their own work, attending to what belongs to them without interfering in others’ work. This definition matters so much that it will need an essay of its own later. Here the focus is the move he makes next.

The city, he says, has three classes. If a person contains three corresponding things, justice in the person will have the same shape. So he walks back from the city to the individual.

Before going back, he carefully lays down a rule. The same thing, in the same respect, toward the same object, at the same time, will not both do something and not do it.

Someone objects at once: a top spins in place. Is it moving or not? His answer is steady. The top’s axis is still, its circumference moving. Those are two respects, not the same thing both moving and not moving in the same respect.

With the rule established, he uses it to take a person apart.

A person is thirsty. Thirst, just as thirst, wants a drink. Yet sometimes a person is painfully thirsty and still refuses to drink. What wants to drink and what holds it back cannot be the same thing in the same respect. They must be two things. He calls what wants the drink appetite, and what holds it back the reasoning part.

The third requires a story to establish it, and the story is one of the most unforgettable passages in the book.

A man named Leontius is coming up from the port, walking outside the north wall. He sees corpses lying at the executioner’s feet. He wants to look and is disgusted at the same time, so he turns his face away. He struggles with himself and raises his hands to cover his eyes. In the end he cannot hold out. He removes his hands, opens his eyes wide, and runs toward the bodies, cursing his eyes as he goes: look, you wretches, take your fill of this fine sight.

Socrates uses the story to say that this anger directed at oneself is neither the desire to look nor the reasoning part. It is a third thing. And, importantly, on this occasion it sides with reason against appetite.

He derived the first two by a rule. For the third he used a story. The change of hands is worth noticing. Wanting to drink and refusing to drink can be separated by reasoning. But a person’s anger at themselves cannot be reasoned into view that way. You have to let people see it once before they recognize it.

It is a remarkable cut. Even after all this time, it is remarkable to read.

He is not saying that people sometimes hesitate. Anyone can say that. He is saying that several things inside a person have demands of their own, that they really fight, and that the fight has winners and losers. Leontius lost that day, and he knew he had lost. That is why he cursed.

Parents know the scene. A child has agreed to eat only one sweet. A second lies there. The child stands motionless, tears gathering in their eyes. The one who wants it is the child. The one holding back is the child. The one angry at failing to hold back is still the child. All three are the child, yet they are not the same thing. Children understand this better than adults because they have not yet learned to hide the fight.

Another detail deserves a note. Before taking the person apart, Socrates himself warns that the method they are using will not yield an exact answer. For precision, a longer road is needed. He labels his method a shortcut before beginning, not after someone has caught him out.

Then comes the point of separation.

What he supplies is a table. Three things, each with its name and position, and labels indicating which should govern which.

Tables have their own character. Their entries can be shuffled and the table remains the same. An entry can be inspected without reading the others first. An entry can be added or removed while the others remain standing. This is the method he uses to place the four virtues in the city.

A table is useful because you can look things up. You need not read from the beginning. Turn to a row, read it, and leave. Once something has become a table, people can take it up at any time and compare it with their own circumstances. That is why it has remained useful for two thousand years.

What SAE supplies is not a table but a chain.

Reconnect the thread here. Knowing something comes down to drawing a boundary, separating this side from that. This series has called that act making a cut. A cut establishes a portion and at the same time leaves what it has not taken in.

The chain proceeds level by level in this way. No level is a newly appended entry. It is cut out by working on the gap left by the previous level. Here lies its greatest difference from a table: the chain’s order is itself content. Exchange the ninth and third levels and you have not merely rearranged things. The chain no longer holds, because the ninth level came from the gap at the eighth.

One tempting formulation must be stopped before it takes hold: the table is finite, the chain infinite. No. The chain has sixteen levels, countable, neither more nor fewer.

The difference is not quantity but how they come about. A table’s entries are listed. A chain’s levels are cut out one by one. To make a table, someone can sit down and write everything they think of. A chain cannot be made that way. You must start from the gap left by this level and see where the next cut can be made. What is listed can withstand a shuffle. What is cut out step by step cannot.

These are also two ways of knowing a person.

You can draw up a list: tall, likes chess, quick-tempered, attached to the past. The list is useful. Read it before meeting and you have some idea of the person.

Or you can listen to a thread: something happened in the family during childhood; in a certain year they met someone; after that, they became less willing to speak in absolutes.

Both are true, but they are not interchangeable. Shuffle the list and it remains the same list. Shuffle the thread and it no longer makes sense, because each stretch grows from the opening left by the one before.

Interestingly, when people really have to deal with someone, most still want to hear the thread. A list helps you recognize a person. A thread helps you understand them.

There is a subtler difference that is easy to slide past.

For Plato, the reasoning part rules the other two, with anger as its assistant. He means rule concretely: controlling, restraining, not allowing disorder.

In SAE, the higher layer does not tell the lower one, “You may not send it.” It says, “I do not accept it.” The difference is considerable. You are hungry and want a third bowl. The wanting remains. It has not been canceled or suppressed. The higher layer simply does not accept it as a proposal to be carried out. It is still hungry and still speaking. It has simply not been adopted.

On one side, the winner holds the loser down. On the other, there is simply non-acceptance.

The difference lies in how the account is kept. Suppression counts the thing as an opponent, requiring continuous force to hold it down. Non-acceptance counts it as a proposal that did not pass. It remains where it was and may be submitted again next time. This time, it was not acted on.

A table has another advantage: it holds the person together.

Plato later states this very clearly. The just person puts the parts within in order, like tuning three notes, lowest, highest, and middle, and all those between. They bind them together, becoming one out of many, and only then proceed to act.

Becoming one out of many. That is the answer he gives and the result he wants.

SAE offers a different account: the cut need not be made. Whether to make it belongs to the person. They may stop at any time.

So he would ask: my three things are present together. Which governs which is a matter among them. However they quarrel, everyone quarreling lives in the same house. Your chain proceeds cut after cut. What makes the earlier and later cuts belong to the same person? After a long pause, is the person making the next cut still the earlier one? To whom do you assign the work of becoming one out of many?

This is not easily answered. It asks not which level of the chain has gone wrong, but what makes the chain count as one chain. A table is a sheet of paper; the paper encloses its entries together. A chain has no such sheet. It relies on each level linking to the next. But what if an interval has been left empty?

Finally, return to Leontius.

He did not win that day. After cursing himself, he still ran over to look. Plato leaves him in the book without touching him up or telling us what became of him.

This was the example beside the author of the three-part account. He had seen what that fight looked like when it broke out.

A person stands outside the north wall, hands covering his eyes, struggling with himself, and finally loses to himself. After all this time, the scene has not aged at all.
