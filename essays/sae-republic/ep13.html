互相照亮，无法互证

Mutual Illumination without Mutual Proof

Han Qin (秦汉)

先把这个系列到底做了什么说准，因为题目会被单独拿出去引，正文不会跟着走。

准确的说法是：两边在共用的地方可以局部对一对，对上的算数；可谁也不能拿自己整套的前提，去给对方当地基。

前一件事叫照亮，后一件叫奠基。这十三篇只做前一件。

现在回到卷二开头那一幕。

苏格拉底把色拉叙马霍斯堵回去了，屋里的人大概都以为这一场完了。格劳孔不干。

他说的那句话，一般转述成你驳倒一个人不等于你说清了这件事。可他原话不是这么说的，他说的更好玩，也更狠。

他说：那个人是被你哄住的，像一条蛇被人吹笛子吹住了一样，而且哄得太早了。至于正义和不正义究竟是什么，我这边还没听见一个说得过去的证明。

他用的词是哄。不是被说服，是被安抚。

一个人不说话了，不等于他被回答了。这是这一整本书的第二句开场白，而全书之所以还有九卷，全靠这一句。

苏格拉底自己在卷一末尾也说过同一件事的另一半，而且说得比谁都干脆。

他驳完了色拉叙马霍斯，回头对自己说：这一场谈下来的结果是，我什么也没弄明白。因为我压根不知道正义是什么；既然不知道它是什么，我自然也说不上它算不算一种德性，更说不上有它的那个人是过得好还是不好。

一个人把对手堵回去了，回头第一件事是说自己什么也没学到。这一句放在卷一末尾，比卷一里任何一个论证都重。

那么为什么两套说法不能互相证明。

证明这样东西，是一套说法内部的钱。在自己这套里通行无阻，出了门就不认。要跨过去证，得先有一个两边都认的底座；可一旦真有了那个底座，那就不是两套说法了，那是一套。

自己证自己也不行，理由是另一样。一个人问自己，问得到的那一块，正是他手上这套东西够得着的那一块；够不着的那一块，恰恰是他问不出来的那一块。首篇里写过这一句，这一路十二篇一直守着它的说法：不是问不出，是担保不了。

那么剩下什么。

剩下三样对得上的东西，而且都是实的。

头一样在最底下。两边都从否定起步。你划一道界，划的意思就是这一边不是那一边；柏拉图整本书都在划界，从正义不是欠债还债开始划。这个起手两边共用，只是共用得薄，它只保证两边能听懂对方在做什么，不保证听懂了以后能说到一块儿去。

第二样在承认那一层。两边都走到了把别人也当成一个人这一步。柏拉图到得没有那么彻底，可他确实到了：他要城里的人互相当兄弟，他要管事的人为被管的人打算。走到这一层的人之间，才有得谈；没走到的，比如那个说自己一个人的想法就是全部的，不在这张桌子上。

第三样在外头。两边活在同一个宇宙里，碰上的是同一些事。人会死，人会看错，人会想让别人听自己的，人会把一件顺手的事做成第二遍第三遍。这些事不归谁所有，两边各自撞上，各自记了一笔。

三样合起来能做的只有一件：局部对一对。

而对上的那些是实得，不是趋近。这两个词的分别在这一路上说过好几次，到这儿是最后一次：实得的意思是你确实拿到了，拿到的那一份不会因为后面没拿到别的就作废；趋近的意思是有一个地方在前头等着，你离它更近了一点。前一句这边说得起，后一句说不起，因为那个等着的地方这边没有。

所以对上的那些一样也不许诺什么。它们不许诺两边最后能合成一套，也不许诺再对下去还会对上。

那么这十二篇实际对上了什么。

不多，而且多半不在人预想的地方。

最近的一处在讲统治那一篇。两边做的是同一个动作：把一个构成条件写进概念本身，不满足的那种压根不叫这个名字。这不是两句相似的话，是一套相同的手法。

第二处在讲各司其职那一篇。同一个词，属于自己的，两边都押了最重的注，而来路正相反：他那个由天性和城的眼睛合起来定，这边那个只能本人立。同一个词两条相反的来路，比说了同一句话更值得看。

还有两处，是他自己留的口子。洞穴讲完他解释了一遍，解释完加了半句：这是不是真的，只有神知道。卷十讲魂的时候他又留了一处：那尊像捞出来敲干净以后究竟是一个样子还是好几个样子，他说他不知道。

一个人对自己讲的东西还能这样，比讲得漂亮难得多。

而分开的地方多数不是两边说了相反的话。是两边根本没在问同一个问题。

他问的是：什么是对的，怎么办才对。这边问的是：这一刀切下去，切走了什么，剩下的那些去了哪里。这两个问题可以摆在一张桌子上，可它们谁也答不了谁。

两口子吵架吵到一半，常有这么一下。一个从头到尾在说钱，另一个从头到尾在说被不被当回事。发现这一点的那一刻，架就吵不下去了，因为原来那个题目不成立。可两个人都比刚才明白了一点，而多出来的那一点，是从对方那句话的方向上来的，不是自己想出来的。

谁也没被说服。谁也没认输。

学两门手艺的人对这个熟。一个人会做木工，也会做饭。这两门谁也证不了谁，木工里没有一条能推出该往锅里放多少盐。可切菜切久了的人再去刨木头，手上会多一点东西，多的那一点说不出来是什么，也没法教给别人。

两门手艺之间发生的就是这个。它是真的，它也不能拿去当证据。

可是这个准字，值得停一下。

前面写了十二篇，每一篇都在说他这里说得准，那里说得不准。三分灵魂写成一幅同时的图，这是准的；把手艺条件写成医生心里的事，这是不准的；说他把退化讲成一代不如一代，这是不准的，得改。

于是他会问。

你说两边不能互证，我认这句话。可这十二篇里，每一处判准不准的，是拿什么定的。

不是拿我的，我那把在书里摆着，还起了名字，叫善。你那把呢。你说你没有终点，没有一个先摆好的那一头；可你判我这一句准那一句不准的时候，手上分明有个东西在量。

你承认了不能互证，这一步做得漂亮。可你没有承认另一件：从头到尾，量的都是你。

而且这一问不是抬杠。你要是答不上来，那这十二篇就不是照亮，是取用：从我这儿取一样东西，拿回你那边，用你那边的话再说一遍。

那和你说的那种独立校验，差得远。

这一问不轻，它问的是这十二篇自己的资格。

最后回到这本书的头一个字。

原文的第一个字是下去。他说，昨天我下到港口去。

这是个再普通不过的词，他自己在别处也随口用过：老人跟他说，你不常下来。

可有意思的是它后来出现的地方。全书最难的那一步，就是这个词：见过太阳的人不肯下去，而立城的人不许他留在上面，说你们每一个，轮到自己那一份，都得下去。

一本从下去开头的书，最后要人下去。这两件事都在纸上。它们合起来是不是他有意安排的，书上没写。

他还写过一句，我把它留到这儿。有人问他，你这座城到底在哪儿。他说，也许天上摆着一个样子，给愿意看的人看；至于它在不在，将来会不会在，都不要紧，看见了的那个人会照着它过日子，不照别的。

首篇末了那一句也是个许诺：只要你还肯落刀，就还有可落之处。

十二篇走下来，那句话得添半句。

还得有一个不站在你位置上的人。他说的可能是错的，你听着多半不舒服，可你自己看不见的那一块，只能等一道跟你不重合的光。

这本书到这儿就完了。它照到的地方，是柏拉图那一侧和 SAE 这一侧。

照不到的那一块在读它的人身上，而那一块的光，这本书给不了。

First, be precise about what this series has done. The title may be quoted on its own; the body will not travel with it.

The precise statement is this: the two sides can make local comparisons where they share ground, and the matches count. But neither can take its entire set of premises and make them a foundation for the other.

The first activity is illumination. The second is grounding. These thirteen essays do only the first.

Now return to the opening of Book II.

Socrates has driven Thrasymachus back. Everyone in the room probably thinks the encounter is over. Glaucon refuses.

His remark is usually paraphrased as “Refuting a person does not mean you have explained the matter.” But that is not what he actually says. His words are more amusing and harsher.

You have charmed the man, he says, like a snake charmed by a flute, and much too soon. As for what justice and injustice actually are, I have yet to hear a satisfactory proof.

The word is charmed. Not persuaded. Soothed.

A person’s falling silent does not mean that person has been answered. This is the whole book’s second opening line. The fact that nine books remain rests on it.

At the end of Book I, Socrates himself gives the other half of the same thought, more plainly than anyone.

After refuting Thrasymachus, he turns back to himself: the result of this conversation is that I have understood nothing. I do not even know what justice is. Without knowing what it is, I cannot say whether it is a virtue, let alone whether someone who possesses it lives well or badly.

A man drives his opponent back, then turns around and first says he has learned nothing. At the end of Book I, that sentence weighs more than any argument in it.

Why, then, can two accounts not prove each other?

Proof is a currency internal to an account. It circulates freely inside, but outside the door it is not accepted. A proof crossing between the two first needs a basis both recognize. Once such a basis truly exists, however, there are no longer two accounts. There is one.

Proving oneself does not work either, for a different reason. What a person can reach by questioning themselves is precisely what the apparatus in their hands can reach. What it cannot reach is precisely what they cannot bring into question. The first essay stated this, and the twelve preceding essays have kept its wording: not that self-questioning cannot succeed, but that it cannot guarantee its own completeness.

What, then, remains?

Three points of contact, all real.

The first is at the bottom. Both sides begin with negation. Draw a boundary and you are saying that this side is not that side. Plato draws boundaries throughout the book, beginning with justice not being the repayment of debts. This opening move is shared, but thinly. It guarantees only that each can understand what the other is doing, not that after understanding they can reach agreement.

The second is at the level of recognition. Both sides reach the point of treating another person as a person too. Plato does not reach it as thoroughly, but he does reach it: he wants citizens to regard one another as brothers and rulers to consider the ruled. Only between those who reach this level is conversation possible. Someone who has not, such as someone who says their own thought alone is the whole, is not at this table.

The third is outside. Both sides inhabit the same universe and encounter the same events. People die, make mistakes, want others to obey them, and repeat something that comes easily a second and third time. Nobody owns these facts. Each side encounters them and makes its own entry.

Together the three permit only one thing: local comparison.

And the matches are actual gains, not approximations. The distinction has appeared several times along the way; this is its last appearance. An actual gain means you have really acquired something. What you acquired is not invalidated because you later fail to acquire something else. Approximation means that a place waits ahead and you have come a little closer. This side can make the first claim, not the second. It has no such waiting place.

So the matches themselves promise nothing further. They promise neither that the two sides will eventually merge into one account nor that continuing the comparison will yield another match.

What, then, did these twelve essays actually match?

Not much, and mostly not where people expect.

The closest contact is in the essay on rule. Both sides perform the same act: write a constitutive condition into the concept itself, so that what fails the condition does not bear the name at all. These are not two similar statements. They are the same method.

The second contact is in the essay on doing one’s own work. Both sides put their greatest weight on the same word, one’s own, but its sources run in opposite directions. His is determined jointly by nature and the city’s eye. This side’s can be established only by the person themselves. One word with two opposite origins deserves more attention than two people saying the same sentence.

Two more contacts are openings he leaves himself. After telling the cave story, he interprets it and adds half a sentence: whether this is true, only a god knows. In Book X, discussing the soul, he leaves another opening. Once the statue is brought up and cleaned, will its form be one or several? He says he does not know.

Remaining like this toward your own account is far harder than speaking beautifully.

Most separations, meanwhile, are not cases of the two sides saying opposite things. The two are not asking the same question at all.

He asks: what is right, and how should things be done rightly? This side asks: when this cut is made, what does it take, and where does what remains go? The questions can sit at one table, but neither can answer the other.

A couple arguing often reaches such a moment. One has been talking about money all along; the other has been talking about whether they matter. When they discover this, the argument cannot continue in its previous form, because its supposed subject does not hold. Yet both understand a little more than before. That extra understanding has come from the direction of the other person’s words, not from thinking it up alone.

Neither has been persuaded. Neither has conceded defeat.

People who learn two crafts know this. Someone can do woodwork and cook. Neither craft can prove the other. Nothing in woodworking determines how much salt should go into a pan. Yet someone who has chopped vegetables for a long time brings something extra in their hands to planing wood. What that extra something is cannot be stated or taught to someone else.

That is what happens between the two crafts. It is real, and it cannot be presented as evidence.

But the word accurate deserves a pause.

For twelve essays, each has been saying that here his account is accurate and there it is not. Depicting the tripartite soul as a simultaneous picture is accurate. Rendering the conditions of a craft as something in a doctor’s mind is inaccurate. Saying he explains decline as each generation being worse than the last is inaccurate and must be corrected.

So he would ask.

You say the two sides cannot prove each other. I accept that. But throughout these twelve essays, what determines each verdict of accurate or inaccurate?

Not my measure. Mine is there in the book, with a name: the Good. Where is yours? You say you have no destination, no far end established in advance. Yet when you call this sentence of mine accurate and that one inaccurate, you plainly have something in your hand with which to measure.

You have acknowledged that mutual proof is impossible. Nicely done. But you have not acknowledged something else: from beginning to end, you are the one doing the measuring.

Nor is this mere quibbling. If you cannot answer, these twelve essays are not illumination but appropriation: taking something from me, carrying it back to your side, and saying it again in your own terms.

That is a long way from what you call independent checking.

This is no light question. It questions the standing of these twelve essays themselves.

Finally, return to the book’s very first word.

The first word in the original is “went down.” Yesterday, he says, I went down to the port.

It is an entirely ordinary word, casually used elsewhere too. The old man tells him: you do not come down often.

What is interesting is where it reappears. The book’s hardest step is this word. Those who have seen the sun refuse to go down. The city’s founders will not let them stay above: each of you, when your turn comes, must go down.

A book beginning with going down ultimately requires people to go down. Both facts are on the page. Whether their conjunction was deliberate is not stated.

There is another remark I have saved for here. Someone asks where his city actually is. Perhaps, he says, a pattern lies in heaven for whoever wishes to look. Whether the city exists or ever will exist does not matter. The person who sees the pattern will live by it, and by nothing else.

The final sentence of the first essay was a promise too: as long as you are still willing to make a cut, there will still be somewhere to make it.

After twelve essays, half a sentence must be added.

There must also be someone who does not stand where you stand. What they say may be wrong and will probably be uncomfortable to hear. But the part you cannot see yourself must wait for a light that does not coincide with you.

The book ends here. What it has illuminated lies on Plato’s side and on SAE’s side.

The unilluminated part belongs to the person reading it. The light for that part is not something this book can supply.
