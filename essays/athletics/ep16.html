把生理常数写成定义

Writing a Physiological Constant into a Definition

Han Qin (秦汉)

一 一个枪响之后的记录，与一次抢跑判罚

2022年尤金世界田径锦标赛，男子一百一十米栏决赛，艾伦的官方反应时是零点零九九秒。

他被取消了资格。

这个数字要看清楚。零点零九九秒是一个正数，表示起跑信息系统在枪响之后零点零九九秒记录到了达到检测条件的蹬力变化。但仪器记录的时间，不等于动作因何发生的答案：它既不能单独证明艾伦在枪响前毫无动作，也不能证明这次蹬力变化一定是听见枪声后的反应，而不是提前启动的动作稍后达到检测阈值。记录、动作和因果，需要分开。

可是它被判为犯规。

世界田径的规则规定，低于零点一零零秒的记录会触发“可能抢跑”的警报；随后召回运动员，由发令员结合反应时和其他可用信息检查、确认是否有人应为抢跑负责。不是机器一响就自动取消资格。艾伦最终被判抢跑，零点零九九落在了这条规则要求进一步裁决的一侧。

世界田径自己在俄勒冈2022的官方总结里，把艾伦这个零点零九九秒，描述为以最小可能的差额为非法。

这句话值得停下来。以最小可能的差额为非法。判罚并没有否认仪器记录到了零点零九九秒，而是按规则对这次起跑作了裁决。读数是真实存在的记录；它是不是枪声引发的反应，却不能从“枪响之后”四个字直接得到证明。

这就是这一篇要讲的东西，而它是这个系列里一个很纯的样本。前面几篇讲过合法却不可比的成绩，讲过整体优势是一个法律标准而不是一个测量，讲过一条精确的线划在说不出理由的位置上。这一篇讲的是一件更基本的事:一个关于人能多快反应的生理假设，被写成了一条关于什么算合法起跑的定义。而假设和定义，是两种完全不同的东西。假设可以因证据而修正；比赛里的定义则需要由机构通过正式程序改变，证据可以成为修订的理由。

这个区分不是文字游戏。作为生理假设，零点一零零秒必须接受新证据的检验；作为比赛规则，它不会随着一项研究发表就自行改变，需要正式修订。证明有些人可能反应得更快，是重新审查阈值的理由，却还不是撤销艾伦那一次判罚的充分证据：个案仍要回答动作的成因和测量条件。这个数从描述人能多快，变成规定怎样裁决；科学判断和规则适用因此不能混成一件事，但规则也没有理由永远拒绝证据。

二 假设和定义的区别

先把这个区别说清楚，因为整篇都建在它上面。

一个假设是关于世界的一个断言，它可以是真的，也可以是假的，而它的真假，由证据决定。人在听到枪声之后，最快能在多少时间里做出反应，这是一个生理学的问题，它有一个事实上的答案，而这个答案要靠实验去逼近。如果新的实验发现人能更快，那么原来的假设就被修正。假设永远对证据开放。

定义则规定怎样使用一个词，比赛规则还规定怎样据此裁决。规则一旦生效，现场裁判不能仅凭某项新研究就自行换一条线。但规则的效力和规则的合理性是两件事：前者来自有效的制定程序，后者仍要面对生理证据、测量条件和误判风险。它不会随证据自动改变，却可以、也应当在充分理由下通过正式程序修订。

零点一零零秒这个数，一开始是一个假设。它是一个关于人类反应速度下限的经验猜测，大意是，人不可能在枪响之后不到零点一秒就真正地对声音做出反应，所以任何快于这个的动作，一定是预判的，是在赌枪响的时机，而不是对枪声反应。

如果它一直是一个假设，那么它就应该随着证据更新。新的实验如果发现人能更快，这个数就该往下调。

写进规则之后，这个数字的更新就不再只是一个实验问题。实验可以对零点一零零秒提出挑战；比赛中的适用却要等正式修订。数字于是同时承担两种工作：一面与关于身体的证据相连，一面维持现场可执行的一致标准。把后一种需要说成前一种真理，就混淆了规则和知识。

这个转变，是这一篇的核心。一个对证据开放的生理假设，同时成了一条由制度维护的规则。现场裁判遵守它，不等于科学只能服从它。规则可以暂时固定，规则与事实之间的联系却不能就此切断。固定的是当下的操作标准，不是人的身体可能性。

这个区分也解释了证据为什么不会直接变成判罚。生理假设要靠事实支持，裁判决定还要靠共同规则；新证据动摇前者，不会自动让后者当场失效。可是现行有效，也不等于科学上已经得到最终证明。阈值要不要改、改到哪里，需要同时考虑研究的测量方法、提前启动与真实反应的区分，以及两类误判分别由谁承担。定义并没有逃出证据的范围，只是证据需要经过制度的修订程序，才能变成另一条现场可用的线。

三 那个数最初怎样被采用，本篇尚未找到完整记录

如果零点一零零秒来自一项明确确立它的实验，至少可以追问那项实验做了什么。可是，在本篇能找到的公开资料里，这个数字首次被采用的完整依据仍不清楚。

最常被放在这个故事开头的研究，是梅罗和科米1990年的一篇论文。而这篇论文，不是一个确立了硬性最小值的大型规范研究。

它测了八名男子短跑运动员，每人做三次最大努力的起跑，越过一块测力台，对部分人记录了腿部肌肉的肌电。后来对这篇论文的转述指出，当力量超过设定位置力量的大约百分之一百一十时，以力量定义的反应时，前腿平均约一百二十一毫秒，后腿约一百一十九毫秒。

看这两个数，一百二十一和一百一十九。它们都在一百二十毫秒附近，不是一百毫秒。

所以说梅罗和科米测出了人类最小反应是一百毫秒，不符合他们的实验。他们主要的，以力量定义的平均值接近一百二十毫秒，他们的样本是八个人，他们的操作性标准是一个力量的变化，不是第一个神经反应，不是第一个可见的动作，也不是一个群体层面的生理下限。把这项研究当作发现了一个精确的物种常数零点一零零秒，是把好几个不同的概念压进了一个。

那么零点一零零秒这个具体的数，是哪一篇论文，哪一次会议提出并采用的。

本篇尚未找到。

佩因和希布斯2007年的研究回顾说，1990年以前使用的是一百二十毫秒，后来采用一百毫秒；另一些后来的记述把这个变化放在1991年前后。但本篇还没有找到一份原始的国际田联大会记录或技术委员会决议，同时写明首次采用零点一零零秒，并列出具体实验依据。后人的回顾是一条线索，还不能替代那份决定本身。

这类档案缺口，在前文也出现过：第二篇找不到最早那份纪录申请表的原件，第十一篇未取得标枪重设的原始条款。第十篇的情况需要说得更准：1974年《Athletics Weekly》的同期报道已经能把前空翻禁令定位到国际田联罗马大会，尚未找到的是原始决议和讨论记录，而不是连会议都无法辨认。本篇没有查到的，则是零点一零零秒首次写入规则的那份决定。今天能读到规则的结论，不等于已经找到了形成它的全部过程。

这个空缺仍然值得追问。规则在现场可以精确执行，支撑它的知识却应当允许别人核对。找不到首次采用的完整记录，不等于证明当年没有依据；它意味着，今天不能只把“规则就是这样”当作“这个生理常数已经被最终证明”的替代。规则的权威与知识的根据，需要分别交代。

这一点在这个系列里已经不是第一次出现了。第三篇里，业余原则用一段并不存在的古代来为自己辩护，而那段古代是假的这件事，没有减弱它的效力。这里是一个相近的情形:零点一零零秒被当作一个科学确立的常数在使用，而它进入规则的那次决定查无实据，它常被追溯到的那篇论文测出的是接近一百二十毫秒。一个规则运行所依赖的正当性叙事，和这个规则实际的效力，是两件独立的事。叙事可以是模糊的，可以是查不到的，甚至可以是不准确的，而规则照样精确地运行，照样取消艾伦的资格。人们服从的是规则的效力，而很少去核对它的出身。

四 构自己的证据，建议改，而它没改

这一节是这一篇里最硬的一处，因为它不是外部批评者的质疑，是这个构自己委托的研究得出的结论，而这个构没有采纳。

2009年，国际田联自己委托了一项研究，直接问它的一百毫秒限制是否还有效。

这项研究测了七名芬兰国家级短跑运动员，四男三女，每人做大约五到八次起跑，结合手臂和腿部的力量测量，肌电和运动学，信号以每秒两千次采样。腿部力量变化的平均起始约为九十八毫秒，而最快的单次试验值低得多，手臂力量和肌电的变化开始得还要更早。研究者发现，一个更重的力量标准，会延迟系统给出的反应时。

这项研究的官方建议，很惊人。

它建议:不要把一百毫秒当作一个生理公理;如果继续用起跑器力量作为操作性的测量，把阈值降到大约八十到八十五毫秒。

这是国际田联自己出钱委托的研究者，在国际田联自己发表的项目里，建议把那个阈值降低。

而世界田径没有采纳这个建议。零点一零零秒保留在规则里，到2026年的技术框架里仍然在。

这个档案点很重要：机构委托的研究建议降低阈值，而规则继续保留原数。它至少说明，研究建议和规则修订并不会自动同步。七名运动员的研究并不是科学的最终表决，但它提出的问题，值得机构回应。零点一零零秒是一条可执行的制度标准，不该只凭它仍在规则里，就被说成已经锁定的生理常数。

这让假设与规则的区别更清楚。生理假设需要接受新证据，也需要比较不同研究的测量方法；一项建议本身不是自动完成的反证。规则则还要经过正式修订才能改变。两者不是互不相干，而是更新的方式不同：研究提出重新审查的理由，机构需要说明为什么改、为什么暂时不改。

这里可以看到一个和后面第十七篇相通的形状。这个构常常备好了改变的手段，证据也在手边，而它选择不用。那里是构备好了闭合的手段而人不用，这里是构备好了修正的证据而它自己不用。构有能力改，不等于构会改。

公道地说，保留标准可能有理由。改动基础判罚线有过渡成本，也要评估研究结果能否适用于认证设备、怎样区分预判与真实反应，以及不同误判的风险。但这些是需要说明和检验的理由，不是本篇已经取得的完整决策记录。我们不能因为那条线没改，就断言只是嫌麻烦；也不能因为它仍有效，就不再追问它与人的真实反应能力是否相称。

五 不同的测量，还没有给出同一个下限

也许保留零点一零零秒，部分正因为研究还没有定论。这个可能性值得认真对待。现有文献没有给出一个脱离刺激条件、反应定义和检测方法，便能直接套进所有比赛设备的公认下限。尚未得到这样的数，不等于证明生理上永远不可能有下限。

研究的分歧，不只是为同一个数给出了相反答案。不同研究还可能在测量不同的反应环节，回答不同的判罚问题。

一个方向说，零点一零零秒太严了。

2007年，佩因和希布斯用装了压电力传感器的起跑器，同步到起跑信号，测了九名运动员。五名运动员在至少一种测试条件下平均反应时低于一百毫秒，头两种条件下大约百分之二十的起跑低于一百毫秒，反应的生理成分可以低于八十五毫秒，肌电的起始可以低于六十毫秒。而且他们防住了那个明显的反驳，猜枪，他们在相关试验里把信号在起跑窗口内随机化，高速摄像显示，外部可见的动作，比最早的生理和机械反应晚得多。他们同时谨慎地指出，这并不证明一个国际田联认证的比赛设备也会给出同样的低于一百的值，因为设备的检测标准，本身是测量的一部分。

另一个方向说，零点一零零秒太松了。

布罗斯南，海斯和哈里森分析了1999到2014年精英锦标赛的反应时分布，估计修正后的下限，男子约一百一十五毫秒，女子约一百一十九毫秒。他们的论证是统计的:在比赛条件下观察到的分布里，低于这些限的值，更可能是预判的，而不是真正由刺激触发的。

还有第三个方向，关于性别和力量标准。利普斯，加莱茨基和阿什顿-米勒分析了北京2008年的官方反应时数据，论证一个统一的力量检测阈值，可能因为女性绝对的力量发展速率较低，而系统性地延迟女性被记录的反应。他们的模型估计，给女性一个大约低百分之二十二的阈值，会消除观察到的大部分性别差异。

把这三个方向放在一起，能看到一件事:文献根本不收敛到零点一零零秒。它同时在争论两件事，一件是什么生理事件应该算作反应，是第一个神经信号，还是第一个力量变化，还是第一个可见动作，这三个发生在不同的时刻;另一件是认证的起跑器如何把那个事件翻译成显示的反应时，而不同的力量标准会给出不同的翻译。

所以，零点一零零秒背后，并没有一个脱离测量方法便获得普遍认同的生理值。比赛需要精确到毫秒的操作线，相关知识却仍带着条件。两者之间有真实的反差。需要警惕的，不是精确本身，而是把规则的精确，讲成它背后所有生理问题都已经解决。

这与前几篇的线还有一层不同。这里不只是问一个数怎样推导，还要问究竟把什么叫作反应：神经活动的开始、力量达到阈值，还是可见动作的开始？统计上怎样判断预判，又是另一个问题。若把这些问题压成同一个“人类极限”，不同结果便容易被误读成对一个常数的简单争执。规则仍需选一条线，但应说清它对应的测量条件与误判风险，而不是假装所有方法都已经给出同一个答案。

2025年一项专门讨论艾伦案的统计研究，比较多项比赛的反应时分布，认为2022年世锦赛的读数分布值得重新审视，并提出真正低于零点一秒的反应并非生理上不可能。这项研究后来发表于《美国统计学家》。但正式发表也不等于为艾伦那一次动作完成了因果证明。它与2007年、2009年的实验一起，使一句“人的极限就是一百毫秒”不足以结束阈值讨论。

六 两个案子，不是一回事

关于起跑，有两个著名的取消资格，经常被放在一起讲，而它们是根本不同的两个案子。把它们分开，是理解这一篇的关键。

第一个是博尔特，2011年大邱世锦赛男子一百米决赛。

官方结果记录博尔特被取消资格，反应时是一个负数，约负零点一零四秒。负数的意思是，他在枪响之前就动了。这是一次抢跑，一次在信号之前的起动，不是一个枪响之后零点零九九的边界情况。他按2010年1月1日起生效的零容忍规则被取消，布莱克赢得了决赛。

所以博尔特这个案子，根本不测试这一篇的问题。这一篇问的是，一个枪响之后，快于零点一零零秒的真实反应，该不该算数。而博尔特的动作在枪响之前，他的案子和这个问题无关。

第二个是开头的艾伦。系统记录了枪响之后的零点零九九秒，落在触发可能抢跑警报的一侧；经过裁判检查，他最终被判抢跑。

艾伦的案子才落在这一篇关注的边界上：一个在枪响之后记录的正数，比警报阈值少千分之一秒，随后伴随一次取消资格。读数与阈值的关系很清楚，动作究竟怎样开始，却不是单靠这个正数就能回答的。

所以不能用博尔特2011年枪响前的动作，证明一个真实的低于一百毫秒的听觉反应遭到了取缔。艾伦2022年的记录则提出另一个问题：一个正的、刚低于警报阈值的读数，怎样与其他信息一起成为现场判罚的依据？这两个案子，不是一回事。

关于博尔特那个案子，还要按指控和裁决分开处理一件事。赛后流传一种说法，说是布莱克或者另一个运动员的动作，导致博尔特起动。这类解读在赛后传过，但裁决始终是博尔特抢跑，从未被正式推翻。关于挑衅的指控，和关于责任的裁决，是两回事，不能把前者抬升成一个官方认定。

艾伦本人的立场，也比官员误读了规则要微妙。他承认，在规则下，零点零九九构成抢跑。他同时主张，设备和阈值应该允许测量的不确定性，并坚持他是对听到枪声反应，而不是预判。这些是关于规则和系统是否有效的主张，不是关于成文规则说了什么的主张。他没有说规则被读错了，他说的是，这条规则和这套设备，本身应该被重新考虑。

艾伦的主张可以放在这个区分上理解。他承认现场按规则作出了判罚，同时要求重新审视阈值、设备和测量不确定性。零点零九九小于零点一零零，这是算术；它是否足以与其他信息一起支持那次抢跑认定，则是个案适用问题；阈值应该放在哪里，又是规则设计问题。不能把这三层合并，也不必把规则说成在自己的框架里永远正确，才能追问规则之外的理由。

七 定义反过来改变了人

这条定义有一个后果，超出了判罚本身:它反过来改变了运动员的行为。

零容忍规则，是2009年8月12日柏林第47届国际田联代表大会通过的。原话是，除全能项目外，任何抢跑的运动员应被取消资格。2010年1月1日生效，当时报道的代表大会投票是九十七比五十五。

在这之前，规则宽松一些。2003年之前，每个运动员各有一次抢跑的机会，第二次才被取消。2003年起，改成整场比赛只允许一次抢跑，之后任何抢跑的运动员被取消，哪怕他不是造成第一次的那个人。政策的理由之一，是减少重复起跑和它造成的比赛拖延。到2009年，变成了零容忍，一次都不许。

而这个变严的过程，可以测量地改变了运动员怎么起跑。

比较连续规则时代的精英比赛，研究发现，随着抢跑的制裁变得更严厉，平均登记的反应时逐步变慢。这和运动员采取一种更保守的起跑策略是一致的，他们宁可起得慢一点，也不冒被淘汰的风险。

这一点值得停下来看。那条线不只参与事后的判定，也可能在事前影响策略。面对取消资格的风险和测量的不确定性，运动员可能选择留出更大的安全余量，不把每一次起跑都压到自己能达到的最早时刻。规则因此可能改变被它记录的行为，而不只是被动记录行为。

于是这个定义做了一件事:它不只是度量了起跑，它塑造了起跑。运动员为了不落在定义的错误一侧，主动改变了自己的动作。那条线本来是用来判定行为的，而它反过来成了塑造行为的东西。人在向定义让步，把自己的真实反应速度，压到定义允许的范围之内。

这与第九篇形成一个对照。铅球的距离栏没有替每个人选定投法，但投掷过程当然仍受规则约束。这里，起跑阈值与制裁风险可能更直接地改变运动员在信号前后的策略。一条用于判断行为的线，同时成为影响行为的条件。它不只是记录人做了什么，也参与塑造人怎么做。

这种变化提醒我们注意度量与被度量者的关系。一把尺子可以只记录已经发生的长度；一条带有取消资格后果的规则，却可能使人事先调整行为。于是记录下来的反应时，既与身体能力有关，也可能包含对风险的规避。规则时代之间平均读数变慢，与这种策略解释相符，但不单独证明每个人故意放慢，也不排除设备、参赛人群和比赛条件的变化。真正需要辨认的，是数字怎样同时受到能力与制度环境的影响。

八 真实的读数，与尚未完结的解释

把这一篇收拢起来。

零点一零零秒承载了一个关于反应下限的生理假设，又成了比赛里的判定标准。受委托的研究曾建议考虑八十到八十五毫秒，相关研究的测量条件和结论也并不完全一致。本文讨论的规则仍保留原来的线。这显示的是研究结论与规则修订之间的距离，不是证据从此失效，更不是定义永远不可能改。问题在于，制度怎样公开说明继续使用这条线的理由，并承担它分配出去的误判风险。

这就带出这一篇的余项，而它的形态是新的。

前面几篇的余项，是没进名单的名字，是算不清的成因，是不可比的成绩，是不存在的身体，是难以安放的连续性。这一篇落在边界上的，是枪响之后、零点一零零秒以内真实记录到的蹬力变化，以及它究竟属于真实反应还是提前启动的未决问题。记录到了，不等于原因已经判明。

艾伦的零点零九九秒，是设备实际给出的记录。这一点不应抹去。但一个正的时间读数，不能单独证明没有预判，也不能自动排除测量误差。它可以确定地告诉我们，系统给出的值比阈值少了千分之一秒；至于那次动作怎样开始，需要更多证据。

而定义说，它不算。

这个判定背后，连接着一项生理假设：低于阈值的动作很可能是预判，而非听到信号后的反应。比赛需要一个可操作的标准，但按这个标准处罚艾伦，并不等于已经独立证明了他那次动作的全部因果。也不能反过来，因为判罚依靠阈值，就认定这次判罚必然错误。

记录是真的，原因却不能只由这一条线替我们回答。

这一篇最锋利的地方，不是制度否认了一个已经证实的真实反应，而是它在尚有不确定时必须划线。零点一零零秒把动作送进不同的裁决路径；若又把它讲成不可动摇的生理真理，就会让比赛里的判定标准冒充身体本身的边界。设备能记录某个变化发生在枪响后多久，却不能凭这一个数，替我们完成关于反应和预判的全部区分。

余项在这里，是记录与解释之间还没有消失的距离。制度需要一个确定的操作标准，但人的生理下限、个体差异、设备如何把动作转成数字，仍可能留有未决之处。统一标准并不自动解决这些问题。它只是使比赛得以继续，同时把某些误判的风险分配给了具体的人。

这个选择未必可以完全避免。放宽阈值，可能放进更多预判；保留阈值，也可能排除少数真正更快的反应。让每次起跑都变成一场生理学争论，比赛就无法继续；完全依赖个别观察，又可能带来另一种不一致。制度必须选一种风险分配。但它应当把这份选择说清楚，而不是把“需要一个现场可用的数”说成“已经知道人的绝对下限”。

系统可以把艾伦的记录显示为零点零九九秒，精确到千分之一秒。要知道这是不是枪声引发的真实反应，还要理解他的动作、设备的触发方式和相关测量条件。科学判断、个案证据、现场裁决，并非同一层。零点一零零秒可以作为规则里的线继续存在，同时接受新的证据和正式修订的检验。需要守住的不是对某次判罚预先翻案，而是别让一条可执行的线，假装自己已经回答了线两边所有人的真实。

1. A Reading After the Gun, and a False-Start Decision

In the men's 110-metre hurdles final at the 2022 World Athletics Championships in Eugene, Allen's official reaction time was 0.099 seconds.

He was disqualified.

Look carefully at that number. 0.099 seconds is positive: it means that 0.099 seconds after the gun, the start information system recorded a change in pushing force that met its detection criteria. But the time recorded by an instrument does not answer what caused the movement. By itself, it proves neither that Allen made no movement before the gun nor that this change in force necessarily resulted from hearing it, rather than from an anticipatory movement that reached the detection threshold a little later. The record, the movement and its cause must be kept separate.

Yet the start was ruled a violation.

World Athletics rules specify that a reading below 0.100 seconds triggers a warning of a possible false start. The athletes are then recalled, and the starter examines the reaction times together with other available information to establish whether anyone should be held responsible for a false start. A machine's alarm does not automatically disqualify someone. Allen was ultimately judged to have false-started; 0.099 fell on the side of the rule that requires further adjudication.

In its own official review of Oregon 2022, World Athletics described Allen's 0.099 seconds as illegal by the smallest possible margin.

Pause over that phrase. Illegal by the smallest possible margin. The decision did not deny that the instrument had recorded 0.099 seconds. It adjudicated the start under the rules. The reading is an actual record; whether it represents a response caused by the gun cannot be proved simply by saying “after the gun.”

That is what this essay concerns, and it is an unusually pure example within the series. Earlier essays examined performances that were legal yet incomparable, overall advantage as a legal standard rather than a measurement, and a precise line drawn at a position for which no reason could be given. This essay addresses something more basic: a physiological hypothesis about how fast a human can react has been written into a definition of what constitutes a legal start. A hypothesis and a definition are entirely different things. Evidence can revise a hypothesis; a definition in competition must be changed by an institution through a formal procedure, with evidence furnishing grounds for revision.

This distinction is not wordplay. As a physiological hypothesis, 0.100 seconds must face the test of new evidence. As a competition rule, it does not change of its own accord when a study is published; it requires formal revision. Demonstrating that some people may react faster is a reason to reconsider the threshold, but it is not sufficient evidence to overturn Allen's particular disqualification. The individual case must still address the cause of the movement and the conditions of measurement. The number has moved from describing how fast people can react to prescribing how officials must decide. Scientific judgment and the application of rules therefore cannot be collapsed into one thing, but neither do rules have reason to refuse evidence forever.

2. The Difference Between a Hypothesis and a Definition

Let us establish that distinction first, because the whole essay rests on it.

A hypothesis is a claim about the world. It can be true or false, and evidence determines which. How soon a person can react after hearing a gun is a physiological question. It has a factual answer that experiments must approach. If new experiments find that people can react faster, the original hypothesis is revised. A hypothesis always remains open to evidence.

A definition specifies how a word is used; competition rules also specify how decisions are to follow from it. Once a rule takes effect, officials at an event cannot move the line on their own merely because of a new study. But a rule's validity and its justification are two different things. The former comes from a valid rule-making procedure; the latter must still answer to physiological evidence, measurement conditions and the risk of mistaken decisions. It does not change automatically with the evidence, but it can, and with sufficient reason should, be revised through formal procedures.

The number 0.100 seconds began as a hypothesis. It was an empirical conjecture about the lower limit of human reaction time: roughly, that a person cannot genuinely respond to a sound less than a tenth of a second after the gun. Any movement faster than that must therefore involve anticipation, a gamble on when the gun will sound rather than a response to it.

Had it remained a hypothesis, it should have been updated with the evidence. If new experiments found that people could react faster, the number should have been lowered.

Once written into the rules, however, updating the number was no longer solely an experimental matter. Experiments could challenge 0.100 seconds, but its use in competition would remain until formal revision. The number thus took on two jobs at once: staying connected to evidence about the body, and maintaining a uniform standard that officials could apply on the spot. To present the second need as the first kind of truth is to confuse rules with knowledge.

That transformation is the heart of this essay. A physiological hypothesis open to evidence also became an institutionally maintained rule. Officials' compliance with it does not mean that science must obey it. A rule may be fixed temporarily, but its connection to facts cannot simply be severed. What is fixed is the current operational standard, not the possibilities of the human body.

This distinction also explains why evidence does not translate directly into a ruling. A physiological hypothesis needs factual support; an official decision also needs common rules. New evidence that unsettles the former does not automatically invalidate the latter on the spot. But being valid at present does not mean being conclusively established by science. Whether to change the threshold, and where to put it, requires considering the studies' measurement methods, the distinction between anticipation and genuine reaction, and who bears the two different kinds of error. A definition has not escaped the reach of evidence. Evidence must pass through institutional revision before becoming another line that can be used at an event.

3. The First Adoption: A Record Not Yet Recovered

If 0.100 seconds came from a study that established it, we could at least ask what that study did. In the public material available for this essay, however, the full basis for the number's first adoption remains unclear.

The study most often placed at the beginning of this story is a 1990 paper by Mero and Komi. It was not a large normative study establishing a hard minimum.

It tested eight male sprinters, each making three maximal-effort starts over a force platform, and recorded leg-muscle electromyography for some of them. Later accounts of the paper report that, when force exceeded approximately 110 percent of the force in the set position, force-defined reaction times averaged about 121 milliseconds for the front leg and 119 milliseconds for the rear leg.

Look at those two figures: 121 and 119. Both are near 120 milliseconds, not 100.

To say that Mero and Komi measured a human minimum reaction time of 100 milliseconds therefore misrepresents their experiment. Their main force-defined averages were close to 120 milliseconds. Their sample consisted of eight people. Their operational criterion was a change in force, not the first neural response, not the first visible movement, and not a population-level physiological lower limit. To treat this study as the discovery of an exact species constant of 0.100 seconds compresses several different concepts into one.

Which paper, then, proposed this particular number, 0.100 seconds, and which meeting adopted it?

This essay has not located it.

Pain and Hibbs's 2007 review says that the earlier criterion was 120 milliseconds until 1990, followed by 100 milliseconds. Other later accounts place the change around 1991. This essay has not located an original IAAF Congress record or Technical Committee resolution that both first adopts 0.100 seconds and identifies a specific experimental basis. Retrospective accounts provide a lead, not the decision itself.

Archival gaps of this sort have appeared earlier in the series. Essay 2 could not locate the original earliest record-application form; Essay 11 did not obtain the original provisions resetting the javelin. Essay 10 requires a more precise description: contemporary reporting in Athletics Weekly in 1974 already locates the somersault ban at the IAAF Congress in Rome. What remains unfound is the original resolution and record of discussion, not the identity of the meeting itself. In the present essay, the missing document is the decision that first placed 0.100 seconds in the rules. Being able to read the rule's conclusion today does not mean that we have recovered the whole process that produced it.

The gap still matters. A rule can operate precisely on the field while its evidential basis remains open to scrutiny. Not finding a complete adoption record does not prove that no grounds existed at the time. It means that “this is the rule” cannot substitute for “this physiological constant has been conclusively established.” Institutional authority and evidential justification require separate accounts.

This is not the first appearance of that pattern in this series. In Essay 3, the amateur principle justified itself by invoking an antiquity that had never existed, and the falsity of that antiquity did not diminish the principle's force. Here we encounter something similar: 0.100 seconds is used as a scientifically established constant, although the decision that put it into the rules cannot be documented, and the paper to which it is often traced measured values near 120 milliseconds. The legitimating story on which a rule's operation relies and the rule's actual force are independent things. The story may be vague, untraceable or even inaccurate, while the rule continues to operate with precision and continues to disqualify Allen. People obey the rule's force; they rarely check its origin.

4. The Construct's Own Evidence Recommended Change. It Did Not Change.

This section contains some of the essay's strongest material. It is not an objection from an outside critic, but the conclusion of research commissioned by the construct itself, a conclusion that the construct did not adopt.

In 2009, the IAAF commissioned a study explicitly asking whether its 100-millisecond limit remained valid.

The study tested seven Finnish national-level sprinters, four men and three women, each performing about five to eight starts. It combined arm and leg force measurements, electromyography and kinematics, sampling signals 2,000 times per second. The average onset of leg-force change was about 98 milliseconds, while the fastest individual trial values were much lower. Changes in arm force and electromyographic signals began earlier still. The researchers found that a higher force criterion delayed the reaction time reported by the system.

The study's official recommendation was striking.

It recommended that 100 milliseconds not be treated as a physiological axiom. If starting-block force continued to serve as the operational measurement, the threshold should be reduced to approximately 80–85 milliseconds.

These were researchers paid and commissioned by the IAAF, recommending a lower threshold in a project published by the IAAF itself.

World Athletics did not adopt that recommendation. The 0.100-second line remained in the rules and was still present in the 2026 technical framework.

This is an important documentary contrast: commissioned research recommended a lower threshold, while the rule retained the existing number. At minimum, research recommendations and rule revisions do not move automatically together. A seven-athlete study is not science's final vote, but its questions deserve an institutional response. The continued existence of an operational standard does not make it a conclusively established physiological constant.

This clarifies the distinction between a hypothesis and a rule. A physiological hypothesis must face new evidence and comparisons between measurement methods; one recommendation is not an automatic refutation. A rule also needs formal revision before it changes. The two are connected but updated differently: research gives reasons for reconsideration, and the institution should explain why it changes the rule or retains it for now.

A pattern appears here that connects with Essay 17. The construct often has the means of change ready, with evidence close at hand, and chooses not to use them. There, the construct has prepared the means of closure and people do not use them; here, it has prepared the evidence for correction and does not use it itself. A construct's capacity to change does not mean that it will.

To be fair, retaining a standard may have reasons. Changing a basic adjudicative threshold has transition costs. It also requires assessing whether study results transfer to certified equipment, how anticipation is distinguished from genuine response, and the risks of different errors. These are reasons to explain and test, not a complete decision record recovered here. An unchanged line does not prove mere administrative convenience, just as a valid rule does not end questions about its fit with human reaction.

5. Different Measurements Have Not Supplied One Agreed Lower Limit

Perhaps the line is retained partly because the research remains unsettled. That possibility deserves attention. The literature does not supply an agreed lower limit that can be transferred to every competition device regardless of stimulus conditions, response definitions and detection methods. Not having such a number is not proof that a physiological lower bound can never exist.

The disagreement is not simply opposite answers to one numerical question. Different studies may measure different stages of response and address different questions about adjudication.

One direction says that 0.100 seconds is too strict.

In 2007, Pain and Hibbs tested nine athletes using starting blocks fitted with piezoelectric force sensors synchronized to the starting signal. Five athletes had mean reaction times below 100 milliseconds in at least one test condition; about 20 percent of starts in the first two conditions were below 100 milliseconds. The physiological component of the response could be under 85 milliseconds, and electromyographic onset could be under 60 milliseconds. They also guarded against the obvious objection, guessing the gun: in the relevant trials, the signal was randomized within the starting window. High-speed video showed that externally visible movement occurred substantially later than the earliest physiological and mechanical responses. They cautiously noted, however, that this did not prove an IAAF-certified competition device would yield the same sub-100 values, because the device's detection criterion is itself part of the measurement.

The other direction says that 0.100 seconds is too lenient.

Brosnan, Hayes and Harrison analysed reaction-time distributions from elite championships between 1999 and 2014, estimating corrected lower limits of approximately 115 milliseconds for men and 119 milliseconds for women. Their argument was statistical: in the distributions observed under competition conditions, values below those limits were more likely to represent anticipation than a genuine response triggered by the stimulus.

There is a third direction as well, concerning sex and force criteria. Lipps, Galecki and Ashton-Miller analysed the official reaction-time data from Beijing 2008 and argued that a uniform force-detection threshold might systematically delay women's recorded responses because their absolute rate of force development is lower. Their model estimated that a threshold approximately 22 percent lower for women would eliminate most of the observed sex difference.

Taken together, these three directions reveal something: the literature does not converge on 0.100 seconds. It is debating two things at once. One is which physiological event should count as the response: the first neural signal, the first change in force or the first visible movement. Those occur at different times. The other is how certified starting blocks translate that event into a displayed reaction time. Different force criteria produce different translations.

Behind 0.100 seconds there is no physiological value universally agreed independently of the measurement method. Competition needs an operational line precise to milliseconds, while the relevant knowledge remains conditional. That is a genuine contrast. The danger is not precision itself, but presenting precision in a rule as proof that all the physiological questions behind it have been settled.

There is another layer here. The question is not only how to derive a number, but what counts as the response: the onset of neural activity, force reaching a threshold, or visible movement. A statistical judgment about anticipation is another task again. Compressing them into one “human limit” makes different findings look like a simple quarrel over one constant. Rules still need a line, but should explain its measurement conditions and error risks rather than imply that every method has supplied the same answer.

A 2025 statistical study addressing Allen's case compared reaction-time distributions across competitions, questioned the 2022 World Championships readings and argued that genuine sub-0.100-second reactions need not be physiologically impossible. The study was subsequently published in The American Statistician. Publication does not, however, establish what caused Allen's particular movement. Alongside the 2007 and 2009 experiments, it makes “the human limit is 100 milliseconds” insufficient to end discussion of the threshold.

6. Two Cases That Are Not the Same

Two famous start-related disqualifications are often discussed together, although they are fundamentally different cases. Keeping them separate is essential to understanding this essay.

The first is Bolt in the men's 100-metre final at the 2011 World Championships in Daegu.

The official result records Bolt's disqualification and a negative reaction time of approximately −0.104 seconds. A negative number means that he moved before the gun. This was a false start, an initiation before the signal, not a borderline 0.099 seconds after it. He was disqualified under the zero-tolerance rule effective from 1 January 2010, and Blake won the final.

Bolt's case therefore does not test the question posed here. This essay asks whether a genuine response after the gun, faster than 0.100 seconds, should count. Bolt's movement occurred before the gun; his case does not address that question.

The second case is Allen's. His recorded 0.099 seconds after the gun fell on the side triggering a possible-false-start warning. Following the officials' examination, he was disqualified.

Allen's case lies on this essay's boundary: a positive reading after the gun, one thousandth below the alarm threshold, followed by disqualification. The relationship between reading and threshold is clear. The positive number alone does not establish how the movement began.

Bolt's pre-gun movement in 2011 therefore cannot demonstrate the prohibition of a genuine auditory response below 100 milliseconds. Allen's 2022 reading raises a different question: how does a positive value just below the warning threshold combine with other information to support an on-field decision? The cases are not the same.

One further issue in Bolt's case requires separating allegations from adjudication. After the race, a claim circulated that movement by Blake or another athlete had caused Bolt to start. Such interpretations circulated, but the decision remained that Bolt had false-started and was never officially overturned. An allegation of provocation and a ruling on responsibility are different things. The former cannot be elevated into an official finding.

Allen's own position is also subtler than a claim that officials misread the rules. He acknowledged that 0.099 constituted a false start under the rules. At the same time, he argued that the equipment and threshold should accommodate measurement uncertainty, and maintained that he had responded to hearing the gun rather than anticipated it. These are claims about the adequacy of the rule and system, not about what the written rule says. He did not say the rule had been read incorrectly. He said that the rule and the equipment themselves should be reconsidered.

Allen's position can be understood through this distinction. He accepted that officials had ruled under the existing rules while calling for reconsideration of the threshold, equipment and measurement uncertainty. That 0.099 is below 0.100 is arithmetic. Whether the reading, together with other information, supports the particular false-start finding is a question of application. Where the threshold should sit is a question of rule design. These are different layers. We need not declare a rule always right within its own framework in order to question its justification.

7. The Definition Changes People in Return

The definition has a consequence beyond adjudication itself: it changes athletes' behaviour in return.

The zero-tolerance rule was adopted on 12 August 2009 at the 47th IAAF Congress in Berlin. Its wording stipulated that, except in combined events, any athlete responsible for a false start would be disqualified. It took effect on 1 January 2010. The reported Congress vote was 97 to 55.

Previously, the rules had been more lenient. Before 2003, each athlete was allowed one false start and was disqualified for a second. From 2003, only one false start was allowed for the entire race; any athlete false-starting thereafter was disqualified, even if that athlete had not caused the first one. One rationale was to reduce repeated starts and the delays they caused. In 2009, this became zero tolerance: not even one was allowed.

That tightening measurably changed the way athletes started.

Comparing elite competitions across successive rule regimes, researchers found that average recorded reaction times became progressively slower as false-start sanctions grew more severe. This is consistent with athletes adopting a more conservative starting strategy, preferring a slightly slower start to the risk of elimination.

Pause here. The line participates not only in later adjudication but may also shape strategy beforehand. Faced with disqualification risk and measurement uncertainty, an athlete may leave a larger safety margin rather than push every start to the earliest possible moment. The rule can therefore shape the behaviour it records, not merely record it passively.

The definition thus does something: it does not merely measure the start; it shapes it. To avoid ending up on the wrong side of the definition, athletes deliberately alter their movements. A line originally meant to judge behaviour becomes something that shapes behaviour. People yield to the definition, bringing their actual reaction speed within the range it permits.

This contrasts with Essay 9. A distance column in shot putting does not choose a technique for each athlete, but the throwing process is still regulated. Here the starting threshold and sanction risk may more directly change athletes' strategies around the signal. A line for judging behaviour also becomes a condition influencing it. It does not merely record what people do; it helps shape how they act.

This brings the relationship between measurement and the measured into view. A ruler may simply record an existing length; a rule backed by disqualification may prompt adjustment in advance. Recorded reaction times can therefore reflect both bodily capacity and avoidance of risk. Slower averages across rule regimes are consistent with that strategic explanation, but do not alone prove deliberate slowing by every athlete or exclude changes in equipment, fields and conditions. The question is how ability and institutional conditions jointly shape the numbers.

8. A Real Reading, and an Unfinished Explanation

Let us draw the essay together.

The number 0.100 seconds carries a physiological hypothesis about the lower limit of reaction time and has also become a standard for adjudication in competition. Commissioned research once recommended considering 80–85 milliseconds; related studies also differ in measurement conditions and conclusions. The rules discussed here retain the original line. This reveals the distance between research findings and rule revision. It does not mean that evidence has lost its force, still less that definitions can never change. The question is how institutions publicly explain their reasons for retaining the line and take responsibility for the risks of error it distributes.

This brings us to the essay's remainder, which takes a new form.

In earlier essays, the remainders were names absent from a list, causes that could not be disentangled, incomparable performances, bodies that did not exist, and continuities difficult to accommodate. At the boundary in this essay lie changes in pushing force actually recorded after the gun but within 0.100 seconds, together with the unresolved question of whether they represent a genuine response or an anticipatory start. A recorded event is not the same as an established cause.

Allen's 0.099 seconds is a reading actually produced by the equipment. That should not be erased. But a positive time reading cannot by itself prove the absence of anticipation or automatically exclude measurement error. It can tell us with certainty that the system's value was one thousandth of a second below the threshold. How that movement began requires more evidence.

And the definition says it does not count.

Behind that judgment lies a physiological hypothesis: movements below the threshold are likely to be anticipatory rather than responses to hearing the signal. Competition needs an operational standard. But penalizing Allen under that standard does not independently establish the complete causal history of his movement. Nor can we reason in reverse that, because the decision relies on a threshold, it must have been wrong.

The record is real. Its cause cannot be answered for us by that line alone.

The sharpest point here is not that an institution denied a genuine response already proven to be real. It is that the institution must draw a line while uncertainty remains. The 0.100-second threshold sends movements down different adjudicative paths. If it is also presented as an unshakeable physiological truth, a competition standard begins impersonating the body's own boundary. Equipment can record how long after the gun a particular change occurs. That one number cannot complete the entire distinction between reaction and anticipation for us.

The remainder here is the distance between record and explanation that has not disappeared. The institution needs a definite operational standard, while human physiological limits, individual differences and the equipment's translation of movement into numbers may remain partly unresolved. A uniform standard does not automatically solve those questions. It merely allows competition to proceed while allocating some of the risk of mistaken decisions to particular people.

This choice may not be wholly avoidable. Relaxing the threshold may admit more anticipation; retaining it may exclude a small number of genuinely faster reactions. If every start became a physiological debate, competition could not continue. Complete reliance on individual observation might produce another kind of inconsistency. The institution must choose a distribution of risk. But it should make that choice explicit, rather than turning “we need a number that works on the field” into “we already know the absolute human limit.”

The system can display Allen's reading as 0.099 seconds, precise to a thousandth. Knowing whether it was a genuine response caused by the gun also requires understanding his movement, the equipment's triggering mechanism and the relevant measurement conditions. Scientific judgment, evidence about an individual case and adjudication on the field are not the same layer. The 0.100-second line can remain in the rules while continuing to face new evidence and the test of formal revision. What must be defended is not a predetermined reversal of a particular decision. It is the refusal to let an enforceable line pretend it has already answered the reality of everyone on either side.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
