零点二四秒

The Missing 0.24 Seconds

Han Qin (秦汉)

一 同一次奔跑，两个数

海因斯1968年在墨西哥城跑出的那个一百米，在后来的纪录进程里有两个数。

一个是九秒九，手计时。一个是九秒九五，全自动计时。

这是同一次奔跑。不是两场比赛，不是两个人，是海因斯在那一天那一条跑道上跑完的同一个一百米。有两块系统在同时计它，一块由人按表，一块由机器触发，它们给出了两个数。

史密斯的二百米也是这样，十九秒八和十九秒八三。埃文斯的四百米也是这样，四十三秒八和四十三秒八六。都是一次奔跑，两个数。

这件事看起来很小，一个是取到十分之一，一个是取到百分之一，好像只是精度不同。但它不是精度不同。九秒九和九秒九五不是同一个数的两种写法，一个粗一个细。它们是两个不同的测量系统，在测同一件事的时候，给出的两个不同的结果。

这一点必须先立住，因为后面所有的东西都从它长出来。一个数字要有意义，必须先知道它是用什么量出来的。九秒九和九秒九五之所以不同，不是因为其中一个错了，而是因为一个是人的手指按出来的，一个是机器的电路触发的，人的手指和机器的电路对同一个瞬间的把握本来就不一样。所以问海因斯到底跑了多快，这个问题没有一个脱离测量方式的答案。他跑了九秒九，也跑了九秒九五，取决于你听谁的。

前一篇的结尾停在两本账上。官方纪录表不区分海拔，统计学家私下维护一份低海拔名单，两本账并存，而官方那一本从不承认另一本。那是关于海拔的两本账。这一篇讲的是关于计时的两本账，而它比海拔那一处更根本，因为它就在成绩本身的那个数字里。海因斯这一跑，同时属于两本账，记着两个数。

问题是，纪录表只能有一个数。

于是需要一个东西，把这两本账连起来。那个东西是一个常数，零点二四秒。

二 一个针对特定位置的平均数

零点二四这个数是从哪里来的。

后来的田径文献一致把它追溯到一项关于专家级手计时员的研究，这项研究与1972年慕尼黑奥运会和吉森大学有关。可以查到的最好的概述说，那项研究发现，当计时员或者发令员靠近发令枪时，手计时与自动计时的平均差值约为零点一八秒;在一百米直道上约为零点二四秒;在二百米的弯道上约为零点二六秒。

这里最要紧的一件事，就藏在这三个数里。

零点二四不是一个放之四海皆准的常数。它是针对一种特定几何位置的平均值。计时员站在一百米直道的终点，得到的平均差是零点二四;站在二百米弯道那一端，是零点二六;靠近发令枪，是零点一八。位置变了，数就变了。

也就是说，这个常数从它诞生的那一刻起，就不是一个常数，是一个随位置变化的量的某一个取值。人们后来把其中一个取值，零点二四，拿出来当作了那个常数。

挑中的偏偏是中间那个值，这件事也值得看一眼。零点一八对应靠近发令枪的位置，零点二六对应弯道，零点二四在中间，但它也对应前面说的那种一百米直道条件。不能仅仅因为它在三个数中间，就断定它是折中选出来的。真正的问题是迁移：一场具体的比赛有自己的发令位置、计时员位置和反应差异。把一种条件下的平均差固定下来，拿去连接不同条件下的成绩，能方便比较，却不能保证每一次都恰好补对。

而它是一个平均值，这一点还有一层更要紧的意思。

平均值是对一批数取的。这批数里，有的比零点二四大，有的比零点二四小。平均值本身不描述其中任何一个具体的数，它描述的是这批数的中心。把一个平均值当作常数用，等于用这批数的中心去代替其中的每一个，而这批数原本是散开的。

这批数散得有多开，是这一篇的关键问题，而这个问题在这一轮拿不到直接的答案。从直接检视的1970年代原始论文里，查不到那项慕尼黑与吉森研究的样本量，完整分布，或者标准差。后来的转述把那些数字描述为平均值，但没有提供原始的表格，没有样本数，没有标准差，没有置信区间，也没有明确说明发表出来的那个统计量究竟是均值，还是按条件汇总的平均。

所以现在能确定的是，零点二四是一个平均值，针对一个特定的位置;不能确定的是，生成这个平均值的那批数，原本散得有多开。

这个空缺不是无关紧要的。一个平均值有没有用，取决于它背后那批数散得开不开。如果那批数都紧紧挤在零点二四附近，那么用零点二四代替每一个，误差很小。如果那批数散得很开，那么零点二四对其中许多个都不准。而恰恰是这个决定它准不准的东西，今天拿不到。

三 散开的那批数

原始论文拿不到，但后来有别的研究测过类似的东西，而那些研究把散开的程度露了出来。

有一项针对八十一名美国大学橄榄球运动员跑四十码冲刺的研究，经验丰富的计时员所记手计时与电计时的平均差为零点二二秒，差值的标准差为零点零七秒；经验不足的计时员，两个数分别为零点二六秒和零点零八秒。这项研究用手离开地面触板来启动电子计时，用红外装置记录终点，和发令枪启动的一百米全自动计时不是同一种布置。

先看那两个平均值，零点二二和零点二六。它们都落在零点二四附近。这似乎在支持零点二四是个不错的数。

再看那两个标准差，零点零七和零点零八。这两个数提醒我们，平均差之外还有离散。

标准差零点零七不是说所有差值都被关在零点一五到零点二九之间。这两个数只是平均值上下各一个标准差的位置，实际差值还可能落在外面。它说明的是，在这项研究的条件下，给每次手计时加上同一个平均数，并不会把每次误差都消掉。至于一百米，零点一五秒足以在一场竞争激烈的比赛中分开金牌和远离领奖台的成绩；但这只是提醒我们小差距的重要，并不是把这项四十码研究变成所有一百米计时的误差界限。

把这个问题放到具体的历史里就更清楚了。许多金牌和银牌之间的差距不到零点一秒，细小差距当然值得认真对待。但这项研究并不推出一个适用于所有一百米成绩的零点一五秒门槛。两个换算成绩之间的小差距能说明多少，还要看原始计时的条件、误差及两者是否可比；不能只凭这个平均常数就宣布名次已经精确到百分之一秒。这个常数能把两块布缝在一起，却不能仅凭一道缝保证细到百分之一秒的名次。

所以这项研究至少说明：一种条件下测得的平均差，可以描述那组计时的整体倾向，却不能直接充当每一次计时的准确修正。若没有那次比赛更完整的独立记录，就不能仅凭平均数知道它究竟差了多少。这也不能反过来证明，另一年代、另一种计时布置里的常数已经得到全面验证。

这里出现了一个和前一篇相通的结构，值得点一下。

前一篇里，比蒙那一跳的问题是，四个变量合成一个数之后无法还原。这里的问题是反的，一个平均值套在每一个个体上之后，无法知道对这一个套得准不准。前者是多个原因合成一个结果无法拆开，后者是一个平均代替多个个体无法验证。两者都是关于这个构在处理不确定性时的办法，而两者的办法都是接受一个无法验证的东西，好让账能记下去。

这句话可以说得更一般一些，因为它是这整个系列反复出现的东西。一个要持续记账的构，迟早会撞上它无法消除的不确定。它可以有两种态度。一种是停下来，不确定的东西一律不记，那样它会记得很少。另一种是接受一个无法验证的处理方式，把不确定压进一个数或者一个常数里，继续记。几乎所有能长期运转的构都选了后者。代价是，它账上的每一个数里，都封着一点它自己也没法验证的东西。

关于这个常数还有一个流行的说法，说手计时永远比电计时快零点二四秒。这个说法不成立。手计时可能快，也可能慢，取决于计时员的行为，训练，预判和赛道几何。历史上那个常数是一个平均的修正，不是关于每一场比赛的机械真理。有长期从事这一行的统计者明确指出，那个普适的快零点二四的故事是一个误解;他同时承认，零点二四对训练有素的计时员而言，作为一个平均的约定是大致可用的。

这两句话必须一起说。这个常数作为约定是可用的，作为对个体的描述是不可靠的。它同时是这两样东西，而这正是它一直被使用又一直被质疑的原因。

四 为什么手计时总是被凑成整的

有一件事让手计时和电计时的差别更大，而它常常被忽略。

手计时的取整规则是向上进位，不是就近取整。

在可查的国际田联规则文本里，手计时的径赛成绩读到下一个更长的零点一秒。如果用的是百分之一秒的表或者手动操作的数字计时器，任何在第二位小数上不以零结尾的成绩，都要进位到下一个更长的零点一秒，比如十秒一一要变成十秒二。

这条规则的意思是，手计时的成绩总是被向慢的方向调整。十秒一一明明更接近十秒一，但它被写成十秒二。这不是四舍五入，这是只入不舍。

这个规则有一个容易被忽略的后果。它意味着手计时的成绩在被写下来的时候，已经经过了一次向慢的调整，而这次调整发生在任何换算之前。所以当人们说手计时比电计时快零点二四的时候，那个被拿来比较的手计时成绩，其实已经是一个被向慢凑过的数了。真实的手指按出来的读数，和最后写进成绩单的数，中间还隔着一次进位。这一层常常被忽略，而它使得手计时和电计时之间的真实差距，比表面上看到的更复杂。

为什么要这样。手计时会受到发令反应和终点判断的影响，某些研究和换算惯例所面对的是平均偏快的手计时。但不能把它解释成“偏早启动、偏晚停止”：那样反而会把用时记长。向上进位规定的是读数如何报出，只朝较慢的一侧取值；单凭这条规则，还不能断定它就是为某一种反应偏差设计的补偿。

在采用这类零点二四秒换算的场合，手计时的成绩里便叠了两层处理。第一层是向上进位，把它往慢里凑。第二层是那个零点二四的常数，再往慢里加一截。两层都朝同一个方向，都在把手计时的数往电计时的数上靠。

电计时的取整也是向上，但取到更细的单位。现行规则要求一万米及以内的径赛，成绩读到，换算到，记录到下一个更长的零点零一秒，除非已经正好;超过一万米的径赛记到下一个更长的零点一秒;公路赛记到下一个更长的整秒。

把这些放在一起，能看到这个构在取整上的一个一贯做法:它宁可让成绩显得慢一点，也不让它显得快一点。所有的进位都是向上的，都是向慢的。

这背后有一个道理，而这个道理和纪录这个构的性质有关。一条纪录一旦被批准，它就成了后来所有人要超越的标杆。如果一条纪录因为取整而显得比实际偏快，那么后来的人要超越的就是一个虚高的目标，这对后来的人不公平。反过来，如果一条纪录因为取整而显得比实际偏慢一点，那么它顶多是让当前这个人吃一点亏，而不会连累后来的所有人。

所以这个构在面对取整这个不可避免的选择时，选择让误差落在当前这个人身上，而不落在后来的所有人身上。这是一个有意的偏向，而它偏向的是账簿的连续性，不是当前这个成绩的准确。

这个选择背后其实有一个关于公平的判断，而这个判断是不对称的。让当前这个人吃亏，亏的是一个人，一次。让后来的所有人面对一个虚高的标杆，亏的是很多人，很多次。两害相权，这个构选择集中伤害当前这一个，以保护后来的许多个。这是一个可以辩护的选择，但它不是中立的。它在当前的运动员和未来的运动员之间，系统性地站在了未来那一边。而站在未来那一边，就是站在账簿那一边，因为账簿是给未来看的。

五 千分之一秒在里面，但不出来

现代的计时系统内部用到千分之一秒，但它不作为纪录的官方精度。

可查的世界田联规则分别规定了全自动终点摄影的采样要求，以及发令信号与计时启动之间恒定且不超过一毫秒的延迟。这里的启动延迟不是系统分辨率，也不是整套测量的总误差；它们是不同的技术指标。然而公布出来的成绩，仍然按项目长度读到零点零一秒或者零点一秒。

这里有一件奇怪的事。这个构有能力测到千分之一秒，它却选择只公布到百分之一秒。它把自己的一部分精度扣在了系统内部，不放出来。

为什么不用千分之一秒作为纪录精度。从可查的规则文本里，找不到一份解释这件事的原始条文。规则只规定了不用，没有说为什么不用。实际的理由几乎可以肯定是标准化和历史的连续，但这个理由在可核实的原文里没有被明确说出来。

不过这件事本身的形状是可以看清的，而它和前面几篇接得上。

一个构公布的精度，不是它能达到的最高精度，是它选择承担的那个精度。测到千分之一，公布到百分之一，中间那一位被主动放弃了。放弃它有一个明显的好处:百分之一秒这个精度，和此前几十年的成绩是接得上的。

这里可以看到一个一般的道理:一个已经运转起来的度量系统，它的精度不是越高越好，而是要和它自己的历史相容。一个孤立的测量，精度当然越高越好。但一个要和几十年的旧数据放在一起比较的测量，如果精度突然提高一档，它就和所有旧数据之间产生了一道无法弥合的缝。所以这个构的精度被它自己的过去锁住了。它不能随技术的进步而随时提高公布精度，因为每提高一档，就作废一次它和历史的可比。1968年海因斯那个九秒九五就是百分之一秒。如果现在改用千分之一秒，那么此前所有以百分之一秒记录的纪录，都要面对一个尴尬:它们的下一位是未知的，而新的成绩有下一位。两者之间又会出现一次不可比。

所以这个构宁可不用那一位精度，也要保住和过去的可比。它在这里做的选择，和第五篇里高原成绩的选择是一样的:为了保住一张表的前后连续，主动放弃一部分本可以有的准确。精度和连续不能同时最大化，它选了连续。

而千分之一秒并没有消失。它在系统内部，在需要判定名次的时候被用到。也就是说，这个构在名次上使用它能达到的最高精度，在纪录上使用一个和历史接得上的较粗精度。判定谁赢用一套，写进账簿用另一套。这两套精度并存，又是一处两本账。

六 不是换算，是扶正

1976年，国际田联同意，短跑世界纪录只接受自动计时，从1977年1月1日生效。

这件事最容易被误解的地方是，人们以为这个构把旧的手计时纪录拿来，加上零点二四，换算成了电计时的等价物。

它没有这样做。

这次切换之后，旧成绩的处理落进三个不同的类别。第一类，如果同一场比赛当时存在一个全自动计时，那么保留那个电计时读数作为电计时基准。第二类，如果那个项目仍然有资格进行手计时，那么保留为手计时或电计时纪录。第三类，如果那个项目本身被废除了，那么从纪录资格里删除。

看第一类，这是最能说明问题的一类。

海因斯那个一百米，旧的手计时列表一直沿用1977年之前的九秒九。而一旦有了单独的电计时列表，它记的是海因斯在墨西哥城1968年10月14日跑出的九秒九五，作为电计时纪录。然后注明，从1977年1月1日起，百分之一秒的电计时成为强制。

所以这个构做的不是把九秒九加零点二四换算成九秒九五。它做的是，把同一场比赛里当时就已经存在的那个九秒九五扶正。

这个区别很要紧。九秒九五不是从九秒九算出来的，它本来就在。海因斯那一跑，当时就有两块系统在计，九秒九是手计时那块给的，九秒九五是电计时那块给的。这个构在1977年做的，是宣布从今往后以电计时那块为准，于是那个一直存在但此前不是官方的九秒九五，变成了官方。

史密斯的二百米，埃文斯的四百米，处理方式完全一样。十九秒八三本来就在，四十三秒八六本来就在。这个构不是换算了旧账，是在旧账旁边，把早就并存的另一个数扶正，然后作废旧账所属的那一列。

这和第二篇里那句话是同一个结构。那个构学会的第一件事是往里加，它没有被教过怎么减。这里也一样:它不是把旧数改成新数，那是一次减法加一次加法;它是把一个一直存在的数的地位提升，同时让旧数所在的整列失效。它做的仍然是承认某个已经存在的东西，而不是抹掉某个东西再换上另一个。

这些做法呈现出一种偏好：改变账面时，尽量通过承认已有读数或调整类别资格来完成，而不是把变化都写成对旧账的否定。第二篇引的那句早期授权条文写的是登记；但不能仅凭那一句，就断定后来从未形成注销权限。扶正一个已经存在的数，是登记；把它提升为官方，是登记；让旧的那一列失去资格，则可以让整个类别退出现行比较。对这些例子，我们可以这样理解：改变账面不必每次都宣布过去记错了。这不等于已经证明机构没有别的权限。

这里必须交代那个至今拿不到的东西。一份完全穷尽的，逐项列出1977年之前每一个短跑，跨栏和接力纪录及其确切法律处置的行政清单，需要直接查阅那几年的国际田联纪录册或者代表大会记录。可查的材料清楚地建立了这个模式，并给出了具体的旗舰例子;但它们没有提供一份官方的合并处置表。所以这个模式是确定的，逐条的处置在这一轮拿不全。

至于那些非公制的短跑和跨栏项目，处理方式又不一样，它们是被从纪录列表里删除，而不是换算。世界田径的历史页说，除了英里之外的英制项目被1976年的决定删除了。它们没有被换算成公制的等价物，它们直接从账上消失了。第七篇会专门讲这种删除。

七 一条线缝两块布

现在可以把零点二四这个常数放回它的位置上看清楚。

它是一条缝合线。

手计时是一块布，电计时是另一块布。这两块布是用不同的机器织出来的，它们的经纬不一样，一块的最小格子是零点一秒，另一块的最小格子是零点零一秒。而这个构需要一张完整的表，把两块布拼在一起。零点二四就是把这两块布缝起来的那条线。

缝合线有一个性质:它在整体上是牢的，在每一针上是松的。一条平均意义上零点二四的缝合线，能把两块布大致对齐，让它们拼成一张看得过去的表。但落到每一针，某一次具体的手计时到底该对到电计时的哪个点，这条线并不准，它可能偏这边零点零五，可能偏那边零点零五。整体对齐，局部错位。

这个性质其实是一切用平均值去处理个体的做法所共有的。一张按平均体型做的桌子，对整个人群是合适的，对某一个特别高或者特别矮的人是不合适的。一条按平均差值定的换算常数，对整批计时是对齐的，对某一次具体计时是错位的。区别只在于，桌子的错位一眼能看见，而计时的错位看不见，因为你没有那个人真正的电计时读数去对照。看不见的错位比看得见的更危险，因为它会被当作没有错位。

这就是这个常数一直被使用，又一直被批评的原因。

批评它的一方说，一个固定的常数对真实世界里的计时人群往往太粗糙了。有观察者比较过某地高中决赛的电计时和名义上的后备手计时，平均差跑到零点四零到零点四五秒，远大于零点二四;他据此指出，在那样的环境里用零点二四，会实质性地扭曲达标和分组。这不是同行评议的田径期刊研究，是一个长期从事统计与解说的直接观察者的实证批评。

为它辩护的一方说，对训练有素的计时员，以及对那些仍然必须把手计时和电计时排在一起的系统的行政可比性而言，这些固定常数仍然站得住。这是排名系统，全能项目换算和长期沿用的学界指南里所嵌的逻辑。

这两方其实都对，而它们对的是不同的东西。

能够看清两方各自对在哪里，是这一篇想要达到的地方，因为它比选边站要接近真相。很多关于测量的争论，表面上是一方说准一方说不准，实际上是一方在说整体一方在说个体，而这两件事可以同时成立。零点二四在整体上可用，在个体上不可靠，这不是一个有待解决的矛盾，这就是一个平均值用在个体上时的常态。承认这个常态，比坚持它要么可靠要么不可靠，要诚实得多。

辩护的一方对的是，作为一个把两块布缝在一起的办法，零点二四在整体上是可用的，而这个构确实需要这样一个办法，否则两块布拼不成一张表。批评的一方对的是，落到每一针，这条线不准，而当计时员的水平偏离了当初生成那个平均值的专家人群时，这条线可能偏得很厉害。

一个公道的总结是，这个常数仍然有制度上的用处，但对个体的精度很弱。它是一条在整体上牢，在局部上松的缝合线，而这两个性质都是真的。

值得注意的是这个构后来的实际反应。它没有发表一份专门为这个常数辩护或者修订它的官方文件。它的实际反应是别的:为纪录要求更多的全自动计时，要求经过认证的起跑信息系统，以及把换算公式大体上留给国家，学界，排名和全能项目的场合，而不是核心的世界纪录批准。

也就是说，这个构没有去改进那条缝合线，它做的是让核心的那张表尽量不再需要缝合。核心纪录只认电计时，一块布，不用缝。而缝合线继续存在于外围，那些仍然必须把两块布拼在一起的地方。这又是前一篇那个模式:面对一个无法彻底解决的问题，这个构不去解决它，而是把它挪到构的外围，让核心那部分不再碰它。

八 手计时还合法，只是不再算数

今天，手计时在世界田径的规则下仍然合法，是被承认的官方计时方法之一。

现行规则承认三种官方计时方法:手计时，来自终点摄影系统的全自动计时，以及应答器计时。所以手计时作为一种产生官方成绩的方法，并没有从合法性里消失。用于不要求全自动计时的比赛，或者作为有全自动计时时的后备手段，它仍然有效。

手计时失去的，不是合法性，是在短项目上参与纪录批准的对等地位。现行规则要求，八百米及以内的项目，纪录必须用全自动计时。

这个状态值得停下来看，因为它是这个构处理一件旧东西的典型方式。

它没有废除手计时。废除是一个决定，要写进条文，要面对所有仍在使用手计时的低级别比赛。它做的是保留手计时的合法，同时抽掉它在最要紧的那件事上的资格。手计时还在，它可以计出一个官方成绩，但那个成绩进不了短项目的纪录表。

这和第三篇里业余那个词的结局是同一个手法。业余没有在某一天被宣布废除，它是被一句新定义掏空的，业余者就是遵守规则的人，这个词从此不再指向任何外部的东西。手计时也没有被废除，它是被抽掉了在纪录上的资格，这个方法从此只在不要紧的地方还算数。构处理一样它想放弃的东西时，很少直接砍掉它，更多是把它架空，让它还在，但不再管用。

这种架空比直接废除高明的地方在于，它几乎不引起反抗。直接废除手计时，所有依赖手计时的低级别比赛都会立刻受影响，都会有理由反对。而只是抽掉它在纪录上的资格，那些低级别比赛照常用手计时，它们不受影响，自然也不反对;真正在乎纪录的顶级比赛，本来就用得起电计时，也不反对。于是这个构在没有引起任何一方强烈反对的情况下，悄悄改变了手计时的地位。架空一样东西，往往比废除它遇到的阻力小得多，因为它让每一个具体的人都觉得自己没受影响。

而那条缝合线，零点二四，今天仍然在制度中被使用。

美国田径仍然指示全能项目的使用者，对四百米以下的手计时项目加零点二四，对四百米加零点一四。美国田径的青少年报名指南仍然解释，手计时的达标成绩如何根据全自动计时标准，用零点二四，零点一四，或者不加，视距离而定。全国高中联合会仍然发布零点二四的手计时到全自动计时换算规则。

所以那两个数，零点二四和零点一四，今天仍然在缝。它们缝的不再是核心的世界纪录那张表，而是外围那些必须把手计时和电计时排在一起的表:达标，分组，排名，全能。核心那张表已经变成了一块布，而这些外围的表还是两块布拼的，还需要那条线。

回到海因斯那一跑。

九秒九和九秒九五，同一次奔跑的两个数。这个构最后选择了九秒九五，让手计时那个九秒九退到旧列表里，然后连那个旧列表一起，随着项目规则的变化慢慢失去资格。不能把这个选择说成已经取得了毫无误差的真实用时；机器给出的也是一套测量系统里的读数。但这并不否认全自动计时能够提高准确性。这里还有一个制度上的决定：从某个时刻起，哪一套测量结果被统一采用，作为正式比较的标准。

这不只是把一个数测得更准，也是选定共同采用的标准。

前一篇里，一个成绩的成因无法被分开。这一篇里，一个成绩的数值取决于用哪块布来量。两篇合起来说的是同一件事:这个构记下的每一个数，都不是那件事本身，是那件事经过某一套装置，某一个常数，某一次取整，某一个被选定的标准之后，留在账簿上的一个印子。印子是准的，可以核对，可以比较。而印子和那件事之间隔着的那些东西，一样都不会进入账簿。

这一层是这一整个前六篇可以收在一起的地方。这个构从古代那份只有名字的名单起步，一路建起了越来越精密的度量，报纸，秒表，电计时，终点摄影。每前进一步，它记下的东西就更准一分。可是无论多准，它记下的始终是一个印子，不是那件事。名字不是那个人，九秒九五是对海因斯那次用时的记录，不是那次奔跑的全部，八米九零不是比蒙那一跳的全部成因。构越精密，那个印子越清晰，而印子和事情之间那道缝始终在，只是变得越来越细，细到几乎看不见，于是人们以为它不在了。它一直在。

尺子还没有量准，它仍旧在量。

I. One Run, Two Numbers

In the subsequent progression of records, the hundred metres Hines ran in Mexico City in 1968 carries two numbers.

One is 9.9 seconds, hand-timed. The other is 9.95 seconds, fully automatically timed.

This was the same run. Not two races, not two people, but the same hundred metres that Hines completed on that track that day. Two systems were timing it simultaneously: one operated by a person's hand, the other triggered by a machine. They produced two numbers.

The same was true of Smith's two hundred metres: 19.8 and 19.83. And of Evans's four hundred metres: 43.8 and 43.86. In each case, one run, two numbers.

It looks like a small matter. One is recorded to a tenth, the other to a hundredth, as though the difference were simply precision. But it is not. 9.9 and 9.95 are not two ways of writing the same number, one coarse and one fine. They are two different results produced by two different measuring systems observing the same event.

This point must be established first, because everything that follows grows out of it. For a number to mean anything, we must first know what measured it. 9.9 and 9.95 differ not because one is wrong, but because one was produced by a human finger and the other triggered by an electronic circuit. A finger and a circuit do not apprehend the same instant in the same way. The question of how fast Hines actually ran therefore has no answer independent of the method of measurement. He ran 9.9, and he ran 9.95, depending on which system you listen to.

The previous essay ended with two ledgers. The official record table does not distinguish altitude, while statisticians privately maintain a low-altitude list. Both coexist, and the official one never acknowledges the other. Those were two ledgers of altitude. This essay concerns two ledgers of timing, a more fundamental division because it lies inside the number that constitutes the performance itself. Hines's run belongs simultaneously to two ledgers, under two numbers.

The problem is that the record table can contain only one number.

Something was therefore needed to connect the two ledgers. That something was a constant: 0.24 seconds.

II. An Average for a Particular Position

Where did the number 0.24 come from?

Later athletics literature consistently traces it to a study of expert hand timers associated with the 1972 Munich Olympics and the University of Giessen. The best available overview says the study found an average difference of approximately 0.18 seconds between hand and automatic timing when the timer or starter was close to the starting pistol; approximately 0.24 seconds on the hundred-metre straight; and approximately 0.26 seconds on the two-hundred-metre bend.

The most important point is concealed in those three numbers.

0.24 is not a universal constant. It is an average for a particular geometrical arrangement. With the timer at the finish of the hundred-metre straight, the average difference is 0.24; at the far end of the two-hundred-metre bend, 0.26; close to the pistol, 0.18. Change the position and the number changes.

In other words, from the moment it came into being, this constant was not a constant at all, but one value of a quantity that varies with position. People subsequently selected one of those values, 0.24, and made it the constant.

The selected value happens to lie between the other two: 0.18 near the pistol, 0.26 on the bend, and 0.24 in between. But 0.24 also corresponds to the particular straight-hundred-metre arrangement described above. Its position in the middle does not establish that it was chosen as a compromise. The real question is transfer. Each race has its own starting arrangements, timer positions and human responses. Fixing an average from one set of conditions makes different results easier to connect; it does not guarantee the right correction for every individual observation.

And its being an average has a further, more important implication.

An average is calculated from a set of numbers. Some are greater than 0.24, others smaller. The average does not describe any one of those individual numbers. It describes their centre. Using an average as a constant means substituting the centre of the set for every member of it, when the members were originally dispersed.

How widely those numbers were dispersed is the crucial question in this essay, and in this round of research no direct answer was available. The original papers from the 1970s directly examined did not yield the sample size, full distribution, or standard deviation of the Munich–Giessen study. Later accounts describe the figures as averages, but provide no original tables, sample counts, standard deviations, or confidence intervals. Nor do they specify clearly whether the published statistic was a mean or an average aggregated by condition.

What can therefore be established is that 0.24 is an average for a particular position. What cannot be established is how widely dispersed the numbers that generated it originally were.

That gap is not trivial. Whether an average is useful depends on how widely the underlying numbers are dispersed. If they are clustered tightly around 0.24, substituting 0.24 for each produces little error. If they are widely scattered, 0.24 will be inaccurate for many of them. And precisely the information that would determine its accuracy is unavailable today.

III. The Numbers That Scatter

The original paper is unavailable, but later studies have measured similar things, and those studies reveal the extent of the scatter.

In a study of eighty-one US college football players running forty-yard sprints, the mean difference between hand and electronic times was 0.22 seconds for experienced timers, with a standard deviation of 0.07 seconds. For inexperienced timers, those figures were 0.26 and 0.08 seconds. Electronic timing began when the athlete's hand left a ground touchpad and ended at an infrared beam. This was not the same arrangement as gun-started fully automatic timing for the hundred metres.

Look first at the two averages, 0.22 and 0.26. Both fall close to 0.24. This seems to support 0.24 as a reasonable number.

Now look at the two standard deviations, 0.07 and 0.08. They remind us that an average difference does not eliminate variation.

A standard deviation of 0.07 does not confine all differences to the interval from 0.15 to 0.29. Those are merely the positions one standard deviation below and above the mean; observations can fall outside them. Under this study's conditions, adding the same average to every hand time would not remove each individual error. In a closely contested hundred metres, 0.15 seconds can separate gold from a finish well away from the podium. That illustrates the importance of small margins; it does not turn this forty-yard study into an error bound for every hundred-metre race.

The question becomes clearer in its historical setting. Many gold and silver medals have been separated by less than a tenth of a second; small margins deserve serious attention. But this study does not establish a universal 0.15-second threshold for hundred-metre performances. How much a small gap between two converted times tells us depends on the original timing conditions, errors and comparability. The average correction alone cannot certify a ranking to the hundredth. The constant can stitch two pieces of cloth together; the seam alone cannot guarantee a ranking to the hundredth.

The study establishes at least this much: a mean difference measured under particular conditions can describe that set of observations without supplying the exact correction for each one. Without a fuller independent record of a particular race, the mean alone cannot tell us its error. Nor does this validate a constant from another period and another timing arrangement.

A structure shared with the previous essay appears here, and deserves a brief mention.

With Beamon's jump, the problem was that four variables combined into one number could not be disentangled afterward. Here the problem runs in reverse: after an average is applied to every individual, we cannot tell whether it fits this particular one. In the former case, several causes combine into one result and cannot be separated; in the latter, one average stands in for many individuals and cannot be verified. Both concern the construct's way of handling uncertainty. In both, it accepts something unverifiable so that the ledger can keep being written.

We can put this more generally, because it recurs throughout the series. A construct that keeps accounts over time will sooner or later encounter uncertainty it cannot eliminate. It can take one of two positions. It can stop, refusing to record anything uncertain, in which case it will record very little. Or it can accept an unverifiable procedure, compress uncertainty into a number or a constant, and continue. Almost every construct capable of sustained operation chooses the latter. The price is that every number in its ledger seals within it a little something that the construct itself cannot verify.

Another popular claim about the constant is that hand timing is always 0.24 seconds faster than electronic timing. This is false. A hand time can be faster or slower, depending on the timer's behaviour, training, anticipation, and the geometry of the track. The historical constant is an average adjustment, not a mechanical truth about every race. A statistician with long experience in the field explicitly identifies the universal “0.24 faster” story as a misconception, while acknowledging that 0.24 is broadly serviceable as an average convention for well-trained timers.

Both statements must be made together. The constant is usable as a convention and unreliable as a description of an individual case. It is both at once, which is precisely why it continues to be used and continues to be questioned.

IV. Why Hand Times Are Always Rounded Up

Something else widens the difference between hand and electronic timing, and it is often overlooked.

The rounding rule for hand timing is upward rounding, not rounding to the nearest value.

In the accessible IAAF rules, hand-timed track performances are read to the next longer tenth of a second. If the watch measures hundredths, or a manually operated digital timer is used, any performance whose second decimal place is not zero must be rounded to the next longer tenth. For example, 10.11 becomes 10.2.

The rule means that hand times are always adjusted in the slower direction. 10.11 is plainly closer to 10.1, yet it is written as 10.2. This is not rounding to the nearest value. It only rounds upward, never down.

The rule has an easily overlooked consequence. By the time a hand-timed performance is written down, it has already undergone an adjustment toward a slower time, before any conversion takes place. Thus, when people say hand timing is 0.24 seconds faster than electronic timing, the hand time being compared is already a number rounded in the slower direction. Another upward rounding separates the actual reading produced by the timer's finger from the number finally entered on the results sheet. This frequently overlooked layer makes the real difference between hand and electronic timing more complicated than it appears.

Why do this? Hand timing is affected by responses to the start and judgments at the finish. Some studies and conversion conventions address hand times that are faster on average. But starting early and stopping late would lengthen a recorded time, not shorten it. Upward rounding specifies how a reading is reported, always toward the slower value. The rule alone does not establish that it was designed to compensate for one particular response bias.

Where this 0.24-second conversion is used, two procedures are superimposed on a hand time. The first is upward rounding, making it slower. The second is the constant of 0.24, adding another stretch of time. Both act in the same direction, bringing the hand-timed number closer to the electronic one.

Electronic times are also rounded upward, but to a finer unit. Current rules require track performances over distances up to and including 10,000 metres to be read, converted, and recorded to the next longer hundredth of a second unless already exact. Track races longer than 10,000 metres are recorded to the next longer tenth; road races to the next longer whole second.

Taken together, these rules reveal a consistent approach to rounding within the construct: it would rather make a performance look slightly slower than slightly faster. Every rounding goes upward, toward slower times.

There is a reason for this, connected to the nature of the record as a construct. Once a record is ratified, it becomes the benchmark everyone afterward must surpass. If rounding makes a record appear faster than it really was, subsequent athletes face an artificially high target. That is unfair to them. Conversely, if rounding makes it appear slightly slower, at worst it disadvantages the present athlete rather than everyone who follows.

Confronted with the unavoidable choice of how to round, the construct therefore chooses to place the error on the present individual rather than on all future individuals. This is a deliberate bias toward continuity of the ledger, not accuracy of the current performance.

Behind that choice lies an asymmetrical judgment about fairness. Disadvantaging the current individual harms one person, once. Making everyone afterward face an artificially high benchmark harms many people, many times. Weighing the two harms, the construct concentrates the injury on the one to protect the many who follow. The choice is defensible, but it is not neutral. Between present and future athletes, it systematically takes the future's side. Taking the future's side means taking the ledger's side, because the ledger is written for the future.

V. Thousandths Stay Inside

Modern timing systems use thousandths of a second internally, but thousandths are not the official precision of records.

The accessible World Athletics rules separately specify photo-finish sampling requirements and a constant delay of no more than one millisecond between the starting signal and activation of the timing system. That trigger delay is neither the system's resolution nor a bound on its total measurement error; these are different technical quantities. Published results, however, are still read to a hundredth or a tenth, depending on the length of the event.

There is something strange here. The construct can measure to a thousandth, yet chooses to publish only hundredths. It withholds part of its precision inside the system.

Why not use thousandths as the precision for records? No original provision explaining this could be found in the accessible rules. They specify that it is not done, without explaining why. The practical reasons are almost certainly standardisation and historical continuity, but those reasons are not explicitly stated in the verifiable original text.

The shape of the decision itself is nevertheless clear, and connects with the preceding essays.

The precision a construct publishes is not the greatest precision it can achieve, but the precision it chooses to take responsibility for. Measuring to thousandths and publishing hundredths means deliberately relinquishing the intervening digit. There is an obvious benefit: hundredths remain compatible with several preceding decades of performances.

Here we can see a general principle. Once a measuring system is established, greater precision is not necessarily better. It must remain compatible with its own history. For an isolated measurement, greater precision is of course preferable. But a measurement intended for comparison with decades of older data opens an unbridgeable gap with those data if its precision suddenly increases by one order. The construct's own past thus locks its precision in place. It cannot raise the precision of published results whenever technology advances, because every additional digit invalidates another degree of historical comparability. Hines's 9.95 in 1968 was measured to a hundredth. If thousandths were now adopted, every previous record written in hundredths would face an awkward situation: its next digit is unknown, whereas the new performances have one. A new incomparability would emerge.

The construct therefore prefers to forgo that digit in order to preserve comparison with the past. Its choice is the same as the choice concerning altitude in Essay Five: to maintain continuity within a single table, it deliberately relinquishes some accuracy that could otherwise be available. Precision and continuity cannot both be maximised. It chooses continuity.

The thousandths have not disappeared. They remain inside the system, used when necessary to determine places. The construct thus uses its highest available precision for placings, and a coarser, historically compatible precision for records. One precision decides who wins; another writes the ledger. The coexistence of these two precisions is yet another instance of two ledgers.

VI. Not Conversion, but Elevation to Official Status

In 1976 the IAAF agreed that only automatically timed sprint performances would be accepted as world records, effective from 1 January 1977.

The easiest misunderstanding is to imagine that the construct took the old hand-timed records, added 0.24, and converted them into electronic equivalents.

It did not.

After the transition, the treatment of older performances fell into three categories. First, where a fully automatic time already existed for the same race, that electronic reading was retained as the automatic benchmark. Second, where the event still qualified for hand timing, the performance remained a hand-timed or automatically timed record. Third, where the event itself was abolished, it was removed from record eligibility.

Consider the first category, which makes the point most clearly.

For Hines's hundred metres, the old hand-timed list continued to use the pre-1977 mark of 9.9. But once a separate automatically timed list existed, it recorded the 9.95 Hines ran in Mexico City on 14 October 1968 as the electronic record, noting that automatic timing to hundredths became mandatory on 1 January 1977.

The construct was therefore not adding 0.24 to 9.9 to turn it into 9.95. It was elevating to official status the 9.95 that had existed at the time of the same race.

The distinction matters. 9.95 was not calculated from 9.9. It was there already. Two systems timed Hines's run at the time: the hand-timing system supplied 9.9, the electronic system 9.95. In 1977 the construct declared that the electronic system would henceforth be authoritative. The 9.95 that had always existed but had not previously been official became official.

Smith's two hundred metres and Evans's four hundred were treated in exactly the same way. 19.83 was already there; 43.86 was already there. The construct did not convert the old ledger. It elevated a long-coexisting number beside that ledger, then invalidated the column to which the old entry belonged.

This has the same structure as the observation in Essay Two: the first thing the construct learned was how to add; it was never taught to subtract. Here, too, it did not change the old number into a new number, which would have required subtraction followed by addition. It raised the status of an existing number while invalidating the entire column containing the old one. Once again, it acknowledged something already there rather than erasing one thing and replacing it with another.

These practices suggest a preference: alter the ledger through recognition of an existing reading or a change of eligibility, rather than describe every alteration as a repudiation of the old account. The early authorising provision quoted in Essay Two speaks of registration. That sentence alone cannot establish that no power of cancellation developed later. Recognising an existing number is registration; promoting it to official status is registration; making an old column ineligible can remove a category from current comparison. In these examples, changing the ledger need not always mean declaring that the past was recorded wrongly. This does not establish that the institution lacked other powers.

We must identify what remains unavailable. An exhaustive administrative list specifying the precise legal disposition of every pre-1977 sprint, hurdle, and relay record would require direct consultation of IAAF record books or congress proceedings from those years. Available material clearly establishes the pattern and provides prominent examples, but not an official consolidated disposition table. The pattern is therefore established; the item-by-item treatment could not be fully recovered in this round of research.

Non-metric sprint and hurdle events were treated differently again: they were removed from the record lists rather than converted. World Athletics' historical page says that the 1976 decision removed imperial events other than the mile. They were not converted into metric equivalents. They simply disappeared from the ledger. Essay Seven will address this kind of deletion specifically.

VII. One Thread, Two Pieces of Cloth

We can now return the constant of 0.24 to its proper place and see it clearly.

It is a stitching thread.

Hand timing is one piece of cloth; electronic timing another. They were woven on different machines, with different warps and wefts. The smallest square in one is a tenth of a second, in the other a hundredth. But the construct needs a complete table, joining the two pieces together. 0.24 is the thread that stitches them.

A stitching thread has a particular property: it is strong overall but loose at each stitch. A thread of 0.24 on average can align the two pieces approximately, making a reasonably presentable table. At each individual stitch, however, it cannot accurately establish which point on the electronic cloth corresponds to a particular hand time. It may miss by 0.05 on one side or 0.05 on the other. Overall alignment, local misalignment.

This property belongs to every practice that uses averages to handle individuals. A table made for the average body suits the population as a whole but not a particularly tall or short person. A conversion constant based on the average difference aligns a batch of timings but misaligns an individual timing. The only difference is that the table's misfit is immediately visible, whereas the timing misfit is invisible, because there is no true electronic reading for that athlete against which to check it. Invisible misalignment is more dangerous than visible misalignment, because it is taken for the absence of misalignment.

That is why the constant continues to be used and continues to be criticised.

Its critics say a fixed constant is often too crude for real populations of timers. One observer compared electronic times with nominal backup hand times at local high-school finals and found average gaps of 0.40–0.45 seconds, much larger than 0.24. He argued that using 0.24 in such settings materially distorts qualification and seeding. This was not a peer-reviewed athletics-journal study, but an empirical criticism by a direct observer with long experience in statistics and commentary.

Its defenders say fixed constants remain defensible for well-trained timers and for the administrative comparability required by systems that still have to rank hand and electronic times together. This is the logic embedded in ranking systems, combined-events conversions, and long-established academic guidelines.

Both sides are in fact right, but about different things.

Understanding what each side is right about is the destination of this essay, because it takes us closer to the truth than choosing a side. Many disputes about measurement appear to pit claims of accuracy against claims of inaccuracy. In fact, one side is speaking about aggregates and the other about individuals, and both claims can be true. 0.24 is usable overall and unreliable individually. That is not a contradiction waiting to be resolved. It is the normal condition of applying averages to individuals. Acknowledging that condition is more honest than insisting that the constant must be either reliable or unreliable.

The defenders are right that, as a means of stitching two pieces of cloth together, 0.24 is broadly usable. The construct genuinely needs such a means, or the two pieces cannot form one table. The critics are right that the thread is inaccurate at each stitch, and may be very far off when the timers' competence departs from that of the expert population that originally produced the average.

A fair conclusion is that the constant retains institutional usefulness but offers weak precision for individuals. It is a thread that holds firmly overall and loosely in particular places. Both properties are real.

The construct's subsequent practical response deserves attention. It did not issue an official document specifically defending or revising the constant. Its actual response was different: require fully automatic timing for more records, require certified start-information systems, and largely leave conversion formulas to national, academic, ranking, and combined-events settings rather than the central process of world-record ratification.

In other words, instead of improving the stitching thread, the construct made its central table need as little stitching as possible. Core records recognise only electronic timing: one piece of cloth, no seam required. The thread survives at the edges, wherever two pieces must still be joined. This repeats the previous essay's pattern: faced with a problem it cannot fully solve, the construct moves it to the periphery so that its core no longer has to encounter it.

VIII. Hand Timing Is Still Legal; It Just No Longer Counts

Today hand timing remains legal under World Athletics rules, one of the recognised official timing methods.

Current rules recognise three methods: hand timing, fully automatic timing obtained from a photo-finish system, and transponder timing. Hand timing has therefore not lost its legitimacy as a way of producing an official result. It remains valid in competitions that do not require fully automatic timing, or as a backup where fully automatic timing is used.

What hand timing lost was not legality, but equal standing in record ratification for shorter events. Current rules require fully automatic timing for records in events up to and including eight hundred metres.

This situation deserves a pause, because it is characteristic of the construct's treatment of something old.

It did not abolish hand timing. Abolition would have required a decision, a written provision, and a confrontation with all the lower-level competitions still using it. Instead, it preserved hand timing's legality while stripping it of eligibility for the most important purpose. Hand timing remains. It can produce an official result, but that result cannot enter the short-event record table.

This is the same manoeuvre as the fate of the word “amateur” in Essay Three. Amateurism was not declared abolished on a particular day; a new definition hollowed it out. An amateur was someone who obeyed the rules, and the word ceased to refer to anything outside them. Hand timing, too, was not abolished. It was stripped of record eligibility, leaving it to count only where less was at stake. When the construct wants to relinquish something, it seldom cuts it off directly. More often it empties it of power, allowing it to remain without letting it do the work it once did.

This hollowing-out is more effective than direct abolition because it provokes almost no resistance. Abolishing hand timing would immediately affect every lower-level competition dependent on it, giving each a reason to object. Merely removing record eligibility leaves those competitions free to continue as before. Unaffected, they have no reason to object. Top-level competitions that actually care about records can already afford electronic timing, and do not object either. The construct quietly changes the status of hand timing without provoking strong opposition from either side. Emptying something of power often meets far less resistance than abolishing it, because each particular person feels personally unaffected.

And the stitching thread, 0.24, remains in institutional use today.

USA Track & Field still instructs combined-events users to add 0.24 to hand times for events shorter than four hundred metres, and 0.14 for four hundred metres. Its youth-entry guidance still explains how hand-timed qualifying marks relate to fully automatic standards, using 0.24, 0.14, or no addition depending on distance. The National Federation of State High School Associations still publishes a 0.24 hand-to-fully-automatic conversion rule.

The two numbers, 0.24 and 0.14, are therefore still stitching. They no longer stitch the central world-record table, but the peripheral tables in which hand and automatic times must coexist: qualification, seeding, rankings, combined events. The central table is now one piece of cloth. These peripheral tables still join two pieces and still need the thread.

Return to Hines's run.

9.9 and 9.95: two numbers for the same run. The construct eventually chose 9.95, relegating the hand-timed 9.9 to an old list that gradually lost eligibility as event rules changed. That choice did not deliver a perfectly error-free elapsed time; a machine, too, supplies a reading within a measurement system. This does not deny that fully automatic timing can improve accuracy. There is also an institutional decision: from a particular point onward, which system's readings will be used consistently as the official basis of comparison?

This was not only a matter of measuring more accurately. It was also the selection of a shared standard.

In the previous essay, a performance's causes could not be separated. In this one, its numerical value depends on the cloth used to measure it. Together they say the same thing: every number recorded by the construct is not the event itself, but an imprint left in the ledger after the event has passed through a particular apparatus, a particular constant, a particular rounding operation, and a particular chosen standard. The imprint is accurate, checkable, comparable. None of the things separating it from the event enters the ledger.

This is where the first six essays can be gathered together. The construct began with an ancient list containing names alone and gradually built ever more precise means of measurement: newspapers, stopwatches, electronic timing, photo finishes. Each advance made what it recorded a little more accurate. Yet however accurate it became, it still recorded an imprint, not the event. The name is not the person. 9.95 records the duration of Hines's run; it does not contain the whole event. 8.90 metres is not the full set of causes of Beamon's jump. The more precise the construct becomes, the clearer the imprint. But the gap between imprint and event remains, growing finer and finer until it is almost invisible, and people suppose it has disappeared. It has always been there.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
