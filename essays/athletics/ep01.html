只有名字，没有数字

Names Without Numbers

Han Qin (秦汉)

一 一份用来纪年的名单

今天能看到的最完整的古代奥林匹亚胜者表，保存在优西比乌的编年著作里。它从第一届排到第二百四十九届，名录的主干通常按每届单程赛跑的冠军姓名排列。单程赛跑指的是跑完赛道全长的那一项，它是奥林匹亚最古老的项目。名录也有少数例外和其他项目的附记；这里要看的是它用胜者给届次定位的基本方式，而不是一张逐项列出成绩的现代结果表。

这张表不是为竞技造的。它是历法。

古希腊没有共同的纪年方式。每个城邦按自己的执政官排年，月份的名字各不相同，一件发生在两个城邦之间的事，双方各有各的年份说法。奥林匹亚赛会四年一次，间隔稳定，而且是全希腊都承认的场合。于是每四年出现一次的那个冠军姓名，变成了一个所有人都能用的时间坐标。要说清某件事发生在什么时候，可以说它发生在某人赢得单程赛跑的那一届。

把胜者姓名整理成连续目录的人，一般认为是埃利斯的希庇亚斯，时间大约在公元前400年。第一届的冠军名叫科罗伊博斯，后来的传统把那一届定在公元前776年。从希庇亚斯往后经过若干代编年家，再经阿非利加努斯，最后进入优西比乌的著作;而优西比乌的原本已经不存，今天所据的是亚美尼亚译本与拉丁传统。

有一件事需要放在最前面。希庇亚斯动手编表的时候，距离他要编的第一届已经过了将近四百年。他不太可能凭空造名字，现代研究倾向于认为他利用了埃利斯的地方传统，奥林匹亚的圣地记述，竞技论著，以及散落在地方史与颂胜诗里的零星胜者信息。但这些东西被凑成一条连续的线之前，并不是一条线。最早那几十届的条目，今天的处理办法是可用而需要分层审查，既不全盘接受，也不全盘丢弃。质疑的理由有三类，时间跨度太长，早期希腊的书写与档案保存条件有限，以及传统内部存在不一致和后来修补的痕迹。保萨尼阿斯关于项目何时增设的记述自己就暴露出这种张力。

克里斯特森还提醒过一件容易被忽略的事。不是所有提到过某位优胜者的文本都等于胜者名录。一首为某人写的颂胜诗提到他赢过奥林匹亚，一篇地方史提到某位先祖曾经夺冠，这些都是关于胜利的记述，却不是一份按届排列的名录。名录的特征不在于它记了谁赢，而在于它承诺每一届都有一个条目，并且这些条目按同一种方式排列。零散的记述和连续的目录之间隔着的不是数量，是形式。希庇亚斯做的正是那一步，把前者变成后者。这一步一旦完成，那些原本各说各话的胜利记述就获得了一个共同的位置，同时也就丧失了它们各自原来的形状。

也就是说，这个构最早的产物是一次追认。它把四百年间散在各处的名字排成一条线，然后这条线开始被当作时间本身来使用。整个希腊世界后来用它给自己的历史定位，而它自己是在被使用的四百年后才被造出来的。

公元前776年这个起点也不是从档案里读出来的，是被推算出来的。有了一份连续的名录，只要知道其中任何一届的绝对年代，往前数就能得到第一届。所以那个年份是名录的产物，不是名录的依据。十九世纪到二十世纪初，这个起点还引发过一轮相当激烈的争论，今天多数人把它当作一个传统年代来使用，而不是当作一个已经证实的历史节点。一个用来给别的事情定年的工具，自己的第一年是算出来的。

一个构生产什么，取决于它被造出来做什么。这张表被造出来是为了给年份定位，那么它需要的只是为每一届写下一个冠军姓名；即使同一个人再次夺冠，届次仍能区分年份。它不需要知道那个人跑了多久。它甚至不需要那个人比上一届跑得快。今天回头读这张表，不能把没有用时一栏，一概解释为那一栏曾经齐全、只是后来散失了。这份名录没有把逐届用时作为固定字段来系统生产；但这并不证明古代从未有人测量，也不证明别处从未留下过相关记载。

二 名单只需要一个名字

在奥林匹亚这样的桂冠赛会里，大会一级的奖赏基本只有胜者才有。没有第二名，没有第三名，没有任何形式的名次序列。克劳瑟把这件事概括得很清楚，希腊主要赛会只有第一名得到大会的奖赏;但他同时提醒，如果把只有胜利算数说得太绝对也会失真，因为文学作品和碑铭里偶尔仍然会出现非冠军的成绩。

最有名的一例是阿尔基比亚德。在关于西西里远征的辩论里，他向雅典人陈述自己的资格，提到他在奥林匹亚的战车赛中同时拿到了第一，第二和第四。修昔底德把这段话记了下来。希罗多德那里也偶尔会提到某人得了第二。

所以第二名并非不可说。要紧的区别在别处。被说出来和被记下来是两件事。阿尔基比亚德的第二名和第四名进入了修昔底德的文本，因为它们在那一天对他的政治论证有用;它们没有进入任何一份名录，因为名录不需要它们。同一个事实，在叙述里有位置，在制度里没有位置。

这个区别在后来会变得非常重要，所以值得在它还这么简单的时候先认出来。叙述是有选择的，它记什么取决于讲述的人当时需要什么。制度不选择，它按预先定好的格式往里填，填不进去的一律在外面。叙述的容量看上去更大，因为它什么都能装;可它不承诺装，所以它装什么完全取决于偶然。阿尔基比亚德那一次如果没有去劝说雅典人出兵，那两个名次就不会有人知道。制度的容量看上去更小，但它承诺，所以在它答应装的那一小块里，东西不会因为没人提起而消失。

这是这个构最干净的一处。它不封杀第二名，不否认第二名跑过，甚至不禁止第二名到处去讲。它只是没有为第二名准备位置。余项在这里不是被删掉的那一栏，是从来没有被画出来的那一栏。后来的整个田径史都会反复回到这种形态，构清不干净的东西，常常不是它清除的，而是它压根没有设过位置去接的。

奖赏的形态也跟着这一点走。赛会现场给的是一顶野橄榄冠，没有别的。真正的报酬在回到本邦之后，现金，终身由公家供膳，剧场前排的座位，免除若干义务。色诺芬尼有一首诗专门抱怨这件事，说一个跑得快的人得到的东西远超过一个有智慧的人。普鲁塔克记载梭伦为雅典人定过赏格，奥林匹亚的胜者五百德拉克马，伊斯特米亚的胜者一百德拉克马。这条记载的出处明确，但普鲁塔克距离梭伦太远，具体数额的可靠性在学界有争议，把它当作古雅典奖励制度的可信保存和把它当作晚期道德化的立法叙事，两种读法都有人持。

这里先不展开业余与职业这一组词，它属于后面的篇目。眼下只需要记住奖赏的分工。赛会给的东西没有价格，野橄榄冠不值钱，它贵重是因为它不能被买;城邦给的东西全部有价格，而且价格明确到可以写进法令。一件不可标价的东西和一笔可以标价的钱，在同一个人身上前后相接，中间的转换环节就是那个被记进名单的名字。

不过无论数额是否可靠，方向是清楚的。城邦付钱买的是一个名字，不是一个数。名字可以兑换成钱，可以兑换成座位，可以兑换成一整个家族在此后几代人里被提起的方式。数没有出现在这个兑换里，因为它不存在。

三 四条不一样长的跑道

四大圣地的赛道不一样长。这不是文本推定，是考古实测，依据是起跑线，距离标志和赛道边沟的遗迹。奥林匹亚的赛道约一百九十二米，德尔斐约一百七十七米半，尼米亚约一百七十八米，伊斯特米亚后期的赛道约一百八十一米，而且有过由六百尺改为五百五十九尺的考古解释。

奥林匹亚比德尔斐长出将近十五米。这个差距不小。如果这四个地方的单程赛跑成绩曾经被计过时，那么这些时间彼此之间没有任何比较的意义。

尼米亚的材料最能说明问题。米勒在那里的发掘刊布和现场说明都强调，赛道是六百当地尺，合一百七十八米左右;而赛道上每一百尺一个的标志，实测间距并不规整。米勒因此直接说明，古人布置这些标志不是为了制造现代意义上的精确成绩。

这一条证据的分量在于它是实物。有人可以说古代文献里没有成绩记载，别人可以回答文献散失了;但赛道就在那里，标志的间距可以量。不能把这些间距不规整的标志，直接读成现代等距而精确的测量刻度。已知距离的不等距标志当然也可以用于测量；这里要辨认的是，它们实际承担什么任务。在一个石工技术足以做到等距的时代，间距却没有统一，这至少说明，不能未经核查就把现代刻度的用途搬给它们。它支持的，是这里并不以产生可跨场地核对的精确成绩为首要目的，而不是古人从未作过任何测量。

需要在这里停一下，因为最容易出错的判断就在这一句上。跑道长度不统一，不是这个构的缺陷。缺陷是相对于目的而言的失败。跨圣地比较从来不是目的，所以四条跑道不一样长，对当时的任何人都不构成任何问题。没有人抱怨过这件事，因为没有人需要它是一样的。

有一个现象可以把这件事说得更死。同一个运动员在同一年里可能先后在几个圣地出赛，四大赛会的周期是错开的，一个志在巡回全胜的人必须走遍这四个地方。也就是说，同一双腿在同一个赛季里跑过了长短不等的四条跑道。如果当时有任何人在意可比性，这个人就是第一个会发现问题的人，因为差异直接落在他自己身上。而没有任何记载表明有谁提出过折算，或者抱怨过在德尔斐跑得比在奥林匹亚轻松。他要的是四顶桂冠，四顶桂冠不需要长度相等。

顺着这一点还可以再问一句。既然长度不同，那么在长跑道上夺冠是不是比在短跑道上更难。这个问题在今天看起来很自然，在当时却问不出来，因为它预设了两场比赛可以放在同一个尺度上评估难度。当时的评估尺度是对手。在奥林匹亚赢，难在那里聚集的人最强，不难在那条跑道最长。构衡量一场胜利的分量时，看的是这场胜利击败了谁，不是这场胜利跨过了多少米。

今天读到这组数字，本能反应是想去换算，想知道如果统一到一百九十二米，德尔斐的成绩该折算成多少。这个本能来自后来的构，不来自那件事。把一个后来才被造出来的需求当作一直存在的需求，再回过头去指认古人没有满足它，这是读古代制度最常见的一种误读，而且它会一路误读到十九世纪。

还有一件事顺带说清楚。赛道的长度是按当地的尺算的，而尺在各地并不相同。这意味着六百尺这个数字在四个圣地是一致的，不一致的是尺。当时的人用的是同一个数，量出来的是不同的长度，而他们并不认为其中有矛盾。数是仪式性的，长度是场地性的。统一数目而不统一实长，在一个不打算跨场地比较的世界里完全自洽。后来的构做的事恰好相反，它统一实长而放弃仪式数目，四百米就是四百米，一米就是一米，一米是什么由别处规定，与赛场无关。这两种做法之间隔着的不是精度，是目的。

四 测量确实发生过，而且到今天没算清

古代不是完全没有数字。有几条，而且都很惊人。

克罗同的法尤洛斯，一段铭体诗传统说他跳了五十五尺，掷铁饼九十五尺。这段文字通过后世的注疏和苏达辞书传下来，哈里斯在1960年专门讨论过这首诗。斯巴达的基俄尼斯也有一条数值记载，而这一条的数字并不稳定，今天可核查的优西比乌传统写的是二十二尺，而另一些研究传统依据阿非利加努斯或文本校勘主张应作五十二尺。这一条要严格处理的话，五十二尺应当标为有争议的校勘结果，不能当作无争议的定值。

五十五尺换算过来是十六米上下。现代跳远的世界纪录是八米九五。所以这个数字要么说的不是同一件事，要么不可靠。

跳远确实用工具。运动员手持一对跳重，保萨尼阿斯描述过它的形制，半圆而略呈椭圆，手指可以穿进去。斐洛斯特拉托斯说跳重是五项全能者的发明，并且提到规则允许用笛声帮助节奏。现代的生物力学实验支持跳重确有作用，合适的重量可以带来厘米级的增益。厘米级。它离十六米差着两倍。

笛声那一条也值得留意。斐洛斯特拉托斯说规则允许用笛声帮助节奏，这意味着跳远在当时是一件有伴奏的事。一个有伴奏的项目和一个用秒表计的项目，关注的东西不一样。伴奏服务于动作的完成度和观赏性，秒表服务于结果的可比性。当时的规则愿意为前者立条文，说明那才是这个项目被认为要紧的地方。

于是解释路径有四条，到今天并列着，谁也压不倒谁。一是多次跳，根特的研究团队和不少后续学者主张这个项目可能是五次连续的立定跳，实验里确实可以做到十五米以上。二是赛制不同，另一些学者主张它不是现代意义的单次跳远，可能有标准助跑区，测量方法也与今天不同。三是文本讹误，尤其基俄尼斯那一条，数字在传抄中变形是完全可能的。四是修辞夸张，哈里斯在讨论法尤洛斯那首诗时明确把文学夸饰和讽刺性夸张列为一条解释路径，因为铭体诗这个体裁允许这样做。

这几个数字之所以到今天还在争，恰恰因为当时没有一本册子可以拿来对。它们不是从档案里出来的，是从一首诗里出来的。诗可以夸张，可以讹传，可以在传抄中掉一个字或者多一个字。册子不能。一个数字要成为可核对的东西，需要的不是有人把它写下来，而是有一套制度承诺它会被写下来，而且承诺它会以同样的方式被写下来第二次第三次。

这就是这一篇里最要注意的一处。零星的数值不足以证明，一套可以持续核对成绩的纪录制度已经存在。要核对法尤洛斯那一跳，我们需要同届其他人的成绩作参照，需要前后几届的同项目数据作背景，需要一个可以判断它是否离谱的分布。现存材料没有提供这些参照。这不能排除一切已经失传的测量，却意味着我们不能仅凭这个数，重建一套可核验的纪录制度。它孤零零地立在一首诗里，两千五百年，四种读法。

换个角度说，一个数字的可靠性从来不只来自它自己，它来自它周围的其他数字。今天如果有人报出一个远超世界纪录的成绩，判断真假不需要回到现场，只需要看它在分布里的位置，看同场其他人的成绩，看这个人此前的成绩曲线，看当天的风速和场地。这些参照物构成了一张网，数字落在网上才有重量。法尤洛斯那个数字没有网。它下面是空的，所以它既不能被证实，也不能被证伪，只能被解释。而解释可以有很多种，这就是四条路径至今并列的原因。

现代人处理这条材料时的第一反应通常是问它到底是多少。这个问法已经预设了那个数是可以被确定的。更准确的问法是，一个不打算被核对的数字，和一个打算被核对的数字，压根不是同一种东西，哪怕它们写出来长得一样。

五 一个高度发达的构，只是它度量的不是成绩

奥林匹亚的裁判叫作全希腊裁判官。保萨尼阿斯明载他们的人数随制度反复变化。最早据说只有伊菲托斯一人;第五十届起改为抽签产生的两人;第九十五届增至九人;后来又增至十人;第一百零三届十二人;第一百零四届因为部族缩减而降为八人;第一百零八届重新回到十人，此后延续。

这串数字自己就说明了不少事。裁判人数跟着埃利斯的部族数目走，部族增减，裁判随之增减。也就是说，决定赛场上有几个人做判定的，是赛场之外的一次行政区划变动。构的内部结构由外部结构决定，而这件事在赛会自己的叙述里完全不构成问题，因为它从来没有声称过自己的裁判人数出于竞技上的理由。

产生方式和职权也有直接的文本。他们从埃利斯人当中遴选，要在埃利斯的裁判官驻所连住十个月，接受护法官的指导。比赛那一天，他们在黎明之前就要到场给赛跑者配组，中午监督五项全能和重竞技。这些人既是裁判也是赛事的行政官。

还有宣誓。保萨尼阿斯记载，运动员本人，他的父亲和兄弟，以及他的教练，要在誓约宙斯像前起誓，誓词的内容是他确实完成了十个月的规定训练，并且不会在赛会上作弊。

起誓的人员范围值得多看一眼。下场的只有一个人，而起誓的还有他的父亲，他的兄弟和他的教练。构在这里把责任分给了一圈并不参赛的人。理由不难推断，一个人是否真的练满了十个月，只有他身边的人知道;赛会没有办法去核查那十个月，只能让知情的人把自己一起押上。这是一种典型的处理办法，当构无法直接观测某件事的时候，它就去找一个可以观测的替代物，然后要求那个替代物为原来那件事担保。

还有惩罚。手段包括体罚，罚金和取消资格，鞭刑由专门的执鞭人执行。利卡斯因为违规给自己的御者系上胜带而被当场鞭打。欧波洛斯的行贿案有详细记载。而罚金的用途是这一整套里最有意思的一项，它被拿去铸成宙斯像，立在通往赛场的路上，像座上刻着受罚者的名字和警告文字。今天奥林匹亚的遗址上，那排石基还在。

把这些放在一起看，很难说这是一个粗糙的构。十个月的集训，赛前的宣誓，按部族调整的裁判人数，抽签，配组，鞭刑，罚金，以及用罚金铸成的立在必经之路上的一排警告。这套东西的精密程度不低于任何一个现代赛会的纪律条款，某些环节甚至更狠。

后来的人常把古代竞技没有数字解释成还没发展到那一步。这组材料不支持这个说法。发达与不发达是同一根轴上的前后，而这里是两根轴。这个构把它全部的精密投在了另一根轴上，它要保证的是这次胜利是干净的，不是这次成绩有多大。它对造假的警惕程度极高，对量级的兴趣接近于零。

而且这两件事之间没有必然联系。一个赛会完全可以既严查作弊又精确计时，今天的赛会就是这样。但也完全可以只做前者，古代就是这样。把两者绑在一起看成同一种进步的两个侧面，是后来才有的习惯。清白与量级是两个独立的问题，构可以只回答其中一个，而且可以回答得非常好。

这一点值得再往下推一步。度量一个人跑得多快，和判定一个人赢得是否合规，是两种不同的工作，需要的装置也不同。前者需要统一的尺和统一的表，后者需要监誓，监督，查证和惩戒。古代把资源全部投给了后者，并且做到了相当的完备。所以精密不是一件线性积累的事，它总是朝着某个具体的方向长，而方向由构要生产的东西决定。

那些用罚金铸成的宙斯像是这一节里最结实的一处。罚金原本是要把作弊从赛会里清出去的，而清出去的方式是把作弊者的名字铸进铜里，立在每一个入场的人必经的路上。被清除的东西以警告的形式留在了原地。更进一步，那些像的石基今天还在，而绝大多数胜者的塑像早已不存。构想要清除的东西，比构想要表彰的东西活得更久。

六 记进去的是谁

库尼斯卡在公元前396年和公元前392年两次赢得奥林匹亚的四马战车赛。保萨尼阿斯记载她在奥林匹亚立了像，铭文保存了下来，里面说她自称是全希腊唯一赢得这顶桂冠的女人。这是铁案，没有争议。

她没有驾车。战车赛按马主记胜，不按御者记胜。

驾车的那个人做了全部的事。他不在名单上。他的名字没有被记载，他属于哪个城邦不清楚，他是自由人还是奴隶不清楚。

这个构不是没注意到他。战车赛的规则必须处理御者，起跑，犯规，撞车，这些都要由御者来承担后果，鞭刑也是打在他身上的。所以他在规则里是存在的，在名单里不存在。一个人可以被一套制度充分地管辖，同时完全不被它记录。管辖和记录是两回事，前者要求他在场，后者要求他被命名。而这个构只在需要他在场的地方需要他。构在这里的规则极其明确，并且执行得毫无含糊，胜利归于马主。

参赛资格也不像通俗写法说的那么硬。希罗多德记载马其顿的亚历山大一世要参赛时，先要证明自己是希腊人，他拿出阿尔戈斯的系谱论证之后才被允许出赛。传统教科书据此写成只限希腊自由男子。但雷米森提出过强修正，她认为所谓泛希腊赛会有一条正式排斥非希腊人的规则，这个观念很大程度上是十九世纪民族主义式的重构;史料读起来更像是在具体情境里协商谁算希腊人，而不是有一条稳定普适的硬法。这是一层解释争议，不是一条已确证的规则，两种读法都要摆在这里。

奴隶和非公民的位置更模糊。奴隶不得参赛可以列为高概率的通说，但直接的明文比通俗写作暗示的要薄;而必须是某城邦公民这一条应当写得比通俗写法更谨慎，因为王室成员，殖民地人口，跨地域的精英，以及按马主记胜的战车制度，都显示实际操作比现代国籍法弹性得多。

女子那边是另一个构。赫拉赛会确有其事，项目是少女短跑，分年龄组，最年轻的先跑，其次中间组，最后最年长的一组，距离比男子的单程赛跑略短。这是一个独立的赛会，不是同一个构的扩容。

这个区别在整个田径史里会反复出现，所以值得在这里就点住。当一个构遇到它安放不下的人时，它有两种办法，一种是把自己的边界推开把人纳进来，另一种是在旁边另起一个构。第二种办法看上去也是接纳，实际上它同时完成了两件事，既让那些人有地方可去，又保证了原来那个构一个字都不用改。赫拉赛会和奥林匹亚赛会是两个赛会，在不同的日子，由不同的人主持，距离也不同。它们从来没有需要过一张共同的名单。至于已婚妇女的观赛禁令，保萨尼阿斯记载埃利斯的法律规定女性若在禁日出现在奥林匹亚应被从提帕昂山崖推下，卡莉帕特拉的故事正建立在这条禁令之上。现代的质疑不在于禁令传统是否存在，而在于适用范围与执行密度。禁令针对的是已婚妇女而非一切女性，女祭司和某些功能性角色可能例外，而推下山崖的极刑更像规范性的威吓，缺乏充分的执行案例。保守的说法是禁令传统确有史料基础，但实施可能比字面法条弹性得多。

这一节里的每一个人都以某种方式站在名单的边上。库尼斯卡进了名单，替她驾车的人没有;亚历山大进了名单，靠的是一份系谱而不是一次比赛;赫拉赛会的少女进了另一份名单;而那条禁令针对的人，连观看的资格都要经过判定。

构在开始度量之前，先划了一道谁可以被度量的线。这道线不产生任何数字，但它决定了后面所有数字是从哪个池子里出来的。这一层在整个田径史里从来没有消失过，它只是不断改换划线的依据，从血统改到国籍，从性别改到激素水平，从身体改到器械。而无论依据换成什么，那道线一直在。

七 古人确实在比较，只是不用数字

巡回全胜者是一个称号。它指的是在四大圣会全部夺冠的人。这个称号是累积性的，它不把某一次比赛的快慢锁进册子，而是把一个人在四个圣地的连续征服累积成一种可识别的身份。

它确实起到了一部分比较的作用。有了它，就可以问谁完成了巡回，谁完成得更早，谁完成了不止一次。与之相近的还有对总冠数的比较，对家族总胜场的比较，以及对前人或后人冠数的超越的比较。保萨尼阿斯书里就有这类家族对比。

还有一点结构上的差别。累积的荣誉不需要一个中央机构来保管。它长在铭文上，长在塑像的底座上，长在颂辞里，长在一个人被介绍时别人加在他名字前面的那个词上。谁都可以复述，谁都可以刻。数值纪录不能这样，它必须有一个地方来认定，否则同一个项目会同时存在好几条互相矛盾的最好成绩。累积制把保管分散给了所有人，数值制必须把认定收拢到一处。累积制当然也有争议，但争议的是荣誉够不够格，不是数字算不算数。

所以古代没有纪录观念这句话说得太满。古人对比较有兴趣，而且专门造了一件用来比较的东西。只是那件东西是一串荣誉，不是一个数。更准确的说法是，古代没有一套统一的，可以跨场地跨年代直接对表的数值纪录制度。这个区别不是措辞问题，它决定了整个构的运行方式。

累积制有一个数值制不具备的性质，而这个性质值得单独提出来。一个巡回全胜者的称号无法被后人打破。它只能被并列，或者被在次数上超过。它不会作废，不会被谁在某个下午划掉。它不是一条线，是一份履历。

数值纪录不是这样。它当前领先的位置，本来就等着被后来的成绩超越，每一次成立也同时预告了这种可能；但被打破，并不抹掉那次成绩发生过的历史事实。这两种构在这里分岔，而这一系列后面许多麻烦都长在数值那一支上。胜者名单通常按届次累加：后来的胜者不必以取消前一届为条件。它不像数值纪录那样，以后来者超越前人的数值为常态；这不等于名单中的认定永远不能纠正，资格永远不能撤销。

代价也在同一处。累积制无法回答任何一个关于快慢的问题。它可以告诉你谁是那个时代最好的运动员，它没有办法告诉你他比另一个时代的人快还是慢。两个相隔三百年的巡回全胜者之间，没有任何一个量可以放在一起看。荣誉序列可以延长，不可以跨代折算。

它还有一个更隐蔽的代价。累积制奖励的是持续和遍历，不是某一天的极致。一个在某个下午跑出了此前无人跑出过的速度，却在别的场次一无所获的人，在这套制度里没有位置，而且他的那个下午不会留下任何痕迹。数值制会把这个人捞起来，累积制不会。反过来，一个每次都赢但每次都赢得很吃力的人，在累积制里是顶级的，在数值制里可能什么都不是。两套构筛出来的不是同一批人，漏掉的也不是同一批人。

这就是这个构的边界。它并不比后来的构差，它只是解决了另一个问题，并且在自己的问题上解决得相当彻底。它留下的余项和数值制留下的余项不是同一批人。

八 后来的人需要它有

古代赛会是怎么结束的，定不下来。

通行的说法是狄奥多西一世在393年下令禁止。现代研究普遍认为这个说法太粗糙，因为并没有一条保存完好的敕令明确点名废止奥林匹亚赛会。较新的研究倾向于把终止理解为一个从四世纪末延伸到五世纪的过程，牵涉反异教立法，祭祀功能的衰退，地方财政，以及环境灾害等多重因素。替代的年代里，常见的一个是426年，那一年有更彻底的反异教整肃与圣域毁坏;也有学者宁愿不用单一的终止年，而写成迟至五世纪中叶才完全不可持续。

一份用来给整个希腊世界的年份定位的名单，自己的终点没有年份。用来纪年的东西没有自己的纪年。这不是巧合，它和这一篇前面所有的内容是同一件事。这个构从头到尾只生产名字，而一个制度的死亡不会以某人的名字进入任何名单。它没有为自己的结束准备条目。

这里还可以再说一句。终止之所以定不下来，一部分原因是赛会并不是一件可以被一次关掉的东西。它是祭祀，是集市，是仲裁场合，是各城邦交换消息的地方，竞技只是其中一层。一道法令可以禁掉献祭，禁不掉人们在同一个时间到同一个地方去。所以它的消失是逐层的，每一层各有各的时间，而那些时间并不重合。一个由多层叠起来的东西，不会有一个整齐的死亡日期。

一千五百年之后，它被重新造了一遍，而重造它的人需要它是另外一个样子。

1896年的复兴把好几样并无古代依据的东西包装成古代精神的回归。业余主义是一样，古代竞技从一开始就处在奖赏，赞助，名望和城邦回馈的体系里，所谓无物质回报的纯粹竞技是十九世纪贵族与绅士体育伦理向古代的投射。马拉松是一样，古代奥林匹亚没有这个项目，它是1896年由布雷阿尔与顾拜旦引入的。以国际和平与普世兄弟情谊为核心的意识形态是一样，那是十九世纪末国际主义与民族国家政治的产物。重在参与也是一样，古代竞技文化强调的是胜利，荣耀与羞耻，不是参与。

还有一样，统一纪录观。

要紧的不是指出复兴者搞错了。他们当中不少人的古典学素养并不差，而且这些建构在当时是公开进行的，不是隐瞒。要紧的是他们为什么需要那个东西存在。

一套刚刚建立的制度，要求全世界的人用同一把尺子量同一件事，并且要求所有人接受量出来的结果具有跨国界跨年代的效力，这套制度需要一个来源来证明这把尺子不是刚刚发明的。一个刚发明的尺子可以被质疑，一个有一千多年历史的尺子不容易被质疑。于是古代被征用了。可是古代那边并没有尺子，只有一长串名字。名字不能证明尺子。所以尺子被补了上去，补在过去。

而且补得很实。业余原则不只是一句口号，它变成了资格审查，变成了取消名次的依据，变成了后来几十年里一批运动员实际的命运。一个关于古代的错误判断，一旦被写进章程，就不再是判断，而是可以据以执行的规则。它有没有古代依据，对被它取消资格的那个人来说没有任何区别。这是这一系列会反复遇到的一种情形，构的合法性来源可以是虚构的，构的效力不会因此减弱一分。

从这里往后，这一系列剩下的全部篇目都在处理一件事，那把补上去的尺子被当真了，而且真的造了出来。它开始量，并且量得越来越准。它每准一分，量不进去的东西就以更清晰的形状显出来一分。

古代赛会运转了一千多年，这份名录留下的是一长串胜者的名字。今天的读者容易期待名字后面还跟着逐项成绩，但名录并没有把它们作为固定栏目系统保存下来。名单边上的那些例子也提醒我们这一点：替库尼斯卡驾车的那个人，阿尔基比亚德那两个不入册的名次，法尤洛斯那一跳究竟有多远，尼米亚那些间距不规整的标志之间实际跑出来的时间。名单没有这些栏，不等于相关测量或记载从来不存在；也不能反过来把名单的空白，当成一套完整成绩制度散佚后的缺口。要先看这本账原本在记什么，再问账外还剩下什么。

还要补一句。它们也不都是委屈。那个御者未必觉得自己被亏待了，他生活在一个战车赛按马主记胜的世界里，这是他知道的规则。余项不等于受害者。余项是构的账目对不上的那一部分，至于那部分里的人自己怎么想，构不知道，今天的人也无从得知。把每一个落在名单外面的人都写成蒙冤者，是在用后来的构替他们发言，而那恰好是这一整篇要避开的事。

后来的构补上了那一栏。补上之后才发现，一栏是不够的。

1. A list for reckoning years

The fullest surviving list of ancient Olympic victors is preserved in the chronological work of Eusebius. It runs from the first Olympiad to the 249th, its main sequence generally identifying each Olympiad by the winner of the stadion race. This was the race over the full length of the track, Olympia's oldest event. There are occasional exceptions and notices of other events. What matters here is its basic way of locating Olympiads through their victors, rather than producing a modern table of performances in every event.

The list was not made for athletics. It was a calendar.

Ancient Greece had no common system for numbering years. Each city dated them by its own magistrates, and the months had different names. For an event involving two cities, each side had its own account of the year. The Olympic festival took place every four years, at a stable interval, and was recognized throughout the Greek world. The champion's name that appeared once every four years thus became a temporal coordinate available to everyone. To establish when something had happened, one could say that it occurred in the Olympiad when a particular man won the stadion.

The person generally credited with arranging the victors' names into a continuous catalogue is Hippias of Elis, around 400 BCE. The first champion was called Koroibos; later tradition dated his Olympiad to 776 BCE. From Hippias, the list passed through several generations of chronographers, then through Africanus, and finally into Eusebius. Eusebius's original no longer survives. What we use today comes through an Armenian translation and the Latin tradition.

One point must come first. When Hippias began compiling the list, nearly four hundred years had passed since its intended first Olympiad. He is unlikely to have invented the names out of nothing. Modern scholarship tends to think that he used local traditions in Elis, accounts of the sanctuary at Olympia, athletic treatises, and scattered notices of victors in local histories and victory odes. But before these materials were assembled into a continuous line, they were not a line. Today the entries for the earliest few dozen Olympiads are treated as usable but requiring assessment in layers, neither accepted wholesale nor discarded wholesale. There are three grounds for doubt: the long interval involved; the limited conditions for writing and preserving archives in early Greece; and inconsistencies within the tradition, including traces of later repairs. Pausanias's own accounts of when events were added expose these tensions.

Christesen has also pointed out something easily overlooked. Not every text mentioning a victor is a victor list. A victory ode says that its subject won at Olympia; a local history mentions an ancestor's championship. These are accounts of victories, but not a catalogue arranged by Olympiad. What distinguishes a catalogue is not that it records who won. It promises an entry for every Olympiad, with all entries arranged in the same way. The distance between scattered accounts and a continuous catalogue is a difference of form, not quantity. Hippias took precisely that step, turning the former into the latter. Once he had done so, victory accounts that had originally spoken separately acquired a common position, while losing their individual earlier shapes.

The construct's earliest product, in other words, was retrospective recognition. It arranged names scattered across four centuries into a line, and that line began to be used as time itself. The entire Greek world later used it to locate its history, although it had been made four hundred years after the beginning of the period to which it was applied.

The starting point of 776 BCE was calculated, too, rather than read from an archive. Given a continuous list, knowing the absolute date of any one Olympiad allows one to count backward to the first. That year was therefore a product of the list, not its foundation. Between the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the starting date prompted a substantial and sometimes heated dispute. Most people now use it as a traditional date rather than a verified historical milestone. A tool for dating other things had its own first year worked out by calculation.

What a construct produces depends on what it was made to do. This list was made to locate years, so all it needed was a champion's name for each Olympiad; the numbered Olympiad still distinguished the date when the same man won again. It did not need to know how long that person had taken to run. It did not even need him to be faster than the previous winner. Reading the list today, we cannot assume that an absent column of times was once complete and simply lost later. The catalogue did not systematically produce a fixed field of elapsed times for each Olympiad. That does not prove that no one ever measured a performance in antiquity, or that no related record ever existed elsewhere.

2. The list needs only one name

At crown games such as Olympia, awards at the festival level went, in essence, only to the victor. There was no second prize, no third prize, no formal sequence of placings. Crowther puts the point clearly: only first place received the festival's award at the major Greek games. But he also cautions that it distorts matters to insist too absolutely that victory alone counted, since non-winning performances occasionally appear in literature and inscriptions.

The most famous example is Alcibiades. During the debate over the Sicilian expedition, he presented his credentials to the Athenians, mentioning that his entries at Olympia had taken first, second, and fourth in the chariot race. Thucydides recorded the speech. Herodotus, too, occasionally mentions someone finishing second.

Second place was therefore not unsayable. The important distinction lies elsewhere. Being spoken of and being entered in a register are different things. Alcibiades's second and fourth places entered Thucydides's text because they served his political argument that day. They entered no victor list because the list had no need of them. The same fact had a place in a narrative and no place in the institution.

This distinction will become very important later, so it is worth recognizing while it is still this simple. Narrative selects: what it records depends on what the teller needs at the time. The institution does not select in that way. It fills a predetermined form; everything that does not fit remains outside. Narrative seems more capacious because it can hold anything. But it promises to hold nothing, so what it contains depends entirely on contingency. Had Alcibiades not urged the Athenians to send an expedition on that occasion, those two placings would not be known. The institution seems less capacious, but it makes a promise. Within the small area it agrees to contain, something will not disappear merely because no one happens to mention it.

Here the construct is at its clearest. It does not suppress the runner-up, deny that he ran, or even forbid him from talking about it everywhere. It simply reserves no place for him. The remainder here is not a column that has been deleted, but a column that was never drawn. The whole subsequent history of athletics will return to this form: what a construct cannot clear away is often not what it has expelled, but what it never provided a place to receive.

The form of the rewards follows this arrangement. At the festival itself, the prize was a wild-olive crown and nothing more. The real remuneration came on returning to one's city: cash, public meals for life, front-row theatre seats, exemption from certain obligations. Xenophanes wrote a poem expressly complaining that a fast runner received far more than a wise man. Plutarch reports that Solon established rewards for Athenians: five hundred drachmas for an Olympic victor and one hundred for an Isthmian victor. The source of the report is clear, but Plutarch lived much later than Solon, and scholars dispute the reliability of the precise amounts. Some read it as a trustworthy preservation of ancient Athens's reward system, others as a later moralizing narrative about legislation.

We need not develop the opposition between amateur and professional here; it belongs to a later essay. For now, remember only the division of labour between rewards. What the festival gave had no price. A wild-olive crown was worth little as an object; its value lay in being unavailable for purchase. Everything the city gave had a price, one precise enough to be written into a decree. Something unpriceable and a sum that could be priced met successively in the same person. The link that converted one into the other was the name entered on the list.

Whatever the reliability of the amounts, the direction is clear. The city paid for a name, not a number. A name could be exchanged for money, a seat, or the way an entire family would be spoken of over generations. A number took no part in that exchange because there was no number.

3. Four tracks of different lengths

The tracks at the four great sanctuaries had different lengths. This is not an inference from texts but an archaeological measurement based on surviving starting lines, distance markers, and drainage channels beside the tracks. Olympia's track was approximately 192 metres long; Delphi's about 177.5 metres; Nemea's about 178 metres; and the later track at Isthmia about 181 metres. An archaeological interpretation also proposes a change at Isthmia from six hundred feet to 559 feet.

Olympia was nearly fifteen metres longer than Delphi. That is no small difference. If stadion races at these four sites had been timed, the times would have meant nothing as direct comparisons.

The Nemean material makes the point especially well. In both his excavation publications and on-site explanations, Miller stresses that the track measured six hundred local feet, or about 178 metres. Yet the measured spacing of the markers placed every hundred feet is irregular. Miller therefore states directly that the ancient arrangement of those markers was not intended to produce precise performances in the modern sense.

The force of this evidence lies in its material existence. Someone says that ancient texts record no performances; someone else replies that the texts were lost. But the track is there, and the spacing of the markers can be measured. We cannot simply read these irregularly spaced markers as the equal, precise intervals of a modern scale. Unequally spaced markers can, of course, serve measurement when their distances are known; the question is what these particular markers actually did. They were not set at uniform intervals despite belonging to an age whose stoneworking could have achieved them. At least that prevents us from assigning them the purpose of a modern scale without further examination. The evidence supports a narrower point: producing exact performances for comparison across venues was not their primary purpose. It does not show that the ancients never measured anything.

We should pause here, because this is where an erroneous judgment comes most easily. Non-standard track lengths were not a defect in this construct. A defect is a failure relative to a purpose. Comparison across sanctuaries was never its purpose, so the four different track lengths posed no problem for anyone at the time. No one complained because no one needed them to be equal.

One feature makes the point even firmer. The same athlete might compete at several sanctuaries within one year. The four great festivals followed staggered cycles, and anyone aspiring to win the entire circuit had to visit all four. The same pair of legs, then, ran four tracks of different lengths in the same season. If anyone had cared about comparability, that athlete would have been the first to discover the problem, because the difference bore directly on his own body. Yet no surviving account shows anyone proposing a conversion or complaining that running at Delphi was easier than running at Olympia. What he wanted was four crowns. Four crowns did not require equal lengths.

We can press the question further. If the lengths differed, was winning on a long track harder than winning on a short one? Today the question seems natural. At the time it could not have been asked in this form, because it assumes that two competitions can be placed on a single scale of difficulty. The contemporary scale was the opposition. Winning at Olympia was difficult because the strongest competitors gathered there, not because its track was the longest. In weighing a victory, the construct considered whom it defeated, not how many metres it covered.

When we read these figures today, our instinct is to convert them: if all distances were standardized to 192 metres, what would a Delphic performance become? That instinct comes from a later construct, not from the event itself. To treat a subsequently created need as though it had always existed, then accuse the ancients of failing to satisfy it, is one of the commonest misreadings of ancient institutions. It will mislead us all the way into the nineteenth century.

One incidental point also needs clarification. Track length was reckoned in local feet, and feet differed between places. Six hundred feet was therefore the same number at the four sanctuaries; what differed was the foot. People used the same number, measured different lengths, and saw no contradiction. The number belonged to the ritual; the length belonged to the site. Standardizing the number without standardizing the actual length was entirely coherent in a world with no intention of comparing across venues. The later construct does precisely the reverse: it standardizes actual length and abandons the ritual number. Four hundred metres is four hundred metres; a metre is a metre. What a metre is has been decided elsewhere, independently of the stadium. What separates the two arrangements is purpose, not precision.

4. Measurement did occur, and we still cannot settle it

Antiquity was not entirely without numbers. A few survive, and all are startling.

An epigrammatic tradition credits Phayllos of Croton with a jump of fifty-five feet and a discus throw of ninety-five feet. The text comes down through later commentaries and the Suda; Harris devoted a study to the poem in 1960. Chionis of Sparta also has a recorded measurement, but its number is unstable. The Eusebian tradition that can be checked today gives twenty-two feet. Other scholarly traditions, drawing on Africanus or textual emendation, argue for fifty-two. Strictly handled, fifty-two feet must be marked as a disputed emendation, not presented as an uncontested value.

Fifty-five feet converts to roughly sixteen metres. The modern long-jump world record is 8.95 metres. Either the ancient number refers to a different activity, or it is unreliable.

Jumping did involve equipment. Athletes held a pair of jumping weights. Pausanias describes their form as semicircular but somewhat elliptical, with openings for the fingers. Philostratus says that the weights were an invention of pentathletes, and mentions rules permitting flute music to assist their rhythm. Modern biomechanical experiments support the proposition that jumping weights can help: suitable weights can produce gains measured in centimetres. Centimetres. That is nowhere near a doubling to sixteen metres.

The flute is worth noticing, too. Philostratus says the rules allowed flute music to support rhythm. Jumping was thus an accompanied event. An accompanied event and a stopwatch-timed event attend to different things. Accompaniment serves the execution and spectacle of movement; a stopwatch serves the comparability of results. The rules of the time were willing to legislate for the former, suggesting where the event's perceived importance lay.

Four explanations therefore remain side by side, with none able to displace the others. First, multiple jumps: a research group at Ghent and many subsequent scholars propose that the event may have consisted of five consecutive standing jumps. Experiments can indeed produce totals exceeding fifteen metres. Second, a different competitive format: other scholars argue that this was not a single long jump in the modern sense, and may have involved a prescribed approach area and a different method of measurement. Third, textual corruption, particularly in Chionis's case: numbers could certainly have changed during copying. Fourth, rhetorical exaggeration. Discussing the poem about Phayllos, Harris explicitly included literary embellishment and satirical exaggeration among the possible explanations, since epigram allows them.

These numbers are still disputed precisely because there was no ledger against which to check them. They came from a poem, not an archive. A poem may exaggerate, be misreported, or gain or lose a character in transmission. A ledger cannot work that way. For a number to become checkable, someone merely writing it down is insufficient. An institution must promise that it will be recorded, and that a second and a third number will be recorded in the same way.

This is the point requiring the greatest care in this essay. Isolated numerical values do not establish that a system for routinely checking performances already existed. To check Phayllos's jump, we would need the marks of his contemporaries at the same festival, results from the same event in adjacent Olympiads, and a distribution against which to judge its implausibility. The surviving material supplies none of these references. That does not exclude every measurement whose record has been lost, but it means that this number alone cannot reconstruct a verifiable record system. It stands alone in a poem: twenty-five centuries, four readings.

Put differently, a number's reliability never comes from itself alone. It comes from the other numbers around it. If someone today announces a performance far beyond the world record, judging its authenticity need not require returning to the scene. We can examine its position in the distribution, the other competitors' results, the athlete's previous performance curve, and the wind and venue on the day. Together those references form a net. A number acquires weight when it lands in that net. The number attached to Phayllos has no net. There is empty space beneath it, so it can be neither verified nor falsified, only interpreted. Interpretation admits many possibilities; that is why the four explanations still stand together.

The modern reader's first response is usually to ask what the distance really was. That question already assumes the number can be determined. A more accurate approach recognizes that a number not intended to be checked and one intended to be checked are not the same kind of thing at all, even if they look identical on the page.

5. A highly developed construct, measuring something other than performance

Olympia's judges were called the Hellanodikai, the judges of the Greeks. Pausanias explicitly records repeated changes in their number as the system changed. At first, Iphitos supposedly acted alone. From the fiftieth Olympiad there were two, selected by lot. At the ninety-fifth their number rose to nine, and later to ten. There were twelve at the 103rd, then eight at the 104th after the number of tribes was reduced. At the 108th it returned to ten and remained there.

This sequence tells us a good deal on its own. The number of judges followed the number of tribes in Elis: when tribes were added or removed, judges were added or removed. An administrative reorganization outside the stadium determined how many people made decisions inside it. The construct's internal structure was determined by an external structure. In the festival's own account, this posed no problem, because it had never claimed that the number of judges arose from athletic considerations.

Their selection and duties also have direct textual attestation. Chosen from among the people of Elis, they had to spend ten consecutive months in the judges' residence there, receiving instruction from the guardians of the laws. On competition day they arrived before dawn to assign runners to heats; at midday they supervised the pentathlon and the heavy events. They were both judges and administrators of the festival.

Then there were the oaths. Pausanias records that the athlete, his father and brothers, and his trainer had to swear before the statue of Zeus of Oaths that he had completed the prescribed ten months of training and would not cheat at the games.

The range of people swearing deserves attention. Only one person competed, but his father, brothers, and trainer swore as well. The construct distributed responsibility among a circle of non-competitors. The reason is not hard to infer: only the people around an athlete knew whether he had really trained for ten months. The festival could not inspect those months, so it required those who knew to stake themselves alongside him. This is a characteristic response. When a construct cannot directly observe something, it finds an observable substitute and requires that substitute to vouch for the original.

There were punishments, too: corporal punishment, fines, and disqualification. Specialist whip-bearers administered flogging. Lichas was whipped on the spot for breaking the rules by tying a victory ribbon around his own charioteer. The bribery case involving Eupolos is documented in detail. Most interesting of all was the use made of fines. They paid for statues of Zeus placed along the approach to the stadium, with the offenders' names and warnings inscribed on the bases. That row of stone bases still stands at Olympia today.

Taken together, these are hardly the features of a crude construct: ten months of training; pre-competition oaths; judge numbers adjusted to the tribes; drawing lots; assigning heats; whippings; fines; and a row of warnings cast with those fines and placed where everyone had to pass. This apparatus was no less elaborate than the disciplinary provisions of a modern competition. Some parts were harsher still.

Later generations often explain the lack of ancient athletic numbers by saying that development had not yet reached that stage. This evidence does not support that explanation. Developed and undeveloped are earlier and later positions on one axis. Here there are two axes. The construct invested all its precision along another axis: it wanted to ensure that a victory was clean, not determine the magnitude of a performance. Its vigilance against fraud was extremely high; its interest in magnitude approached zero.

Nor is there any necessary connection between those two things. A festival can rigorously police cheating and time performances precisely; today's competitions do both. It can also do only the first, as the ancient games did. Treating them as two aspects of the same progress is a later habit. Integrity and magnitude are independent questions. A construct can answer only one, and answer it very well.

The point can be pushed further. Measuring how fast someone runs and judging whether someone has won lawfully are different jobs, requiring different apparatuses. The former needs standardized rulers and clocks; the latter needs supervised oaths, oversight, investigation, and punishment. Antiquity devoted its resources to the latter and developed it thoroughly. Precision is therefore not something that accumulates along a single line. It always grows in a particular direction, determined by what the construct intends to produce.

The Zeus statues cast from fines are the most solid instance in this section. Fines were intended to remove cheating from the festival. The method of removal was to cast the cheats' names in bronze and place them on the route every entrant had to take. What had been expelled remained on the spot as a warning. More than that: the bases remain today, while the great majority of victors' statues have vanished. What the construct sought to eliminate outlived what it sought to honour.

6. Who gets entered?

Kyniska won the four-horse chariot race at Olympia twice, in 396 and 392 BCE. Pausanias records that she erected a statue at Olympia. Its inscription survives and declares her the only woman in all Greece to have won that crown. This is securely established, not disputed.

She did not drive the chariot. Victory in chariot racing was credited to the owner of the horses, not the driver.

The driver did everything. He is absent from the list. His name was not recorded; we do not know his city or whether he was free or enslaved.

The construct had not failed to notice him. Chariot-racing rules necessarily governed drivers: starts, fouls, crashes. Drivers bore the consequences, and the whip struck their bodies. He therefore existed in the rules but not in the list. A person can be fully governed by an institution while remaining entirely unrecorded by it. Jurisdiction and recording are different acts. The first requires his presence; the second requires his being named. This construct needed him only where it needed him present. Its rule was perfectly explicit and unambiguously enforced: victory belonged to the horse-owner.

Eligibility was less rigid than popular accounts suggest, too. Herodotus reports that Alexander I of Macedon first had to prove that he was Greek before competing. He was admitted after presenting a genealogical argument connecting him with Argos. Traditional textbooks turn this into a restriction to free Greek males. Remijsen, however, has proposed a substantial revision: the notion of a formal rule excluding non-Greeks from the Panhellenic games is, in large part, a nineteenth-century nationalist reconstruction. The evidence reads more like negotiations over who counted as Greek in particular situations than a stable, universally applicable law. This is a dispute of interpretation, not an established rule. Both readings must remain visible here.

The position of enslaved people and non-citizens is more obscure. The exclusion of enslaved people can be treated as a highly probable consensus, but direct, explicit evidence is thinner than popular writing implies. The proposition that an entrant had to be a citizen of a particular polis requires even greater caution. Royalty, colonial populations, elites who moved across regions, and the chariot system crediting horse-owners all suggest practices more flexible than modern nationality law.

For women there was another construct. The Heraean games certainly existed. They offered footraces for girls, divided by age: the youngest ran first, then the middle group, then the oldest. Their course was slightly shorter than the men's stadion. This was a separate festival, not an expansion of the same construct.

The distinction will recur throughout the history of athletics, so it should be marked now. When a construct encounters people it cannot accommodate, it has two options: push its own boundary outward to include them, or establish another construct alongside it. The second may also look like acceptance, but accomplishes two things at once. It gives those people somewhere to go while ensuring that the original construct need not change a word. The Heraean and Olympic festivals were separate events, held on different days, directed by different people, over different distances. They never needed a common list. As for the prohibition on married women watching, Pausanias reports an Elean law requiring women found at Olympia on forbidden days to be thrown from Mount Typaion. The story of Kallipateira rests on this prohibition. Modern doubts concern not the existence of the prohibition tradition, but its scope and the frequency of enforcement. It targeted married women rather than all females; priestesses and certain functional roles may have been exempt. The extreme penalty of being thrown from a cliff looks more like a normative threat, with insufficient evidence of actual executions. The cautious conclusion is that the tradition has a historical basis, but enforcement may have been considerably more flexible than the literal law suggests.

Everyone in this section stands, in one way or another, beside the edge of the list. Kyniska entered it; the man who drove for her did not. Alexander entered by virtue of a genealogy rather than a race. The girls at the Heraean games entered another list. Those targeted by the prohibition had even their eligibility to watch adjudicated.

Before measuring, the construct draws a line around who may be measured. That line produces no number, but determines the pool from which every subsequent number will emerge. This layer has never disappeared from the history of athletics. Only the grounds of the line change: from ancestry to nationality, from sex to hormone levels, from the body to equipment. Whatever the grounds, the line remains.

7. The ancients did compare, just not through numbers

Circuit victor was a title. It identified someone who had won at all four great festivals. The title was cumulative. Rather than fixing the speed of one performance in a ledger, it accumulated successive conquests at four sanctuaries into a recognizable identity.

It did perform part of the work of comparison. One could ask who had completed the circuit, who had done so earlier, and who had done so more than once. Related comparisons concerned total crowns, total family victories, and the surpassing of earlier or later competitors' crown counts. Pausanias includes comparisons of this kind between families.

There is another structural difference. Accumulated honours need no central institution to keep them. They grow on inscriptions, statue bases, victory odes, and the word placed before someone's name when he is introduced. Anyone may repeat them; anyone may carve them. Numerical records cannot work this way. They need a place of ratification, or several contradictory best marks will coexist for the same event. The cumulative system distributes custody among everyone; the numerical system must concentrate certification in one place. Cumulative honours can, of course, be disputed, but the dispute concerns entitlement to the honour, not whether a number counts.

To say that antiquity had no concept of records is therefore too sweeping. The ancients were interested in comparison and made something specifically to perform it. That something was a sequence of honours rather than a number. More precisely, antiquity lacked a unified numerical-record system permitting direct comparison across venues and eras. This is not a verbal nicety. It determines how the whole construct operates.

The cumulative system has a property that the numerical system lacks, and it deserves separate attention. No later athlete can break someone's title of circuit victor. It can only be matched or exceeded in frequency. It does not become invalid; no one crosses it out on some later afternoon. It is a biography, not a line.

A numerical record is different. Its current leading position awaits a better performance; each establishment also announces that possibility. Being broken does not erase the historical fact that the performance occurred. Here the two constructs diverge, and many difficulties in the rest of this series grow along the numerical branch. A victors' list ordinarily accumulates entries by Olympiad: a later winner need not cancel an earlier one. Unlike a numerical record, its normal operation does not require a successor to surpass a predecessor's figure. This does not mean that entries can never be corrected or eligibility revoked.

The cost lies in the same place. The cumulative system cannot answer any question about speed. It can tell you who was the best athlete of an age, but not whether he was faster or slower than someone from another age. Between two circuit victors separated by three hundred years, no quantity can be set side by side. A sequence of honours can be extended, but cannot be converted across generations.

It has a less obvious cost, too. The cumulative system rewards persistence and completeness, not an extreme reached on a particular day. Someone who ran faster than anyone had ever run on one afternoon, but won nothing elsewhere, would have no place in this system. His afternoon would leave no trace. A numerical system would retrieve him; the cumulative system would not. Conversely, someone who won every time but struggled to win each time would rank at the top in a cumulative system and might count for nothing in a numerical one. The two constructs select different people and miss different people.

That is this construct's boundary. It is not worse than its successor. It solves another problem, and solves that problem thoroughly. The people it leaves as remainders are not the same people left by the numerical system.

8. Later generations needed it to have one

We cannot settle how the ancient games ended.

The familiar account says that Theodosius I banned them in 393 CE. Modern scholarship generally finds this too crude: no fully preserved edict explicitly names and abolishes the Olympic festival. More recent research tends to understand the end as a process stretching from the late fourth into the fifth century, involving anti-pagan legislation, the decline of sacrificial functions, local finances, and environmental disasters. Among alternative dates, 426 is common, associated with more thorough anti-pagan repression and destruction of sanctuaries. Other scholars prefer no single terminal year, describing the festival as becoming wholly unsustainable only as late as the mid-fifth century.

A list used to locate years for the whole Greek world has no year for its own ending. The instrument of chronology has no chronology of its own. This is not a coincidence; it is the same matter as everything preceding it in this essay. From beginning to end, the construct produced names. The death of an institution does not enter any list under someone's name. It had prepared no entry for its own conclusion.

One further point is possible. Part of the reason the end cannot be fixed is that the festival was not something that could be switched off in a single act. It was sacrifice, a market, an occasion for arbitration, a place where cities exchanged news. Athletics was only one layer. A decree could prohibit sacrifice, but not prevent people from going to the same place at the same time. Its disappearance was therefore layered. Each layer had its own time, and those times did not coincide. Something built from many layers will not have a tidy date of death.

Fifteen hundred years later it was made again, and those who remade it needed it to have been something else.

The revival of 1896 packaged several things with no ancient basis as a return to the ancient spirit. Amateurism was one. Ancient athletics had belonged from the outset to a system of rewards, patronage, fame, and civic compensation. Pure competition without material return was a projection of nineteenth-century aristocratic and gentlemanly sporting ethics onto antiquity. The marathon was another. Ancient Olympia had no such event; Bréal and Coubertin introduced it in 1896. An ideology centred on international peace and universal brotherhood was another, a product of late nineteenth-century internationalism and nation-state politics. The priority of participation was another. Ancient competitive culture emphasized victory, glory, and shame, not participation.

There was one more: a unified conception of records.

The important thing is not to catch the revivalists making mistakes. Many were competent classicists, and these constructions were made openly at the time, not concealed. The important question is why they needed those things to have existed.

A newly established institution asks the whole world to measure the same activity with the same ruler, and asks everyone to accept that its results are valid across national borders and historical periods. Such an institution needs an origin that establishes that its ruler was not invented yesterday. A newly invented ruler can be challenged; a ruler with more than a thousand years behind it is harder to question. Antiquity was therefore requisitioned. But antiquity had no ruler, only a long sequence of names. Names could not validate a ruler. So the ruler was supplied afterward, inserted into the past.

It was supplied in very concrete form. The amateur principle did not remain a slogan. It became eligibility screening, grounds for annulled placings, and the actual fate of athletes over the decades that followed. Once a mistaken judgment about antiquity enters a constitution, it ceases to be merely a judgment. It becomes an enforceable rule. Whether or not it has an ancient basis makes no difference to the person it disqualifies. This is a situation we will encounter repeatedly: a construct's source of legitimacy can be fictional without diminishing its practical force in the slightest.

From here on, the remaining essays in this series address one matter: the ruler added retrospectively was taken seriously, and really was built. It began to measure, with increasing precision. With every increment of precision, what could not fit its measurements emerged in a clearer shape.

The ancient festival operated for more than a thousand years, and this catalogue left a long sequence of victors' names. A reader today may expect detailed performances after those names, but the list did not systematically preserve them as fixed fields. The examples at its edges make the point: the man who drove for Kyniska; Alcibiades's two unregistered placings; the actual distance of Phayllos's jump; the times run between Nemea's unevenly spaced markers. Missing columns do not prove that related measurements or records never existed. Nor can we turn the list's blank spaces into gaps left by a lost, comprehensive performance-recording system. First ask what this ledger was recording; then ask what remains outside it.

One last qualification is necessary. They are not all grievances. The charioteer may not have felt mistreated. He lived in a world in which chariot victories belonged to horse-owners; those were the rules he knew. A remainder is not synonymous with a victim. It is the part that the construct's accounts do not accommodate. What the people in that part thought of themselves is unknown to the construct, and inaccessible to us today. To make every person outside the list into a wronged innocent would be to let a later construct speak for them, precisely what this essay has tried to avoid.

The later construct added that column. Only then did it discover that one column was not enough.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
