构在被越过时才发现边界

A Construct Finds Its Boundary Only When Crossed

Han Qin (秦汉)

一 两个一样的问题，两个相反的答案

有两件事，形状几乎一样。

一件是，有人在跳远里发明了一种新技术，前空翻式。它被禁了。

另一件是，有人在撑竿跳里换了一种新材料，玻璃纤维竿。它没有被禁，而且它带来的所有纪录都被保留了。

两件事都是这项运动里出现了一样新东西，一样能让成绩变好的新东西。一样引发了争议，一样被质疑不公平。而这个构给了它们相反的答案:一个禁掉，一个放行。

前两篇讲的是规则的沉默。跳高规则不规定怎么过杆，于是背越式钻进这个沉默里，活了下来。铅球规则不规定用滑步还是旋转，于是两种技术在这个沉默里自1970年代以来并存了半个多世纪。这一篇要问的是:规则的沉默什么时候结束。一个构不可能对所有事情永远沉默，总有一刻它要开口。而它开口和不开口的分界线在哪里，这一篇要从这两件相反的事情里找出来。

选这两件事来对照，不是随手挑的。它们在几个关键方面相似，却有相反结果：都发生在田径里，都提出了改善成绩的可能，都引来公平与安全讨论。但这种对照不是控制实验，不能把其他差异全部排除。它能帮助我们辨认一个常被安全争论遮住的问题：新东西是否仍被看作原来的项目。沿着这条线，这一篇最后会走到一个词上：定义。

先说清楚一件事。这两个相反答案，不能只靠一句“为了安全”就解释完。同期报道既提到前空翻可能伤及颈部，也提到它是否仍符合跳远的精神。现有公开材料没有提供足以核对风险大小的完整统计，但这不证明决策者从未考虑过安全，也不证明安全理由只是借口。这一篇着重追问的是另一个已经被提出的理由：一种革新是否越过了项目对自己的理解。

这一篇因此和前两篇构成一个完整的序列。前两篇讲规则的沉默，这一篇讲沉默的终点。一个构不可能对一切永远不开口，它总有一条底线，越过这条底线它就必须说话。而这条底线在哪里，不能靠读规则找到，因为规则恰恰在这条底线之外是沉默的。只能靠观察它在什么时候破了沉默，来反推那条线的位置。前空翻式和玻璃纤维竿，一个让它开了口，一个没有，两件事合起来，正好把那条线标了出来。

二 一种已经跳出实绩的新技术

1974年，德拉米尔在美国大学的重要比赛里用了前空翻式跳远。

体育画报1974年7月29日的报道说，他在奥斯汀的全美大学锦标赛上用了这个技术，没有进入决赛;而三周之前，他在太平洋八校联盟的比赛上，用这个技术跳出二十五英尺六又四分之三英寸，平了奥运冠军威廉姆斯。同一篇报道里还给出德拉米尔用常规姿势顺风跳出的最好成绩，二十五英尺九又二分之一英寸。

所以这不是一个马戏团式的噱头。用前空翻，他跳出了平奥运冠军的成绩。这个技术是真的能跳。

这一点必须先坐实，因为它决定了整件事的性质。如果前空翻式只是一个跳不远的杂耍，那么禁不禁它无关紧要，这个构随手处理掉一个没有威胁的怪招，谈不上什么边界问题。可它不是。它跳出了平奥运冠军的成绩，而且被认为还远没到极限。正因为它真的能跳，禁它才成了一个真正的决定，一个这个构必须权衡的决定。一样东西越有效，禁它的分量就越重，而前空翻式恰好是一样很有效的东西。

而且不止他一个人在用。同一篇1974年的报道点出爱荷华大学的尼尔森是一个早期的示范者，他用这个翻转把个人最好成绩提高到二十二英尺六英寸;报道说他把这个技术展示给拉格奎斯特，之后西德的施蒂尔勒采用了它，跳到七点五米。报道还说，到1974年年中，对这个技术的信息需求表明，已经有数百名大多是学龄的试验者。

这里要立刻纠正一个流传的错误。匡德不属于前空翻式跳远的实践者名单。可查的记录把匡德放在福斯贝里之前的跳高故事里，他是蒙大拿一个更早试验背向过杆的跳高运动员。前一篇之前那一篇讲过他。把他放进1974年的前空翻式跳远，是后来的以讹传讹。

关于这个技术为什么能跳得更远，当时的论据是力学的，不只是好看。体育画报1974年的文章概括了埃克的论证:前空翻利用了向前的转动，而不是去对抗它;蜷缩的身体形状减小了阻力;并且可能允许以一个更有利的角度更快地起跳。德拉米尔本人告诉体育画报，他觉得等自己伤好了，技术打磨好了，能跳到二十八英尺。

有一个更大的说法要谨慎处理。有一种流行的讲法说前空翻式跳远很可能会突破三十英尺。这个说法在后来的评论里被反复提起，但它在比赛中从未被证明，不应当被夸大。现代的概述和物理科普重复三十英尺这个可能，可是那始终是一个叠在理论和模拟之上的推测，不是在可批准条件下取得的结果。所以准确的说法是:这个技术真的能跳得很远，平过奥运冠军的成绩，但三十英尺是一个推算，不是一个发生过的事实。

把这两件事分清楚很重要，因为它们常常被混为一谈。一个技术被禁，而它本可能达到的高度是一个推算，这两件事叠在一起，很容易被讲成这个构禁掉了一个本可以破三十英尺的技术，仿佛那三十英尺是确凿的，被禁令扼杀的成就。而实际上那三十英尺从未发生，它只存在于模拟和推测里。这个构禁掉的，是一个已经能平奥运冠军，并且看起来还有潜力的技术;它没有禁掉一个已经跳出三十英尺的事实，因为那个事实不存在。夸大被禁掉的东西，和低估它，都不是准确的历史。

三 边界在被越过时才被画出来

这个技术被禁了。而禁它的过程，恰好演示了这一篇的题目。

先看规则文本，它是清楚的。在后来的国际田联和世界田径规则里，直到今天仍然明确:在跳远里，如果运动员在助跑或者起跳的动作中使用任何形式的空翻，判失败。这句话出现在现行的世界田径技术规则里，和更早的国际田联措辞明显是连续的。

1974年的全美大学体育协会规则中，已经有禁止试跳空翻的条款。国际田联这一边，也并非连会议都无法定位：1974年10月5日《Athletics Weekly》的同期报道，把禁令明确放在罗马大会，并记述了技术委员会关于安全与项目性质的理由。同期报道能帮助定位决定，但它还不是原始决议文本。

所以这里的档案空缺应当缩小：可以依据同期报道确定大会和禁令，尚未找到的是原始决议、具体条文的通过记录，以及完整讨论过程。一个决定留下了新闻中的踪迹，不等于它如何形成已经毫无遗漏地呈现在我们面前。这个系列关心的，正是证据能把我们带到哪里，又在哪里停下来。

现在看这个禁令是什么时候出现的。

前空翻式跳远是1974年出现在大赛上的。禁它的规则，也是1974年成文的。这两个时间几乎重合。

这一点是这一篇的核心，但需要把范围说准。1974年的报道让我们看见，一种正在比赛与训练中尝试的技术，怎样受到国际规则的明确禁止。它不能替我们证明，在此前所有机构、所有年份的规则里，都从未有过相关限制。也不能因为技术新出现，就断言规则不会预先禁止尚未实践的动作。能够看见的是：这种翻转把原先尚未被充分说清的项目边界，推到了必须讨论并作决定的位置。

这和前一篇之前那一篇里跳高的情形，恰好是一对镜像。

背越式进入了跳高规则没有指定唯一过杆姿势的空间。旧限制的取消与软垫的普及，为它的采用提供了条件。前空翻式也在当时一些比赛和训练里获得了试验空间。但它遇到另一种结果：主管机构明确开口，在国际比赛中把这种动作列为失败。两种革新获得的空间不一样，不能简单当成同一条沉默规则下的两次实验。

背越式得到物质条件的放行，前空翻式则被明确规则追上。这里有一种值得考虑的差别：发展的节奏。背越式随软垫普及，逐渐累积成绩与使用习惯；前空翻式在短时间里聚集了试验、一次与奥运冠军并列的成绩，以及三十英尺的远大预测。这种关注程度的差异，可能影响决策。但这不等于背越式从未惊动机构，也不等于前空翻式仅仅因为太扎眼就被禁掉。

于是这个构开口了。而它一开口，那条本来不存在的边界，就被画出来了。

这里可以提出一个关于显眼程度的解释。一种革新在既有规则没有明确回答的地方出现，能否被接受，也许与它的发展节奏、既成成绩和引起的关注有关。发展久了再禁止，机构需要面对已经形成的使用习惯；刚出现时作决定，阻力可能不同。背越式与前空翻式的遭遇使这种差别值得研究。但它还不是已经排除了安全、动作特征和管理层选择的唯一原因，不能把命运全部归给“谁长得更悄悄”。

四 安全理由，与仍不完整的证据

禁令提出了安全理由。但在本次能查到的公开材料中，还找不到足以重建风险大小的完整证据。

当时官方的反对，明确地被表述为安全，但还有第二个更概念性的反对。体育画报报道，国际田联技术委员会的成员既觉得这个技术可能危及运动员的颈部，又觉得它如此不同，以至于可能不是传统的跳远。这是两个不同的理由，不应当被合并。

先看这两个理由。第一个是安全，怕伤到颈部。第二个不是安全，是这个动作太不一样了，不像跳远了。这第二个理由和运动员会不会受伤毫无关系，它关心的是这还算不算跳远。后来的转述通常把这两个理由压成一句因安全被禁，而丢掉了第二个。恰恰是第二个理由，更接近这一篇要找的那条分界线。

再看安全这个理由的证据。

找不到任何当时的伤害数据集，医学附录，或者量化的风险研究，附在这个禁令的决定上。1974年在体育画报上的辩论几乎全是假设性的:万一运动员蜷缩的时机没掌握好怎么办，万一脚踝撑不住怎么办，颈部受伤怎么办。而埃克和华盛顿州立大学的助理教练斯隆明确否认这个技术特别危险，同样也没有他们自己的伤害统计。

所以，在这篇同期报道呈现的争论里，双方都没有列出可核对的伤害统计。说它危险的一方提出风险情形，说它安全的一方提出相反判断。我们能够确认的是这些公开论据的形状，不能由报道没有列数字，就断定委员会掌握的全部资料也必然是空白。

这一点值得和前面几篇连起来看。成绩要进入纪录，通常需要能按具体标准复核的计时、丈量、风速和器材资料。技术禁令则还包含另一层判断：即使没有已经积累的大量事故，机构也可能需要评估潜在风险。这里可追问的是，它提出的风险依据怎样公开、怎样接受检验，而不是因为暂时找不到数字，就认定决定没有任何依据。

这种差别不使判断免于证据要求。测量通常围绕一个明确的量，安全判断还要比较可能的伤害、不确定性和等待的代价。后者未必能化成同一种精度的数字，却仍需要说明理由、认真对待相反证据，并留下修正的可能。同一个机构可以在不同任务上采用不同标准，但不能因此把感觉本身当成无需解释的结论。

这不是说禁令一定错误。前空翻可能有真实风险，预防性禁止也可能有理由。现有材料不足以让我们把它定为纯粹凭感觉的决定。能够继续追问的是：除了风险，当时的人还明说过它不像传统跳远。这个关于项目性质的理由，不应被安全一句话遮住，也不需要靠宣布安全必是借口来成立。

五 另一个被提出的理由

它太不一样了，可能不是传统的跳远。

这是另一个值得单独看的理由，也使这一篇能够追问：项目的边界怎样被说出来。

一个跳远运动员，助跑、起跳，在空中翻一个跟头，落进沙坑。在允许这种动作的比赛里，距离可以按既有方法丈量。但一些技术委员会成员仍觉得，这不像传统的跳远。他们心里的画面，是人助跑、起跳，在空中保持某种伸展姿势，然后落地，翻跟头不在这个画面里。另一些教练和运动员却不同意这种理解。正是这种分歧，迫使机构作出决定。

这里出现了一条尚未充分说清的线。它不只关乎起跳板、沙坑、风速与丈量，也关乎一部分管理者心里的项目形象。这个形象不等于此前所有规则的总和，也不是所有参与者一致同意的理解。争论让一种落差显形了：既有的允许空间，与管理者现在想要排除的东西，并不完全重合。

前空翻式触动了这部分人对项目的理解。在允许使用这种动作的比赛中，它可以按既有办法被丈量；而主管机构仍可能决定以后不再允许。于是问题摆在眼前：是扩大项目的解释，承认这种翻转也是跳远，还是把它明确排除？同期报道显示，国际机构选择了后者；这不意味着所有运动员与教练原先就共享同一条边界。

它选择了守住。它把那条一直看不见的线，变成了一条成文的规则:不得空翻。

构在被越过时发现边界，可以这样理解。一部分决策者带着尚未充分说出的项目形象，新动作向它提出挑战，逼他们表明是守住这个形象，还是把它扩大。这并不意味着此前谁也没想过这个问题。德拉米尔的跟头让我们看见：一种并未预先决定一切的理解，怎样变成具有约束力的明确决定。

所以题目用了“发现”：一个新案例，让尚未充分澄清的期待显现。但发现并不排斥在当下重新划线。那条边界未必早已完整地沉睡在暗处，它也可能在旧有形象与新实践的冲突中，才取得具体形状。碰撞让构看见自己的决定权，也逼它面对：接下来，这条线究竟要画在哪里。

前一篇之前那一篇里，1936年取消挡住背越式的条款，是这个构在清理一条它以为多余的旧规则，它没想到那条规则是一道门。这里是反过来的:这个构发现自己需要一道本来没有的门，于是临时造了一道。一个是无意中拆了一道门，一个是被逼着造了一道门。两件事都说明，这个构并不预先知道自己的边界在哪里，它要等到有人走到边界上，才知道那里应该有一道门，或者不应该有。

六 另一样新东西，规则早就留了门

现在看那件相反的事。

撑竿跳的撑竿，材料换过好几次。世界田径自己的历史概述给出这个时间线:有记录的竹竿最早在1857年使用;钢竿在1940年代引入;柔韧的玻璃纤维竿，然后是碳纤维竿，成了现代的标准。

玻璃纤维竿带来的变化是巨大的。它没有被禁，而且它带来的纪录一个都没有被清掉。

第一个用玻璃纤维竿创造世界纪录的突破来自戴维斯，1961年;于尔塞斯在1962年跟上;到1963年，这个项目已经进入常规的玻璃纤维破纪录状态。到1963年，大多数顶尖撑竿跳选手已经换了竿。

变化的幅度可以量化。俄勒冈大学的一项革新研究大致这样概括:在竹竿时代，从1899到1944年，世界纪录提高了三英尺九英寸;而在玻璃纤维和相关的聚合物时代革新到来之后，自1960年起，纪录上升了大约四英尺六英寸。这是一场不亚于任何技术革命的变化，而它没有触发任何一次纪录重置。

为什么。因为规则早就把门留在了开着的位置。

关于撑竿材料的规则，一直异常宽松。现行的世界田径规则说:撑竿可以由任何材料或者材料的组合制成，长度和直径不限，但基本表面必须光滑。而1966年一项关于玻璃纤维撑竿跳的历史研究说，国际田联没有立法反对玻璃纤维，因为它自己的规则已经说了，撑竿可以由任何材料，任何长度和任何直径制成。

这一句是关键。已有规则里的“任何材料”，给玻璃纤维竿提供了明确的合法空间。材料变新，并不自动越界。不能仅凭这一点断定，管理者完全没有考虑过公平或安全；但在已经存在的文字许可之内，革新被接纳时，不必先改写撑竿的材料定义。

玻璃纤维竿来的时候，也有过争议。这里要把争议和裁决分开。

争议这一边:当时的人把玻璃纤维竿叫作花招，或者一种不公平的机械辅助，杂志的报道公开地辩论这个革新是否公平。

裁决这一边:这个器械保持合法，因为它落在那条本来就开放的撑竿规则之内;找不到任何一手证据显示国际田联曾经决定为玻璃纤维竿的使用者另立一份世界纪录名单。

所以玻璃纤维竿和前空翻式的差别，不在于一个有争议、一个没有，两个都有争议。差别在于，前空翻式引发了关于跳远定义的冲突，最终被明确排除；玻璃纤维竿则落在撑竿规则早已写得很宽的许可之内。一边是宽泛的明文允许，一边是继承下来的项目形象在争论中被明确成限制，两条边界并不具有同样的来历。

容易忽略的是，玻璃纤维竿留在界内，并不是因为影响小。它在成绩发展中作用很大，前面引用的综述把相关时代的纪录增幅记为四英尺六英寸。如果规则只准竹竿，机构就得考虑修改规则、设置例外，或拒绝新竿，并不是禁用或纪录重置会自动发生。决定命运的不只是技术有多激进，还有定义留下了多大空间，以及机构怎样使用这个空间。

七 从案例里长出来的一条原则

把这两件事，加上别的几件，放在一起，能归纳出一条原则。但必须先说清楚，这条原则不是写在任何地方的。

找不到任何一份成文的国际田联或者世界田径的总则，在一个地方说什么时候重置纪录，什么时候不重置。世界田径的纪录规则描述的是纪录如何在合规的比赛里被批准，并且允许世界田径的领导层或者理事会来决定有疑问的情形，但它没有为每一个未来的技术变化设定一个普遍的哲学检验。

所以下面这条原则，是从实际发生的决定里归纳出来的，是对这些决定实际上如何做出的最好的，基于证据的综合。它是一个推断，不是一条被找到的教义。

这条原则是这样。

当这个构重新定义了合法的器械本身，从而改变了这个项目的合法性条件时，它重置纪录，建立一套新的纪录。1986年男子标枪的重设是这样，1991年反锯齿尾的回撤也是这样。

当革新落在一个已经合法的器械定义之内，或者当规则只是增加了前瞻性的认证要求时，它不重置。玻璃纤维竿是这样，合成跑道是这样，起跑信息系统是这样。

标枪那一边，值得展开，因为它是最干净的重置案例。

世界田径自己的历史概述说，男子标枪在1986年被重新设计，因为越来越频繁的平落，关于有效性的争论，以及在霍恩创造一百零四米八零的纪录之后，对一个体育场内场来说可能危险的距离。世界田径明确地说，1986年的重设需要一套新的纪录。这次重置的规模是巨大的:男子纪录实际上从旧器械的一百零四米八零，重设到新器械的八十五米七四，下降了十九米零六。这不是一次单纯的前瞻性批准微调，这是一套新的器械规格，配一本新的纪录簿。

标枪还提供了第二个重置的例子。在制造商加上锯齿尾，尾部阻力的改动，恢复了一些损失的距离之后，那些器械在1991年被取缔，用它们创造的最好成绩被撤销，纪录回到用1986年之后常规器械投出的最好成绩。

现在把标枪和撑竿跳并排看。标枪1986年重置了纪录，因为它重新定义了标枪这个器械本身，改变了合法的规格。撑竿跳没有重置纪录，因为它没有重新定义撑竿，那条任何材料的规则从头到尾没变。同样是器械变了，一个重置，一个不重置，分界线就在于这个构有没有动那个器械的合法定义。

这条分界线有一个很实际的后果，值得点出来。它意味着，一样新器械会不会触发纪录重置，不取决于它让成绩变好了多少，而取决于旧规则的定义碰巧写得多宽。标枪的规则写得具体，规定了重心的位置，重量的分布，所以一旦这些参数被改动，就是一个新器械，就要重置。撑竿的规则写得笼统，只说任何材料，所以材料怎么变都还在定义之内，不触发重置。同样的技术进步，落在写得具体的规则上会触发重置，落在写得笼统的规则上不会。重置与否，一半取决于当年立规则的人是把话说细了还是说宽了。

这条原则还解释了合成跑道和起跑器。合成跑道取代煤渣跑道，是一次巨大的场地变化，可是它没有重置纪录，因为世界田径没有把项目重新定义到一个更窄的场地类别上，它只是要求纪录必须在认证的场地上创造。这是一个前瞻性的批准条件，不是对旧纪录的追溯废除。

八 一条从来没有写下来的线

把这一篇收拢起来。

这个构对相似问题给出了相反答案，而定义是一条值得单独辨认的分界线。公开材料中，安全与项目性质曾同时被提出；没有找到完整伤害统计，不能让前者自动消失。我们仍能看见另一件事：一种革新怎样被纳入项目的解释，又怎样被明确挡在外面。

前空翻式越过了跳远的定义，它太不一样了，可能不是传统的跳远，于是它被禁。标枪的重设越过了合法器械的定义，一百零四米八零的标枪和八十五米七四的标枪是两种不同的器械，于是纪录被重置。而玻璃纤维竿没有越过撑竿的定义，那条任何材料的规则本来就把它包在内，于是它被吸收，纪录被保留。越过定义就重写，落在定义内就吸收。

但这条原则里藏着一件更深的事，就是这些边界大多不是预先画好的。

1974年的报道让我们看见关于空翻的明确国际决定，却不能替我们重建此前所有规则。与撑竿的差别，可以从文字上看出来：一条宽泛的材料许可已经包容了玻璃纤维。单凭这一点，我们无法知道旧规则的起草者当时把未来想到了多远。说明宽规则的效果，并不需要替他们断言是随意，还是远见。

这里，边界以两种方式出现。撑竿有宽泛的明文许可，可以容纳后来出现的材料；跳远的新实践则与某种继承下来的项目形象冲突，随后形成明确限制。我们不能仅凭这个结果，断言过去的设计未经思考，或已经深思熟虑。能够辨认的是：一种允许早已很宽，另一种决定则被新案例推到了眼前。

这就带出这一篇的余项。

前一篇之前那一篇里，背越式是一片被物质条件封住的可能，等物质松开就涌了出来。前空翻式不一样。它没有被物质封住，软垫早就有了，沙坑也在，翻跟头落进沙坑在物质上是可行的。挡住前空翻式的，不是物质，是那条关于这个项目是什么的看不见的线。而这条线是可以被移动的，它不是物理定律，是一个共同的理解，是一种约定。这个构本可以把它往外推，承认翻跟头也是跳远。它选择了不推。

于是前空翻式成了一种特别的余项：一种确实做得到、已经在比赛里跳出有分量成绩，却后来被国际规则排除的技术可能。它不是没有发生，而是不再被那套规则允许。德拉米尔的翻转、其他试验者的动作、施蒂尔勒那七点五米，都属于它留下的踪迹。这些材料能证明它值得认真对待，不能证明它已经在控制条件下优于常规技术。禁令守住了一种对项目的理解；这项选择本身，仍然可以被追问。

这个可能因此失去了在国际正式比赛中继续发展的空间。同期报道还说明，国内比赛是否跟随禁令，需要各国机构另作决定；训练中的尝试也不因国际条款而在物理上消失。二十八英尺、三十英尺所指向的更远前景，仍是未被同等正式比赛充分检验的推测。制度画下一条线，也改变了一条技术道路能够怎样继续。那条路到底会走多远，我们不能替历史补出答案。

这种被决定限制的余项，值得单独辨认。前面有被排到账外的名字，有尚未解释清楚的成因，有机构不进入的技术优劣问题。这里，机构确实有选择：它可以在评估风险的同时扩大理解，也可以写下限制。它选择了禁止。这样的选择不同于物理上的做不到；它的理由与其他可能方案仍然可以被追问，而不必先断言唯一的原因就是旧约定。

第十一篇会把这一层讲得更彻底。霍恩的104.80米是合法而有效的历史成绩；新规格实行后，旧规格的纪录序列不再接收新条目。封存的是那套比较条件下的账本，不是宣布104.80米这个距离在人类身体上永远不可达到。

I. Two Similar Questions, Two Opposite Answers

Two developments had almost the same shape.

In one, someone invented a new long-jump technique: the forward somersault. It was banned.

In the other, someone introduced a new material into pole vaulting: the fibreglass pole. It was not banned, and all the records it produced were retained.

Both were new things appearing within the sport, capable of improving performance. Both provoked controversy and were challenged as unfair. Yet the construct gave opposite answers: prohibition for one, admission for the other.

The previous two essays concerned the silence of the rules. High-jump rules did not prescribe how to clear the bar, so the flop entered that silence and survived. Shot-put rules did not prescribe the glide or the rotation, so the two techniques coexisted within that silence for more than half a century, since the 1970s. This essay asks when that silence ends. A construct cannot remain silent about everything forever. At some point it must speak. By comparing these opposite outcomes, we will try to locate the boundary between speaking and remaining silent.

The comparison is not arbitrary. The cases resemble one another in several important respects yet have opposite outcomes: both belong to athletics, promise improved performances, and provoke debate about fairness and safety. But this is not a controlled experiment eliminating every other difference. It helps identify a question that a safety debate can obscure: whether an innovation is still understood as part of the same event. Following that question brings the essay to one word: definition.

One point should be clear at the outset. Saying “for safety” does not fully explain the opposite outcomes. Contemporary reporting raised both possible neck injury and whether somersaulting remained within the spirit of long jumping. The accessible material does not supply complete statistics with which to assess the risk. That does not establish that decision-makers never considered safety or that it was merely a pretext. This essay concentrates on the other reason already present in the discussion: whether an innovation crossed the event's understanding of itself.

This essay and the previous two thus form a complete sequence. They concerned the silence of the rules; this one concerns its endpoint. A construct cannot remain silent about everything. It has a limit beyond which it must speak. But reading the rules cannot tell us where that limit lies, because the rules are precisely what remain silent beyond it. We can only infer the boundary's position by observing when the silence breaks. The somersault made the construct speak; fibreglass did not. Together, they mark the line.

II. A New Technique with Real Competitive Results

In 1974, Delamere used the somersault long jump in major American collegiate competition.

Sports Illustrated's report of 29 July 1974 said that he used the technique at the NCAA championships in Austin without reaching the final. Three weeks earlier, at the Pacific-8 meet, he had jumped 25 feet 6¾ inches with it, tying Olympic champion Williams. The same report gave Delamere's best wind-assisted jump using a conventional technique as 25 feet 9½ inches.

This was therefore no circus stunt. With a forward somersault, he matched an Olympic champion's performance. The technique genuinely worked.

That must be established first, because it determines the nature of the entire case. If the somersault had merely been an acrobatic trick that produced short jumps, banning it would hardly matter. The construct would simply have disposed of a harmless oddity, without any real question of boundaries. But this was different. It matched an Olympic champion's performance and was thought to be far from its limit. Precisely because it could really produce distance, banning it was a consequential decision the construct had to weigh. The more effective a thing is, the greater the significance of prohibiting it. The somersault was highly effective.

Nor was Delamere alone. The same 1974 report identified Nielsen of the University of Iowa as an early demonstrator. The rotation had improved his personal best to 22 feet 6 inches. It reported that he showed the technique to Lagerqvist, after which West Germany's Stierle adopted it and reached 7.5 metres. By mid-1974, according to the article, demand for information suggested that several hundred people, mostly of school age, were experimenting with it.

A circulating error must immediately be corrected. Quande does not belong on the list of somersault long-jump practitioners. The available record places him in the high-jump story before Fosbury: a Montana high jumper who experimented earlier with backward clearance. Essay 8 discussed him. Inserting him into the somersault long jump of 1974 is a later error repeated from one account to another.

The contemporary argument for the technique's potential was mechanical, not merely visual. Sports Illustrated's 1974 article summarised Ecker's reasoning: the somersault exploited forward rotation rather than resisting it; the tucked body reduced drag; and it might allow a faster take-off at a more favourable angle. Delamere himself told the magazine he believed that once his injury healed and his technique improved, he could reach twenty-eight feet.

A larger claim requires caution. One popular account suggests that the somersault long jump would very likely have broken thirty feet. Later commentary repeatedly invokes that possibility, but it was never demonstrated in competition and should not be exaggerated. Modern summaries and popular physics accounts repeat thirty feet as a possibility, yet it remains speculation resting on theory and simulation, not a result achieved under ratifiable conditions. The accurate statement is that the technique could genuinely produce very long jumps and matched an Olympic champion's performance. Thirty feet was a projection, not an event that happened.

Separating these matters is important because they are often conflated. A technique is banned, and its potential achievement is a projection. Put those facts together and the story easily becomes one of a construct banning a technique that could have exceeded thirty feet, as though that distance were a definite achievement killed by prohibition. In fact, thirty feet never happened. It existed only in simulation and conjecture. The construct banned a technique that could already match an Olympic champion and appeared to have further potential. It did not ban an accomplished thirty-foot jump, because no such accomplishment existed. Exaggerating what was prohibited is no more accurate history than understating it.

III. A Boundary Drawn Only When It Is Crossed

The technique was banned. The process demonstrates the proposition in this essay's title.

Begin with the rule text, which is clear. Later IAAF and World Athletics rules, continuing to the present, explicitly make a long-jump attempt a failure if the athlete employs any form of somersault during the run-up or take-off. This wording appears in the current World Athletics Technical Rules and shows clear continuity with earlier IAAF language.

The 1974 NCAA rules already contained a provision prohibiting somersaults in an attempt. On the IAAF side, it is not even the case that the relevant meeting cannot be located. Contemporary reporting in Athletics Weekly on 5 October 1974 explicitly places the ban at the Rome Congress and records the Technical Committee's reasons concerning safety and the nature of the event. Contemporary reporting helps locate the decision, but is not the original resolution.

The archival gap should therefore be defined more narrowly. Contemporary reporting establishes the Congress and the ban. What has not yet been found is the original resolution, the record of adoption of the precise wording, and the complete discussion. A decision's trace in the news does not mean its formation is available to us without omission. This series is concerned precisely with how far evidence can take us, and where it stops.

Now consider when the prohibition appeared.

The somersault long jump appeared in major competition in 1974. The rule banning it was also written in 1974. The dates almost coincide.

This is the heart of the essay, but its scope matters. Reporting from 1974 shows an emerging technique, tried in competition and training, becoming the subject of an explicit international ban. It does not establish that no relevant restriction existed in every earlier rulebook of every governing body. Nor does a technique's novelty prove that rules never prohibit an action before it is tried. What we can see is this rotation forcing an insufficiently articulated boundary into debate and decision.

This is a mirror image of the high-jump situation in Essay 8.

The flop entered a space in which high-jump rules did not prescribe one clearance style. The removal of earlier restrictions and the spread of soft landing mats provided conditions for its adoption. The somersault also found room for experiment in some competitions and training. It met a different response: an explicit international rule making the movement a failed attempt. The spaces available to the two innovations differed; these were not two controlled tests under one identical silent rule.

Material conditions helped the flop; an explicit prohibition caught up with the somersault. One possible difference concerns the pace of development. The flop spread with soft mats, accumulating performances and habits. The somersault drew concentrated attention through experiments, one result matching an Olympic champion and large projections of thirty feet. That difference in attention may have mattered to decisions. It does not establish that the flop went unnoticed, or that conspicuousness alone caused the somersault's ban.

The construct spoke. As it did, the previously nonexistent boundary was drawn.

One interpretation concerns conspicuousness. An innovation entering a space without a clear answer may be received differently depending on its pace of development, established results and public attention. A later ban confronts habits already formed; an early decision may meet different resistance. The contrast between the flop and the somersault makes that possibility worth examining. It does not eliminate safety, the movements' features or the choices of particular officials as contributing explanations. Their fates cannot be assigned wholly to which grew more quietly.

IV. A Safety Rationale and an Incomplete Record

The ban invoked safety. The publicly accessible material examined here does not provide enough evidence to reconstruct the size of the risk.

Official objections at the time were explicitly framed in terms of safety, but there was a second, more conceptual objection. Sports Illustrated reported that members of the IAAF Technical Committee both feared the technique might endanger athletes' necks and considered it so different that it might not be traditional long jumping. Those are two distinct reasons and should not be merged.

Look at them separately. The first is safety: fear of neck injury. The second is not about safety. The movement is too different; it no longer looks like long jumping. That objection has nothing to do with whether athletes will be hurt. It asks whether this still counts as the long jump. Later accounts commonly compress both reasons into banned for safety, losing the second. Yet the second comes closer to the dividing line this essay seeks.

Now examine the evidence for the safety rationale.

No contemporary injury dataset, medical appendix or quantified risk study has been found attached to the decision. The debate in Sports Illustrated in 1974 was almost entirely hypothetical. What if an athlete mistimed the tuck? What if the ankle could not withstand it? What about neck injuries? Ecker and Washington State University assistant coach Sloan explicitly denied that the technique was particularly dangerous, but offered no injury statistics of their own either.

In the debate presented by this contemporary report, neither side supplied injury statistics that could be checked. Those calling the technique dangerous described possible hazards; those defending it offered a different judgment. We can describe those public arguments. The absence of numbers in a magazine report does not establish that every item of information available to the committee was absent too.

That deserves comparison with earlier essays. Record ratification normally requires timing, measurement, wind and equipment evidence that can be checked against specific standards. A technical ban also involves another judgment: a body may have to assess a potential danger before many accidents have accumulated. The question is how its reasons are made public and open to examination, not whether failure to locate statistics proves that it had no grounds at all.

This difference does not exempt judgment from evidence. Measurement usually concerns a defined quantity; a safety decision also weighs possible harm, uncertainty and the cost of waiting. It may not produce a number of the same precision, but it still needs reasons, attention to contrary evidence and room for revision. An institution can use different standards for different tasks without treating its own unease as a conclusion that requires no explanation.

This does not establish that the ban was wrong. The somersault may have posed real risks, and precautionary prohibition may have had grounds. The material available does not justify declaring the decision pure intuition. What remains worth examining is the other reason contemporaries stated: that the movement did not fit traditional long jumping. That reason about the event's character need not disappear behind the word safety, and recognizing it does not require calling safety a pretext.

V. Another Reason Given at the Time

It was so different that it might not be traditional long jumping.

This is another reason worth examining on its own. It lets the essay ask how an event's boundary becomes explicit.

A long jumper runs up, takes off, turns a somersault and lands in the sand. In a competition permitting that movement, the distance can be measured by the existing method. Some committee members nevertheless saw something outside traditional long jumping. Their picture was of a person running, taking off, remaining in a more or less extended posture and landing; a somersault did not fit. Other coaches and athletes disputed that picture. It was precisely the disagreement that demanded a decision.

An insufficiently articulated line appears here. It concerns not only the board, sand, wind and measurement, but also some officials' image of the event. That image is not identical with every earlier rulebook, nor was it the unanimous view of participants. The dispute exposes a gap between existing permissions and what officials now want to exclude.

The somersault challenged that understanding of the event. In a competition permitting the movement, its distance could be measured by the existing method; a governing body could still decide not to allow it in future. The choice was whether to broaden the interpretation and admit the rotation as long jumping or explicitly exclude it. Contemporary reporting shows the international body chose exclusion. That does not mean all athletes and coaches had previously shared the same boundary.

It chose to defend it. The previously invisible line became a written rule: no somersaults.

This is one way to understand a construct discovering its boundary when crossed. Some decision-makers brought an image of the event that had not been fully articulated. The new movement challenged it and forced them to say whether they would defend or broaden it. That does not mean nobody had considered the question before. Delamere's rotation shows an understanding that had not settled everything becoming an explicit, binding decision.

The title therefore speaks of discovery: a new case makes insufficiently examined expectations visible. Discovery does not exclude drawing something new at that moment. A boundary need not have lain complete and dormant in the shadows; it may take concrete form in the conflict between an inherited image and a new practice. The collision reveals the construct's own power to decide, and forces it to face where the line should now be drawn.

In Essay 8, the 1936 removal of a clause that would have barred the flop was the construct clearing away an old rule it considered unnecessary, without realising that the rule was a door. Here the process is reversed. The construct discovers that it needs a door where none existed and hurriedly builds one. In one case it inadvertently removed a door; in the other it was forced to build one. Both show that it does not know in advance where its boundaries lie. It must wait for someone to reach the boundary before discovering whether a door should be there.

VI. For Another Innovation, the Rules Had Already Left a Door Open

Now consider the opposite case.

Pole-vault poles have changed material several times. World Athletics' own historical overview gives this chronology: the first recorded use of bamboo poles was in 1857; steel poles were introduced in the 1940s; flexible fibreglass poles, followed by carbon-fibre poles, became the modern standard.

The transformation brought by fibreglass was enormous. It was not banned, and none of its records were removed.

The first world-record breakthrough with fibreglass came from Davies in 1961. Uelses followed in 1962. By 1963, fibreglass record-breaking had become routine, and most leading vaulters had changed poles.

The scale can be quantified. A University of Oregon study of innovation summarised it approximately this way: during the bamboo era, from 1899 to 1944, the world record rose by 3 feet 9 inches; after innovations of the fibreglass and related polymer era arrived, it rose by approximately 4 feet 6 inches from 1960 onward. This was a transformation equal to any technical revolution, yet it triggered no reset of the records.

Why? Because the rules had already left the door open.

The rule on pole materials was exceptionally permissive. Current World Athletics rules say that a pole may be made from any material or combination of materials, of any length or diameter, provided its basic surface is smooth. A 1966 historical study of fibreglass pole vaulting said that the IAAF did not legislate against fibreglass because its own rules already allowed poles of any material, length and diameter.

That sentence is decisive. The existing words any material gave fibreglass a clear space of legality. A new material did not automatically cross the boundary. This alone does not establish that officials gave no thought to fairness or safety. But admitting an innovation already within that permission did not require rewriting the pole's material definition first.

Fibreglass also provoked controversy. Here controversy must be separated from adjudication.

On the controversy side, contemporaries called fibreglass poles a gimmick or an unfair mechanical aid, and magazines openly debated the innovation's fairness.

On the adjudication side, the equipment remained legal because it fell within the already open pole rule. No primary evidence has been found of an IAAF decision to establish a separate world-record list for fibreglass users.

The difference between fibreglass and the somersault was not that only one was controversial. Both were. The somersault provoked a dispute about the definition of long jumping and was explicitly excluded; fibreglass fell within a pole rule already written broadly. One boundary was a broad, express permission. The other emerged as a restriction when an inherited image of the event came into dispute. The two boundaries did not have identical origins.

What is easily missed is that fibreglass stayed inside the boundary not because its effect was small. Its part in the development of performances was considerable; the account cited earlier gives a rise of 4 feet 6 inches over the relevant era. A bamboo-only rule would have required consideration of an amendment, an exception or refusal of the new pole. Neither a ban nor a record reset would follow automatically. What matters is not only how radical a technology is, but the space opened by a definition and how the institution uses it.

VII. A Principle Emerging from the Cases

Set these two cases beside several others and a principle can be inferred. First, however, we must be clear that it is not written down anywhere.

No general IAAF or World Athletics provision has been found that states in one place when records are reset and when they are not. World Athletics' record rules describe ratification within compliant competitions and allow its leadership or Council to decide doubtful cases. They do not set out a universal philosophical test for every future technological change.

The following principle is therefore inferred from decisions that actually occurred. It is the best evidence-based synthesis of how those decisions operated, not a discovered doctrine.

The principle is this.

When the construct redefines the legal implement itself, changing the event's conditions of legality, it resets the records and establishes a new series. The men's javelin redesign in 1986 did this, as did the rollback against serrated tails in 1991.

When innovation falls within an already legal definition of equipment, or when the rules merely add prospective certification requirements, it does not reset the records. Fibreglass poles, synthetic tracks and start information systems follow this pattern.

The javelin deserves elaboration because it is the clearest reset case.

World Athletics' own historical account says the men's javelin was redesigned in 1986 because of increasingly frequent flat landings, disputes over validity, and distances potentially dangerous for a stadium infield after Hohn's 104.80-metre record. It explicitly states that the 1986 redesign required a new set of records. The scale was enormous: the men's record effectively reset from 104.80 metres with the old implement to 85.74 with the new, a drop of 19.06 metres. This was not merely a prospective adjustment to approval. It was a new specification accompanied by a new record book.

The javelin also supplies a second reset example. Manufacturers introduced serrated tails, modifying tail drag and recovering some of the lost distance. Those implements were outlawed in 1991, the best marks achieved with them were rescinded, and the record reverted to the best performance with a conventional post-1986 implement.

Now compare the javelin and pole vault side by side. The javelin's records reset in 1986 because the implement itself was redefined and its legal specifications changed. Pole-vault records did not reset because the pole was not redefined: the any-material rule remained unchanged throughout. In both cases equipment changed, but only one produced a reset. The dividing line was whether the construct altered the implement's legal definition.

That distinction has a practical consequence worth identifying. Whether new equipment triggers a reset depends not on how much it improves performance, but on how broadly the old rule happened to be written. Javelin rules are specific, prescribing the centre of gravity and distribution of weight. Changing those parameters creates a new implement and requires a reset. Pole rules are general, allowing any material, so material changes remain within the definition and trigger none. The same technological progress may cause a reset under a precise rule but not under a broad one. Whether records reset depends in substantial part on how narrowly or broadly the original rule-makers expressed themselves.

This principle also explains synthetic tracks and starting blocks. Replacing cinder with synthetic surfaces was a vast change of venue, but it did not reset records. World Athletics did not redefine the events into a narrower class of facility. It required records to be achieved at certified facilities. That is a prospective ratification condition, not retrospective abolition of old records.

VIII. A Line That Was Never Written Down

Let us bring the essay together.

The construct gave opposite answers to related questions, and definition is one boundary worth distinguishing. Safety and the event's character both appeared in public discussion; failure to locate complete injury statistics does not erase the former. Another question remains visible: how an innovation is included in the understanding of an event, or explicitly kept outside it.

The somersault crossed the definition of long jumping. It was too different and might not be traditional long jumping, so it was banned. Redesigning the javelin crossed the definition of the legal implement. The javelins of 104.80 and 85.74 metres were different implements, so the records were reset. Fibreglass did not cross the definition of a pole. The any-material rule already encompassed it, so it was absorbed and the records retained. Cross the definition and the system rewrites; remain within it and the system absorbs.

But something deeper lies within that principle: most of these boundaries were not drawn in advance.

The reporting from 1974 shows an explicit international decision about somersaulting. It does not reconstruct every earlier rulebook. The contrast with the pole appears in the wording: a broad material permission already included fibreglass. That alone does not tell us how far earlier rule-makers had considered future possibilities. The effects of a broad rule can be described without attributing either carelessness or foresight to its writers.

Boundaries appear here in two ways. Pole vaulting had a broad written permission accommodating a new material. In long jumping, a new practice conflicted with an inherited image of the event and led to an explicit restriction. The result alone does not tell us whether earlier thinking was casual or carefully developed. What becomes visible is the contrast between an already broad permission and a decision made urgent by a new case.

This brings us to the essay's remainder.

In Essay 8, the flop was a possibility enclosed by material conditions, emerging once those conditions loosened. The somersault was different. It was not materially blocked. Soft mats already existed, as did sandpits; turning a somersault into a sandpit was physically feasible. What blocked it was not matter but the invisible line defining what the event was. That line could be moved. It was not a law of physics, but a shared understanding, a convention. The construct could have moved it outward and recognised somersaulting as long jumping. It chose not to.

The somersault thus became a particular remainder: a technically feasible possibility with substantial competitive results, later excluded by international rules. It had happened, but that rule system no longer allowed it. Delamere's rotations, other experiments and Stierle's 7.5 metres were traces of its reality. They made it worth taking seriously; they did not establish superiority over conventional technique under controlled conditions. The ban defended one understanding of the event. That choice remains open to examination.

The possibility consequently lost room to develop in international competition. Contemporary reporting also said that domestic competitions could continue unless their governing bodies adopted a ban; experiments in training did not become physically impossible. The further possibilities of twenty-eight or thirty feet remained projections not fully tested in comparable sanctioned competition. Drawing a rule changed how a technical path could continue. We cannot supply history with a result it never established.

A remainder constrained by a decision deserves separate attention. Earlier cases involved names excluded from the ledger, explanations left open, or questions of technical merit outside the institution's remit. Here the institution had choices: it could broaden its understanding or write a restriction, while also assessing risks. It chose prohibition. That choice is different from physical impossibility. Its reasons and alternatives remain open to examination without our first asserting that an old convention was the only cause.

Essay 11 will develop this distinction further. Hohn's 104.80 metres remains a lawful and valid historical performance. After the new specification came into force, the old-specification record series stopped accepting entries. What was closed was the ledger under those comparative conditions, not the possibility that a human body might ever again achieve 104.80 metres.

The ruler has not yet measured accurately. It is still measuring.
