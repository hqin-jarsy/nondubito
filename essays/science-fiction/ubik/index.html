Reading Ubik

Sep 3, 2026Han Qin (秦汉)English · Simplified · Traditional英文 · 简体 · 繁体英文 · 簡體 · 繁體

Five essays on half-life, rented psychic labor, shared testimony, salvation sold as a product, and the face on a coin that unsettles reality again.

Philip K. Dick's 1969 work Ubik. Every essay in this series discusses the complete story and contains full spoilers.

I
English · 简 / 繁 · Science fiction
Is Ella Still Alive When She Can Continue to Speak?

Half-life preserves intermittent conversation after bodily death, creating a relation whose remaining time is real precisely because it cannot be replenished.

Does Runciter Associates Protect Clients—or Rent Out Its Employees?

The firm markets people as counters to other psychic powers, while the lunar assignment shows how little the roster reveals about risk and consent.

III
English · 简 / 繁 · Science fiction
Why Do People Need One Another as Reality Regresses?

As objects and bodies decay into earlier forms, Joe first loses confidence in his judgment; shared testimony becomes a defense against isolation, not a vote on truth.

English · 简 / 繁 · Science fiction
What Does Ubik Save Once Salvation Is Packaged as a Product?

The chapter advertisements promise a universal commodity, while Joe’s poverty and Ella’s gift reveal that access to rescue is itself politically arranged.

V
English · 简 / 繁 · Science fiction
What Remains of Moral Judgment When Even Runciter’s Reality Is Unreliable?

Joe’s face on a coin overturns the latest explanation, but uncertainty about who is alive does not erase the difference between care and predation.
