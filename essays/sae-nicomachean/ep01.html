朋友和真理，两样都亲

Friends and Truth, Both Dear

Han Qin (秦汉)

这是第二本。上一本挨着柏拉图的《理想国》读，这一本挨着亚里士多德的《尼各马可伦理学》读。

先消一处歧。这个系列不是 SAE 自己的一部伦理学，它没有一套关于人该怎么活的说法，往后十三篇也不会长出来一套。它做的还是上一本那件事：挨着一本书读，读的时候动手。

两本书接得上，而且接在一个很具体的地方。这一篇要说的就是那个地方。

再交代这本书的样子。《尼各马可伦理学》留下来的是讲论体，不是对话。它不靠一场谈话和一个大故事往前走，一句接一句地推。所以这一本读起来跟上一本不一样：上一本有人物有场面，这一本只有一道一道的难题。

难题倒是真难，而且每一道都是他自己摆上桌的。有的他明写着这里有人会问，有的他只说一句这个改天再看，就搁下了。

第一道在第一卷第六章。

前面五章他在数人们把幸福当成什么。数出三种活法：图快活的那一种，图荣誉的那一种，还有沉思的那一种，第三种他先押后不讲。挣钱那一条他顺手驳掉了，说挣钱这件事是被逼着干的，钱明摆着不是我们要找的那个好，它总是为了别的什么才要的。

数完他收了尾，说这个话题就到这儿。

然后他掉过头来。

第五章和第六章之间没有过渡。他另起一段，说那个普遍的善我们最好还是看一看，把这里头的难处走一遍，看这个词到底是怎么用的。

紧接着他自己承认这是条上坡路，而且他说了难走的原因。

原因是：把那套说法立起来的，是自己人。

于是有了那句话。

他说，可是为了保住真理，把贴身的东西也拆掉，这大概更好，甚至是我们该做的事，尤其我们做的就是这一行。朋友和真理，两样都亲；可敬重真理是本分。

这一句值得停一下，因为它里头有两件事，而他没有把它们并成一件。

一件是：那套说法要拆。

另一件是：他没有把拆一套说法写成给立说法的人定罪。他说的是"自己人"。

而且他把这话放在动手之前，不是放在事后。这个位置要紧。事后再说一句我并无恶意，那是补票；动手之前先说明白拆的是什么不拆的是什么，那是把界划在前头。

上一本里有一句很近的话，隔着一代人。

《理想国》卷十，苏格拉底要动手把模仿那一路的诗人逐出城去。动手之前他说了一句私心话，还说得有点难为情：我从小就敬重荷马，甚至可以说是爱，这份东西到现在还在；可人不能比真理更值得敬重。

两句不是同一句，句式也不一样。可它们落在同一处：先认下舍不得，再动手。一个是对养大自己的那些诗说的，一个是对教会自己怎么想事的那个人说的。

接下来看他怎么拆。

他给的理由不止一条。头一条挺硬也挺绕：立理念的那些人，凡是有先有后的一类东西，他们自己就不给它立理念；而好这个字既说得上一样东西本身是什么，也说得上它跟别的东西什么关系，前一种在先，后一种在后。既然如此，就不该有一个理念罩着它们全体。

第二条清楚得多，而且这一条是关于一个字的。

他说，好这个字，和"是"这个字一样：能按多少种方式说，就有多少种说法。他在这儿列了六格，列完还添一句，说这样的还有别的。它可以说一样东西是什么，比如神，比如理智；可以说一样东西什么样，比如那些德性；可以说多少，比如恰到好处的那个量；可以说跟什么有关，比如有用；可以说什么时候，比如时机对了；可以说在哪儿，比如住得合适。

要留意的是他没有就此打住。他不满足于说这几个好只是碰巧叫了同一个名字。往下他还要问：它们会不会都从同一个好那儿来，会不会都朝着同一个好去，或者像眼睛之于身体那样，像理智之于灵魂那样，按比例连着。

他眼下要拆掉的，是一张完全相同的说明横跨所有位置。

这件事日常里常见，平时不计较，也确实不用计较。

好这个字就是现成的例子。这个菜做得好，这个人好，今天天气好，这笔生意做得好。四个好，平时用起来不打架。可它们确实不是一个好：菜的好要进嘴才知道，天气的好得看你今天要做什么，一笔生意的好未必和一个人的好落在同一处。

平时不分它们，一点问题也没有。出问题的是有人拿其中一个去推另一个：他是个好人，所以他做的菜也差不了。这句话一说出口，那个好就被带出了它站得住的地方。

到这里，两边碰上了。

两边拦的是同一件事：一套说明，不管挪到哪儿都照旧使唤。可用的不是同一把手法。

亚里士多德拆的是一个字：好这个字跨过那么多不同的位置，还能不能保住同一套说明。他看的是这个词。

SAE 这边不从词入手，看的是一句话靠什么站住：这一次面对的是什么，认了哪几条前提，在什么场子里划的界，出了那个范围还算不算数。它看的是那句话的来路和管辖。

同一种越界，两条不同的路去防它。

因为拆完以后，两边收的东西不一样。

亚里士多德拆完，头一件事是让每一个好各归各位。

而他归位归得很硬。他说，就算真有那么一个普遍的好，或者有那么一个自己单独立着的好，那也不是人做得出来拿得到手的东西；而我们眼下要找的，正是拿得到手的那一个。

紧接着他问了一句更实在的：一个织布的，一个做木工的，知道了那个好本身，对他手上这门活有什么帮助。看过那个东西的人，就因此成了更好的医生或者更好的将军吗。

然后是那一句：医生其实也不看那个抽象的健康。他看人的健康，或者说得更准一点，看某一个人的健康，因为他治的是一个一个的人。

不过各归各位不是他的终点。他没有再搬一件单独立着的好来顶替，可他也没有就此散摊子。下一章他立刻转回来问：那么我们要找的那个好到底是什么。他把话头拉回人做得到拿得到的那一头，一层一层往上问，最后落在一个最高的人之善上。

各归各位是他重建之前先清掉的一层，不是他停下的地方。

这一整套的骨头，在第一卷第三章他就先说过了。他在那儿立了一条规矩：不能在一切事情上要求同样的精确。受过教育的人的标志，是在每一类事情上只要求那一类事情的本性所容许的那个准。而他举的两个例子是对称的：从一个数学家嘴里接受一个只是听着有理的说法，和要一个讲演的人拿出严格的证明，一样地傻。

两半说的都是听的人该向说的人要什么。要多要少都不对，而对错不由听的人定，由这一类事情本身定。

SAE 这边拆完，收的是另一样。

不是各归各位，是每一句话说出来的时候，随身带着它管到哪儿为止：哪一次划的界，在什么场子里，认哪几条前提。出了那个范围，不是这句话变假了，是它在那儿不予结算。

开会的时候常有这种事。会上有人说这事由他负责，那一句在那个会上是分工，说得清清楚楚。可散了会以后，同一句话被拿出来堵别人的嘴，谁再问都拿它挡回去。它已经走出了当初立住它的那块地。而它走没走出去，不是说这话的人说了算，得看它当初靠什么成立，后来又被拿去做了什么。

他那个各归各位，靠的是外面有东西管着。一类事情容得下多少准，是那类事情本身说了算；医生该看这一个人的健康，因为医术这门手艺本来就有它照管的那一头；眼下是哪一个具体的人，也在管着他能说到多准。说话的人不能独自题写他要多少准。

这边那个随身标注，也不是不看外面。它记的头一样，就是这一次说的到底是什么，什么在被判。不同的是它不先摆一张本性图，不说一件事本来该归哪一格；它记的是这一次判断实际站在哪儿：面对的是什么，在哪一层上说的，走的哪一条路，认了哪几条前提。

一个问这一类事情容得下多准，一个问这一句话凭什么在这儿有发判的资格。

按他自己走疑难那个办法，这里还该有一问入表。

一句话给自己标出来的那个范围，凭什么担保它覆盖得住真正撑着它的全部前提，又没有在用的时候悄悄走出去。

他那一边，这一问有一半是有着落的：准头不归说话的人独自题写，对象是什么，这门手艺在办什么，眼前是哪一个人，这几样一起管着他。

这边也不是交代完就算数。那张单子自己也是一条要入账的说法：可以查，可以驳，可以增删。而且它写着一句更要紧的话：把哪几条前提写进去，这件事本身就是一次判断，不是全知的抄录。

所以这一问没有消失，只是挪了个地方。查得出漏项的是别人来查；可来查的那个人手里，也带着他自己的一份单子。开放的互核查得出漏，给不了任何一次登记终局的担保。

而且交代得不全这件事，未必出于存心。一个人正说得顺的时候，最不容易觉察的就是自己什么时候多用了一条没写下来的前提。

回到第六章。他在那一整章里一次也没有写下柏拉图三个字，只用了两个说法：自己人；把这套说法带进来的那些人。

可这一章不是一个不写名字的章。

同一章里他落下了名字，而且要紧的是那些名字落在哪一边。他说毕达哥拉斯那些人把"一"放进好的那一栏，这个讲法更像话；而斯彪西波看来是跟着他们走的。斯彪西波是柏拉图的外甥，柏拉图死后接掌学园的就是他。

那不是判词。那一句是称许，两个名字都被写在"更像话"的那一头。

柏拉图的名字，就在同一卷里，两章之前。第四章他写：柏拉图把这一处当成难处提出来，而且一直追着问，这就对了；我们是从始点走，还是往始点走。那里也是称许。

动手拆的那一整章，他落在纸上的名字都在称许那一边。而被拆的那套说法是谁的，他一个字没落。

拆掉一套说法，没有把那个人一并写进判词里去。

This is the second book. The first read alongside Plato’s Republic; this one reads alongside Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics.

One ambiguity first. This series is not an ethics of SAE’s own. It has no account of how people ought to live, and the thirteen essays ahead will not grow one. It is doing what the previous book did: reading alongside a book, and getting to work as it reads.

The two books meet at a very particular place. That place is what this essay is about.

A word, too, about the shape of this book. The Nicomachean Ethics has come down to us as lectures, not a dialogue. It does not move forward on a conversation and a large story. It advances one claim at a time. Reading this book therefore feels different from reading the last: that one had characters and scenes; this one has one difficult question after another.

They really are difficult, and he puts every one of them on the table himself. Sometimes he explicitly says someone will ask this; sometimes he merely says we will look at it another day, and leaves it there.

The first is in Book I, chapter 6.

In the five chapters before it, he has been counting what people take happiness to be. He counts three ways of life: one after pleasure, one after honor, and one devoted to contemplation. He postpones the third. He dismisses money-making in passing: it is something people are compelled to do, and money plainly is not the good we are looking for, since we always want it for something else.

Having counted, he closes the subject. Enough of that.

Then he turns around.

There is no transition between chapters 5 and 6. He starts afresh: perhaps we had better examine that universal good, work through its difficulties, and see just how the word is being used.

Immediately he admits that this is an uphill road. He also says why.

The reason is that the people who put the account in place are his own people.

And so comes that sentence.

Still, he says, to preserve the truth it may be better, even our duty, to dismantle what is close to us, especially when this is our calling. Friends and truth are both dear; but honoring truth is our duty.

It is worth stopping here. There are two things in that sentence, and he has not made them one.

One is that the account must be dismantled.

The other is that he has not written the dismantling of an account as a conviction of the people who put it forward. He calls them his own people.

And he says this before getting to work, not afterward. The position matters. Saying afterward that no harm was meant is buying the ticket after the journey. Saying first what is to be dismantled and what is not draws the boundary before the work begins.

There was a very similar sentence in the previous book, a generation earlier.

In Book X of the Republic, Socrates is about to expel the poets of imitation from the city. Before doing so he admits a private feeling, with some embarrassment: he has revered Homer since childhood, one might even say loved him, and that feeling remains. Yet a person must not be honored above truth.

These are not the same sentence, nor are they built alike. But they arrive at the same place: first acknowledge what is hard to let go of, then get to work. One speaks of the poetry that raised him; the other, of the person who taught him how to think.

Now watch how he dismantles the account.

He gives more than one reason. The first is forceful and rather winding: the people who posit Forms do not themselves posit one for things admitting a before and an after. Yet “good” can speak both of what a thing is in itself and of its relation to something else. The former comes first, the latter afterward. There should therefore be no single Form covering them all.

The second reason is much clearer, and it concerns a word.

“Good,” he says, works like “being”: it can be said in as many ways. He lists six categories, then adds that there are others. It can say what something is, as with God or intellect; what it is like, as with the virtues; how much, as with the right amount; in relation to what, as with the useful; when, as with the right moment; or where, as with a suitable dwelling.

Notice that he does not stop there. He is not satisfied to say that these goods merely happen to share a name. He will also ask whether they all come from one good, all lead toward one good, or are connected by analogy: as sight is to the body, so intellect is to the soul.

What he wants to dismantle for now is one identical account stretched across every position.

This happens all the time in ordinary life. Usually we do not fuss about it, and there is no need to.

“Good” is a ready example. This is good cooking; this is a good person; the weather is good today; that was a good deal. The four goods coexist perfectly well in conversation. But they are not the same good. You have to taste the food to know whether it is good. Good weather depends on what you mean to do today. A good deal need not be good at the same place where a person is good.

There is nothing wrong with leaving them undistinguished in everyday talk. Trouble begins when someone uses one to infer another: he is a good man, so his cooking cannot be bad. The moment that is said, the good has been carried beyond the place where it holds.

Here the two sides meet.

Both stop the same thing: an account being used unchanged wherever it is moved. But they do not use the same method.

Aristotle takes apart a word. Across all those different positions, can “good” retain one and the same account? He is looking at the word.

SAE does not begin with the word. It asks what holds a statement up: what it faces on this occasion, which premises it accepts, where its boundary was drawn, and whether it still counts beyond that range. It examines the statement’s grounds and jurisdiction.

The same overreach, prevented by two different routes.

Because after the dismantling, the two sides collect different things.

Aristotle’s first task afterward is to put each good back in its own place.

And he is quite firm about it. Even if there really were a universal good, or a good standing separately on its own, it would not be something human beings could bring about or attain. What we are looking for now is precisely the good within reach.

Then he asks something more down to earth: what help would knowing the Good itself be to a weaver or a carpenter at work? Does seeing it make someone a better doctor or a better general?

Then comes this sentence: in fact, the doctor does not look at abstract health either. The doctor looks at human health, or, more precisely, at the health of a particular person, because it is particular people who are treated.

But putting things back in their places is not his destination. He does not replace the separate Good with another separate thing, but neither does he pack up and go home. In the next chapter he turns straight back: what, then, is the good we are looking for? He brings the question back to what people can do and attain, works upward step by step, and finally arrives at a highest human good.

Putting things in their places clears the ground before he rebuilds. It is not where he stops.

He already set out the bones of this approach in Book I, chapter 3. There he lays down a rule: we must not demand the same precision in everything. An educated person asks, in each kind of matter, only for the accuracy its nature permits. And his two examples are symmetrical: accepting a merely plausible account from a mathematician is just as foolish as demanding strict proofs from an orator.

Both halves concern what a listener should demand of a speaker. Too much and too little are both wrong. And what is right is decided not by the listener but by the kind of matter itself.

What SAE collects after dismantling is something else.

Rather than putting each thing in its place, it has each statement carry, as it is spoken, a note of where its authority ends: when the boundary was drawn, in what setting, on which premises. Outside that range, the statement has not become false. It simply cannot be credited there.

This often happens in meetings. Someone says that a matter is their responsibility. In that meeting the sentence allocates the work, clearly enough. Afterward the same sentence is used to silence others: whoever asks a question gets it thrown back at them. It has left the ground on which it first stood. Whether it has crossed that boundary is not for the speaker alone to decide. We must examine what originally made the statement valid and what it was later used to do.

His way of putting things in their places is governed by something outside the speaker. How much accuracy a kind of matter allows is determined by that matter itself. The doctor should attend to this person’s health because medicine already has something it is concerned with. Which particular person stands before the doctor also governs how precisely the doctor can speak. Speakers cannot prescribe the required accuracy all by themselves.

The note carried by a statement on this side does not ignore the outside either. The first thing it records is what is being spoken about on this occasion: what is being judged. The difference is that it does not first lay out a map of natures and say which category a thing inherently belongs to. It records where this particular judgment actually stands: what it faces, at what level it speaks, by which route it proceeds, and which premises it accepts.

One asks how much precision this kind of matter permits. The other asks what entitles this statement to pass judgment here.

Following his own way of working through difficulties, there should be one more question on the list.

What guarantees that the scope a statement marks out for itself covers all the premises that really sustain it, and that it has not quietly crossed that scope in use?

On his side, half of this question has a place to land. The speaker does not prescribe accuracy alone. What the object is, what the craft is doing, and which person is here now all constrain the speaker together.

On this side, giving an account of oneself is not the end of the matter either. The checklist is itself a claim to be entered in the account: it can be examined, challenged, added to, or cut. It also says something more important. Deciding which premises to put on the list is itself a judgment, not an omniscient transcription.

So the question has not disappeared; it has moved. Other people’s scrutiny can find what is missing. But the person doing the checking carries a checklist of their own. Open mutual checking can discover omissions; it cannot give any single registration a final guarantee.

Nor need an incomplete account be deliberate. When a person is speaking most fluently, what they are least likely to notice is the moment they begin using an extra premise they have not written down.

Back to chapter 6. Throughout the whole chapter he never writes Plato’s name. He uses only two expressions: his own people, and those who introduced this account.

But this is not a chapter without names.

He does put names into this very chapter, and what matters is which side they fall on. The Pythagoreans, he says, place the One in the column of goods, and this is a more plausible account. Speusippus seems to follow them. Speusippus was Plato’s nephew and succeeded him as head of the Academy.

That is not a verdict against them. It is praise. Both names are written on the side of “more plausible.”

Plato’s name is in the same book, two chapters earlier. In chapter 4 Aristotle writes that Plato was right to raise this difficulty and pursue it: are we proceeding from the starting points, or toward them? There too, the name accompanies praise.

Throughout the chapter in which he takes the account apart, the names he sets down are on the side of praise. Whose account he is dismantling, he never names.

He dismantles an account without writing the person into the verdict.
