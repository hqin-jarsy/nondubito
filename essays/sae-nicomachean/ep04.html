做那件事和成为那种人

Doing the Act, Becoming the Person

Han Qin (秦汉)

第二卷开头他立了一条，立得很平常。

他说德性是我们先做起来才得着的，跟别的手艺一样：那些得学会了才做得来的事，我们正是靠着做它们学会的。盖房子的人是盖出来的，弹琴的人是弹出来的。

接着那一句，是这一卷的骨头：照样，做正义的事我们就成为正义的人，做节制的事就成为节制的人，做勇敢的事就成为勇敢的人。

第四章他自己回过头来，把这句话拿起来问了一遍。

他一开口就是"有人会问"。

这四个字在第二卷只出现这一次。整卷九章，就这一处他把话头交给了对面。

那一问是这样的：我们说人得靠做正义的事成为正义的人，靠做节制的事成为节制的人。这话怎么讲得通。因为要是他们做的就是正义的事和节制的事，那他们已经是正义的人和节制的人了。就像一个人做的是合语法的事和合音律的事，他就已经是懂语法的人和懂音律的人了。

这一问不是抬杠。它打的是一个真的口子：要是做那件事就等于是那种人，那"成为"这两个字就没地方安放。

而要留意他摆上来的只有这一头。

还有另一头，读到后面自己会冒出来：一个还不是正义的人，怎么做得出正义的事。这一头他在这一章里没有另立成难处。他摆的是"已经是了，所以不用成为"，不是"还不是，所以做不出来"。这个分别到后面那个圈那里会显出分量。

他的回答分两层，而第一层是把对面的类比先接过来用。

他说，就连手艺上也不是这么回事。

一个人可以碰巧写对一个字，也可以是旁边有人指点着写对的。这两种情形他都写对了，可他还不是懂语法的人。得他既写对了，而且是照着自己心里那点语法写对的，才算。

做对了那件事，和照着自己的本事做对了那件事，是两回事。

这一层他没有多费口舌，因为第二层才是他要立的。

他说，手艺和德性还不一样。手艺做出来的东西，好不好在东西身上；东西做成那个样子，就够了。德性上的事不是：不是这件事本身长成什么样就算数，还得看做的那个人是在什么状态里做的。

于是他列了三样。

一，他得知道自己在做什么。

二，他得是选了它的，而且是为它自身选的。

三，他得是从一个稳住了挪不动的状态里做出来的。

这三样列完，他随手排了一下轻重，而这一排出人意料。

他说，这三样，对于掌握别的手艺来说不算数，只有"知道"那一样算。可对于德性，反过来：知道那一样没有分量，或者只有很小的分量；另外两样不是有一点分量，是全部的分量。

一个人可以把该做的事说得头头是道，一样也不算数。这不是他顺口说的重话，是他给三个条件排出来的位次。

接下来那两句，摆在一起看着像个圈。

他说，一件事之所以叫正义的或者节制的，是当它是正义的人或者节制的人会做的那一种。而一个人之所以是正义的或者节制的，不在于他做了这些事，在于他是照着正义的人和节制的人做事的那个样子做的。

拿人定事，又拿事定人。

而他紧接着写下的是"所以"。

所以人们说得对：靠做正义的事成为正义的人，靠做节制的事成为节制的人。而不去做这些事的人，谁也别指望有一天会变好。

他写得出这个"所以"，是因为那个圈他前面已经拆开了。拆它用的正是写字那个例子。

"做正义的事"这几个字有两层意思。

头一层只问做出来的是什么：一个还不正义的人，可以碰巧做出正义的人会做的那一类事，也可以是旁边有人指点着做出来的。这一层新手够得着。

第二层问他是在什么状态里做的：知不知道，是不是为这件事自身选的，那个状态稳没稳住。这一层才属于正义的人。

而头一层那一句，正好答了他没有摆上桌的那另一头。一个还不是正义的人怎么做得出正义的事：他做得出头一层那一种。

两句话里的"做正义的事"，一句用的是头一层，一句用的是第二层。所以那不是一个自己咬自己的圈，是一条路：靠头一层一遍一遍地走，走到第二层。"成为"两个字的位置，就在这两层之间。

他因此不必绕开，也不必道歉。

章末他还发了一通火，这一段很不客气。

他说，多数人不做这些事，躲进道理里，以为自己在做哲学，以为这样就能成为好人。这就跟病人一样：医生的话听得很仔细，吩咐的事一件不做。那些人这么治，身子好不了；这些人这么做哲学，心也好不了。

而他这一通火底下压着的那一句，这边也认。

两边都不让一次产出直接换来另一种地位。

他那里：做出正义那一类的动作，不等于这个人已经是正义的人。

这边那里：一次划界的所得可以沉淀成稳定的分辨，可那仍旧是一项关于分辨的产出，不是一句关于整个人的判词。这边有一句写在公设里的话，说得很干脆：先天在这里是沉淀。意思是那些看上去本来就有的分辨，其实是一层一层落下来的。

要说准的是两边挨得近在哪儿。不在都要求做很多回。沉淀那一条没有说非得反复；一次真的划界，也可能就沉下来了。挨得近的是另一件：一样东西做出来了，不等于做它的那个人因此换了个身份。

学做菜是现成的例子。照着方子做对一回，和成了会做饭的人，不是一回事。第一回做对可能是碰巧火候正好，也可能是旁边有人一直在说下盐下盐。而做上三年以后，你不必想就知道这锅该收了。中间那三年不是知识长出来了，是别的什么东西定下来了。

他那三个条件里的第一条和第三条，在厨房里分得清清楚楚。

可那个"定下来的东西"说的是谁，两边答得不一样。

他那个稳住的状态是这个人的。它叫性子，它长在人身上，所以他才说得出"这个人是正义的"这句话。

这边那个沉淀是认识的。沉淀成的是稳定的分辨。它给得出一个关于分辨的说法，给不出一个关于人的说法。

照沉淀那一条，这边说得出"这个分法立住了"，说不出"这个人立住了"。

那么这边关于人的说法在哪里。

有一处，而它走的是另一条路。这边叫它署名。

那一段说的是判断：每一项哲学判断都有实际的承担者，每一个主体有他自己的哲学。而"自己的"这三个字落在承担上，不落在生成上。一门哲学不必自造，承担一门已经有的，它就因为承担而成了自己的。住得进去的人不必是盖房子的人。

这句话管的范围要说准，它管的是哲学判断和一门哲学，不是一切东西的归属。

在这个范围里，一句是"我从头走到今天，所以是我的"。另一句是"不管这套说法从哪儿来的，我拿它作了判断，所以那个判断是我的"。

这就差了一整个方向。

他问的是一个人怎么变成那种人，答案在时间里的反复。这边问的是一句判断怎么成了一个人自己的，答案不在那套说法怎么来的，在他署没署名。

这一层日常里也有它的样子，只是不在厨房里，在写东西的时候。

一个人写文章，用的是一套早就摆在那儿的道理。那套道理不是他想出来的，他一个字也没添。可他拿它作了一个判断，那个判断底下署的是他的名字。

日后这个判断被人挑出毛病，他说不出"这套道理是别人先说的"来脱身。道理是现成的，那一次判断是他的。

署名那一下他没有变成另一个人。他也没有练三年。他只是拿它作了判。

而一项判断可以当下就署上名，一门哲学不是一句判断。

一项判断在作出的那一刻就已经有人署名，这一格不是空的。上游明写着每一项判断都有实际的承担者。

空着的是更长的那一格。

今天承担一门哲学，明天改它一处，后来不再用它，再后来当众说它不对。这些时刻之间，什么使"这是我的哲学"还是同一句话。是每一次发判重新署一次名，还是头一次承担就一路有效。而它在哪儿终止，谁说了算。

他那边有一套现成的答话，虽然答的是另一个问题：一次动作不算数，是时间里一遍一遍地做，末了还要那个稳住了挪不动的状态。他把跨时间那一段，写成了性子怎么长出来。

这边说清了一句判断归谁。

一门哲学跨过许多次判断，那一段路上它归谁，这边还没有写。

At the beginning of Book II he lays down a claim, quite matter-of-factly.

We acquire the virtues by doing things first, he says, just as with the other crafts. The things we must learn before we can do, we learn by doing. Builders become builders by building, and lyre players by playing the lyre.

The next sentence is the backbone of the book: in the same way, by doing just things we become just, by doing temperate things temperate, and by doing courageous things courageous.

In chapter 4 he turns back and puts this sentence to the question himself.

He opens with “someone might ask.”

That expression appears only once in Book II. Across its nine chapters, this is the only place where he hands the opening to the other side.

The question goes like this. We say that people must become just by doing just things and temperate by doing temperate things. How can that make sense? If what they do is just and temperate, they are already just and temperate, just as someone who does what accords with grammar or music is already a grammarian or a musician.

This is not quibbling. It strikes a real opening: if doing the thing already means being that sort of person, where can “becoming” fit?

Notice, though, that he puts only this side of the problem on the table.

There is another side, which will appear of its own accord as we read on: how can someone who is not yet just do just things? In this chapter he does not separately formulate that as a difficulty. He poses “already is, so need not become,” not “not yet, so cannot do.” This distinction will matter when we reach the circle later on.

His answer has two layers. In the first he borrows the other side’s analogy.

Even with a craft, he says, things do not work that way.

A person can write something correctly by chance, or because someone beside them tells them how. In both cases they have written it correctly, but they are not yet a grammarian. They count as one only if they both write it correctly and do so from the grammatical knowledge within themselves.

Getting something right and getting it right by one’s own skill are different things.

He does not spend many words on this layer, because the second is what he really wants to establish.

Craft and virtue differ further, he says. In a craft, the goodness of the product is in the product. It is enough for it to turn out a certain way. With virtue, that is not enough. What counts is not merely what the deed looks like, but the condition of the person doing it.

So he lists three things.

First, the person must know what they are doing.

Second, they must choose it, and choose it for itself.

Third, they must do it from a firm and unchanging state.

Having listed the three, he casually ranks their importance. The ranking is surprising.

For possessing the other crafts, he says, these do not count, except for knowing. With virtue the order is reversed: knowing carries no weight, or very little; the other two carry not some weight, but all the weight.

Someone may explain exactly what ought to be done and still have nothing that counts here. This is not a severe remark thrown in casually. It is the ranking he gives the three conditions.

The next two sentences, set side by side, look like a circle.

A deed is called just or temperate when it is the sort a just or temperate person would do, he says. But a person is just or temperate not because they do these things, but because they do them in the way just or temperate people do them.

Use the person to define the deed, then the deed to define the person.

And the very next word he writes is “therefore.”

Therefore people are right to say that we become just by doing just things and temperate by doing temperate things. Anyone who does not do them should not expect ever to become good.

He can write this “therefore” because he has already opened up the circle. The example of writing was what did it.

“Doing just things” has two meanings.

The first asks only what was produced. Someone not yet just can, by chance or with guidance from another person, do the kind of thing a just person would do. This layer is within a beginner’s reach.

The second asks what condition the person acts from: whether they know, whether they choose the act for itself, whether the state has become firm. Only this layer belongs to the just person.

The first-layer statement answers precisely the other side he did not put on the table. How can someone not yet just do just things? They can do the first kind.

“Doing just things” in the two statements uses the first layer in one and the second in the other. So this is not a circle biting its own tail. It is a road: repeatedly walk the first layer until you reach the second. The place for “becoming” is between them.

He therefore need neither evade the problem nor apologize.

At the chapter’s end he also loses his temper. The passage is not polite.

Most people, he says, do not do these things. They take refuge in argument, thinking they are doing philosophy and will thereby become good. They are like patients who listen carefully to their doctors but do none of what they are told. Patients treated that way will not get their bodies well; people doing philosophy that way will not get their souls well either.

This side also accepts the claim beneath that outburst.

Neither side lets a single output be exchanged directly for a different status.

On his side, performing the sort of action that is just does not mean the person is already just.

On this side, what a particular distinction achieves can settle into a stable capacity to distinguish. But that remains an output concerning distinctions, not a verdict on the whole person. A sentence in this side’s axioms puts it plainly: the a priori here is sedimentation. Distinctions that look as though they were always there have, in fact, settled layer by layer.

We must be exact about where the two sides come close. It is not that both demand many repetitions. The sedimentation claim does not say repetition is necessary; a single genuine act of drawing a distinction may settle. The proximity lies elsewhere: producing something does not mean the producer has thereby acquired a new identity.

Learning to cook is a ready example. Getting a recipe right once is not the same as becoming someone who can cook. The first success may be a lucky temperature, or someone standing beside you saying “salt, salt.” After three years, you know without thinking when this pot is done. What happened during those years was not knowledge simply growing. Something else became settled.

The first and third of his conditions come apart clearly in the kitchen.

But whose “settled something” is it? The two sides answer differently.

His firm state belongs to the person. It is called character, and it grows in a person. That is why he can say, “this person is just.”

The sedimentation on this side belongs to cognition. What settles is a stable distinction. It supplies a claim about a distinction, not a claim about a person.

Under the sedimentation claim, this side can say “this way of distinguishing has taken hold.” It cannot say “this person has taken hold.”

Where, then, is this side’s claim about the person?

There is one, but it takes another route. This side calls it signing one’s name.

That passage concerns judgment. Every philosophical judgment has an actual bearer; every subject has a philosophy of their own. “Their own” rests on taking responsibility, not on generating it. A philosophy need not be self-invented. Taking responsibility for an existing philosophy makes it one’s own through that undertaking. The person who lives in a house need not be its builder.

We must state the scope precisely. The passage concerns philosophical judgments and a philosophy, not ownership of everything.

Within that scope, one sentence says, “I have traveled from the beginning to today, so it is mine.” The other says, “Whatever the source of this account, I used it to make a judgment, so that judgment is mine.”

That is a difference of an entire direction.

He asks how a person becomes that sort of person, and the answer lies in repetition over time. This side asks how a judgment becomes someone’s own. The answer lies not in where the account came from, but in whether the person has signed their name to it.

This layer has an everyday form too, though not in the kitchen. It appears when we write.

Someone writes an essay using an account that was already there. They did not invent it and have not added a word. But they use it to make a judgment, and their name is signed beneath that judgment.

If someone later finds fault with the judgment, they cannot escape by saying, “Someone else said this first.” The account was ready-made; that particular judgment was theirs.

Signing did not turn them into another person. They did not practice for three years. They simply used it to judge.

Yet a judgment can be signed on the spot, while a philosophy is not a single judgment.

At the moment a judgment is made, someone already signs for it. This column is not blank. The earlier account explicitly says that every judgment has an actual bearer.

The blank lies in the longer column.

Today I take responsibility for a philosophy; tomorrow I change one part; later I stop using it; later still I publicly say it is wrong. Across those moments, what keeps “this is my philosophy” the same statement? Is a new signature required for each judgment, or does the first undertaking remain in force throughout? Where does it end, and who decides?

He has an answer ready, though it answers a different question. One act does not suffice. There must be repeated doing over time, and finally a firm and unchanging state. He writes the span across time as the growth of character.

This side has explained whose judgment a judgment is.

Across the many judgments of a philosophy, whose it remains along that road has not yet been written here.
