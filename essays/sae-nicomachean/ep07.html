能不能自己对自己不正义

Can One Be Unjust to Oneself?

Han Qin (秦汉)

第五卷第九章，他一口气摆了一串难处，开头就是"有人会问"。

头一个他引的是欧里庇得斯，而且他说那句话说得古怪：

我杀了我母亲，话就这么短。她愿意我也愿意，还是她不愿意我也不愿意。

引完他问：一个人到底能不能自愿地被人不正义地对待。做不正义的事全是自愿的，那么受不正义的事呢，是全不自愿，还是有的自愿有的不自愿。

接着他往"被正义地对待"那一头也推了一遍，说要是那也全是自愿的，就说不过去，因为有些人明明是不情愿地被正义地对待的。

再往下他又起了一道：一个受了那件不正义的事的人，是不是就一定算被不正义地对待了。还是说受这一头也跟做那一头一样，可以只是碰上，不是那么回事。

这一章就是这么一串往下滚的，一道接一道，都是他自己起的。而其中一道他点了名。

这也是那些难处里的一个：一个人能不能对自己不正义。

它是这么冒出来的。要是所谓做不正义的事就是明知故犯地害一个人，而明知故犯的意思是知道对谁，拿什么，怎么做；而不能自制的人正是明知故犯地在害自己，那么他就是自愿地被不正义地对待了，而且等于自己对自己做了不正义的事。

第十一章他回来答。

开口第一句就是：这一问从前面说过的那些已经明白了。所以整章是答，不是问。

他的答不是一句，是几层。

第一层是法。有些正义的事是法照着整个德性安排下来的。比方法不叫人自杀，而法不叫做的事，就是它禁止的事。

再往下一步：凡是违着法明知故犯地害人而对方没有先害他的，就是做了不正义的事；而明知故犯就是知道对谁，拿什么。那么一个因为一时火气把自己抹了脖子的人，是明知故犯地做了正当道理不许的事，而法不许这么做。所以他做了不正义的事。

然后是这一章里最好看的三句。

那么他对谁不正义了呢。是对城邦，不是对他自己吧。因为他是自愿受的，而没有人是自愿被不正义地对待的。

所以城邦也罚他。自毁的人身上还带着一份不名誉，就当他是对城邦做了不正义的事。

第二层更硬，是笔算术。就一个人只是不正义而还不到整个人下作的那一层说，他没法对自己不正义。因为那样一来，同一样东西就得同时从同一个人身上被拿走，又被加到同一个人身上，而这不可能。所以正义和不正义总得落在一个人以上。

第三层是数具体的事。没有那些一件一件的不正义事，谁也做不成不正义的事；而没有人跟自己的妻子通奸，没有人挖自己家的墙，没有人偷自己的东西。

这三层下完，"自己对自己不正义"在严格的意思上已经不成立了。

可他没有把这一问一脚踢开。

他留了一条，而且留得很仔细：

按比方说，按相似说，是有一种正义的。不是一个人对他自己的那种，是他身上某些部分之间的那种。而且不是随便哪一种正义，是主人对奴仆那一种，或者当家的那一种。

为什么是这两种。因为在这些说法里，灵魂中讲理的那一部分跟不讲理的那一部分是分开站着的。人们正是看着这个，才觉得有一种对自己的不正义：因为在这些部分里头，确实可能有一部分受着违背它自己想望的事。

所以就像管的和被管的之间有一种正义，这两部分之间也有一种。

他没有说"其实是有的"，也没有说"根本没有"。他做的是：把一个严格要求两个人的说法，往一个人里头移；移进去以后，他明说那只剩比方。

而这一个动作，这边也做过。

《第一批判》登记公设那一章里，第六条叫反身，写的是：否定能以自身的施行者和产物为对象，工具和对象可以重合。

所以"移得进去"这件事，两边都认。他认，因为灵魂里本来就分着几部分；这边认，因为那把工具本来就转得过来。

而要说准的是这边认下的到底是哪一件。这边没有说过"追问这件事本来要两个人"。说过的是另一句，在讲幻相那一章里：自问担保不了发现自己的全部盲区，所以他者的追问提供一种自问自己拿不到的独立校验。

要一个不同的位置，跟要两个人，不是同一句话。

移进去以后剩下的是什么，两边不一样。

他剩下的是比方。严格说不成立，打个比方说得通。名分变了：那不再是正义，那是像正义。

这边剩下的不是比方，是一样担保不住的东西。动作照旧成立，只是它拿不到那个不同的位置。

差别就在这儿。他说移进去以后那个关系换了名分；这边说移进去以后那个动作还是同一个动作，只是它做不到它在有两个位置的时候做得到的那一件事。

一个是名分变了，一个是效力短了。

而两边收手的地方是同一处，收手的理由不是同一个。

他的理由是关系的语法。拿走和加上不能同时落在同一个人身上；受和作，多得和少得，这一套要两方才摆得开。前面那三层他一层一层数过了，末了压成一句：正义和不正义总得落在一个人以上。

这边的理由是位置。不是一个人身上不能分成两部分，是那两部分共用着同一套看不见的地方。你转过身来查自己，转的还是你那双眼睛。

他那一头短的是人数，这边这一头短的是位置。

自己校自己写的东西，是这件事最平常的样子。

一段话你写完读三遍，那个错字还在。不是你不认真，是你读的不是纸上写的，是你以为你写的。换个人来，一眼就看见了。

这一处两件事同时成立：你确实在校，这是真的，工具转得过来；而你确实校不出那一个，这也是真的。动作成立，效力短一截。

另一头那句话也很平常，只是它成立的方式是打比方。

一个人说好了不再拖，结果又拖了，事后会说一句"我对不起自己"。

这句话说得通，听的人也懂。可它说得通的方式，正是他讲的那一种：你没法真的告自己，也没法真的赔自己。那句话里的"我"和"自己"，是拿主人和奴仆那个样子借来的两方。

而一问"那份校验为什么短"，事情就不再只停在效力上了。

这边登记的是一个上限：自问担保不了发现全部盲区。上限是一个结果。它没有说这个结果是怎么来的。

来的路子是明摆着可问的：校验的那一头和被校验的那一头，共用了哪些东西。共用的是同一批前提，还是同一套地址，还是同一条取信息的路。共用得越多，那份校验就越短；而共用到什么程度，这边一个字也没写。

这一问得写清是谁在问。它不是说上游本该给出一套内部的两方。反身那一条许的只是工具和对象可以重合，它没有许诺把一个人拆成动手的那一份，受着的那一份，看不见的那一份，也不欠这一套。是这一篇看着他那张图，问出了这边这一处。

而他那张图确实是一张图。

讲理的那一部分，不讲理的那一部分，主人和奴仆的那个样子，一部分受着违背它自己想望的事。这些拼起来，是一张能指得出"哪一头短在哪儿"的关系图。图对不对是另一回事，灵魂分成几部分是他自己那一套，这边不接。

可这边手上现在只有一个上限，还没有一张图。

上限告诉你走不到哪儿。图告诉你为什么走不到。

In Book V, chapter 9, he sets out a string of difficulties in one breath, beginning with “someone might ask.”

First he quotes Euripides, and says that the line is a strange one:

I killed my mother. That is the short of it. Willingly, with her willing too, or unwillingly, with her unwilling too.

Having quoted it, he asks: can a person be willingly treated unjustly? Doing injustice is always voluntary. What about suffering it? Is it always involuntary, or sometimes voluntary and sometimes not?

Then he pushes the question toward “being treated justly” as well. If that too were always voluntary, it would be absurd, since some people plainly receive just treatment unwillingly.

Further down he raises another question: does someone who has suffered the unjust thing necessarily count as having been treated unjustly? Or can suffering, like doing, be only incidental, without being that thing in the proper sense?

The chapter rolls on like this, one difficulty after another, all raised by him. And one of them he names explicitly.

This too is one of the difficulties: can a person be unjust to himself?

It arises like this. Suppose doing injustice means knowingly harming someone, and knowingly means knowing whom, with what, and how. Someone without self-control knowingly harms himself. He would therefore be willingly treated unjustly, which would amount to doing injustice to himself.

In chapter 11 he returns to answer.

His opening sentence says that this question is already clear from what has been said. So the whole chapter is an answer, not a question.

His answer is not a single statement. It has several layers.

The first is law. Some just acts are those the law prescribes in accordance with virtue as a whole. The law, for instance, does not command a person to kill himself, and what the law does not command, it forbids.

One step further: someone who knowingly harms another contrary to law, without having first been harmed by that person, does injustice. Knowingly means knowing whom and with what. So a person who cuts his own throat in a fit of anger knowingly does what right reason does not allow, and the law does not allow it either. He has therefore done an injustice.

Then come the chapter's three most striking sentences.

Whom, then, has he treated unjustly? The city, rather than himself, surely. For he suffers willingly, and nobody is willingly treated unjustly.

So the city punishes him too. A certain dishonor attaches to the person who destroys himself, treating him as someone who has done injustice to the city.

The second layer is firmer: a piece of arithmetic. In the sense in which someone is unjust without being base as a whole, he cannot be unjust to himself. For then the same thing would have to be taken from and added to the same person at the same time, which is impossible. Justice and injustice must therefore always involve more than one person.

The third layer counts particular acts. Nobody can do injustice without doing particular unjust things. But nobody commits adultery with his own wife, breaks into his own house, or steals his own belongings.

After these three layers, “being unjust to oneself” no longer holds in the strict sense.

But he does not simply kick the question aside.

He leaves one route open, and does so carefully:

By metaphor, by resemblance, there is a kind of justice. Not that of a person toward himself, but that between certain parts of him. And not just any kind of justice: the kind between master and slave, or the kind within a household.

Why these two? Because in these accounts the rational part of the soul stands apart from the nonrational part. It is in view of this that people think there is an injustice toward oneself: among these parts, one really may undergo something contrary to its own desire.

So just as there is a kind of justice between ruler and ruled, there is one between these two parts.

He has not said “actually there is,” nor “there is none at all.” What he does is this: he moves a description that strictly requires two people inside one person. Having moved it, he expressly says that what remains is only a metaphor.

And this side has made a similar move.

In the chapter of the First Critique that registers the postulates, the sixth is called reflexivity. It says: negation can take its own performer and products as objects; tool and object can coincide.

So both sides accept that it can be moved inside. He accepts it because the soul already has distinct parts. This side accepts it because the tool can already turn upon itself.

What needs precision is exactly what this side has accepted. This side has never said that questioning originally requires two people. It has said something else, in the chapter on illusion: self-questioning cannot guarantee discovery of all one's own blind spots, so another's questioning offers an independent check that self-questioning cannot obtain on its own.

Needing a different position is not the same statement as needing two people.

What remains after the move inside differs on the two sides.

What remains for him is a metaphor. It does not hold strictly, but works as a comparison. Its status has changed: it is no longer justice, but something like justice.

What remains on this side is not a metaphor, but something it still cannot guarantee. The operation remains valid. It simply cannot obtain that different position.

Here lies the difference. He says that once moved inside, the relation changes its status. This side says the operation remains the same operation, but cannot do the one thing it can do when there are two positions.

In one case the status changes; in the other, the reach falls short.

And the two sides stop at the same place, though not for the same reason.

His reason is the grammar of the relation. Taking away and adding cannot happen to the same person at once. Suffering and doing, receiving more and receiving less: these require two parties to be set out. He counted the three layers one by one, then compressed them into a sentence: justice and injustice must always involve more than one person.

This side's reason is position. It is not that one person cannot be divided into two parts. It is that those two parts share the same unseen places. When you turn around to inspect yourself, the eyes that turn are still your own.

What is missing on his side is another person; what is missing on this side is another position.

Proofreading your own writing is the most ordinary form of this.

You finish a paragraph and read it three times. The typo is still there. It is not that you are careless. You are reading not what is on the page, but what you think you wrote. Someone else looks and sees it at once.

Two things hold here at the same time. You really are proofreading; that is true, the tool can turn back. And you really cannot catch that particular error; that is true too. The operation holds, but its reach falls short.

The statement on the other side is ordinary too, except that it holds by way of metaphor.

Someone promises to stop putting things off, then does it again. Afterward he says, “I've let myself down.”

The sentence makes sense, and its hearer understands. But it makes sense in just the way he describes: you cannot really sue yourself, or really compensate yourself. The “I” and “myself” in that sentence are two parties borrowed from the pattern of master and slave.

But once we ask why that check falls short, the matter no longer stays only at the level of its reach.

This side registers an upper limit: self-questioning cannot guarantee discovery of all blind spots. An upper limit is a result. It does not say how that result comes about.

The route to it plainly invites a question: what do the checking end and the checked end share? Do they share the same premises, the same addresses, or the same channel for obtaining information? The more they share, the shorter the check's reach. Yet this side has not written a word about the extent of that sharing.

We need to be clear about who is asking this question. It is not a claim that the earlier account ought to have supplied a pair of internal parties. The postulate of reflexivity permits only tool and object to coincide. It does not promise to divide a person into the part that acts, the part that undergoes, and the part that cannot see; it owes no such account. It is this essay, looking at his diagram, that has asked about this place on our side.

And his diagram really is a diagram.

The rational part, the nonrational part, the pattern of master and slave, one part undergoing something contrary to its own desire: together these form a relational diagram in which one can point to which end falls short, and where. Whether the diagram is correct is another matter. The division of the soul into parts is his own account, which this side does not adopt.

But what this side currently has is only an upper limit, not yet a diagram.

An upper limit tells you where you cannot reach. A diagram tells you why.
