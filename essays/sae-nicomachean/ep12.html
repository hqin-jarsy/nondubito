最高的那一样是学来的，还是碰上的

Is the Highest Good Learned or Given?

Han Qin (秦汉)

第一卷第九章开头就是一问，而且他明写这是一处难处：

幸福是学得来的，是习出来的，还是靠别的什么法子练出来的；还是凭一份神给的份额，或者干脆是碰上的。

四支。四支都摆在那儿，没有一支带着"某某人说"的名头。这是他自己摆的。

这一问跟下一章那一问只隔一章，问的却不是一件事。下一章问的是一段日子要不要等到人死了才算得清，那一问归另一篇。这一章问的只有一件：这一样东西是打哪儿来的。

而这一问他没有留着。他当场就处置了，而且处置得不平均。

神给的那一支他搁下，说这该归另一种考察。可搁下之前他先动了一手：就算它不是神送来的，是靠德性和某种学习或者训练得的，它照样是最神圣的那一类东西。

这一手要看清。他不是把这一支要了回来，他是把赌注从这一支上取下来了。神给不神给，这一问的输赢不再压在上头。

接着他添了一样：这样一来，够得着的人还多。因为只要不是在德性上残废了的，凭着某种学习和用心，人人都可能有它。

碰上的那一支他当场打死。而打死的理由要照他的写，不许替他造。他说的是：要是这样比靠运气得着幸福更好，那么事情本就该是这样，因为凡照着自然来的，本性就是尽其所能地摆到最好那个样子；照着技艺来的，照着任何一种原因来的，也都这样，尤其是照着最好那种原因来的。接着才是那一句：把最大最美的那件事交给运气，太不成话了。

这两句都带着"要是"和"也许"。他给的是一个说得过去的理由，不是一道证明。

学与习那一支，他收下了。

所以第一卷已经答了。四支里搁下一支，打死一支，收下一支，还顺手把够得着的人算宽了。

那么隔着九卷的第十卷，答的是什么。

不是这一问。第十卷第七章第八章里，学，习，练，运气，这几个字一个也没有出现。它们要到最后一章才一起回来，而那一段归另外两篇。

神给的那一支更干脆：他在第一卷搁下以后，那个说法全书再没有回来过。

第十卷第七章答的是另一件事：那一样到底是什么。

他往上顶。幸福既然是照着德性的活动，那就该照着最好的那一样德性；那一样是我们身上最好的那一部分的德性。这样一种日子，比照着人这个身份过的日子高。因为一个人这样过，不是就他是个人这一面过的，是就他身上有一点神样的东西这一面过的。

然后是那几句最有名的：

不要听那些劝人的话，说你是人就只想人的事，你是会死的就只想会死的事。要尽你所能地让自己不朽，一切都朝着照你身上最强的那一样活。

它的个头虽小，力气和贵重却远远超过其余的一切。

而这一样看来就是每一个人自己，因为它是那个作主的，也是更好的。

这句话他不是头一回说。第九卷讲好人跟自己那一头的时候，他就说过一句几乎一样的：那个想的部分，看来最是每一个人自己。第十卷只是把它抬到了顶。

要留住这句话里的两样东西：一是"看来"，二是那半句理由：因为它是那个作主的，也是更好的。他给的是一句带理由的话，不是一张不由分说的全身像。

抬完他自己收了一道限。就他是个人，跟一群人一块儿过日子这一面说，他还是要那些寻常的东西，为的是把人做下去。这个说法在《尼各马可伦理学》里只出现这一次。

所以第一卷和第十卷不是一问一答，是两件事：一件是这一样从哪儿来，一件是这一样是什么。

而把这两件事接起来的，是他在第一卷用来路那两下的方向。

他用了两次来路，方向正好相反。第一次是把来路从分量上摘开：就算不是神送来的，照样最神圣。第二次是拿分量去限制来路：正因为它最大最美，才不该交给运气。

摘开的是"来路定分量"，用上的是"分量定来路"。

第一下这边也做，而且立成了规矩。审那条路怎么走出来的那一章，开工前先声明它不做什么：不拿"路是这样走来的"去证"所以有效"。而从发生那一头的判决直接跨到规范那一头照常发判，在那本书里是登记在册的一宗冒领，有案由，有门牌。

第二下这边做不了。

因为第二下要有一个最高的那一样摆在那儿，分量先定住了，才轮得到它去限制来路。

这就是两边真正分开的地方。

他有一个固定的最高者摆在那儿，所以"它从哪儿来"这一问才非问不可，也才值得他动手去打运气那一支。

这边不设那一样。每一次取走的都是实得：一处已经登记的亏欠得着了回应，一样分得清也复核得了的区别真的做了出来，记进账里。它不是"离那个最高的近了一步"。

因为"近了一步"这句话要成立，得先有两头：一头是你，一头是那个最高的，中间量得出一段路。而这边根本没有那一头。

这边还专门写死了一句，防的正是这里最容易长出来的那个误会："既然到不了，那何必走"，这个读法错在把没有终点当成了没有所得。

到不了，是因为压根没有那个"到"。取走的那些，是真的。

第一卷那一问，换个问法就在日子里。

一件事办成了，旁边容易有人问一句：这是他有本事，还是他运气好。

这一问其实是两问叠在一起的。一问是来路：这件事究竟怎么成的。另一问是分量：这件事算不算数，往后能不能拿来当凭据。

这类争吵常把第一问当成第二问的赌注。说他运气好，为的是把第二问一起说掉。

亚里士多德那一手，正是先把这两问拆开。拆开以后他再回身去打运气那一支，而这一打就不再是为了争分量了。先拆再打。顺序反过来，那一打就成了争分量，而争分量正是他刚刚声明不必争的。

另一句在家里最常听见。

吵起来的时候有一句话特别重：你就是这样的人。

这句话不是凭空说的。他确实一再这样做，不是一回两回。这一半有账可查。

可它还多说了一样。它把"一再这样"说成了"就是这样"，把一段日子里查得出来的事，说成了一句不带日子，不带场合，也不容再改的判。

多说的那一样，正是那个不带地址的"就是"。

而他自己在全书的最后一页，把碰上那一支又摆了一次。

讲完法与教养，他问到最后那一处，然后说：那些收集起来的法条和政体，对那些已经有了那份心性又看得来也判得来的人是有用的；而没有那份心性的人翻这些东西，判不好，除非碰巧。

有的英译把这里处理成“除非碰巧”，也有译本把它解释成“除非天生就有这种判断力”。这里不能靠中文里的“碰巧”去做词频判断；要紧的是论证位置：没有养成判断能力的人，即便偶然判对，也不能把那次结果当成可重复的本事。

第一卷他说，把最大最美的那件事交给运气，太不成话了。最后一页，判得好一部法这件事，他给没有那份心性的人留下的，正是这一样。

一头拒了运气作总来源，一头承认没有受过训练的人可能偶然判对。这不是他前后不一：前一处说的是幸福本身该不该以运气为来路，后一处说的是一个还没有那份心性的人手上还剩下什么。这个分别不需要靠两个用词是否相同来成立。摆在那儿的是同一件事：这一回成不成，不由这个人的本事定。

而不设那一样，还带着一件顺带的后果，说一句就够，不在这一篇里展开。

他把那一样安顿好以后，还能再多说一句：这一样就是每一个人自己。

这一句这边跟不上。跟不上的不是任何一句关于自己的话。说得出的是带地址的那一种：在这一层上，在这一件事上，作主的是这一样。说不出的是不带地址的那一种：无条件的，终局的，把一个人整个说尽的那一句"就是"。

理由写在纸上。那本书专门给"主体拿自己当对象"立过一条：每一个关于自己的说法，至少短缺它自己的作成。给红画像的手不在红里；给自己画像的手，恰恰就是画像这个动作本身，而动作不在它的产物里。

还有第二重：被画的那个在移动。只要他还在做事，他的状态和他站的位置就在改变；对他的任何一次收纳，收的都是那时候的他。

所以两边分开的不是能不能说一句"这是我"，是这一句能不能取得那个终局的地位，把一个人整个说尽。

他给最高的那一样安了个位置，然后给了它一个名分。

而这边只说得出一件事：写下"这就是我"的那一刻，写的那个人不在那张纸上。

Four Possible Origins

Book I, chapter 9 asks where happiness comes from. Is it learned, acquired through habituation, produced by some other form of training, bestowed by divine allotment, or left to fortune? Aristotle does not distribute attention evenly among the alternatives.

He postpones the theological question, but first lowers its stakes. Even if happiness is not sent by the gods and is instead acquired through virtue, learning, or practice, it would still be among the most divine things because it is the prize and end of virtue. Origin does not determine worth.

This also broadens access. If happiness can be reached through learning and effort, everyone whose capacity for virtue has not been badly impaired may in some measure attain it.

Fortune fares worse. Aristotle argues conditionally that things produced by nature, art, and good causation tend toward the best arrangement available to them; it would be unfitting for the greatest and noblest human good to be entrusted to luck. The passage offers a reason, not a demonstration. Yet the direction is clear: learning, practice, and virtue are accepted; sheer fortune is refused the governing role.

Origin Does Not Set the Price

The first move is powerful beyond Aristotle's theory of happiness. People often try to settle the value of an achievement by narrating its origin. A successful career is dismissed as luck or inherited advantage; a belief is discounted because of the culture in which it arose; an act is praised as authentic because it was spontaneous.

Origins matter. They explain conditions, distribute credit, and may reveal hidden dependence. But “this arose from fortune” does not by itself establish “this has no value,” just as “this was difficult” does not establish that it was good. The causal and normative questions must be separated before either can be answered accurately.

SAE makes that separation a rule. A history of how a judgment arose cannot substitute for an examination of its address, grounds, and liabilities. The origin story may become evidence within the audit; it cannot issue the verdict merely by being earlier.

Aristotle's next move is less available to SAE. Having detached worth from divine origin, he uses worth to constrain origin: because happiness is the greatest and noblest good, abandoning it to fortune would be unfitting. The highest good is already fixed strongly enough to make a claim about the kind of source it ought to have.

The Highest Activity

Book X asks a different question. It does not return to the four origins; it identifies the highest form of happiness. If happiness is activity in accordance with virtue, the best happiness will be activity of the best element in us. Aristotle names contemplation.

The life of contemplation exceeds the human insofar as something divine is present in us. We should not restrict mortal beings to mortal thoughts but, as far as possible, “make ourselves immortal” by living according to what is best. Though small in bulk, the intellectual element surpasses the rest in power and worth; it seems to be what each person most truly is because it is authoritative and better.

Aristotle then places a limit beside the elevation. Insofar as the contemplative person is human and lives with others, ordinary virtuous actions and external conditions remain necessary. The highest activity does not erase composite human life.

Book I and Book X therefore answer different questions: where happiness comes from, and what its fullest activity is. The fixed highest object connects them. Once the summit is named, origin matters partly because it must be fitting to the summit.

Gain Without a Summit

SAE refuses that architecture. It records real gains: a liability answered, a distinction made and opened to review, a person recognized where recognition was due. The gain is not a step measured against a fixed highest state.

This is why “If completion is impossible, why continue?” misfires. The question assumes a destination from which progress acquires distance. SAE's answer is local: what was gained here is not unreal because there is no final arrival. The point is not endless approximation to perfection but continued capacity to make warranted distinctions and answer what they leave out.

The refusal has a cost. Aristotle can say not only that contemplation is highest but that the highest element is, most of all, the person himself. SAE can make addressed claims—this capacity is governing in this decision, this commitment expresses me here—but it cannot produce an unaddressed, final portrait that contains the whole person.

Any self-description omits the act of describing, and the subject continues to change while the description is made. “This is who I am” may be responsible and illuminating. It cannot become a timeless closure without hiding its address.

Chance at the End

Near the end of Book X, fortune returns in a modest role. Collections of laws and constitutions are useful to readers with the formed capacity to judge them. Without that capacity, someone may at best happen upon a correct assessment. English translations render the phrase differently—some emphasize chance, others a natural gift—but the argument is stable: material does not produce judgment in an unformed reader.

This does not contradict Book I. There Aristotle refuses to make happiness itself depend on fortune. Here he acknowledges that, absent formation, a particular correct result may occur without competence. The successful outcome does not prove the state required to reproduce it.

Aristotle can make both moves because he has an account of the highest good and of the character fitted to it. SAE can audit the result and refuse to infer character from one lucky success. It cannot use a highest good to rule out whole classes of origin in advance.

The disagreement is exact. Both separate provenance from validity. Aristotle then lets the fixed worth of the highest constrain its proper source. SAE has no summit from which to issue that constraint. It can say that an account has earned this result, here. It cannot say that every life is distance from one completed form.

Aristotle gives the highest activity a place and then gives it a name: most of all, the self. SAE can offer only a situated sentence. At the moment “this is me” is written, the writer is the one thing the page cannot contain.
