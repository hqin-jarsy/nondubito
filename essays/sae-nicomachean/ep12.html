最高的那一样是学来的，还是碰上的

Is the Highest Good Learned or Found by Chance?

Han Qin (秦汉)

第一卷第九章开头就是一问，而且他明写这是一处难处：

幸福是学得来的，是习出来的，还是靠别的什么法子练出来的；还是凭一份神给的份额，或者干脆是碰上的。

四支。四支都摆在那儿，没有一支带着"某某人说"的名头。这是他自己摆的。

这一问跟下一章那一问只隔一章，问的却不是一件事。下一章问的是一段日子要不要等到人死了才算得清，那一问归另一篇。这一章问的只有一件：这一样东西是打哪儿来的。

而这一问他没有留着。他当场就处置了，而且处置得不平均。

神给的那一支他搁下，说这该归另一种考察。可搁下之前他先动了一手：就算它不是神送来的，是靠德性和某种学习或者训练得的，它照样是最神圣的那一类东西。

这一手要看清。他不是把这一支要了回来，他是把赌注从这一支上取下来了。神给不神给，这一问的输赢不再压在上头。

接着他添了一样：这样一来，够得着的人还多。因为只要不是在德性上残废了的，凭着某种学习和用心，人人都可能有它。

碰上的那一支他当场打死。而打死的理由要照他的写，不许替他造。他说的是：要是这样比靠运气得着幸福更好，那么事情本就该是这样，因为凡照着自然来的，本性就是尽其所能地摆到最好那个样子；照着技艺来的，照着任何一种原因来的，也都这样，尤其是照着最好那种原因来的。接着才是那一句：把最大最美的那件事交给运气，太不成话了。

这两句都带着"要是"和"也许"。他给的是一个说得过去的理由，不是一道证明。

学与习那一支，他收下了。

所以第一卷已经答了。四支里搁下一支，打死一支，收下一支，还顺手把够得着的人算宽了。

那么隔着九卷的第十卷，答的是什么。

不是这一问。第十卷第七章第八章里，学，习，练，运气，这几个字一个也没有出现。它们要到最后一章才一起回来，而那一段归另外两篇。

神给的那一支更干脆：他在第一卷搁下以后，那个说法全书再没有回来过。

第十卷第七章答的是另一件事：那一样到底是什么。

他往上顶。幸福既然是照着德性的活动，那就该照着最好的那一样德性；那一样是我们身上最好的那一部分的德性。这样一种日子，比照着人这个身份过的日子高。因为一个人这样过，不是就他是个人这一面过的，是就他身上有一点神样的东西这一面过的。

然后是那几句最有名的：

不要听那些劝人的话，说你是人就只想人的事，你是会死的就只想会死的事。要尽你所能地让自己不朽，一切都朝着照你身上最强的那一样活。

它的个头虽小，力气和贵重却远远超过其余的一切。

而这一样看来就是每一个人自己，因为它是那个作主的，也是更好的。

这句话他不是头一回说。第九卷讲好人跟自己那一头的时候，他就说过一句几乎一样的：那个想的部分，看来最是每一个人自己。第十卷只是把它抬到了顶。

要留住这句话里的两样东西：一是"看来"，二是那半句理由：因为它是那个作主的，也是更好的。他给的是一句带理由的话，不是一张不由分说的全身像。

抬完他自己收了一道限。就他是个人，跟一群人一块儿过日子这一面说，他还是要那些寻常的东西，为的是把人做下去。这个说法在《尼各马可伦理学》里只出现这一次。

所以第一卷和第十卷不是一问一答，是两件事：一件是这一样从哪儿来，一件是这一样是什么。

而把这两件事接起来的，是他在第一卷用来路那两下的方向。

他用了两次来路，方向正好相反。第一次是把来路从分量上摘开：就算不是神送来的，照样最神圣。第二次是拿分量去限制来路：正因为它最大最美，才不该交给运气。

摘开的是"来路定分量"，用上的是"分量定来路"。

第一下这边也做，而且立成了规矩。审那条路怎么走出来的那一章，开工前先声明它不做什么：不拿"路是这样走来的"去证"所以有效"。而从发生那一头的判决直接跨到规范那一头照常发判，在那本书里是登记在册的一宗冒领，有案由，有门牌。

第二下这边做不了。

因为第二下要有一个最高的那一样摆在那儿，分量先定住了，才轮得到它去限制来路。

这就是两边真正分开的地方。

他有一个固定的最高者摆在那儿，所以"它从哪儿来"这一问才非问不可，也才值得他动手去打运气那一支。

这边不设那一样。每一次取走的都是实得：一处已经登记的亏欠得着了回应，一样分得清也复核得了的区别真的做了出来，记进账里。它不是"离那个最高的近了一步"。

因为"近了一步"这句话要成立，得先有两头：一头是你，一头是那个最高的，中间量得出一段路。而这边根本没有那一头。

这边还专门写死了一句，防的正是这里最容易长出来的那个误会："既然到不了，那何必走"，这个读法错在把没有终点当成了没有所得。

到不了，是因为压根没有那个"到"。取走的那些，是真的。

第一卷那一问，换个问法就在日子里。

一件事办成了，旁边容易有人问一句：这是他有本事，还是他运气好。

这一问其实是两问叠在一起的。一问是来路：这件事究竟怎么成的。另一问是分量：这件事算不算数，往后能不能拿来当凭据。

这类争吵常把第一问当成第二问的赌注。说他运气好，为的是把第二问一起说掉。

亚里士多德那一手，正是先把这两问拆开。拆开以后他再回身去打运气那一支，而这一打就不再是为了争分量了。先拆再打。顺序反过来，那一打就成了争分量，而争分量正是他刚刚声明不必争的。

另一句在家里最常听见。

吵起来的时候有一句话特别重：你就是这样的人。

这句话不是凭空说的。他确实一再这样做，不是一回两回。这一半有账可查。

可它还多说了一样。它把"一再这样"说成了"就是这样"，把一段日子里查得出来的事，说成了一句不带日子，不带场合，也不容再改的判。

多说的那一样，正是那个不带地址的"就是"。

而他自己在全书的最后一页，把碰上那一支又摆了一次。

讲完法与教养，他问到最后那一处，然后说：那些收集起来的法条和政体，对那些已经有了那份心性又看得来也判得来的人是有用的；而没有那份心性的人翻这些东西，判不好，除非碰巧。

有的英译把这里处理成“除非碰巧”，也有译本把它解释成“除非天生就有这种判断力”。这里不能靠中文里的“碰巧”去做词频判断；要紧的是论证位置：没有养成判断能力的人，即便偶然判对，也不能把那次结果当成可重复的本事。

第一卷他说，把最大最美的那件事交给运气，太不成话了。最后一页，判得好一部法这件事，他给没有那份心性的人留下的，正是这一样。

一头拒了运气作总来源，一头承认没有受过训练的人可能偶然判对。这不是他前后不一：前一处说的是幸福本身该不该以运气为来路，后一处说的是一个还没有那份心性的人手上还剩下什么。这个分别不需要靠两个用词是否相同来成立。摆在那儿的是同一件事：这一回成不成，不由这个人的本事定。

而不设那一样，还带着一件顺带的后果，说一句就够，不在这一篇里展开。

他把那一样安顿好以后，还能再多说一句：这一样就是每一个人自己。

这一句这边跟不上。跟不上的不是任何一句关于自己的话。说得出的是带地址的那一种：在这一层上，在这一件事上，作主的是这一样。说不出的是不带地址的那一种：无条件的，终局的，把一个人整个说尽的那一句"就是"。

理由写在纸上。那本书专门给"主体拿自己当对象"立过一条：每一个关于自己的说法，至少短缺它自己的作成。给红画像的手不在红里；给自己画像的手，恰恰就是画像这个动作本身，而动作不在它的产物里。

还有第二重：被画的那个在移动。只要他还在做事，他的状态和他站的位置就在改变；对他的任何一次收纳，收的都是那时候的他。

所以两边分开的不是能不能说一句"这是我"，是这一句能不能取得那个终局的地位，把一个人整个说尽。

他给最高的那一样安了个位置，然后给了它一个名分。

而这边只说得出一件事：写下"这就是我"的那一刻，写的那个人不在那张纸上。

Book I, chapter 9 opens with a question, one he explicitly calls a difficulty:

Is happiness acquired by learning and habituation, trained through some other means, received as a divine allotment, or simply happened upon?

Four branches. All four are laid out, none under the heading “so-and-so says.” He puts them there himself.

This question is only a chapter away from the next one, but it asks something different. The next chapter asks whether a life cannot be properly reckoned until the person dies. That belongs to another essay. This chapter asks just one thing: where does this thing come from?

He does not leave the question open. He deals with it on the spot, and not evenly.

He sets aside the divine-gift branch, saying it belongs to another inquiry. But first he makes a move: even if happiness is not sent by the gods, but acquired through virtue and some sort of learning or training, it is still among the most divine things.

We need to see this move clearly. He has not reclaimed that branch. He has taken the stakes off it. Whether it is divinely given no longer decides who wins this question.

Then he adds something: on this account, more people can reach it. Anyone whose capacity for virtue is not impaired can acquire it through a certain kind of learning and care.

He strikes down the chance branch on the spot. The reason must be written as he gives it, not invented for him. If it is better this way than for happiness to come by fortune, he says, it is reasonable for things to be this way. What comes by nature is naturally arranged as well as it can be; so too what comes by craft or any other cause, especially the best cause. Only then comes the sentence: entrusting the greatest and noblest thing to fortune would be altogether unfitting.

These sentences carry “if” and “perhaps.” He supplies a plausible reason, not a proof.

He accepts the branch of learning and habituation.

So Book I has already answered. Among the four branches he sets one aside, strikes one down, accepts one, and in passing widens the range of people who can reach it.

What, then, is Book X answering, nine books later?

Not this question. In Book X, chapters 7 and 8, the words learning, habituation, training, and fortune do not appear. They return together only in the final chapter, whose passage belongs to two other essays.

The divine-gift branch is clearer still. After he sets it aside in Book I, that formulation never returns in the book.

Book X, chapter 7 answers something else: what exactly is this thing?

He pushes upward. If happiness is activity in accordance with virtue, it should accord with the best virtue, the virtue of the best part in us. Such a life is higher than a life lived merely as a human being. A person lives it not insofar as they are human, but insofar as something divine is present in them.

Then come the most famous sentences:

Do not listen to those who advise that, being human, you should think only human thoughts, or, being mortal, only mortal thoughts. Make yourself immortal as far as you can, and do everything to live according to the strongest thing in you.

Though small in bulk, it far surpasses everything else in power and worth.

And this seems to be each person’s self, since it is the ruling and better part.

This is not the first time he has said it. In Book IX, discussing the good person’s relation to themselves, he said almost the same thing: the thinking part seems to be each person’s self most of all. Book X simply raises it to the top.

Keep two things in this sentence: “seems,” and the reason in its second half, “since it is the ruling and better part.” He offers a statement with reasons, not a full-length portrait imposed without argument.

After raising it, he adds a limit himself. Insofar as this person is human and lives among other people, they still need ordinary things in order to go on living as a human being. This expression occurs only once in the Nicomachean Ethics.

So Book I and Book X are not question and answer. They concern two things: where this thing comes from, and what it is.

What joins the two is the direction of the two moves he makes with origin in Book I.

He uses origin twice, in exactly opposite directions. First he detaches origin from worth: even if it is not divinely sent, it remains supremely divine. Second he uses worth to limit origin: precisely because it is the greatest and noblest, it ought not to be entrusted to fortune.

What he detaches is “origin determines worth.” What he uses is “worth determines origin.”

This side makes the first move too, and establishes it as a rule. Before the chapter auditing how the path came about begins, it declares what it will not do: use “the road came about this way” to prove “therefore it is valid.” Directly carrying a verdict about genesis across to pass judgment in the normative domain is registered in that book as a case of claiming an unearned entitlement, with its grounds and address recorded.

The second move is unavailable on this side.

It requires a highest thing to be set out there, its worth already fixed, before it can constrain its origin.

This is where the two sides really divide.

He has a fixed highest thing standing there. That makes “where does it come from?” a question he must ask, and makes the chance branch worth attacking.

This side posits no such thing. Each gain is real: a registered debt receives a response; a distinction that can be identified and reviewed is actually made and entered in the account. It is not “one step closer to the highest.”

For “one step closer” to hold, there must first be two ends: you at one end, the highest at the other, and a measurable road between them. This side has no such other end at all.

It even writes down a firm sentence to block the misunderstanding most likely to grow here: the reading “If we cannot arrive, why go?” mistakes having no endpoint for gaining nothing.

There is no arriving because that “arrival” does not exist in the first place. The gains are real.

Rephrase Book I’s question and it appears in daily life.

Something succeeds, and someone nearby is likely to ask: was that skill, or was it luck?

This is really two questions stacked together. One concerns origin: how did it actually succeed? The other concerns worth: does the achievement count, and can it be used as a credential later?

Arguments of this sort often make the second question depend on winning the first. Calling someone lucky is meant to dismiss the second question along with it.

Aristotle’s move is first to separate the questions. Only afterward does he turn back to attack the chance branch, and that attack is no longer a contest over worth. First separate, then attack. Reverse the order and the attack becomes a dispute over worth, precisely what he has just said need not be disputed.

Another sentence is heard most often at home.

In an argument, one sentence lands especially hard: this is just the kind of person you are.

It does not come from nowhere. The person really has acted that way repeatedly, not just once or twice. That half has an account that can be checked.

But the sentence says something further. It turns “repeatedly like this” into “just is like this,” turning checkable events over a stretch of time into a verdict without a time, without a setting, and without room for revision.

The further thing is precisely that “is” with no address attached.

On the book’s last page, he himself sets out the chance branch once more.

After discussing law and education, he reaches the last question. Collected laws and constitutions, he says, are useful to people who already have the disposition and can see and judge well. Those without it cannot judge such materials well, except by chance.

Some English translations render this “except by chance”; others understand it as “unless they have a natural gift for such judgment.” We cannot use the Chinese word for chance here to count occurrences in the original. What matters is the argument’s position: someone without a formed capacity for judgment may occasionally judge correctly, but cannot treat that result as a repeatable skill.

In Book I he says it is altogether unfitting to entrust the greatest and noblest thing to fortune. On the final page, for someone without the disposition, that is exactly what he leaves available for judging a law well.

At one end he refuses fortune as the overall source; at the other he acknowledges that an untrained person may happen to judge correctly. This is not inconsistency. The first passage concerns whether happiness itself should derive from fortune; the second concerns what remains available to someone without the disposition. The distinction does not depend on the two passages using the same word. What stands there is the same situation: whether this attempt succeeds is not determined by the person’s skill.

Not positing that highest thing has a further consequence. One sentence will do; this essay will not develop it.

Once he has settled that thing, he can say something more: this is each person’s self.

This side cannot follow that sentence. What it cannot follow is not every statement about the self. It can make the kind with an address attached: at this level, in this matter, this is what governs. It cannot make the kind without one: the unconditional, final “is” that says everything about a whole person.

The reason is on the page. The book lays down a rule specifically for the subject taking itself as an object: every account of oneself falls short of at least its own making. The hand painting red is not in the red. The hand painting the self is precisely the act of painting, and the act is not in its product.

There is a second layer: the one being painted is moving. As long as the person acts, their state and position change. Every account that gathers them in gathers the person they were at that time.

So the difference is not whether one can say “this is me,” but whether that sentence can gain final status and say the whole person completely.

He gives the highest thing a place, then gives it a designation.

This side can say only this: at the moment “this is me” is written, the person writing is not on the page.
