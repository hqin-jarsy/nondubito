自足的人还要朋友干什么

Why Does the Self-Sufficient Person Need Friends?

Han Qin (秦汉)

第九卷第九章开头一句，是把一件有争的事摆出来：

幸福的人要不要朋友，这件事有争。

他不急着答，先把对面的话按原样摆足。

有福气又自足的人不需要朋友，因为好东西他都有了；既然自足，就不再缺什么；而朋友是另一个自己，朋友的用处是替你弄来你自己弄不来的东西。摆完他引了欧里庇得斯一句：运气好的时候，要朋友做什么。

摆得很足，足到像是他自己在说。

然后他回身。把一切好东西都给了幸福的人，偏不给他朋友，这看着说不过去；何况在身外的好东西里，朋友看来是最大的一样。

往下他先承认对面对了一半，而且指出对在哪儿。

那些先说话的人究竟在说什么，他们哪一点说对了。他自己答：多数人心里的朋友，指的是有用的那种。这一种，有福气的人一个也不需要，因为好东西他都有了。为着快活的那一种也不需要，或者只需要一点点，因为他的日子本来就是快活的，用不着从外头再搬进来。既然这两种都不需要，看上去他就是不需要朋友了。

"可这话大概不对。"

这一步是整章的关节。他不是把对面驳倒的。他是把对面说的那两种朋友一种一种承认掉，剩下的那一种才是他要谈的。

所以这一章从头到尾没有走"你缺什么，朋友替你补上"这条路。那条路是他自己拆掉的。

他那条路从一句旧话起：幸福是一种活动，而活动是做出来的，不是像一份家产那样搁在那儿的。

搁着的东西越多越自足；做出来的事情不这么算。

往下两步。一步是：我们看得清旁边的人，比看得清自己容易；看得清别人做的事，比看得清自己做的事容易。好人做的那些事，在同样是好人的朋友看来是快活的。

另一步更实：一个人独过，日子难。因为一个人自己一刻不停地做下去，不容易；跟别人一起，冲着别人，就容易些。这样那个活动才接得下去。

最后落到最深的一层。一个人看见的时候知道自己在看，听见的时候知道自己在听，走路的时候知道自己在走；照这样推下去，我们觉着的时候知道自己在觉，想的时候知道自己在想。而知道自己在觉在想，就是知道自己在着。

而朋友是另一个自己。所以对朋友的那一份，跟对自己的那一份，形状一样：知道他在着，跟知道自己在着，是同一种快活。

于是那一句才有内容：得一块儿觉着他在。这件事怎么才成，他说得很具体：一块儿过日子，共有话和想法。人说的一块儿过是这个意思，不是牲口在同一片地上吃草那个意思。

章末那一句因此毫不含糊：幸福的人，会需要好的朋友。

有人会把整章读成他在改自己的第一卷。

不是。第一卷定自足的时候他已经写明：自足不是指一个人自己过，是把父母妻子儿女朋友和同城的人一起算在内的。所以第九卷这一章不是他忘了再回头补，是他把第一卷早就排除过的那个读法重新请上桌，让它以最强的样子再问一遍。

话还得说全。第十卷讲那一样最高的活动的时候，他说有智慧的人一个人也做得了，越有智慧越做得了；有人一起做也许更好，可他还是最自足的那一个。这两处怎么合得起来，是他自己那一套里的一桩公案，本篇不判。摆在这儿，只为不让人以为他就说过一句。

他亲手拆掉的那条路，这边在同一个位置上也没有走。

《第一批判》里他者出场的那一处，写得很小心：自问担保不了发现自己的全部盲区，所以他者的追问提供一种自问没法自己担保的独立校验。紧跟着还有两句限：这不是说自问无效，也不是说他问是唯一的校验；把地址显式地维护住，另外设一道复核程序，各有各的用处。

这两句限一加，补缺口那条路在这边也走不通了。他者不是来把你少的那一块补上的。自问，把地址显式地记住，另设一道复核，这几样各有各的用处；而它们里头没有一样能替自问担保它自己的独立性。短的那一截不是内容，是那份独立。

一边把有用的朋友和图快活的朋友一种一种承认掉，一边把"另设一道复核也有用"明写在旁边。两边都不肯让他者站在补缺口那个位置上。

他把朋友安在日子里。这边把他者安在账上。

他把朋友放进"活着"这件事里。活动要接得下去，一个人接不下去；活动要看得见，看自己比看旁人难；而知道朋友在着，跟知道自己在着是同一种。他者进来的位置，是那个活动本身的条件。

这边把他者放进"算数"这件事里。一门哲学立起来，效力不由自家宣布，须经追问参与结算。他者进来的位置，是效力的结算。

一个说少的是那一段接得下去也看得见的活，一个说少的是那一道结算。

这两样在日子里分得很开。

一个人写完一份东西，自己读三遍，觉得没问题。这时候他要的是别人来挑错。这是结算那一头。

同一个人做完一件很久的事，一件他做得很好也确实做成了的事，他会想找个人说一声。不是要建议，也不是要夸。说完了那件事一分没变，可他还是要说。这是活着那一头。

两样谁也替不了谁。挑得出错的人不一定是能一块儿说话的人；能一块儿说话的人不一定挑得出错。

把这边调用过的几处他者接口一处一处点出来，就看得见少的是什么。

一处在幻相那一章：他问是自问担保不了的那一种校验。一处在发生那一章的最高一站：两个主体互相认对方是目的本身，那一站的否定不留下东西，留下的是对方的长。旁边还挨着一样叫涵育：让别人那条路能在不被人占去的条件下走下去，明写含着不去挡着和主动帮着两面。此外还有交叠里那一条共享的后验输入，说的是两条路在同一个世上，互相是对方的对象。

这几处能说明的是校验，是互认，是交叠，是替别人留出走得下去的条件。

没有一条把一块儿过日子本身算成好日子的一部分。

这句话得说准，它不是判这一整套里没有他者，也不是判这一整套里没有友爱可讲。它只判一件事：本篇调用的这几处，不给这一样。

而他给。他那一章收在一个判词上：不给他这个，他在这一处就有缺。

这个判词落得下来，是因为他先做过一件事：他把幸福定成活动，不是一份攒下的家当。定成活动，一段活动接不下去就是真的少了一段；缺口是可以算的。

这边这几处给的是校验的成色和越界的风险，不是好日子里有哪几样。要下他那个判词，得先有一个像他那样能算出缺口的量法。

这一笔不能算他多说了。是他先给了量法，判词才落得下来。

这边把一门哲学写成一本要外人来对的账，这一条站得住。

可账对完了，人还是想找个人说一声。

他那一章末了说的正是这件事：得一块儿觉着他在，而这件事得靠一块儿过日子，共有话和想法，不是牲口在同一片地上吃草。

账可以由别人来对。一块儿过日子算不算好日子的一部分，不在这本账里结。

The opening sentence of Book IX, chapter 9, puts a disputed matter on the table:

Whether the happy person needs friends is disputed.

He is in no hurry to answer. First he gives the other side's account in full, as it stands.

The blessed and self-sufficient person needs no friends, since he already has all good things. Being self-sufficient, he lacks nothing further. A friend is another self, and a friend's use is to provide what you cannot provide for yourself. Having set this out, he quotes Euripides: when fortune is good, what need is there of friends?

He gives it so fully that it sounds as though he is speaking for himself.

Then he turns. It seems absurd to give the happy person every good thing except friends, especially since, among external goods, friends seem the greatest.

He first concedes that the other side is half right, and identifies where.

What exactly do those who first made the claim mean, and where are they right? He answers: by friends, most people mean useful friends. The blessed person needs none of that kind, since he already has all good things. He does not need friends for pleasure either, or needs them only a little, since his life is already pleasant and needs no pleasure brought in from outside. With neither of these two kinds needed, he seems to need no friends.

“But perhaps that is not true.”

This is the chapter's hinge. He has not defeated the other side. He concedes, one by one, the two kinds of friends they mean. The kind left over is the one he wants to discuss.

So from beginning to end, this chapter never takes the route of “whatever you lack, a friend supplies.” He dismantles that route himself.

His own route begins with an earlier statement: happiness is an activity, and activity is something done, not something left sitting there like property.

The more things you have stored away, the more self-sufficient you are. Things done do not work like that.

Then two steps. One: we can see those beside us more easily than we can see ourselves, and others' actions more easily than our own. The actions of good people are pleasant to their friends who are good themselves.

The other is more concrete: life alone is hard. It is not easy to keep acting continuously by oneself. With others and toward others, it is easier. That is how the activity can continue.

Finally he reaches the deepest layer. When seeing, a person knows he sees; when hearing, that he hears; when walking, that he walks. Following this through, when we perceive we know we perceive, and when we think we know we think. And knowing that we perceive and think is knowing that we exist.

And a friend is another self. So what concerns the friend has the same shape as what concerns oneself: knowing that he exists is the same kind of pleasure as knowing that one exists oneself.

Only then does the sentence have its content: one must share in the awareness of his existence. He is quite specific about how this comes about: living together, sharing words and thoughts. This is what living together means for human beings, not what it means for cattle grazing in the same place.

The chapter's final sentence is therefore unambiguous: the happy person will need good friends.

Some will read the whole chapter as his revision of Book I.

It is not. When defining self-sufficiency in Book I, he already made clear that it did not mean a person living alone: parents, wife, children, friends, and fellow citizens were all included. So this chapter in Book IX is not his return to repair something forgotten. He invites back to the table a reading already excluded in Book I, and lets it ask its question again in its strongest form.

The account must also be complete. In Book X, speaking of the highest activity, he says that the wise person can perform it alone, the wiser the more readily. Companions may make it better, but he is still the most self-sufficient. How these two passages fit together is an unresolved case within his own account. This essay does not rule on it. It is placed here only so that nobody supposes he said just one thing.

The route he dismantles with his own hands is one this side also declines at the same place.

The passage where the other enters the First Critique is carefully written: self-questioning cannot guarantee discovery of all one's own blind spots, so another's questioning provides an independent check that self-questioning cannot itself guarantee. Two limits follow immediately: this does not mean self-questioning is ineffective, nor that questioning by another is the only check. Explicitly maintaining addresses and establishing a separate review procedure each have their uses.

With those two limits, the gap-filling route is closed on this side too. The other does not come to supply your missing piece. Self-questioning, explicitly recording addresses, and establishing a separate review each have their uses. None can guarantee self-questioning's own independence for it. What falls short is not content, but that independence.

One side concedes, one by one, the cases of useful friends and friends sought for pleasure. The other explicitly writes beside its claim that a separate review is useful too. Neither side will put the other in the position of filling a gap.

He places the friend in life. This side places the other in the account.

He puts the friend within the business of living. Activity must continue, and alone one cannot keep it going. Activity must be visible, and seeing oneself is harder than seeing another. Knowing that a friend exists is of the same kind as knowing that one exists oneself. The other enters as a condition of the activity itself.

This side puts the other within the business of counting as valid. Once a philosophy is established, its validity cannot be announced by itself. Questioning must participate in settling its account. The other enters at the settlement of validity.

One says what is missing is that stretch of life that can continue and be seen. The other says what is missing is that act of settlement.

In daily life the two are quite distinct.

Someone finishes a piece of writing, reads it three times, and thinks it is fine. At this point he wants someone else to find mistakes. That is the settlement side.

The same person finishes something that took a long time, something he did well and really accomplished, and wants to tell someone. He is not seeking advice or praise. Telling it changes nothing about the accomplishment, yet he still wants to tell it. That is the living side.

Neither can replace the other. Someone who can find mistakes is not necessarily someone with whom one can share a conversation. Someone with whom one can share a conversation is not necessarily able to find mistakes.

Name, one by one, the places this side has called upon where the other enters, and what is missing becomes visible.

One is in the chapter on illusion: another's questioning is the kind of check that self-questioning cannot guarantee. Another is the highest station in the chapter on genesis: two subjects recognize each other as ends in themselves. Negation at that station leaves no thing behind; what it leaves is the other's growth. Beside it stands cultivation: allowing another's path to continue without being appropriated, expressly including both not obstructing and actively helping. There is also the shared a posteriori input in overlap: two paths are in the same world, and each is an object for the other.

These places can explain checking, mutual recognition, overlap, and leaving others the conditions in which to continue.

None counts living together itself as part of a good life.

This statement needs precision. It does not rule that the whole account lacks the other, or that it has nothing to say about friendship. It rules on only one thing: the places this essay has called upon do not supply this.

But he does. His chapter ends in a judgment: withhold this from him, and in this respect he is lacking.

That judgment can land because he has first done something else: defined happiness as activity, not accumulated property. Once it is activity, a stretch that cannot continue really is a missing stretch. The gap can be reckoned.

These places on this side give the quality of checking and the risk of crossing boundaries, not an inventory of what a good life contains. To make his judgment, one first needs a measure like his, capable of reckoning the gap.

This cannot be counted as an overclaim on his part. He first provides the measure; only then can the judgment land.

This side writes a philosophy as an account that outsiders must check. That claim stands.

But after the account has been checked, the person still wants someone to tell.

That is precisely what the end of his chapter says: one must share in awareness of the friend's existence. This requires living together, sharing words and thoughts, not cattle grazing in the same place.

An account can be checked by someone else. Whether living together is part of a good life is not settled in this account.
