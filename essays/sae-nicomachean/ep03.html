幸福要不要等到死

Must Happiness Wait Until Death?

Han Qin (秦汉)

梭伦留下一句老话：要看结局。

第一卷第十章，他把这句话拿出来问：那么活着的人里头，是不是谁也不能叫幸福，非得照梭伦说的看到结局不可。

紧接着他自己追了一句，而这一句才是圈套：那么要是照这么定，一个人死了那会儿，他就是幸福的了吗。

这一句他当场判了。他说这话完全说不过去，尤其对我们这些把幸福说成一种活动的人来说。

理由是硬的，硬得不必多讲：死人不做活动。

得认准这一下打的是哪一句。打的是"人死了那会儿他才幸福"，不是打梭伦那条规矩。梭伦那条还站着，他只是把它的一个读法先挡掉了。

然后他替梭伦想了一个好一点的讲法：也许梭伦的意思不是说死人幸福，是说到了那时候，你才能稳稳当当地把一个人称作有福的，因为他这时已经在祸患和不幸之外了。

稳稳当当。这四个字是梭伦要买的东西。

可他说，就连这个讲法也有得争。因为死人身上好像还是有好有坏：有荣也有辱，子孙后代过得顺不顺。

由此长出一个真难处，他自己用了"难处"这两个字。

一个人一直活到老都过得很好，末了也走得体面。可他的子孙后来出了各种变故，有的成器，得着配得上的日子，有的正相反。而这些后人跟他隔了几代，远近各不相同。

两头都荒唐。

一头是：死了的人跟着子孙的境遇一起变，一会儿幸福一会儿倒霉，这说不过去。

另一头是：子孙的事一点都够不着先人，一时半刻也够不着，这也说不过去。

这个两难他在这一章里没有解，他把它放下，回到前面那个难处上去。（下一章他给了个了结：后人的事对先人确实有点影响，可那点影响又小又是那么个性质，不至于把本来不幸福的人变成幸福的，也不至于把本来有福的人那份福夺走。）而回去的第一句，是这一章最重的一句。

他说：要是非得看到结局才能称某个人有福，而且称的时候他已经不是有福的了，只是从前是过，那么一个人正幸福的时候，属于他的那个说法偏不能真真切切地说到他头上，这不荒唐吗。

荒唐的不是心情上的委屈，是话说不通：一样东西确实属于他，就在它确实属于他的那个时候，你偏不许把它说出口。

理由他也替对面写了：不肯把活着的人称作幸福，怕的是变故；把幸福当成一样长住的东西，一点也不该翻覆，可运气偏偏常在同一批人身上来回转。

这句写完，"活着不许叫幸福"就倒了。

他接着走正面。

他说，跟着运气走这件事，本来就一点也不对。过得好过得坏不在运气里头。人的日子确实还需要运气搭把手，可做主的是那些合乎德性的活动，反过来也一样。

然后他说了一句很得意的话：刚才走的那个难处，反倒替这个说法作了证。

为什么。因为人的种种作为里头，没有哪一样有合乎德性的活动这样牢靠。这些活动比学问还经久；而其中最贵重的那些又更经久，因为有福的人最多也最连着地就住在里头过日子，所以它们不容易被忘掉。

难处本来是问"幸福怎么这么容易被运气掀翻"。他的回答是：你要的那个不翻覆，正是我这个说法给得出来的，而运气那一头给不出来。你的难处是替我提的。

于是幸福的人一辈子都是那个样子。他一直在做那些合乎德性的事，也一直在看着它们，或者说，做得比别的都多；运气来了他扛得最好看，处处都合调子，只要他真是那个好人，那个用旧诗里的话说四四方方挑不出毛病的人。

他还给了两个比方，都是干活的比方。真正好而且明白事理的人，扛起所有的运气都体面，就像一个将军拿手上现有的兵去打最合算的那一仗，像一个鞋匠拿手上现有的皮子做出最好的那双鞋。不是挑材料，是拿到什么做什么。

这里他还留了一道分寸。他把两个词分开了。落到普里阿摩斯那样的运气里，一个幸福的人不会变成可怜人，因为他永远不会去做那些可恨的下作事；可他也称不上有大福。扛得住不等于还光鲜。

而且他说得很具体：这个人不会花里胡哨说变就变，不会轻易被挪出他的幸福，也不是随便碰上点倒霉事就挪得动，得是又大又多的那种；而从那样的倒霉事里出来，他也不会短时间内重新幸福，要有的话，得在一段又长又完整的时间里，在那段时间里挣着大而好的东西。

到这儿他收口。

收口那一句是问句：那么，一个人合乎完全的德性在活动，外物也够用，而且不是一时是一辈子，把这样的人叫作幸福，有什么拦得住呢。

拦不住。可他自己又添了一问：还要不要再加一句，说他还会这样活下去，也会这样收场。

而加这一句的理由，他明写着：因为将来对我们是看不见的；而我们把幸福定成一个终点，定成完完全全的。

于是全章落在这一句上：

要是这样，我们就把活着的人里头那些已经有这些东西而且还会有这些东西的人，称作有大福的；而是作为人有大福。

作为人。三个字。

梭伦要的是稳稳当当，他的办法是等到人死。亚里士多德把那份稳当退了回去，把话留住了，代价是加上这三个字。

而这三个字，这边有一样东西对着。

两边都不肯把一个当下的说法当成终审。

这边的说法是分两栏。一笔记进账，和这笔账过了复核，是两回事：记在册上不等于过了核对，记错了也照样记在那儿。而且这边把"拿在账上"当"已经验过"来使这件事，专门立了一条，说那是出示一样自己没挣着的凭证。

分两栏这件事，账房里本来就是这么办的。记账的人和查账的人不是一道手续。月底把一笔支出记上，年底才有人拿着单据翻一遍。这中间那段时间，那笔账在册上，可它什么也没证明。

日常里还有一句更近的：他是个靠得住的人。

这句话什么时候说得出口。说了以后他出了事，那是你当时说错了，还是当时说得对，后来他变了。这两句差得很远。前一句是你判错了，后一句是他变了，而这两句容易被搅成一句，搅成"看走眼了"。

分不分得开，就看你把那句话记在哪一栏。

可这两栏为什么不能合成一栏，两边给的理由不是一个。

他的理由是时间。将来对我们是看不见的，所以那句话得留一手。留的那一手对着的是还没发生的事。

这边的理由不是时间。一笔记进账，本身就还没审；审它的不是后来发生了什么，是有没有人来对这笔账。这边有一句话说得很实在：把哪几条前提写进去，这件事本身就是一次判断，不是全知的抄录。

这一层之差落到实处，就分得很清楚了。

按他在这一段明写的理由，要是将来看得见了，至少那一层由看不见带来的保留可以拿掉。他自己写的就是这个：加这一句，是因为将来看不见。（那三个字里还压着别的分量，等下就到。）

按这边那个理由，将来看得见也拿不掉。因为拦着终审的不是将来，是记账这个动作本身还是一次判断。

一个在等事情，一个在等对账。

可人的一辈子会到头。

而这一点并不会让复核跟着到头。被判的那个人收住了，关于他的判断照旧可以改，照旧可以有人来翻。对象闭合和复核闭合不是一回事，这一处不能混，混了就成了拿"人会死"去逼"账得结"。

真正空着的是另一格，而且比结账那一格深。

这边能把每一笔判断记下来，也能让每一笔继续受查。可这些笔是零零散散的：有的彼此顶着，有的后来改了，有的连着一段现在看来白费的日子。这些笔怎么由同一个人接成一句话，接成"他这一辈子过得怎么样"，这边没有在这里说过。

他有那个主词。他把"完整的一辈子"直接写进了幸福的定义里：不是随便哪一段时间，是一辈子。他那个"作为人"退让的也是这一句的分量。

这边有每一笔的地址。还没有那一整段日子的主词。

最后回到那三个字。

梭伦买稳当，出的价是等到人死。亚里士多德不肯出这个价。他把稳当退了，留住了那句话，然后加上三个字：作为人。

那三个字是一张写明了的欠条。他没有假装账已经结清，他把没结清的那一部分写在了句子里。

这边把每一笔都写得比他细，一笔一个地址，一笔一份还没结清。

可那些笔加起来是谁的一辈子，还没有它的三个字。

Solon left an old saying: look to the end.

In Book I, chapter 10, Aristotle brings it out for questioning. Does this mean that no living person can be called happy, and that we must, as Solon says, wait to see the end?

He immediately follows with another question, and this is the trap: if that is the rule, is a person happy at the moment of death?

He judges that sentence on the spot. It is altogether absurd, he says, especially for those of us who call happiness an activity.

The reason is firm enough to need little explanation: the dead do not act.

We must identify exactly which claim this strikes. It strikes “a person is happy only once dead,” not Solon’s rule. The rule still stands. He has first blocked one reading of it.

Then he finds a better version on Solon’s behalf. Perhaps Solon does not mean that the dead are happy, but that only then can you safely call someone blessed, because the person is now beyond evils and misfortunes.

Safely. That is what Solon wants to buy.

Even this version, Aristotle says, is open to dispute. Good and bad things still seem to belong to the dead: honor and dishonor, and whether their descendants fare well.

A real difficulty grows out of this. He calls it a difficulty himself.

Someone has lived well into old age and ended life fittingly. But later the descendants undergo all sorts of changes. Some turn out well and receive the lives they deserve; others do the opposite. These descendants are separated from the ancestor by differing numbers of generations, some close, some distant.

Both extremes are absurd.

At one extreme, the dead person changes along with the descendants’ circumstances, now happy, now unfortunate. That will not do.

At the other, the descendants’ affairs never reach the ancestor at all, not even for a moment. That will not do either.

He does not solve this dilemma in the present chapter. He sets it aside and returns to the earlier difficulty. (In the next chapter he supplies a resolution: descendants’ affairs do have some effect on their ancestors, but the effect is small and of such a kind that it neither makes the unhappy happy nor robs the blessed of their blessedness.) And the first sentence on his return is the weightiest sentence in this chapter.

If we must see the end before calling someone blessed, he says, and by the time we say it the person is no longer blessed but only was so before, is it not absurd that precisely when someone is happy we cannot truly say of them what belongs to them?

The absurdity is not an emotional grievance. It is that the words make no sense: something really belongs to a person, and at the very moment it belongs to them you forbid anyone to say so.

He also writes out the other side’s reason. We refuse to call living people happy because we fear changes of fortune. We think of happiness as something lasting, not readily overturned, whereas fortune often turns back and forth for the same people.

Once that sentence is written, “you may not call the living happy” has fallen.

He moves on to the positive account.

Following fortune, he says, was never right in the first place. Living well or badly does not lie in fortune. A human life does need fortune’s assistance, but what governs happiness is activity in accordance with virtue, and the opposite activities govern the opposite result.

Then he says something with evident satisfaction: the difficulty we have just worked through actually confirms this account.

Why? Because nothing people do is as stable as activity in accordance with virtue. Such activities last longer even than knowledge, and the most valuable of them last longest, because blessed people spend their lives in them most fully and continuously. That is why they are not easily forgotten.

The difficulty originally asked, “Why is happiness so easily overturned by fortune?” His answer is: the stability you want is precisely what my account can supply and fortune cannot. Your difficulty has been making my case.

The happy person therefore remains that way throughout life. They keep doing and contemplating virtuous things, or do so more than anything else. They bear fortune most nobly and with fitness in every circumstance, provided they really are good, that person whom the old poem calls foursquare and beyond reproach.

He gives two comparisons, both drawn from work. A truly good and sensible person bears every fortune fittingly, as a general uses the troops available to fight the best possible campaign, or a shoemaker uses the leather available to make the best possible shoes. It is not choosing the materials, but making something of whatever arrives.

He leaves a qualification here. He distinguishes two words. Under a fate like Priam’s, a happy person will not become wretched, because they will never commit hateful and base acts. But neither can they be called blessed. Holding up is not the same as still flourishing.

He is quite specific. Such a person is no chameleon, changing at a touch. They will not easily be displaced from happiness, nor by just any misfortunes, but only by great and numerous ones. And after such misfortunes they will not become happy again in a short time. If they do, it will be over a long and complete stretch in which they attain great and noble things.

Here he draws to a close.

His closing sentence is a question. What prevents us from calling someone happy who acts in accordance with complete virtue, has sufficient external goods, and does so not for a while but over a whole life?

Nothing prevents it. Yet he adds another question himself: should we also add that the person will go on living this way and come to an end in the same way?

He states the reason for adding it: because the future is hidden from us, while we define happiness as an end, something complete in every way.

And so the chapter lands on this sentence:

If that is so, we shall call blessed those among the living who have and will continue to have these things; but blessed as human beings.

As human beings. Three words.

Solon wanted safety, and his method was to wait until a person died. Aristotle gives that safety back and keeps the statement. The price is adding those three words.

On this side, something answers to those words.

Neither side will treat a present statement as a final judgment.

This side uses two columns. Entering something in the account and having that entry pass review are different matters. Being on the books does not mean having been checked; an erroneous entry is still on the books. This side even singles out the use of “held on the account” as “already verified”: it means presenting a credential one has not earned.

Bookkeeping already works with that separation. Recording an entry and auditing it are not the same procedure. An expense goes into the books at the end of the month; someone checks it against the receipts at the end of the year. In between, the entry is there, but it has proved nothing.

There is an even more familiar everyday sentence: he is a reliable person.

When can we say it? If we say it and he later goes wrong, were we mistaken at the time, or right at the time before he changed? Those claims are very different. The first means your judgment was wrong; the second means he changed. Yet they are easily stirred into one: “I misjudged him.”

Whether we can keep them apart depends on which column holds the statement.

But the two sides give different reasons why the columns cannot be merged.

His reason is time. The future is hidden from us, so the statement must hold something in reserve. What it reserves against is what has not yet happened.

This side’s reason is not time. An entry, just by being entered, has not yet been audited. What audits it is not what happens later, but whether someone comes to check the account. This side has a concrete sentence for that: choosing which premises to write down is itself a judgment, not an omniscient transcription.

Put into practice, the difference becomes quite clear.

On the reason he explicitly gives in this passage, if the future became visible, at least the reservation caused by its invisibility could be removed. That is what he writes: we add this because the future is hidden. (Those three words carry further weight too; we will come to that.)

On this side’s reason, even a visible future would not remove it. What blocks final judgment is not the future, but the fact that recording an entry is itself still a judgment.

One waits for events. The other waits for the accounts to be checked.

But a human life does come to an end.

That does not bring review to an end with it. The life of the person being judged has closed; judgments about that person can still change and still be examined anew. Closure of the object and closure of review are different things. Confuse them, and “people die” becomes a way of forcing “the accounts must be closed.”

The real blank lies in another column, deeper than the one for settling accounts.

This side can record each judgment and keep each open to scrutiny. But the entries are scattered. Some contradict one another, some are later revised, and some belong to stretches of life that now seem wasted. How these entries are carried by one person into a single statement, “how this person’s life went,” has not been explained here.

He has that subject. He writes “a complete life” directly into the definition of happiness: not any stretch of time, but a life. The qualification “as human beings” also limits the weight of that whole statement.

This side has an address for each entry. It does not yet have the subject of the whole span of days.

Finally, back to those three words.

Solon buys safety at the price of waiting until a person dies. Aristotle will not pay that price. He gives safety back, keeps the statement, and adds three words: as human beings.

Those words are an explicitly written IOU. He does not pretend the account is settled. He writes the unsettled part into the sentence.

This side writes out each entry more finely than he does: one address for each entry, one unsettled balance for each.

But whose life those entries add up to still has no three words of its own.
