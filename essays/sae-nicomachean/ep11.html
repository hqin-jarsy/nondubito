对邻人的友爱从哪儿来

Where Does Friendship for Others Begin?

Han Qin (秦汉)

第九卷第四章讲完友爱的那些标志，他自己摆下一问，又自己把它放下了：

一个人跟他自己之间到底有没有友爱，这一问眼下先放下。

放下的是这一句。可紧挨着的下一句他又给了半个答案：

从前面说过的那些看，一个人在他"是两个或更多"的那一面上，看来是有友爱的；而且友爱到了过头处，样子就像人对自己的那一种。

所以他不是干净地搁下。他搁下一个名分，同时在旁边填了点东西。

有两件事在这儿粘着，得先分开。

他搁下的是名分，没搁下的是来源。一个人跟自己算不算得上有友爱，这个名分他放下了；而这一章开头那一句他照用不误，整章都靠它撑着：对邻人的那些友爱的表现，看来是从对自己的那些关系里过来的。

名分那一头另有一篇管：之七跟的是从正义那边往里移的那一路。这一篇跟的是来源这一头，方向相反，是从里往外走。

那么他说的对自己的那些关系，是什么样的关系。

不是随便谁跟自己相处的那种关系。原文写得很死：那几样标志，是对好人才成立的；其余的人，只在他们自以为是那样的人的那一面上成立。

好人是什么样子。他跟自己想到一处去，整个心都朝着同一批东西。他为自己想要好的东西，也真去做，而且是为着他自己；他要自己活着，要自己保全，尤其要保全那个用来想的部分。往下几行他还添一句：那个想的部分，看来最是每一个人自己。

还有一句短的：他不后悔。

坏人正相反，而且亚里士多德写得毫不留情。

坏人跟自己合不上：他们贪的是一样，想要的是另一样。比方不能自制的人，明明有他们自己认作好的东西，却挑了那些让人快活而其实有害的。

坏起来的人到处找人一块儿消磨白天，自己却躲着自己：一个人待着，他们会想起许多难受的事，还料着往后也是这样；跟别人在一块儿，就忘掉了。

他们也不跟自己同乐，不跟自己同苦。因为他们的心是内讧的：一头因为坏而在忍着不去碰什么，忍得难受；另一头正快活着；一头往这边拽，一头往那边拽，像要把人扯开。

所以他收得很硬：这样的人对自己都做不成朋友，因为他们身上没有一样可爱的。

这就是他那条路的起点：不是"人都跟自己有点关系"，是一种已经理顺了的自我关系。友爱的那些标志先在这一种关系里成立，然后往外，成为对邻人的友爱。

这边那条路上有一站，正好站在他这个起点的位置上。

那条路上有一站叫意义：不无理由地行动，把自己当作目的本身。这一站是一个人自己那一头的顶。再上去那一站是伦理：不只有我是目的。

而这两站中间，走这条路的人自己留下一句警告：这一站要是运作过了头，或者把自己关起来，那正是最精致的一种侵占；健康的这一站，是上一站的必要前提。

侵占这个词在那边有个准确的用法：一条路被另一条压着走，走不成它自己。而最精致的那一种，是被自己压着。

他说：坏人跟自己内讧，所以对别人也长不出友爱。这边说：自己那一头要是拧了或者闭上了，往上那一步就没有条件。两边在这里有同一种依赖的形状：往外那一步的条件，在自己那一头。

而两边的病理也挨得近。他写坏起来的人到处找人消磨白天，自己却躲着自己；这边写那一站运作过头或者把自己关起来。一头是躲开自己，一头是把自己关严实；两样都不是不够看重自己，两样都是那一头出了毛病。

只碰这一句，再往前推就不是同一件事了：他那一套里心分几部分，这边不接；这边那条路上一层一层的编号，他那边也没有。

而往外这两个字，两边走的不是同一种往外。

他把对邻人的友爱说成对自己那一种关系往外展开。外面那一份，形状是里面那一份的形状；朋友是另一个自己，所以对朋友的那一份跟对自己的那一份是同一种。里面那一头是来源。

这边把自己那一头只算作前提，不算作来源。从"把自己当目的"到"不只有我是目的"，这边登记的是一步有岔的路，而且明写着岔口那边是有人真住过的：把价值收归自己一个人的那一支，历史上实住。既然有岔，这一步就不是前一步长出来的；它是在那儿另下的一判。

一个是把同一种关系推出去，一个是在同一处另起一手。

这两条路在日子里分得出来。

一种常见的说法是：一个人先得跟自己过得去，才谈得上跟别人过得去。它说的是同一样东西往外长。

另一种说法不这么走。一个人跟自己过得去，完全可以过成一个谁也不需要的人。他自己那一头越顺，往外那一步越没有非发生不可的理由。那一步不是前一步的延长，它是另外一件事。

两种说法都不假。分歧不在哪一种对，在那一步是长出来的，还是做出来的。

而在他那一边，长出来这件事是有一根链子的，一环一环都写在纸上。

性格上的那些好是从习惯来的，连名字都是从"习"上稍稍偏出来一点。而习惯不是自己长的：立法的人靠让公民养成习惯把他们变好，每一个立法者要的都是这个，做不好的就是失手；好政体和坏政体的分别就在这一处。

到了第十卷他把人变好的那几条路一条一条过。天生那一条明摆着不由我们做主，那是某些神样的原因给真正有运气的人的；而讲道理和教不是对谁都管用，听的人那颗心得先被习惯耕过，像地得先能养得住种子。

往下他说得更死。从小得着一条正路这件事，没有那样的法养着就难。父亲的吩咐没有强制的力气，法有，因为法是从某种明智和心思来的道理。

这里得补一句，不补就把他写窄了。他没有说只剩下法这一条路。他明写：最好是城里有一份公共的照管；可公共的照管要是荒着，那就该由各人替自己的孩子和朋友出力，至少也该存着这个心。他还说，一个一个地教反倒更到家，就像看病：发烧的人一般该静养忌口，可某一个人也许不该；教拳的也不会给所有人排同一套打法。

可他接着把这条私路也送回了同一处。一个一个地教要教得最好，还得是懂那个普遍的人；而一个人想靠照管把别人变好，不管是多是少，都得试着成为会立法的那种人。

两条路合到一处：好人的自我关系从习惯来，习惯从法来，法从会立法的人来。私养这一条不是绕开了立法，是绕了一段以后还是走了进去。

这边没有这一根链子。

这边最靠近的一样叫涵育：替别人那条路做出不被侵占的条件，明写含着不去挡着和主动帮着两面。可它的效力被整条交给了第二批判，而且它说的是不去挡着别人长，不是怎样把一个人养成。

这不是这边漏掉了一格。这边这条路是一层一层往上走出来的，每一层记出口有几个，记担保从哪儿来，记岔在哪儿，一共六栏。至于那一层在一个具体的人身上实际怎么长起来，靠谁养，靠什么养，靠哪一套安排一年一年地养，逐层那张总表上没有开这一栏。

登记了站位，分支，担保和条件，不等于已经铺出一条养成的路。

这一层学手艺的人最清楚。

一个人手艺学得好，未必是因为他自己想学得好。也可能是他跟的那个师傅规矩严，铺子里的活按老法子排，做坏了要重做。等他自己带徒弟，传下去的还是那套规矩。

你问他这套规矩打哪儿来，他答得出上一辈。再往上问，就没人答得出了。

而亚里士多德不是没往上问。

第十卷最后，他问：接下来是不是该看看，一个人是打哪儿来的，又是怎么成得了会立法的人。他试了一条现成的路，跟办政事的人学，走不通：真能办事的那些人不写也不讲，连自己儿子都没教成。于是他把同一句话又问了一遍。

问完他说：前人把立法这件事留着没人查过，不如我们自己来查。

而他没有停在这一句上。接着他把要查的一条一条排了出来：先看前人说得好的那些部分，再从收集来的那些政体里看什么保得住一座城，什么毁得掉一座城，然后才判得出哪一种政体最好，该怎么摆，该用哪些法和哪些风俗。

《尼各马可伦理学》的最后一句是：那么我们就开讲吧。

所以这一笔债的形状要说准。不是他没写养成，不是他把问题往后挪了一站，也不是他站在尽头指着一块空地停笔。他把那条链子铺到头，铺到头才发现头上那一问自己答不了，于是他把那一问改写成了一张工单，当场开了工。

这跟上一本第九篇那一问是同一个回退：头一个肯认别人是个人的人是从哪儿来的；他认的时候没有人认过他，他是跟谁学的。只是那一篇是从外头往里问，这一篇是顺着他自己那条链子走到了头。

而上一本那三处，是明写空着不补的。

记下一个回退，和把它改成一张工单，不是同一件事。

After setting out the marks of friendship in Book IX, chapter 4, he poses a question and sets it aside himself:

Whether there is friendship between a person and themselves may be left aside for now.

That is the question he sets aside. But the very next sentence supplies half an answer:

From what has been said, there seems to be friendship insofar as a person is “two or more”; and friendship in its extreme form resembles the sort a person has toward themselves.

So he has not put it cleanly aside. He suspends a designation while filling in something beside it.

Two things are stuck together here. We must first separate them.

He suspends the designation, not the source. Whether a person’s relation to themselves qualifies as friendship is left aside. But the chapter’s opening sentence remains fully in use, carrying the entire chapter: the marks of friendship toward our neighbors seem to come from our relations to ourselves.

Another essay handles the designation: Essay 7 followed the inward movement from justice. This essay follows the source, in the opposite direction, from inside outward.

What, then, are these relations to oneself?

They are not just anybody’s way of getting along with themselves. The text is firm. These marks hold of the good person. Of other people they hold only insofar as those people think themselves to be that sort.

What is the good person like? They agree with themselves, and their whole soul desires the same things. They wish good things for themselves and actually do them, for their own sake. They want themselves to live and be preserved, especially the part that thinks. A few lines later he adds that the thinking part seems to be each person’s self most of all.

There is another short sentence: they do not regret.

Bad people are the opposite, and Aristotle writes about them without mercy.

Bad people cannot agree with themselves: they crave one thing and wish for another. The incontinent, for example, choose pleasant but harmful things instead of what they themselves recognize as good.

People who have become bad seek others everywhere to pass the day with, while avoiding themselves. Alone, they remember many painful things and expect more of the same; with others, they forget.

Nor do they share their own pleasure or pain. Their soul is in civil war. One part, because of its badness, painfully restrains itself from something while another enjoys it. One pulls this way, the other that, as though to tear the person apart.

So he closes firmly: such people cannot even be friends to themselves, because there is nothing lovable in them.

That is the starting point of his road. Not “everyone has some relation to themselves,” but a self-relation that has already been put in order. The marks of friendship first hold within this relation, then move outward as friendship toward others.

On this side’s road, one station stands just where his starting point does.

That station is called meaning: acting not without reason, treating oneself as an end in oneself. This is the highest station on the person’s own side. The next station up is ethics: I am not the only end.

Between those stations, however, the person laying out the road leaves a warning: if this station operates excessively or closes itself off, that is precisely the most refined kind of encroachment. A healthy form of this station is necessary for the one above.

Encroachment has a precise use there: one path is pressed into following another and cannot become itself. The most refined kind is being pressed down by oneself.

He says bad people are at war with themselves, so friendship for others cannot grow from them. This side says that if the self-directed station becomes twisted or shuts itself off, the next upward step lacks its conditions. The two sides have the same shape of dependence here: the conditions for going outward lie on one’s own side.

Their pathologies are close too. He writes of people who have become bad seeking company all day to avoid themselves; this side writes of the station operating excessively or closing itself off. One avoids the self, the other seals it shut. Neither is a failure to value oneself enough. Both are something going wrong at that end.

That sentence is where they touch. Push further and they are no longer the same thing. This side does not take over his divisions of the soul, and his side does not have the numbered levels on this road.

Nor do the two sides make the same movement outward.

He describes friendship for others as the outward extension of the relation to oneself. Its outer form is the inner form. A friend is another self, so the relation to a friend is of the same kind as the relation to oneself. The inward side is the source.

This side counts the inward station only as a prerequisite, not a source. The move from “treating myself as an end” to “I am not the only end” is registered as a road with a branch. It explicitly says that people have actually lived on the other branch: the branch that reserves value to oneself alone has been inhabited in history. Because there is a branch, the step does not simply grow out of the preceding one. It is another judgment made there.

One extends the same kind of relation; the other makes a fresh move at the same place.

These two roads can be distinguished in daily life.

One familiar saying is that a person must first get along with themselves before they can get along with others. It speaks of the same thing growing outward.

The other saying does not go that way. Someone at peace with themselves can perfectly well become a person who needs nobody else. The smoother their own side runs, the less reason there is why an outward step must occur. That step is not an extension of the preceding one. It is another thing.

Neither saying is false. Their difference is not which one is right, but whether that step grows or is made.

On his side, that growth has a chain, every link written on the page.

The excellences of character come from habit; even their name is a slight variation on the word for habit. And habit does not grow by itself. Legislators make citizens good by forming their habits. That is what every legislator aims at; those who do it badly fail. This is where a good constitution differs from a bad one.

In Book X he examines, one by one, the routes by which people become good. Nature is plainly not under our control; that comes through certain divine causes to the truly fortunate. Argument and teaching do not work on everyone either. The listener’s soul must first have been cultivated by habit, as soil must first be able to nourish the seed.

He goes on more firmly. It is hard to receive a right upbringing from childhood without laws of the right kind. A father’s instruction lacks coercive force; law has it, because law is an account proceeding from a kind of practical wisdom and intellect.

Something must be added here, or we will make him narrower than he is. He does not say law is the only remaining road. He writes explicitly that public care in the city is best. But if public care is neglected, individuals should work for their own children and friends, or at least intend to. Individual teaching, he adds, can be more exact, as in medicine. A feverish person generally needs rest and a restricted diet, but a particular person may not. A boxing instructor does not prescribe the same fighting style to everyone either.

Yet he then sends this private road back to the same place. To teach individuals best still requires someone who knows the universal. Anyone who wants to make others better through care, whether many or few, must try to become capable of legislation.

The two roads meet: the good person’s self-relation comes from habit, habit from law, and law from someone capable of legislating. Private formation does not bypass legislation. It takes a detour and enters it again.

This side has no such chain.

The closest thing on this side is called cultivation: making conditions in which another person’s path is not encroached upon. It explicitly includes both refraining from obstruction and actively helping. But its efficacy is handed over in full to the Second Critique. It speaks of not blocking another’s growth, not of how to form a person.

This is not a missing cell that this side forgot. The road here is worked out level by level. At each level it records the number of exits, where the guarantee comes from, and where the branches are, in six columns altogether. How that level actually grows in a particular person, who cultivates it, by what means, and through which arrangements year after year: the level-by-level table has no column for that.

Recording positions, branches, guarantees, and conditions does not mean that a road of formation has already been laid.

People learning a craft know this layer best.

A person may learn a craft well without having particularly wanted to. Perhaps the master was strict, the workshop arranged its work by old rules, and anything badly made had to be made again. When that person later takes apprentices, the same rules are passed down.

Ask where the rules came from, and they can name the previous generation. Ask further back, and nobody can answer.

But Aristotle did ask further back.

At the end of Book X he asks: should we next examine where a person comes from, and how they become capable of legislation? He tries an available route, learning from people who conduct public affairs, but it does not work. Those who can actually do it neither write nor teach, and have not even succeeded in teaching their own sons. So he asks the same question again.

Having asked, he says: our predecessors have left legislation unexamined. Let us examine it ourselves.

He does not stop at that sentence. He immediately lists what must be examined. First the valuable parts of what predecessors have said; then, from the collected constitutions, what preserves a city and what destroys one. Only then can we judge which constitution is best, how it should be arranged, and which laws and customs it should use.

The last sentence of the Nicomachean Ethics is: let us begin, then.

So the shape of this debt must be stated precisely. It is not that he failed to write about formation, postponed the question one station further, or reached the end, pointed at an empty lot, and laid down his pen. He lays the chain all the way out. Only at its end does he discover that he himself cannot answer the question at its head. So he turns the question into a work order and starts on it there and then.

This is the same regress as the question in Essay 9 of the previous book: where did the first person willing to recognize another as a person come from? When that person recognized another, nobody had recognized them. Who taught them? Only there the question moved from outside inward, whereas here it follows his own chain to its end.

And the three places in the previous book are explicitly left blank, without being filled.

Recording a regress and turning it into a work order are not the same thing.
