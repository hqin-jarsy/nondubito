自足要算到哪儿为止

How Far Does Self-Sufficiency Extend?

Han Qin (秦汉)

全书第二章，他就把话说到了底。

他说，我们做的事都有个所为。要是每一件都是为了别的什么，为了的那个又是为了另一个，一路往后没有尽头，那么欲求就是空的，是白费的。所以总得有一件东西，是我们为它自身而要的，别的都为着它；而那一件，明摆着就是好，就是最好。

这一段值得先站住看一眼，因为它跟上一本里的东西不是一路货。

《理想国》里那个太阳是摆在那儿的，苏格拉底请你抬头看。这一段不请你看什么。它是算出来的：先假定没有那样一件东西，然后指出这个假定要付的价钱。

价钱他说得很白：欲求是空的，是白费的。

这里得说准，因为很容易把他说过头。

他不是说每一件事本身都不值什么。一件事可以自己就值得要，而同时还为着另一件。他后面举的四样正是这种：荣誉，快乐，理智，还有每一样德性。

他说的是另一件事。你问为什么要这个，答案往后指；再问那一件，答案又往后指。要是这一路问下去，没有哪一件是只为它自身而要的，那么这条追问就没有一个不再往后的落点。

借的不是每一分，是最后那一下。

这个算法很硬，硬在它不劝人。它没有说你应该有个人生目标。它说的是：要么这一路上有一件不再为着别的，要么这一整条要它的劲头没有一个无条件的所为。

第七章他回来把那件东西定下来。

他先定"最终"，而且是分三级定的。因它自身被追求的，比为着别的被追求的更最终一级。从不为着别的被选的，又比那些既为自身也为着它被选的更最终一级。最终到底的那一个，是永远因它自身被选，从不为着别的。

然后他举了四样对照：荣誉，快乐，理智，还有每一样德性。这四样我们确实是为它们自身选的，就算什么也不落着，我们还是会要它们。可我们同时也为了幸福才选它们，指望靠这几样过得好。

反过来那一句是硬的：幸福，没有谁为了这几样去选它，也不为着别的什么去选它。

末端就这么定住了。

接着他用另一条路再走一遍，走的是自足。

自足这个词容易被读小，读成一个人关起门来什么都不缺。他先把这层意思挡掉了，而且挡得很宽：所谓自足，不是说对一个人自己够了，不是说过一份独自的日子；是连父母，连儿女，连妻子，连朋友和同城的人一起算在内，因为人生来就是过群居日子的。

这一句写得实在。他没有把幸福缩成一个人心里的事。

可就在这一句的下一口气里，出事了。

他自己说：这些人得取一个界。

为什么得取。他接着给理由：往父母的上头推，往子孙的下头推，再往朋友的朋友推，这么推下去就没有尽头了。

那个"没有尽头"，用的是第二章那两个字。

字是一样的，动词也是同一个，只是这一回位置换了个个儿。这两个字在整本书里出现三回：第二章这一回，讲所为的后退；第七章这一回，讲人的外推；还有第三卷一回，讲一个人拿主意，要是一直往下想，那也没有尽头。

三回里头，只有第七章这一回，他写下了那句话。

那句话是：这个改天再看。

得说准这里发生了什么。

他不是没辙了。他说的是这些人得取一个界，而且把得取界的理由一并给了。他没有说这个界取不到，也没有说他那个末端在这儿使不上劲。他说的是：这一处不在这儿办。

写成他在这儿失了手，是替他下了一个他没下的判。

可要紧的不在他行不行。要紧的是：这两回后退，问的不是同一件事。

第一回问的是"为了什么"。这件事为了那件，那件为了另一件。一个自身即目的的末端能收住它，因为整条链上每一环问的都是同一个问题，末端只要能对这个问题说一句"这个不为什么"，链就有了底。

第二回问的不是"为了什么"，是"算不算进来"。父母算，儿女算，妻子算，朋友和同城的人算。那么父母的父母呢，朋友的朋友呢。这一串不是一条为了什么的链，是一张名单。

而一个自身即目的的东西，怎么就定得下一张名单，这一步他没有走。他标出来说这儿得有个界，然后写下改天再看。

这件事日常里也照样发生，而且发生在同一处。

小孩问为什么要上学，答说为了考上学校；为什么要考上学校，答说为了找份合意的活干；为什么要找份合意的活干。问到某一处，大人多半会说一句"这个不为什么，人活着就图这个"。说得出说不出另讲，可那个位置是明摆着的。

家里办喜事，请客的名单是另一回事。父母当然请，儿女当然请。堂表亲请到第几层。多年不来往的那一个请不请。朋友的朋友，去年在你家吃过一顿饭的那一位，请不请。

这张名单上看不出一处天然的断口。可名单总得定下来，因为酒席就那么些桌。

定它的不是"人活着图什么"。你把人生的道理想得再透，那张名单也不会自己长出一条边来。

那句"最后那一下"，这边也是这么算的。

两边认下的是同一句：一样东西的分量要是全从后面那一件来，后面没有尽头，它就真的空。

这句话这边不打折。这边也没有一句现成的宽心话可以躲进去，就是"反正总归做了点什么"那一句。做了点什么，如果全是往后指的，那就还是没有。

他给的这个代价，这边接着。

而下一句就岔了：一次具体的取得，是不是也非得这么往后指。

他的办法是给这一路问下去安一个不再往后的落点。有了那一件不再为着别的，整条路才有一个无条件的所为，整个人的日子才有一个形。

这边的办法是把分量放回每一次里头。

这边每一回取走的东西，不往后指。它是当场生出来的：原先没有的那个分辨，这一回真的有了，站不站得住可以再问，可它已经发生这一点，不靠远处那一件担保。

而那个远处的标准，从来没有被给出过。

所以这边连"离得更近"这句话都不说。要说离得近，得有一段可以量的距离；要有距离，得有两头都摆在那儿。而那一头从来没摆出来过。它不是立在远处可以走近一点的一个标的，它是"每一次收进来的都短于它"这件事的名字。

《第一批判》里有一句话，正对着第二章那个算法："既然到不了，何必走"这个读法，错在把无终点当成了无所得。

这句话不是冲着亚里士多德写的。可它答的正是那个价钱。他说没有末端就一分不剩；这边说，一分不剩这个结论，是从"分量都是借的"那个前提来的，而这边不认那个前提。

这句话只认下描述的那一半：往下走是实得，不是错觉。往下走该不该走，这边不裁；那是各人自己立的法，归另一门功课去问。

这边的落点也不是被划掉的。

不是划掉了，然后咬牙说没有它也行。是这边这一路上，没有哪一处需要它来兜底。往下能走是一种本事，不是一份义务：没有哪一处缺口逼着你再往下动，也没有哪个落点在前头拽着你。能走和能停是同一枚钱的两面，合起来才是那块地方。

不过两边要的资格不一样，这一处得分清。他要的是一个无条件的最终：不再为着任何别的。这边给的是一次一次的局部的实得：这一回真有所得，而它不声称自己是那个最终。局部的实得够不够让一段劲头不落空，是这一篇要问的；它够不够格升上去当一辈子的最高善，这边根本没有提这个要求。

回到那张名单。

这边说主体可停：一件事往下走，中间得有一处，他按一下就走不动。

可名单不是靠这个定下来的。

"我可以喊停"和"这件事该把谁算进来"，是两个问题。酒席上你确实可以不去，这是可停；可谁该在名单上，跟你去不去没有关系。一个人过得好不好，一家人算不算安顿，这一类判断本来就是以过日子为条件的；它该把关系人算到哪一层，可停结算不出来。

而他至少把这一处标出来了。他说这些人得取一个界，说了得取的理由，说了这儿不办。

这一篇从这边带来的那几条里，没有一条替这张名单取界。可停不取，实得不取，那个不设的落点更不取。别处有没有另外的东西可用，这一篇不知道，也不替它说话。

最后回到第七章那一口气。

他说这些人得取一个界，说往上往下推下去没有尽头，说这个改天再看。

紧接着的下一句，他把自足定了下来：所谓自足，是这样一样东西，它单摆着就使一份日子值得过，而且什么也不缺。

再往下，他补了一句防着人抬杠的。他说幸福是一切里头最可取的，而且不是跟别的好东西摆在一处数出来的那种最可取；因为要是摆在一处数，那显然再添上一样最小的好就比它更可取了，添进去的那一样把好加多了一点，而好东西里头多的那一份总是更可取。所以幸福不进那个清单。

这一步走得很干净。他把自己那个末端从"最大的一样好"里拆了出来，免得有人拿加法把它比下去。

再往下一行，那个定义合成了一句：幸福看来是最终的，而且是自足的，它是我们所做的事情的那个所为。

他就在这一口气里，把最终和自足合成了一个定义。

而"自足"这两个字里头，有一处界他自己标着，还没有取。

By the second chapter of the whole book, he has taken the question all the way down.

Everything we do, he says, is for something. If each thing is for something else, and that in turn for another, with no end to the succession, desire will be empty and futile. There must therefore be something we want for itself, with everything else wanted for its sake. And that, plainly, is the good, the best.

It is worth standing still for a moment here, because this is not the same sort of thing we found in the previous book.

In the Republic, the sun is set before us and Socrates asks us to look up. This passage does not ask us to look at anything. It works something out: first suppose there is no such thing, then show what that supposition costs.

He states the cost plainly: desire is empty and futile.

We need to be exact here. It is easy to make him say too much.

He is not saying that each thing is worthless in itself. Something can be worth wanting for itself and also be wanted for something further. The four examples he later gives are exactly like this: honor, pleasure, intellect, and every virtue.

His point is different. Ask why you want this, and the answer points onward. Ask about the next thing, and the answer points onward again. If nothing along the way is wanted solely for itself, the questioning has nowhere to land that does not point further still.

It is not every bit of worth that is borrowed. It is the final grounding.

This argument is forceful because it does not try to persuade you. It does not say you ought to have a goal in life. It says: either something on this road is no longer for anything else, or the whole drive to want it has no unconditional purpose.

In chapter 7 he returns to identify that thing.

First he defines “final,” in three steps. What is pursued for itself is more final than what is pursued for something else. What is never chosen for anything else is more final again than what is chosen both for itself and for something further. What is final without qualification is always chosen for itself and never for anything else.

Then he gives four comparisons: honor, pleasure, intellect, and every virtue. We do indeed choose these for themselves. Even if nothing came of them, we would still want them. Yet we also choose them for happiness, expecting through them to live well.

The reverse claim is firm: no one chooses happiness for the sake of these, or for anything else.

That secures the endpoint.

Next he takes another route through the question: self-sufficiency.

It is easy to read that word too narrowly, as a person shutting the door and lacking nothing. He rules that meaning out first, and very broadly. Self-sufficiency does not mean enough for one person alone, living a solitary life. It includes parents, children, wife, friends, and fellow citizens, because human beings are by nature creatures who live together.

That is a concrete sentence. He has not shrunk happiness into something inside one person’s head.

But in the very next breath, something happens.

He says himself: a boundary must be set to these people.

Why? He supplies the reason. Extend upward through ancestors, downward through descendants, and outward through friends of friends, and the extension has no end.

That “no end” uses the same words as chapter 2.

The words are the same, and so is the verb; this time only their position is reversed. The expression appears three times in the book: here in chapter 2 for the regress of purposes, in chapter 7 for the extension of people, and once in Book III for a person deliberating, whose thinking would likewise have no end if it kept going.

Of those three occasions, only the one in chapter 7 adds this sentence.

The sentence is: we will examine this another time.

We must be exact about what has happened.

He has not run out of answers. He says a boundary must be set, and gives the reason it must be set. He does not say it cannot be set, nor that his endpoint is powerless here. He says this matter will not be dealt with here.

To write that he failed here would pass a verdict he did not pass.

But whether he can do it is not the important point. The point is that these two regressions do not ask the same question.

The first asks “for what?” This thing is for that, and that for another. An endpoint that is an end in itself can close the chain, because every link asks the same question. If the endpoint can answer “this is for nothing further,” the chain has a bottom.

The second asks not “for what?” but “does this person count?” Parents count, children count, a wife counts, friends and fellow citizens count. Then what about parents’ parents, or friends’ friends? This sequence is not a chain of purposes. It is a list of names.

How does something that is an end in itself settle a list of names? He has not taken that step. He marks the need for a boundary, then writes: another time.

This happens in daily life too, at precisely the same place.

A child asks why they must go to school. To get into a school, comes the answer. Why get into a school? To find work you like. Why find work you like? At some point the adult will probably say, “There is no further reason; this is what life is for.” Whether they can say it satisfactorily is another matter, but the place for that answer is clear.

The guest list for a family celebration is another matter. Of course you invite parents and children. How far out among the cousins do you go? Do you invite the one you have not spoken to for years? What about the friend of a friend who ate at your house last year?

There is no natural break visible on that list. Yet the list has to be settled, because there are only so many tables at the banquet.

“What life is for” does not settle it. However thoroughly you think through the meaning of life, the guest list will not grow a boundary of its own.

This side works out that “final grounding” in the same way.

Both accept the same claim: if a thing gets all its weight from what comes after it, and what comes after has no end, then it really is empty.

This side makes no discount on that claim. Nor does it have a ready comfort to hide in: “At least something got done.” If everything that got done merely points onward, there is still nothing there.

This side accepts the cost he names.

The next sentence is where they part: must a particular gain also point onward in that way?

His method gives the questioning somewhere to land that no longer points further. Only with something that is not for anything else does the whole road have an unconditional purpose, and a person’s whole life a shape.

The method on this side is to put the weight back into each occasion.

What is gained on each occasion does not point onward. It arises there and then. A distinction that did not exist before really exists now. Whether it holds can be questioned again, but the fact that it has happened does not need a guarantee from something far away.

And that far-off standard has never been given.

So this side does not even say “closer.” To be closer, there must be a measurable distance; for a distance, both ends must be set out. The far end has never been set out. It is not a target standing in the distance that we can approach. It is the name for the fact that whatever is gathered in on each occasion falls short of it.

There is a sentence in the First Critique that faces chapter 2’s argument directly: the reading “If we cannot arrive, why go?” mistakes having no endpoint for gaining nothing.

That sentence was not written against Aristotle. But it answers precisely the cost he names. He says that without an endpoint nothing remains; this side says that “nothing remains” follows from the premise that all weight is borrowed, and this side does not accept that premise.

The sentence accepts only the descriptive half: going on brings real gains, not an illusion. Whether one ought to go on is not decided here. That belongs to the law each person lays down for themselves, a question for another inquiry.

Nor has the landing point on this side been crossed out.

It is not that the endpoint was removed and we now grit our teeth and say we can manage without it. Rather, nothing on this road requires it as a backstop. Being able to go on is an ability, not an obligation. No gap compels you to move further, and no destination ahead pulls you toward it. Being able to go and being able to stop are two sides of the same coin. Only together do they make up that space.

But the two sides seek different credentials, and we must keep this clear. He seeks an unconditional final end, no longer for anything else. This side offers repeated local gains: something was really gained this time, without claiming to be that final end. Whether a local gain is enough to keep an effort from coming to nothing is this essay’s question. Whether it qualifies for promotion to the highest good of an entire life is a demand this side never makes.

Back to the list of names.

This side says the subject must be able to stop: as something proceeds, there must be a point at which the subject can press stop and it goes no further.

But that does not settle the list.

“I can call a halt” and “who should count in this matter?” are two different questions. You can indeed decline to attend the banquet; that is being able to stop. But who belongs on the list has nothing to do with whether you attend. Judgments about whether a person is living well, or a family is settled, are already conditional on living a life. How far out they should count the people concerned cannot be settled by the ability to stop.

He, at least, has marked this place. He says these people need a boundary, says why, and says he will not settle it here.

Not one of the claims brought from this side into this essay sets a boundary to that list. The ability to stop does not, real gains do not, and the endpoint that is not posited certainly does not. Whether something elsewhere might help is unknown to this essay, and it will not speak for it.

Finally, back to that single breath in chapter 7.

He says the people need a boundary, that extending upward and downward has no end, and that this will be examined another time.

In the very next sentence he defines self-sufficiency: it is something that, taken by itself, makes a life worth living and lacking in nothing.

Then he adds a sentence to head off an objection. Happiness is the most choiceworthy of all, he says, but not in the sense of being counted alongside other good things. If it were counted with them, plainly adding even the smallest good would make it more choiceworthy. The addition would increase the good a little, and of good things the greater amount is always more choiceworthy. So happiness does not enter that list.

This is a clean move. He separates his endpoint from “the largest good thing,” so no one can use addition to outbid it.

One line later, the definition comes together: happiness appears to be final and self-sufficient; it is the end for which we act.

In this single breath he combines finality and self-sufficiency into one definition.

And inside “self-sufficiency” there remains a boundary he has marked himself, but has not yet drawn.
