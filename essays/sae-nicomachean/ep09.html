明知而故犯

Knowing Better, Doing Otherwise

Han Qin (秦汉)

第七卷第二章一开口，就是一道他自己标出来的难处：

有人会问：一个人想得明明是对的，怎么还会不能自制。

难在对面站着谁。

这一段是两层，不是一层。头一层是"有人说"：一个人身上真有那门知识，还能有别的东西压过它，把它像个奴隶一样拖着走，这太怪了；苏格拉底当年就是这么看的。第二层才是他自己转述苏格拉底本人：苏格拉底整个跟这个说法作对，认为根本没有不能自制这回事；没有人是在心里那样认着的时候还偏要做违背最好的那件事，之所以那样做，是出于不知道。

接下来那一句是整章的分水岭。而且它是一整句，中间只有一个逗号，不许拆成两句读：

这套道理跟摆在眼前的事明摆着不合；而既然该就这件事查一查，那就要问，要是出于不知道，这个不知道是哪一种样子的不知道。

这一句要分两半读。

前半是一记重手。在他自己那套办法里，一套道理跟摆在眼前的事对不上，就是这套道理照原样立不住。他开卷讲办法的时候写得清清楚楚：疑难走完，那些话还站着，才算证够了。所以他确实判了：苏格拉底那句"根本没有不能自制这回事"，照原样不能留。

后半是他没有停在这一记上。不再问知不知道，改问那个不知道长什么样。

判了，然后改问法。最后救回来的是一层，不是整套。

第三章他就照这个问法往下拆，一连拆了四道。

第一道：知道这个词本来就有两种用法。一个人有那门知识而没在用它，和一个人正在用它，两个都叫知道。所以事情有分别：一个人做那件不该做的事，是拿着那门知识而没在照看它，还是正照看着。

第二道：前提有两种。两个都拿在手里也不妨碍一个人做出违背知识的事，只要他用的是那个普遍的，不是那个一件一件的。因为做出来的事都是一件一件的。

第三道：还有一种拿着的方式。有而不用的时候，那个状态本身就不一样，一个人可以又算拿着又算没拿着，比方睡着的那个，疯了的那个，喝醉了的那个。而正在那种情绪里的人，样子正是这样。

第四道最狠，也最好记。

说得出那些从知识来的话，这不算凭据。因为正在那种情绪里的人也背得出证明，也背得出恩培多克勒的诗句；刚学会的人也把话一句一句串得下来，可他们还不知道，那得长到一块儿去，长要时间。所以不能自制的人说话，得照台上演员那样看。

这一拆跟前三拆不一样，要留神。前三拆里那个人是持有着的，只是没在照看，或者只用了普遍那一半。这一拆里，那个人还没有持有：话串得下来，可它还没长到一块儿去。

四道拆完，他回头结账：苏格拉底要的那一层看来是成立的。因为那个状态发生的时候，在场的不是那门正经算得上知识的东西，被拖着走的也不是它，是管感觉的那一头。

还有一件他当场交了出去。那个人怎么从那个状态里出来，他说这跟喝醉的和睡着的是同一笔账，不是这一处特有的，得去听研究自然的人怎么说。

他要的只有一件：动手那一刻，那个知是怎么个在场法。

这一道跟上一本第五篇挨着，可两篇动的不是同一样东西。

柏拉图那一处，是让一个从港口上来的人当场作证：一个人身上确实有好几样各要各的，而且会当着他自己的面分出胜负。他要证的是人里头不止一个。

亚里士多德这一处不碰人里头有几样。他碰的是"知道"这个词。同一个词，可以指手里拿着，也可以指正在用；可以指那个普遍的到了手，也可以指那个一件一件的到了手；还可以指嘴里说得下来而心里还没长到一块儿。一个把人拆开，一个把词拆开。

而两处的用处也不一样。人拆开了，能说明打架怎么发生；词拆开了，能说明为什么问"他到底知不知道"问不出东西来。

他那一句里有"凭据"两个字。把这两个字拎出来，就到了这边的地界。

《第一批判》审幻相那一章，给冒领定了一个很干脆的判法：一项主张在使用中出示的凭证，只要有一项不在它经检验和复核所获准的条目里，就算冒领。说短一点，出示的越出了持有的。

而紧跟着的那一句，管的正是本篇这一道：这个判定是回溯的，不问动机的。一样东西呈现出来的时候本来就默认不带地址，所以冒领多半没有人在行骗；被诊断的是呈现的样子，不是呈现的人存的什么心。

这两句是同一种凭据形状：出示得全，不担保这一刻以获准的方式持有着。

真正相接的是第四拆那一层，不是第一拆。第一拆里那个人持有着，出示没有越出持有，越出的是使用；第四拆里那个人话串得下来而还没长到一块儿，出示才真的越出了持有。演员嘴里的话一个字不差，出示的是那一整套，手上持有的是另一回事。

而亚里士多德没有说那个人在骗人。他问的只是这句话在这一刻是以哪一种方式在场的。

同一章还写着一条纪律，本篇非把它抄进来不可：诊断点名栏位，不点名人。一纸合格的诊断写的是哪一种凭证被出示了，账上哪一栏没有这一条，出处在哪儿；它不写的是出示的人存的什么心，该当何罪。理由写在同一处：定罪会把人逼得藏起来，藏起来的那些说法就免掉了对账，而免掉对账正是那条越滚越大的反馈要的条件之一。用猎巫的方式追问，有反过来把幻相养大的风险。

这一条在办公室里验得着。

交上来的东西出了错，会上有两种问法。一种是问这是谁做的，他当时怎么想的。另一种是问这一条的依据在哪儿，出处填了没有。

第一种问完，下一回错的东西不会变少，只会藏得深一点。第二种问完，纸上多一栏。

他没有停在栏位上。

他分完那几种在场的方式，是要回过身去给那个人一个说法。最后那一句他给足了：那一刻在场的是管感觉的那一头，不是那门正经的知识。这是一句关于那个人当时身上出了什么事的话。

这边分完就停。诊断的产出不是把那条东西划掉，是放回它自己的地址：已经挣着的一分不没收，成色由复核那边另算。而下一句写死了不许往前走：点栏位，不点人。

一个要说清那一刻他身上出了什么事，一个明写只到栏位为止。

这一层在日常里的一个样子，是复盘。

一件事办砸了，坐下来复盘。当时该怎么做，说得出一条不落的时候不少见。说得清楚，说得有条理，连别人为什么也会做错都说得出来。

而当时他确实没那么做。

这时候要是问一句"你到底知不知道"，这一问当场就废了。他知道，眼前这一堆话就是证据；他不知道，事情办成那样也是证据。两个答案各有各的证据，所以这一问分不出事情来。

亚里士多德换掉的正是这一问。他不问知不知道，他问那一刻那句话是怎么在场的：是拿着而没在照看，还是那个普遍的在手上而那个一件一件的不在，还是话串得下来而还没长到一块儿去。

他那一头有一格，这边找不出对应的位置。

这边给每一笔判定都配一个四栏的地址：这一判管的是哪个对象，站在哪一层，走的是哪一支，靠着哪几条设下来的东西。条件和依赖另有登记。地址是这边最细的一样活计，越界不越界全看它。

可地址记的是这一判用上了什么。

他要的那一样不在这上头。他要的是这一判本来够得着而这一次没有用上的那些。够得着而没用上，这边的地址上没有这一栏。

这边离得最近的一条叫冒领今站：拿一个旧站上做出来的说法，不经重审就对今天发判。那一条管的是拿出来了而拿错了。该拿出来而没有拿，这边没有对应的一条。

有人会说，把这一栏添上不就完了。

不是添一栏的事。地址是一次判定留下的记录，它写得下的只有这一次实际经手的东西。够得着而没有伸手的那些压根没有经手过，所以它们进不了这一栏。不是这一栏太窄，是它们不在这一次里。

要记它们，得另立一张单子：这个人此刻手边摆着哪些东西。这张单子不是没法立。它可以限定到某一个人，某一刻，某一类事情，而且事后可以复盘着重建。

可它自己也是一次作业。它得有自己的地址，它也只记它自己经手过的，它照样会漏。所以这不是一句"补上就完了"，也不是一句"永远补不上"。是这边现在手上没有这张单子；而它一旦立起来，会带着跟地址同一种的短缺。

而这一格不是可有可无的。他那一整套答案就架在这一格上：两个前提都在手里，用的只有一个。

这边另有一条写得很硬：账不因遗忘而销。一个再没有人过问的问题，仍然在账上。

这一句管的是它没有消失。

它没有说，动手那一刻，人伸不伸得到它。

Book VII, chapter 2, opens with a difficulty he marks out himself:

Someone might ask: how can a person lack self-control when his thinking is right?

The difficulty lies in who stands on the other side.

This passage has two layers, not one. The first is “some say”: it would be strange if a person genuinely possessed knowledge, yet something else could overpower it and drag it about like a slave. This was how Socrates saw it. Only in the second layer does he report Socrates himself: Socrates opposed the whole account, holding that there is no such thing as lack of self-control. Nobody, while believing as he does, deliberately acts against what is best. When someone does so, it is out of ignorance.

The next sentence is the chapter's watershed. And it is one whole sentence, with only a comma in the middle. It must not be read as two:

This account plainly conflicts with what is before our eyes, and since the matter should be investigated we must ask what sort of ignorance it is if ignorance is the cause.

This sentence needs to be read in two halves.

The first half strikes hard. By his own method, if an account does not fit what is before our eyes, it cannot stand as it is. At the opening of the book he states the method clearly: only when the difficulties have been worked through and those claims still stand has enough been proved. So he really has ruled that Socrates' “there is no such thing as lack of self-control” cannot remain as it stands.

The second half shows that he does not stop at that blow. He stops asking whether the person knows, and asks instead what that ignorance looks like.

He gives his judgment, then changes the question. What is eventually rescued is one layer, not the whole account.

In chapter 3 he follows this new question through, making four successive distinctions.

The first: “knowing” already has two uses. A person who possesses knowledge without using it, and a person using it, are both said to know. So there is a difference between doing what one ought not while possessing the knowledge but not attending to it, and doing so while attending to it.

The second: there are two kinds of premise. Having both in hand does not prevent someone from acting against knowledge, provided he uses the universal premise rather than the particular one. For actions are always particular.

The third: there is another way of possessing it. When one has knowledge without using it, the state itself can differ. A person may count as both possessing and not possessing it, as with someone asleep, mad, or drunk. People in the grip of such passions are in just this condition.

The fourth is the sharpest, and easiest to remember.

Being able to say the words that come from knowledge is no evidence. For people in the grip of such passions can recite proofs and verses of Empedocles. Those who have just learned can string the words together too, but they do not yet know: it has to grow into them, and growth takes time. So the speech of someone lacking self-control must be regarded as one regards an actor's speech onstage.

This distinction differs from the first three, and needs care. In those three, the person possesses the knowledge, but is not attending to it, or uses only its universal half. Here the person does not yet possess it: the words can be strung together, but they have not yet grown into one whole.

After the four distinctions he returns to settle the account: the layer Socrates wanted seems to hold. For when that condition occurs, what is present is not knowledge in the proper sense, nor is that what is dragged about. It is the perceptual side.

There is something else he hands elsewhere on the spot. How a person emerges from that condition, he says, belongs to the same account as drunkenness and sleep, not to this case alone. We must listen to those who study nature.

He wants only one thing: in what way that knowing was present at the moment of action.

This question sits beside essay five in the previous book, but the two essays work on different things.

In Plato's passage, a man coming up from the harbor is called to testify on the spot: a person really contains several things, each wanting its own, and they can win or lose in full view of the person himself. Plato wants to show that there is more than one thing inside a person.

Aristotle's passage does not touch how many things are inside a person. It touches the word “knowing.” The same word can mean having something in hand or using it; having the universal or having the particular; even being able to say the words without their having grown together within. One divides the person; the other divides the word.

The uses differ too. Dividing the person explains how the conflict occurs. Dividing the word explains why “does he know or not?” gets us nowhere.

His sentence contains the word “evidence.” Pick that word out, and we reach this side's territory.

The chapter examining illusion in the First Critique gives a direct test for a false claim to authority: if even one warrant a claim presents in use is absent from the entries authorized for it through examination and review, it is a false claim to authority. In short, what is presented exceeds what is held.

And the sentence immediately after it concerns exactly this essay's question: this determination is retrospective and does not inquire into motives. Something, on appearing, is by default presented without an address, so in most false claims to authority nobody is deceiving anyone. The diagnosis concerns how something is presented, not what its presenter intends.

The two statements share the same evidential shape: presenting the whole thing does not guarantee that it is held in an authorized way at this moment.

The actual connection is with the fourth distinction, not the first. In the first the person possesses the knowledge: presentation does not exceed possession; it exceeds use. In the fourth the person can string the words together, but they have not yet grown into one whole. Only here does presentation truly exceed possession. The words in the actor's mouth are faultless. What is presented is the whole account; what is held is another matter.

And Aristotle has not said that the person is lying. He asks only in what way this statement is present at this moment.

The same chapter contains a discipline this essay must copy here: diagnosis names the field, not the person. A proper diagnosis states what kind of warrant was presented, which field in the account lacks that entry, and where its source lies. It does not state the presenter's intentions or what offense he deserves to be convicted of. The reason is given in the same place: conviction drives people into hiding; the claims hidden there escape checking against the account; and escape from checking is one of the conditions that the expanding feedback needs. Questioning in the manner of a witch hunt risks feeding illusion in return.

This can be tested in an office.

Something submitted contains an error. At the meeting there are two ways to ask about it. One asks who did it and what he was thinking. The other asks where the grounds for this entry are and whether its source has been filled in.

After the first, next time there will not be fewer errors, only better-hidden ones. After the second, there is an extra field on the page.

He does not stop at the field.

Having distinguished those ways of being present, he means to turn back and give an account of the person. His last sentence supplies it fully: what was present at that moment was the perceptual side, not knowledge in the proper sense. This is a statement about what was happening in the person at the time.

This side stops after making the distinctions. Diagnosis does not produce the deletion of the entry, but restores it to its own address. Nothing it has earned is confiscated; its quality is assessed separately by review. And the next sentence fixes a limit beyond which one must not go: name the field, not the person.

One wants to explain what happened in the person at that moment; the other explicitly stops at the field.

An everyday form of this layer is the retrospective review.

Something has gone wrong, and people sit down to review it. It is not uncommon for someone to be able to state, without missing a thing, what should have been done at the time. Clearly, coherently, even explaining why others would have made the same mistake.

Yet at the time he really did not do it that way.

Ask “did you know or not?” here, and the question fails at once. He knew: all these words before us are evidence. He did not know: the way things turned out is evidence too. Both answers have evidence of their own, so this question cannot distinguish what happened.

That is precisely the question Aristotle replaces. He asks not whether the person knew, but how the statement was present at that moment: held but unattended to; the universal in hand but not the particular; or words that could be strung together but had not yet grown into one whole.

His side has a field for which this side has no corresponding place.

This side gives every judgment a four-field address: which object the judgment concerns, at which level it stands, along which branch it proceeds, and on which posited commitments it depends. Conditions and dependencies have separate registers. The address is this side's most fine-grained work; whether a boundary is crossed depends entirely on it.

But the address records what this judgment used.

What he wants is not there. He wants the things this judgment could have reached but did not use on this occasion. Within reach but unused: there is no such field in this side's address.

The nearest claim this side has is called falsely claiming the present standpoint: taking an account made at an earlier standpoint and passing judgment on today without re-examination. That concerns something brought out and used wrongly. For what ought to have been brought out but was not, this side has no corresponding claim.

Someone will say: why not just add the field?

It is not a matter of adding a field. An address is the record left by a particular judgment. It can record only what that judgment actually handled. The things within reach toward which no hand was extended were never handled at all, so they cannot enter the field. It is not that the field is too narrow. They are not part of this occasion.

To record them would require a separate list: what this person has at hand at this moment. Such a list is not impossible to make. It can be limited to one person, one moment, one kind of matter, and reconstructed afterward in a review.

But it too is a piece of work. It needs its own address, records only what it has itself handled, and will miss things too. So this is neither “just add it and we are done” nor “it can never be added.” This side does not currently have that list; and once made, it will carry the same kind of shortfall as the address.

And this field is not optional. His whole answer rests on it: both premises are in hand, but only one is used.

This side has another firm statement: forgetting does not cancel the account. A question nobody asks about anymore remains on the account.

That statement concerns its not having disappeared.

It does not say whether, at the moment of action, the person can reach it.
