Essay 06 of 13

石头扔出去了

The Stone Has Left the Hand

Han Qin (秦汉)

第三卷第五章，他先把一条立住了。

凡是做在我们手上的事，不做也在我们手上；凡是不做在我们手上的，做也在我们手上。所以要是做那件漂亮事在我们手上，不做那件难看事也就在我们手上。既然做漂亮事和做难看事都在我们手上，而这正是做好人和做坏人这回事，那么做个正派人还是做个下作人，就在我们手上。

立完他没有停，他把不认这一条的代价当场开了出来。

他说：要不然，刚才这些话就得推翻，就得说人不是自己行为的开头，不是自己行为的生父，像他生自己的孩子那样。

这不是一句吓唬话。他没有说"你得承认人不自由"，他说的是一个具体的代价：你要否掉前面那一串，就得连人是自己行为的开头这一条一起否掉，而他给这一条配的比方是生孩子。开头这个词在他那儿两处通用：孩子从你这儿出来，行为也是。

然后他叫了一个证人，而这个证人不是哲学家。

立法的人是这么办的。他们罚做坏事的，除非那人是被逼的，或者是出于一种不该由他负责的不知道；做好事的他们给荣誉。凡是既不在我们手上又不出于自愿的事，没有人劝你去做；劝一个人别怕热别喊饿是白费的，热该怎么样还怎么样。

而底下这一条，是这一段最狠的：

他们连"不知道"本身都罚，只要那个不知道看着是他自己招来的。比方喝醉的人，罚加倍。因为开头在他身上：他本来做得了主不喝醉，而喝醉是那个不知道的原因。

赏罚这一段是他叫来的见证，不是这一章要立的东西。这一章要立的是德性和恶为什么以同一种方式算自愿，赏罚只证到"行动在我们手上"这一层为止。

法条里那些该知道而且不难知道的，不知道也罚。别的事情上也一样，凡是看着因为疏忽才不知道的，都罚，因为不去疏忽本来在他手上：上心这件事，本来他做得了主。

到这儿有人插了一句。

他把那一句写了出来：可他也许就是那么个人，上不了心。

他答得很快，快到几乎是抢白：他们成了那样的人，是他们自己弄的，日子过得散漫；成了不正义的人和放纵的人也是自己弄的，一拨是靠着做坏事，一拨是靠着泡在酒里和那一类事情当中。因为一件件做下来的事，把人做成那样。

这一点他还给了个现成的证据：看那些为着哪一场比试练功夫的人就知道，他们一直在做。

所以不知道"性子是从一件件做出来的"，那是完全没长眼睛。

接下来是石头。

他说，生病的人不是健康的人。要是事情就那样，他是自愿病的：日子过得没个节制，也不听医生的。

那时候他本来做得了主不病；撒了手就不能了，就像石头扔出去了，你不能再把它捡回来。可扔和撒手这两下本来在他手上，因为开头在他身上。

不正义的人和放纵的人也一样：一开头本来做得了主不成那样，所以他们是自愿的；成了以后，就由不得他们不是了。

这一段说的不是一句劝人回头的话，是一句关于时刻的话：做得了主的那一下已经过去了，而它当时确实在。

再往下，他让对面把话说完了，说得比前面那一句长得多。

对面说：人人追求的都是他看着好的那个东西，可那个"看着"不归他管；他是什么样的人，那个终点在他看来就是什么样。

那么两条路。要是每个人对自己的性子多少负点责，他对那个"看着"也就多少负点责。要是不负责，那就没有人对自己做坏事负责了；他做那些事，是因为不知道终点在哪儿，还以为靠这些能给自己弄到最好的。

而对终点的那份想望不是自己挑的。一个人得生下来就带着那样一只眼睛，靠它判得准，选得中真正好的东西。生来带得好的人就叫天分好。因为这是最大最好的一样东西，而且是从别人那里拿不来也学不来的：他生成什么样就有什么样。

这一段里那句关于眼睛的话，很容易被当成他自己的主张。

它不是他的主张。可它到底是谁在说，希腊文本身没有给答案，因为那上面没有引号。

对面开口在哪儿没有分歧：从"要是有人说"起，1114a31。对面在哪儿收口，是编者断的。拉克姆把引号一直拉到"天分好"，把整段都归对面；而他自己在那一句上加了个脚注，说这段异议是不是真要走这么远，并不十分确定。罗斯断得早，在"要是每个人对自己的性子多少负点责"那里就让亚里士多德接过话去，于是后半连同那只眼睛都成了他替对面把话说到底。

两种断法在一件事上是一样的：那只眼睛落在他要驳掉的那一支上，不是他自己的说法。这一篇要用的正是这一件；至于整段归谁，本篇采前一种断法，可这是采，不是核出来的。

他的回答只有一句反问，而在拜沃特那个本子的断句里，这是整章唯一带问号的句子。

要是这些是真的，那德性凭什么就比恶更算自愿的呢。

因为在好人和坏人那里，终点同样是天生就那么显着，就那么摆在那儿，剩下的事两边都是照着那个终点做的。

所以两条路都堵上了。要是终点不全由天生，还有一点在他自己身上，那坏人那一点也在他自己身上。要是终点是天生的，而好人做剩下那些事是自愿的，德性因此算自愿，那恶也一样算自愿。因为坏人那些行为里同样有"由他自己"这一层，就算终点那一层没有。

于是他收口：既然像人们说的那样德性是自愿的（因为我们对自己的性子多少也算共同的原因，而且我们是什么样的人，就把终点摆成什么样），那恶也是自愿的。一样的道理。

这一手值得停一下。

他没有正面驳"看着不归你管"这句话。他做的是把这句话原样接过来，然后指出它把德性和恶一起带走了。

对面本来只想免掉坏人那一份责任。他说：照你这么讲，好人那一份功劳也一并没有了。

而"开头"这两个字，这边也在用，用在另一头。

那一下是可以不落的。

这边讲得很硬：没有哪一处缺口逼着你再往下动，也没有哪个终点在前头拽着你。这边把这件事写成一句话：认识这一层上，没有一台保证这台序列转下去的马达。

马达这个词是这边自己用的，而用它的那一页上还钉了两根桩，一根比一根紧。

第一根：可停本身还不是自由，它是自由的空间。倘若认识把人锁死在必然往下走上，那条"不往下走"的路就根本没法选。

第二根，而且这一根这一篇非说不可：那一页明写着它裁的是认识这一层，实践上的自由归另一部批判管。所以"没有马达"这句话，说的是认识的序列可以停，不是说人的每一个行动当时都可以不那样。

这一条得先钉住，后面那笔账才记得清。

钉住以后，两句还是挨着的，只是挨得比原先窄。

他那一句"开头在他身上"说的是行动；这边那一句"没有马达"说的是认识往下走的那一步。两句共有的是同一个形：那一步不是被推着走的。

同一句话，两边拿它做的事不一样。

他拿它往回追。石头那一段撑得住追责：你现在收不回来了，可当初扔是你扔的。不过这一章要立的不止"罚你不冤"，是德性和恶为什么以同一种方式算自愿，追责只是它顺带撑住的一件。

这边拿它往前留门。可停不是为了定责任，是为了让另一条路留得住：一个人可以给自己立"穷尽地做下去"这条法，也可以立"不做"这条法，而两条都得是他自己立的才算。

一个用它往回追责，一个用它往前留门。

这两个用法不打架，可它们要的东西不一样。往回追，要的是"当时那一下确实在"。往前留门，要的是"以后那一下还在"。

而"以后那一下还在"这句话，比看上去难说。

这一件夜里最好懂。

夜里两点决定明天六点起。闹钟响的那一下，起不起来确实在你手上，这一句是真的。可你昨晚几点睡，把今天早上这一下变得多难，那是昨晚的事。这一下还在，只是它比昨天贵了。

再举一个更轻的。一个人上下班走同一条路，走了三年，到了那个岔口不用想就拐了。要拐别的方向不是不行；难的是先想起来这儿可以拐。那一下也还在，只是它得先被想起来。

而这一章的最后一段，正好落在这两个例子上，落得比它们都准。

那一段常被跳过去，因为它看着像收尾套话。它不是。

他说：行动和性子不是照同一种方式算自愿的。

行动，我们从头到尾做得了主，只要知道那些具体情形。性子不是：我们做得了主的只有开头；一件件加上去的那些增量，是看不见的，就像生病那样长起来。可正因为当初怎么使这一下是在我们手上的，性子照样算自愿。

那句"像生病那样长起来"要停一下。他前面已经拿生病打过一次比方，那一次讲的是撒手；这一次讲的是没撒手的时候也在长。夜里两点那一下，岔口那一下，都在这一句底下。

他把两种自愿分了账。当初在与现在不在，中间那段路，他不是只用"日子过得散漫"五个字带过去的：他说了那段路的样子，说它不显眼。

而这一段路，正是这边欠他的那一笔，只是那一笔的形状跟原先想的不一样。

这边不是漏了后一半。是这边把后一半交出去了。

马达那一页明写着：它裁的是认识那一层，实践上的自由归另一部批判。所以"长年的习惯怎样改变下一次的代价，显不显眼，够不够得着"这几样，不在那一条的辖内，那一条也没打算管。

可交出去不等于已经写了。他这一章一口气把三层都摆完了：单次的开头在人身上，一件件做下来把人做成那样，成了以后那个状态不再像起初那样由得动；而且他明说了这三层不是同一种自愿。

这边那本还没写的书要处理的，正是这三层。而他在这儿摆出来的，是一张现成的清单：这一段路上要交代的是什么。

石头那个比方之所以厉害，就在这儿。

它说的不是"你扔不回来了，认命吧"，也不是"你随时可以扔回来"。它说的是这个：一个可以做主的时刻曾经在过，而它现在不在了；中间隔着的，是你自己一下一下扔出去的那些。

而他最后那一段说的是比这更难的一件：那些扔出去的，你当时并不觉得自己在扔。增量不显眼。

马达那一句，是站在手还没松开的时候说的，而且只说到认识那一层。

石头已经在空里的那一段，那本书还没有写。

In Book III, chapter 5, he first establishes one point.

Whatever it is in our power to do, it is also in our power not to do; whatever it is in our power not to do, it is also in our power to do. So if doing the fine thing is in our power, not doing the shameful thing is in our power too. Since both fine and shameful actions are in our power, and this is what being good or bad consists in, being decent or being base is in our power.

Having established it, he does not stop. He sets out, then and there, the cost of refusing it.

He says: otherwise we must overturn what we have just said, and say that a human being is not the origin of his own actions, not their begetter as he is the begetter of his children.

This is not a threat. He does not say, “You will have to admit that people are not free.” He names a specific cost: to reject that whole chain, you must also reject the claim that a person is the origin of his own actions, and the comparison he gives for this is having children. “Origin” works in both places: your children come from you, and so do your actions.

Then he calls a witness, and this witness is not a philosopher.

This is what lawmakers do. They punish wrongdoers unless they acted under compulsion or out of an ignorance for which they should not be held responsible; those who do good they honor. Nobody urges you to do something that is neither in your power nor voluntary. Urging someone not to mind the heat or complain of hunger is wasted effort. The heat will do what it does.

And the next point is the sharpest in the passage:

They even punish ignorance itself, when the person appears to have brought it on himself. A drunk, for instance, receives twice the penalty. For the origin lies in him: it was in his power not to get drunk, and getting drunk caused the ignorance.

The passage on rewards and punishments is testimony he calls in, not what the chapter is trying to establish. What this chapter seeks to establish is why virtue and vice count as voluntary in the same way. Rewards and punishments bear witness only as far as “action is in our power.”

Ignorance of provisions in the law that one ought to know and that are not difficult to know is punished too. The same applies elsewhere: any ignorance apparently due to negligence is punished, because not being negligent was in the person's power. Taking care was something he could have done.

At this point someone interjects.

He writes the objection down: but perhaps he is just the sort of person who cannot take care.

His answer comes quickly, almost cutting the objection short: they have made themselves that sort of person by living carelessly. They have made themselves unjust and self-indulgent too, the former by wrongdoing, the latter by spending their time drinking and the like. For what people do, act after act, makes them what they are.

For this he offers evidence ready to hand: look at people training for a contest. They keep practicing.

So not knowing that dispositions are formed by one action after another is sheer blindness.

Next comes the stone.

A sick person, he says, is not healthy. If the circumstances are as described, he is sick voluntarily: he has lived without restraint and ignored his doctors.

At that time it was in his power not to become ill. Once he let go, it was no longer so, just as, once you have thrown a stone, you cannot recover it. Yet throwing it and letting it go were in his power, because the origin lay in him.

The unjust and self-indulgent are alike in this: at the beginning it was in their power not to become that way, so they are so voluntarily. Once they have become that way, it is no longer up to them not to be.

This passage is not an exhortation to turn back. It is a statement about a moment: the moment when it was in one's power has passed, and it really was there at the time.

Further on, he lets the other side finish speaking, at much greater length than in the earlier objection.

The objection says: everyone pursues what appears good to him, but that appearing is not under his control. As a person is, so the end appears to him.

There are then two paths. If each person is in some measure responsible for his disposition, he is in some measure responsible for that appearing too. If he is not responsible, then nobody is responsible for doing wrong. He does those things because he does not know where the end lies, and thinks he can get the best for himself by doing them.

And the desire for the end is not something one chooses. A person has to be born with a certain eye by which to judge correctly and choose what is truly good. Someone born with it in good condition is called naturally gifted. For this is the greatest and finest thing, and it can neither be acquired from another nor learned: what one has is what one is born with.

The sentence about the eye in this passage is easily mistaken for his own position.

It is not his position. But the Greek text itself does not answer whose voice it is, because it has no quotation marks.

There is no disagreement about where the objection begins: at “if someone were to say,” 1114a31. Where it ends is an editorial judgment. Rackham carries the quotation marks through “naturally gifted,” assigning the whole passage to the objector. Yet he adds a footnote there saying it is not entirely certain that the objection is really meant to extend so far. Ross ends it earlier, handing the speech back to Aristotle at “if each person is in some measure responsible for his disposition.” The latter half, eye and all, then becomes Aristotle following the other side's argument through to its end.

The two divisions agree on one thing: the eye belongs to the branch of the argument he means to reject, not to his own account. That is what this essay needs. As for who speaks the whole passage, this essay adopts the former division. It is an adoption, not a verified finding.

His answer is a single rhetorical question. In Bywater's punctuation, it is the only sentence in the chapter with a question mark.

If these things are true, why should virtue be any more voluntary than vice?

For in the good person and the bad alike, the end appears by nature in the same way, stands before them in the same way, and both do the remaining things with that end in view.

So both paths are blocked. If the end is not wholly given by nature, if something of it depends on the person, then that something depends on the bad person too. If the end is given by nature, but the good person does the remaining things voluntarily and virtue therefore counts as voluntary, then vice counts as voluntary too. For the bad person's actions equally contain an element of “from himself,” even if the end does not.

He therefore concludes: since virtue is voluntary, as people say (because we are in some measure joint causes of our dispositions, and the kind of people we are determines the end we set before us), vice is voluntary too. For the same reason.

This move deserves a pause.

He has not directly refuted “what appears good is not under your control.” He takes the claim just as it stands, then points out that it takes virtue away along with vice.

The other side wanted only to remove the bad person's share of responsibility. He says: on your account, the good person's share of credit goes too.

And “origin” is a word this side also uses, at another end.

That step need not be taken.

This side puts it firmly: no gap compels you to move further, and no end pulls you from ahead. It writes this in one sentence: at the level of cognition, there is no motor guaranteeing that the sequence will keep running.

“Motor” is this side's own word. On the page that uses it, two stakes are driven in, one more firmly than the other.

The first: being able to stop is not itself freedom; it is the space for freedom. If cognition locked a person into necessarily continuing, “not continuing” would not be a path one could choose at all.

The second, which this essay must state: that page explicitly says it adjudicates the level of cognition; practical freedom belongs to another critique. So “no motor” means that the cognitive sequence can stop. It does not mean that every human action could have been otherwise at the time.

This has to be fixed in place first if we are to keep the later account straight.

Once it is fixed, the two statements still stand beside each other, but along a narrower edge than before.

His “the origin lies in him” concerns action. This side's “no motor” concerns the next step in cognition. What the two share is a form: that step is not forced onward.

The same statement is put to different uses on the two sides.

He uses it to trace backward. The stone passage supports assigning responsibility: you cannot retrieve it now, but you were the one who threw it then. Yet the chapter seeks to establish more than “punishing you is not unfair.” It asks why virtue and vice count as voluntary in the same way. Assigning responsibility is only one thing it supports along the way.

This side uses it to leave a door open ahead. Being able to stop is not there to assign responsibility, but to keep another path open: a person may give himself the law “continue exhaustively,” or the law “do not.” Either counts only if he gives it to himself.

One uses it to trace responsibility backward; the other, to leave a door open ahead.

The uses do not conflict, but they need different things. Tracing backward needs “that moment really was available then.” Leaving a door open ahead needs “that moment will still be available later.”

And “that moment will still be available later” is harder to say than it looks.

This is easiest to understand at night.

At two in the morning you decide to get up at six. When the alarm rings, getting up or not really is in your power. That is true. But how difficult this morning's moment has become because of when you went to bed is a matter of last night. The moment is still there. It just costs more than it did yesterday.

Take a lighter example. Someone has walked the same route to and from work for three years, and turns at the fork without thinking. Turning the other way is not impossible. What is difficult is first remembering that a turn is possible here. That moment is still there too, but it first has to be remembered.

And the chapter's final paragraph lands precisely on these two examples, more precisely than either of them.

That paragraph is often skipped because it looks like a routine conclusion. It is not.

He says: actions and dispositions are not voluntary in the same way.

Actions are in our power from beginning to end, provided we know the particulars. Dispositions are not: only their beginning is in our power. The increments that accumulate one by one are imperceptible, as illness grows. Yet precisely because how we acted at the beginning was in our power, the disposition still counts as voluntary.

Pause at “as illness grows.” He has already used illness once as a comparison. That time he was speaking of letting go; this time he speaks of growth even before one has let go. The moment at two in the morning and the moment at the fork both fall under this sentence.

He has kept two kinds of voluntariness in separate accounts. Between what was available then and is unavailable now, he has not dismissed the intervening path with “living carelessly.” He has described what that path is like: it goes unnoticed.

And that path is precisely what this side owes him, though the debt has a different shape from what first appeared.

This side has not overlooked the latter half. It has handed the latter half elsewhere.

The motor page explicitly says that it adjudicates cognition; practical freedom belongs to another critique. So how years of habit change the cost of the next act, whether it is visible, whether it is within reach: these lie outside that claim's jurisdiction, and it never intended to cover them.

But handing something elsewhere does not mean it has been written. In this chapter he lays out all three levels at once: the origin of a single act lies in the person; one act after another makes the person that way; once formed, the condition is no longer as amenable to change as it was at first. And he expressly says that these three levels are not voluntary in the same way.

Those are precisely the three levels that this side's unwritten book must address. What he lays out here is a ready-made list: what must be accounted for along this path.

This is where the force of the stone comparison lies.

It does not say, “You cannot throw it back, so resign yourself,” or “You can throw it back whenever you like.” It says this: a moment of control once existed, and no longer does. Between then and now lie the things you yourself threw, one after another.

And his last paragraph says something harder still: when you threw those things, you did not feel yourself throwing them. The increments went unnoticed.

The sentence about the motor is spoken while the hand has not yet opened, and only at the level of cognition.

The part in which the stone is already in the air: that book has not yet been written.
