Useless Magic

没有用的魔法

Aug 27, 2026Han Qin (秦汉)Independent English edition中文原作 · 简 / 繁

Spoiler note: This essay discusses story material from the manga and anime.

Fern has ample reason to complain about Frieren's collecting habits. The party may detour for months because a village offers a grimoire whose spell does nothing more than remove rust from bronze statues or turn sweet grapes sour. Neither will defeat a demon, protect a town, or improve the standing of an already legendary mage.

Frieren's answer is simple: collecting magic is her hobby.

The line is played lightly because the habit is comic. Frieren can remain in bed through the morning, misjudge how long an errand will take, and then pursue some absurdly narrow spell with inexhaustible patience. But the trivial magic gradually forms an archive. A spell for sour grapes recalls Eisen's favorite food. A spell for cleaning bronze answers the weathering of heroic statues. Flower magic reaches back to Flamme and forward to Himmel.

The archive does not look like memory because Frieren does not collect the spells under other people's names. She chooses them because they appeal to her. Only in use do they reveal how much of her desire has been shaped by encounters she once thought too brief to count.

I. A record that does not know it is recording

At Himmel's funeral, Frieren says she knew almost nothing about him. The statement is sincere, but the word “nothing” is not exact. She remembers his poses, habits, promises, and interventions. She knows what kind of request made him stop and which form of magic delighted him. What she lacks is not every fact. It is an account of what those facts mean to her.

The minor spells preserve that unarticulated attention. Frieren remembers that Eisen likes sour grapes, so magic capable of producing them attracts her. A strangely specific cleaning spell later lets her answer the corrosion of bronze monuments. These are not diary entries. They store the contour of a relation without announcing themselves as testimony.

This makes them unusually faithful to the series' treatment of memory. A formal monument can survive while its story changes. A private recollection can be accurate while its significance remains invisible to the person remembering. Magic moves between the two: learned in one encounter, carried silently, and activated when a later situation gives the old detail a new meaning.

Frieren often discovers what she noticed only when the present asks her to use it.

The archive is therefore neither fully conscious nor purely accidental. She repeatedly chooses these spells. She simply does not turn each choice into a declaration about love, grief, or loyalty. The work leaves those labels to the reader.

II. Why uselessness matters

Frieren possesses magic of immense strategic value. Her power helps defeat the Demon King, terrifies surviving demons, and allows human settlements to continue under the peace created by the Hero Party. Even her lifelong suppression of mana, learned from Flamme, is a weapon refined for a civilizational struggle.

All of this power is genuinely hers. Yet usefulness gives other people a claim on it. Humans need her strength; demons define her by her lethality; history remembers her as the mage in Himmel's party. The title attached to her measures what she can do for a war.

A spell for sour grapes is harder to appropriate. It does not increase her rank, guarantee safety, or make the wielder historically important. No institution needs her to own it. When she seeks it, the explanation cannot be outsourced to public necessity.

That does not make useless action inherently virtuous. Vanity, waste, and cruelty can all be useless. Useful work can be loving and freely chosen. The point is narrower: remove expected return, and one common explanation disappears. What remains may reveal a desire not dictated by achievement.

Frieren's hobby is the part of her magical life least reducible to the functions others assign her. It is where “I wanted this” survives without needing to become “the world required this.” That is why the line about a hobby carries more weight than its tone admits.

III. Himmel takes the hobby seriously

Himmel never treats Frieren's minor magic as an embarrassment to the quest. He waits while she searches for grimoires, accepts strange rewards, and permits the journey to bend around her interest. One of the spells central to their first meeting produces a field of flowers—the magic Flamme taught Frieren and the magic that first made magic beautiful to Himmel.

His response differs from Macht's research in the previous essay. Macht wants to know humans in order to solve a problem he has defined. Himmel does not require Frieren's hobby to become evidence of progress. He joins the detour without owning what it must mean.

The manga later gives this attitude an architectural scale. At the Great Tor Canyon, the dwarf Gehen spends two centuries completing a bridge. Long before, when the project lacked funds, Himmel contributed his reward and asked that the bridge be made to last a thousand years. He knew he would never cross the finished structure. Frieren, he said, could receive it in his place.

The bridge is useful in the ordinary sense—it joins lands and serves future travelers. But Himmel's personal return cannot explain the gift. He orders on Frieren's timescale, pays from his own, and releases the result to people he will never meet. The bridge is another version of the path described in the second essay: an opportunity extended into a future its giver cannot supervise.

For Frieren, receiving that distant reward does not mean decoding a hidden command. Gehen's labor, the communities that grow during the centuries, and the party that finally arrives all exceed Himmel's plan. The gift has remained alive precisely because others have made it their own work.

IV. The flower he could not show her

Himmel once wanted Frieren to see the blue-moon weed that grew around his home. The journey ended before the promise was fulfilled, and in later years the flower becomes rare enough that local people have not seen it for decades.

When Frieren and Fern are asked to clean one of Himmel's neglected statues, the task expands. Frieren could use ordinary flower magic to decorate the monument quickly. Instead she decides that only blue-moon weed will do. She and Fern spend half a year searching. Seeds from a related plant and the inconvenient work of seed rats eventually lead them to an abandoned tower where the lost flower still grows.

Frieren cultivates the blue-moon weed around the statue and places a flower crown on Himmel's head, answering the crown he once placed on hers. Fern and the local herbalist are present; the gesture is not a secret performed without witnesses, and the cleaning began as a village request. What makes it exceed exchange is the scale and specificity of the response. No commission requires six months, the flower of Himmel's home, or the returned crown.

The bronze cannot see, and Himmel cannot acknowledge the gift. The act corrects no past mistake. Frieren does not finally show the living Himmel the flowers he wanted to share. She creates a present relation to that unfulfilled wish.

This is how the series avoids turning remembrance into restoration. Nothing brings the missed opportunity back. A later gesture can neither erase lateness nor make death fair. It can answer the past without pretending to repair it.

V. Beyond the ledger

The first essay argued that Frieren initially treats ten years as a fraction of a millennium. The third showed Macht treating decades of closeness as preparation for an experiment. In both cases, relation enters a ledger: duration is assigned a proportion, or intimacy becomes a variable expected to produce knowledge.

Useless magic interrupts the ledger because the return is missing. Making grapes sour for the memory of Eisen will not strengthen the party. Cleaning Himmel's statue cannot make him love Frieren, because he is dead. The blue-moon weed produces no new chance to understand him in life.

It would be too absolute to say that only unrewarded acts recognize a person. Fern's care, Stark's protection, and Himmel's public service all produce real benefits. Nor is every generous act secretly corrupted if the giver feels satisfaction. Human motives do not arrive in pure containers.

But the absence of return performs a useful test. If the act still makes sense when approval, advantage, and future reciprocity have been removed, then the other person is no longer present solely as a source of results. Frieren's most apparently pointless magic shows that a relation can continue after exchange has become impossible.

The spells also resist heroic scale. The defeat of the Demon King matters to millions; a particular grape matters to Eisen. Public history has no reason to preserve the preference. Frieren does. Her collection gives small facts a duration that monuments usually reserve for great deeds.

VI. Learning to travel slowly

Frieren remains slow after Himmel's death. She still underestimates how humans experience delay and still irritates Fern by extending a journey for magic of questionable utility. The external behavior has not reversed.

Its meaning, however, begins to change. Earlier, slowness followed from abundance: with centuries ahead, everything could be postponed. Now slowness increasingly makes room for the thing in front of her. She accepts local jobs, waits for companions, notices moods, and permits a minor desire to alter the route.

She knows more clearly than before what will happen. Fern and Stark are human; if the journey continues long enough, Frieren will outlive them as she outlived Himmel and Heiter. Anticipating loss does not supply a formula for avoiding regret. It does remove the innocence of believing that attention can always be deferred.

The series offers no final conversion in which Frieren becomes human in every relevant sense. Her timescale remains elven. Her appetite for grimoires remains her own. What changes is not the quantity of time available but the authority she grants quantity over a relation.

A decade can be less than one hundredth and still alter a millennium. A spell can have no strategic use and still carry the exact taste of a friend. A flower crown can reach no dead recipient and still be a real response. These statements do not defeat arithmetic. They name the things arithmetic was never qualified to decide.

Frieren continues north, slowly. Once, she moved slowly because the future seemed unlimited. Now she can move slowly because the present is not merely a portion of that future. It contains persons who cannot be converted into time, power, knowledge, or return.

Primary text: Kanehito Yamada and Tsukasa Abe, Frieren: Beyond Journey's End; manga and anime adaptation.

剧透提示：本文涉及漫画与动画情节。

菲伦抱怨过好几次。

芙莉莲一路上收集魔法，为了一本魔导书能绕路半年。而她收集到的东西是这样的：一个可以瞬间清除铜像锈迹的魔法。一个可以把甜葡萄变酸的魔法。

菲伦说，这些既无聊又没用。

芙莉莲说，这是我的兴趣。

一份看不出是记录的记录

后来，这些魔法一个一个派上了用场。

清除锈迹的那个，被她用在了辛美尔的雕像上——她接了一个清理雕像的委托，把那座已经生了几十年锈的铜像擦得像新的一样。

把甜葡萄变酸的那个，是因为艾冉爱吃酸葡萄。

这是这部作品最不动声色的一次回收，而它的分量要退回到第一篇才看得清。

她在葬礼上说，我对这个人一无所知。

可是她收集了一个清理铜像的魔法。

她以为自己没在看。她一直在看——她记得矮人爱吃什么样的葡萄，记得那些铜像会生锈，记得那个爱面子的勇者不会想让自己的像脏成那样。她只是把看到的东西存进了一个她自己也认不出来是记忆的地方。

作品没有让任何人把这件事说破。菲伦不知道，芙莉莲自己也从来没有意识到。它就那样放在那里，等着某一次她掏出那个魔法的时候，读者自己看见。

没有用，是它唯一的资格

现在要问一个更硬的问题：为什么偏偏是这些魔法？

芙莉莲活了一千多年，她身上有用的东西太多了。她的魔力深不见底。她的战斗技术让七崩贤都要绕着走。她研究出的杀魔族的方法，是这个大陆能撑到今天的原因之一。

而这些东西没有一样是她的。

"葬送的芙莉莲"这个名字，是魔族给她起的——那是敌人按她的杀伤力发的一张标签。人类记住她，是因为她是打倒魔王的那一支队伍里的魔法使。她的强大，属于所有需要她强大的人。

一个人身上凡是有用的东西，都同时属于那个用途，也属于那个使用它的人。

只有那些没用的魔法，不属于任何人。

它们不为任何目的存在，不能换到任何东西，不会让她更强，不会让谁更安全。她收集它们的理由只有一个，就是她想要。

在漫长的一千年里，这是作品让我们看得最清楚的、她纯粹因为自己想做而做的事。

所以那句"这是我的兴趣"，其实是全作最重的一句台词之一，只是它被说得非常轻。

辛美尔一直知道

这就要说到那个爱面子的勇者。

他从来不笑那些没用的魔法。别人觉得芙莉莲在浪费时间，他不这么看——他跟着她去找那些魔导书，跟着她绕路，把这件事当成正经事。

他还做过一件更远的安排。

旅途中他们遇到一个矮人工匠盖安，想在峡谷上造一座桥，钱不够了，工程无限期搁置。辛美尔把手上的一笔赏金全部给了他，嘱咐他造一座能立一千年的桥——并且说，将来把这座桥当作报酬，交给芙莉莲。

八十多年后，芙莉莲再走到那里。历经两百年工程的桥已经造好了。

一千年这个数字不是形容词。辛美尔是按照芙莉莲的寿命下的单子。

那种他没能给出去的花

还有一件他没做成的事。

辛美尔的故乡产一种花，叫苍月草。他很喜欢它，一直想带芙莉莲回去看一看。

这件事最终没有实现。他先死了。

又过了很多年，苍月草几乎绝迹了。

芙莉莲和菲伦走到中央诸国的一个地方，遇到一位药草学家，她手里保存着一份苍月草近缘种的种子。靠这份种子，加上一种爱藏种子又老是忘记藏在哪儿的小鼠帮的倒忙，芙莉莲最后在一座废弃的高塔上，找到了一大片苍月草。

她做的事是这样的：

她用魔法在辛美尔的雕像周围种满了苍月草。

然后她用苍月草编了一顶花冠，戴到雕像头上。

这件事没有任何用处。

铜像不会看。辛美尔不会知道。菲伦和药草学家在场，但她们的见证无法把这件事变成辛美尔能收到的回报。没有人要求芙莉莲用苍月草还债，也没有报酬。她花了很大的力气，为一个已经不在的人，做了一件没有实用目的的事。

而这正是辛美尔一生都在做的事。

他为村里的小事停下来，为一个矮人的桥掏空钱袋，为一片她可能永远看不到的花惦记了十年。他做那些事，不以未来的见证与回报为条件。

芙莉莲不是被教会的。没有人告诉过她应该这样做。她只是走着走着，成了那个样子。

这从来不是时间问题

回到第一篇留下的那个问题。

芙莉莲有一千多年。她的时间在任何标准下都是无限的。而她还是错过了辛美尔，错过得干干净净——她跟他一起走了十年，站在他的墓前才发现自己什么都没看清。

所以这从来不是时间的问题。

第一篇说过，她的错不在算错了，而在于她以为那种东西可以算。她把辛美尔放进了一个换算：十年，占一千年的百分之一，配百分之一的注意力。

那么她后来是怎么变的？

不是因为她想通了什么道理。作品没有给她任何一场顿悟，也没有给她一段总结陈词。

变化的标志是：她开始为一个人做没用的事。

这是一个可以用来检验的东西，而且比任何表白都准确。你为一个人做的事，如果每一件都能换到点什么——安全、回报、他的好感、你自己的安心——那么他还在你的账本上。他是一个项目。

只有当你为他做的事完全换不到东西的时候，他才从账本上出来了。

辛美尔那十年，在芙莉莲的账上是百分之一。她现在做的这些事，不在任何账上。

走得慢一点

芙莉莲还在往北走。

她还会活很久很久。她很清楚接下来会发生什么：菲伦会老，会死；休塔尔克会老，会死；她会站在两座新的墓前，就像当年站在辛美尔的墓前一样。

她知道这一点，而且她这次是提前知道的。

她还是在走。而且走得越来越慢——为了一本没什么用的魔导书绕半年的路，在村子里替人做一些不重要的事，在雕像前面坐一会儿。

以前她慢，是因为一千年很长，什么都可以往后放。

现在她慢，是因为她不想再把任何东西放进那个换算里。

同样的速度，完全不同的两件事。
