LIVES BEYOND THEIR ENDINGS · READER ESSAYS · 04

Must a Continuation Obey the Marginal Notes?

A voice beside the text may have seen chapters we no longer possess. That makes it worth hearing—not merely selecting the remarks that suit us.

Han Qin · 2026-10-03

Discusses the commentary’s hints about later events; does not disclose the new fiction’s particular endings.

Someone else is reading beside us

An annotated edition of Dream of the Red Chamber can make a reader feel that someone has taken the next chair. An apparently ordinary event occurs, and a note says to pay attention: more will follow. A character appears, and the commentator remembers an important later scene whose draft is now lost.

For anyone hoping to continue the novel, such remarks are hard to put down. We reach chapter eighty and cannot see farther. Someone beside the text seems to have been there.

Yet worth reading carefully is not the same as ready to use. Whose remark is this, and in which manuscript does it appear? Does the speaker claim to have seen something, infer that it will happen, or wish that it might? Flatten these differences, and a clue begins to carry more than it can support.

The early commentary is commonly gathered under the name Zhiyan Zhai, or Red Inkstone Studio, and associated commentators. It should not be imagined as one uninterrupted voice, speaking at one moment about one unchanging draft. Recollection, interpretation, regret, and conjecture need different places in our reading.

Two remarks that do not join neatly

Consider Baoyu’s future. A note in the Gengchen manuscript speaks of his abandoning things and becoming a monk. A different note, attributed to the lost Jing manuscript and surviving through transcription, connects his final letting go with awakening and asks why shaving his head should be necessary.

Both deserve attention. Their transmission does not become equally secure because I prefer the second. The Jing manuscript is no longer available; the wording reaches us through a record of its notes, whose authenticity is disputed. That distance must travel with the quotation.

Nor can I immediately announce that Cao Xueqin first wrote one ending and then replaced it. The commentators might have seen different drafts. They might have understood a passage differently. A plausible explanation is not a recovered record of revision.

My continuation makes a choice, and the accompanying paper registers its costs. The point here is not to make the reader choose with me. It is that after I choose, the other remark remains. Inconvenience is not a reason to remove it from view.

Look for what the story leaves out

Research can begin with a reassuring habit. Write a scene, then find a marginal note that supports it. Once the note has been found, the scene seems to have roots.

But does my scene have support? is only one question. What support points toward something I did not write? is another. A persuasive answer to the first can leave the second entirely unasked.

I therefore reversed the direction of the inquiry. Instead of finding witnesses only for the continuation already written, I examined the notes that pointed elsewhere. An adopted hint was recorded; a partial adoption identified which part; a changed reading was not reported as literal fulfillment. Rejected possibilities kept their reasons.

This may sound like paperwork. In practice it produces uncomfortable discoveries. A note believed to have been satisfied may contain several claims, of which the scene meets only one. A remark used to establish a character’s final destination may not actually say that much.

One round of checking began without showing the analyst my continuation. The task was to read the note with the passage it annotated and identify what it asserted. Only then was that account compared with the new fiction. The procedure cannot eliminate mistakes, but it interrupts a strong temptation: knowing what I wrote, and letting the commentator conveniently know it too.

Leave the unchosen road visible

Gathering the unused clues reveals other possible shapes for the second half. They are not obstacles to be cleared away. Nor do they establish that several complete lost manuscripts once existed. First of all, they show that the surviving material can sustain more than one route through it.

My route consequently has to explain its turns. Does an event already narrated in the first eighty chapters rule something out? Do two hints pull against one another? Or does the interpretation I bring make me unwilling to take a character toward that outcome? These reasons cannot pass for one another.

In particular, does not fit my reading cannot quietly become the commentator was wrong. I do not possess that shortcut. A reader can pick up a note I leave aside and use it to imagine a different continuation.

The person in the margin is still speaking. Listening seriously need not mean doing what the voice proposes. It also means not covering the sentence with my hand when I decide otherwise.
