LIVES BEYOND THEIR ENDINGS · READER ESSAYS · 02

Why Write Beyond Chapter Eighty?

An ending does more than tell us what happened next. It changes how we understand the life that came before.

Han Qin · 2026-10-03

Discusses endings in the received forty-chapter continuation; does not disclose the new fiction’s specific alternatives.

The poems in the fire

Daiyu burning her poems is difficult to forget. The sheets still exist; the person who wrote them has little time left. Knowing what will soon be lost makes the fire unusually vivid.

The scene belongs to chapter ninety-seven of the forty-chapter continuation printed in the Cheng–Gao editions, which gave generations of readers a complete, 120-chapter Dream of the Red Chamber. Its chapter title connects burning the manuscripts with severing her passionate attachment. The scene has moved many readers. Writing a different ending does not require denying its power.

Yet a question remains for me. Is the fire consuming a few papers associated with Baoyu, or Daiyu’s entire life in poetry?

Her poems contain love, but also the seasons, flowers, displacement, pride, and vulnerability. Poetry is not merely her way of conducting a romance. If breaking that attachment becomes the final explanation for destroying her work, what happens to everything else from which the poems arose? This is not evidence that Cao Xueqin’s lost ending could not have contained such a scene. It is a question about the reading I am willing to carry forward.

Must you succeed before you can leave?

Baoyu’s received ending raises a different question of order. He passes the provincial civil-service examination before leaving his family. He has apparently satisfied the household’s ambition and then transcended it.

But must the Baoyu of the first eighty chapters, so resistant to the expected pursuit of office, first win on those terms before he can leave them behind? There is a difference between relinquishing a success you have secured and recognizing that this was never the life you wished to live.

Both can look like turning away. The first may nevertheless preserve success as an entry requirement: prove you could do it before saying you do not want it. The second has to live without that certificate.

This is not a verdict against religious renunciation, nor a claim that remaining in ordinary society is always superior. A path has to make sense within the life of the person taking it. Baoyu and Xichun need not have the same answer. One character’s departure cannot settle what another ought to do.

When the house recovers

The received continuation also holds out the prospect of family restoration through successful descendants. After so much loss, that promise can feel like relief.

My unease concerns the measure of recovery. Are the lives we have followed ultimately to be accounted for by the renewed fortunes of the Jia household? If the family prospers again, does that settle what happened to the people who died, were driven away, or were never heard?

I want to remain with the people after the household’s collapse. Some lose protection and become more exposed. Others, separated from the old arrangements, have to decide for themselves in unfamiliar ways. The family’s fortunes matter enormously. They cannot stand in for everyone’s future.

That is my disagreement with this ending, not a ruling on all other readings. Another reader may find something different in the same chapters. To answer such a disagreement, it is not enough to announce an objection. I have to write the alternative and let it be encountered.

A work of fiction, not a recovered answer

The result is my own original continuation, chapters 81–110: thirty new chapters following the first eighty, which I leave unchanged. Some decisions draw on the novel and its early commentary. Others involve inference, borrowing, or choices for which I alone must answer.

I call the method interpretive reconstruction: attending closely to what survives, then developing one reading into a sustained fictional life. It is not a claim to have found Cao Xueqin’s missing manuscript. Nor does a sound interpretive argument automatically produce a living scene.

That last gap is the difficult one. An essay can say that Daiyu is more than a lover. Fiction must show how she spends time, what she says and withholds, how she handles her possessions, and how she responds to the people beside her. No admirable argument can live that life on her behalf.

Simply reversing the received ending would not solve the problem. Where another writer kills, I save; where another separates, I reunite. Characters written by that rule would still be serving an argument rather than inhabiting their own story.

What I want to offer is not a more authoritative ending but an answer that can be read. Readers should be able to see why I took another route—and remain free to disagree after following it. The fate of those poems cannot be settled by a few sentences in an essay.
