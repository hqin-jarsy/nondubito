LIVES BEYOND THEIR ENDINGS · READER ESSAYS · 03

After a Fate Is Written, Can a Life Still Change?

The reader is shown an ending before the character reaches it. Do we keep watching her live, or only wait for the prediction to come true?

Han Qin · 2026-10-03

Discusses people and scenes from the first eighty chapters; withholds the new continuation’s crucial turns.

The page we see first

In chapter five, Baoyu enters a dream realm and sees pictures and enigmatic verses in registers of women’s fates. He cannot make sense of them. A first-time reader may not either. As the novel develops, however, names begin to attach themselves to the images. We meet a character again carrying something she does not have: advance knowledge of her end.

This is a powerful arrangement. The household is still lively, but dispersal is already present within it. Someone speaks lightly, and the reader hears an echo she cannot hear.

Such knowledge can also narrow our attention. When Xiangling learns to write poetry, do we see her achievement, or merely another step toward her foretold suffering? When Miaoyu serves tea, are we watching an encounter, or assembling reasons for the disaster we expect?

The verses can deepen our reading. They can also make us impatient. Before a person has reached the event, we are already nodding: exactly as predicted.

What will happen is not what someone deserves

One distinction must come first. A prediction of suffering is not a judgment that suffering is deserved.

Miaoyu, the young Buddhist nun associated with the garden, can be proud and fastidious. She can make others uncomfortable. But moving from I dislike her to her disaster is fitting is no small step. When we turn tragedy into punishment for an irritating personality, we can find ourselves siding with the harm.

Nor is misfortune Xiangling’s deepest identity or a task she must accomplish. The effort she gives to poetry is not retrospectively wasted because something terrible may follow. That life has already happened.

When I say that a written fate should not seal a person’s future, this is where I begin. Not with a promise to save everyone, but with refusing to treat suffering as the answer a person has earned.

Someone has to make the difference

A demanding objection follows. If the writer can save anyone at will, what remains of the book’s foreshadowing?

The objection deserves an answer. Suppose danger has been carefully established, only to disappear when an all-powerful benefactor arrives because the writer cannot bear the outcome. Neither the danger nor the rescue has been taken seriously. The character has exchanged service to destiny for service to the author’s kindness.

In my continuation, events already narrated in the first eighty chapters cannot be erased. With texts that point forward, I try to distinguish an anticipated course of events from a judgment about what those events mean. This is the project’s interpretive approach, not a universal key that conclusively unlocks every verse.

A departure from an anticipated outcome must therefore occur through particular actions. Who decides? Why would this person make that decision? What earlier relationship makes it possible? What does intervention cost, and what consequences survive it? Saying that people have choices cannot answer those questions on its own.

Nor does every change originate in the endangered person. Someone may be exhausted, confined, or unable to make a plea heard. Another person’s intervention may matter decisively. If we explain every disaster by saying its victim did not choose bravely enough, we have found another way to blame her.

Admit what the story cannot accommodate

There is also the problem of incompatible demands. Prophetic verses, songs, and different marginal notes do not always point neatly toward a single outcome. Following one possibility may leave another unfulfilled.

I cannot first write the plot I want and then announce that every surviving hint was waiting for it. Where I do not follow a hint, I need to say so. Where I change a meaning in order to retain a connection, the change has to remain visible. An acknowledged mismatch is more honest than universal agreement manufactured after the event.

This discipline applies to the writer’s claimed generosity too. I cannot stand above the registers and simply award freedom. I can write circumstances in which someone acts, but a reader can still ask: is this genuinely her action, or have you put your preferred answer into her mouth?

You need not know what happens in my thirty new chapters to begin reading this way. Return to Xiangling learning poetry. Put down the register for a moment. Today she has changed a word, and someone beside her is listening carefully. The day itself deserves to be written.
