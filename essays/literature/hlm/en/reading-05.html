LIVES BEYOND THEIR ENDINGS · READER ESSAYS · 05

Why Does a Book Leave More Than One Set of Footprints?

Deleted passages, unfinished sections, and uneven joins deserve attention. They do not automatically tell us who made every mark.

Han Qin · 2026-10-03

Discusses sources and creative choices without revealing crucial rescues, turns or final destinations in the new fiction.

Someone worked here

Near the end of chapter twenty-two, a marginal note introduces a grief different from anything happening inside the story: it says the chapter was unfinished when Xueqin died. The characters are engaged with lantern riddles; beside them, the commentary records a place the writer could no longer return to revise.

Other notes recall deleted passages or drafts once seen and subsequently lost. The opening chapter itself narrates a chain of copying, titles, and revision. Before deciding exactly how much each statement establishes, we can at least notice that the book presents a history more complicated than one uninterrupted act of composition.

A modern printed volume hides this easily. Its uniform type and continuous pagination make everything appear to have belonged together from the beginning. An unfinished join lets some of the time concealed by the binding become visible again.

I read the making of Dream of the Red Chamber in this light: writing followed by repeated alteration. But recognizing revision is not the same as knowing who made every change, or when.

A join does not explain itself

A character’s age, a family relationship, or an object’s description may fail to align perfectly across a long text. Once readers notice a seam, they naturally ask how it arose. The question is worth pursuing.

It is also a place where an argument can move too quickly. This does not look as though it was written all at once becomes this must be another author’s work; an apparently suitable historical figure then becomes the secret behind the entire novel. Each step requires further evidence. The first observation cannot pay for all the later conclusions.

Likewise, a note reporting a deletion invites us to investigate revision. It does not restore the deleted passage. It may show where a door stood without furnishing the whole room behind it.

Presenting theories of composition therefore need not become a tournament. But neither should competing accounts be treated as equally supported merely because each makes a good story. The reader needs to distinguish a trace in the text, a scholar’s explanation, and a possibility explored in my own writing.

An attractive conjecture can be withdrawn

My working process supplied an example. Two seasonal dates in the novel—the winter solstice and the solar term marking the ripening of grain—suggested a particular historical calendar. I tried connecting them with a historical figure and a chronology of composition. Scattered details suddenly seemed to run along one line.

On recalculation, the dates did not match. I withdrew the conjecture. Its usefulness to my imagination could not entitle it to another improvised explanation that would keep it appearing true.

Even a future discovery of a year matching both dates would establish much less than the original speculation claimed. It might make the story’s calendar compatible with that year. It would not, by itself, establish when the book was completed, still less who wrote it. Calendar, composition, and authorship are separate questions.

Yet some seasonal arrangements in the fiction could remain after the conjecture was withdrawn. They could function as a working calendar without serving as evidence of a historical secret. Rejecting an explanation need not erase every scene it helped organize. What remains must stand on the reasons it actually has.

We can read before every origin is settled

Does all this uncertainty make continuing the novel less defensible? It makes restraint more necessary. It does not leave us with nothing to read.

Whatever changes in our account of composition, the characters have already spoken and acted in the surviving text. Daiyu teaching Xiangling, or Baoyu encountering another person’s concern, does not need a perfected genealogy of authorship before it can matter.

The project accepts layered composition and treats the Cao Xueqin named within the textual tradition as the figure who integrated the book. That states a working position; it does not certify a detailed hidden history. My interpretation of a character must still answer to that character on the page. A conjectured author cannot guarantee it.

I therefore do not want these essays to require a course in identity puzzles before letting a reader enter the Grand View Garden. Sources deserve to be described. What remains unknown deserves to remain unknown.

A book has left many footprints. We can stop to examine them, distinguish some, and reconsider others when new evidence arrives. But when we look up, the people in the story are still waiting. Following the tracks should not make us forget them.
