LIVES BEYOND THEIR ENDINGS · READER ESSAYS · 01

Can an Ending Tell You Who Someone Was?

We remember how Daiyu dies. Before we return to that ending, there is someone waiting for her to teach a lesson.

Han Qin · 2026-10-03

Discusses people and scenes from the first eighty chapters; withholds the new continuation’s crucial turns.

She taught someone to write

Ask about Lin Daiyu, one of the central figures in Dream of the Red Chamber, and the answer may begin at the end: tears, destroyed poems, a love that cannot survive. The final days arrive before the person does.

Set the ending aside and return to the first eighty chapters. Xiangling wants to learn poetry. Daiyu takes the request seriously. She recommends poets, explains how to read, and examines what her pupil produces. When a poem is not good, she says so. Xiangling has not come merely to be encouraged.

Here is a Daiyu who knows something and can teach it. She is exacting because she believes the other person can learn. The familiar description of her as melancholy does not disappear, but it no longer tells us enough.

Nor is Xiangling simply the girl with a tragic history. While learning, she has lines to understand, failures that frustrate her, and the pleasure of finally finding words. We may know what has happened to her. That is different from seeing what she is reaching toward.

A fate is not a person

When discussing a long novel, we need shortcuts. Daiyu is sensitive; Baochai, composed; Xifeng, capable; Xiangling, unfortunate. Such descriptions help us begin. They can also stop us reading. Once the category has been supplied, every scene becomes supporting evidence.

The novel keeps supplying details that are harder to contain. What a person says in company may differ from what matters to them alone. Cruelty and tenderness can occur in the same life. Competence can serve generosity or calculation. The days are wider than the verdict.

This is the starting point of my reading: people have qing. The Chinese word can encompass feeling, attachment, desire, and the ways another life matters to us. I do not use it here to mean that everyone is kind. Jealousy and possessiveness are part of the book too. Having a wound does not entitle someone to wound another person.

What I want to keep in view is simpler. Each person has a life happening from where they stand. Something that looks replaceable to an observer may be indispensable to them. An episode everyone else has forgotten may still be unfinished inside them. They do not begin to exist only when the novel’s young hero, Baoyu, enters the room. Their suffering is not there merely to make his story more moving.

Care has something to learn

Part of Baoyu’s appeal is his willingness to linger over what others dismiss: someone’s tears, a private grief, an attachment that the household considers absurd. He makes time for it. We recognize feeling in that pause.

But caring about someone and understanding them are not the same achievement. Even an eager sympathizer can put his own wishes inside another person’s heart. Of course she would want this, he thinks, before she has had a chance to speak.

My reading asks us to preserve that distance. Concern does not confer the right to finish someone else’s account of herself. She may welcome it or refuse it. She may love you and still have poems, interests, and intentions that are not about you.

That is an interpretation I bring to the book, not a claim that its author, Cao Xueqin, secretly wrote a modern philosophical system. The people are there first. An interpretation has to follow them, rather than lining them up to demonstrate its correctness.

The ending matters more, not less

None of this makes Daiyu’s death less important. It makes the loss harder to reduce. If she represents only doomed love, death completes a theme. If she is also the reader, teacher, irritated companion, and person capable of reconsidering another, what disappears is a life that could have continued in ways no description exhausts.

A happy ending is not an automatic remedy. Rescue, marriage, or universal recognition can be just as mechanical if the writer has not allowed the person to live on the page. What matters is not only the outcome but how the character reaches it, and whether her presence exceeds the job the plot assigns her.

That question first held me when I began writing my own thirty chapters after chapter eighty. We know the household will disperse and much will be lost. Inside those events, how do these people speak, choose, and come to see one another differently?

Before we write Daiyu’s final sentence, Xiangling is still waiting for her lesson. Let her teach it.
