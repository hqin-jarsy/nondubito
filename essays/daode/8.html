大同有余项

Great Convergence, With Remainder

Han Qin (秦汉) · 2026

一个系列写了九篇论文，建起自己的一整套架构之后，终于要转身面对所有问过同样问题的其他传统——道家、康德、Levinas、Ubuntu、佛教、伊斯兰——这时候摆在面前其实只有两条路：把自己讲成对所有传统的校正和升级，或者别的什么。道德律系列第零篇选的是别的什么：不批判，只阐述结构上的异同。它不说"某传统错，SAE对"，只说清楚自己继承了什么、拓展了什么、框架在哪里不一样。评价、取舍，留给读者自己在具体处境里决定。这不是外交辞令式的客气——是同一条纪律的跨文明版本：第一篇说过，14DD（自目的主体）在自己的纪元里做的事情，是那个结构下自然合适的事情，不该被事后追认为道德失败。这里把这条纪律铺得更开：康德的定言命令、儒家的礼乐仁义、佛教的苦集灭道、伊斯兰的kalam与苏菲，每一个在自己的文明和范围内都是精确且充分的阐述。一个框架如果开始把自己讲成凌驾于这些之上的终极版本，那一刻，它自己就已经从涵育滑向了殖民——正是这个系列从第六篇开始最想守住的那条线。

有一件事这个系列做了七篇都没直说：四条道德律，从第二篇起，一直写成"不能不"的形式——不是"应当做X"，是"不能不做X"，一个否定式的构造。这不是修辞选择。道家讲"道可道，非常道"——开篇第一句就是一次否定；佛教中观讲空，讲四句不立：非有，非无，非亦有亦无，非非有非无；印度奥义书讲梵，用的方法是neti neti，非此非彼；伊斯兰苏菲传统里，Ibn 'Arabi讲fanā——自我在真主中的消融，能说的只是自我"不是"什么，说不出它"是"什么；西方神秘主义里Pseudo-Dionysius和Eckhart走的也是这条否定神学的路；到了二十世纪，维特根斯坦把它收成一句话：凡不可说的，必须保持沉默。这几支传统彼此大多没有接触过，却在同一个地方达成了同一个动作：最根本的结构，正面说不出来，只能从"少了它会怎样"这个方向去逼近。道德律的"不能不"，是这同一个古老直觉的一次具体应用。

第四篇讲过物自体不可穷尽——孔子见老子，说"吾今日见老子，其犹龙邪"，那是历史记录里一次具体的互凿正和。这条线往回追，能追得更远。康德讲现象与物自体的区分：人类的言说能把握现象，对物自体只能说它存在，说不出它的内容；叔本华把这个说不尽的物自体，具体化成了"意志"；印度奥义书讲的是梵，讲一个人穷尽一生的言说也说不完它；佛教中观走的是另一条路——不是说有个说不尽的"东西"悬在那里，是说"东西"这个范畴、"自性"这个预设，从根子上就是要拆掉的。这四条线不是同一个答案：康德和叔本华那边，物自体是有，只是够不着；中观那边，压根不该问"有没有"。异同都要说清楚，不必强融成一个结论。但有一点，四条线在完全不商量的情况下，还是走到了同一个姿态：都拒绝让最深的那个东西，被压成一句一个人能说完的话。两个15DD主体（即两个自我立法主体）真诚互凿，凿出的每一面都不是全部——孔子和康德、叔本华、《奥义书》的作者们，说的其实是同一件事的不同侧影。

二十世纪西方伦理学里，有两个人的话，几乎是道德律第一定理的另一种语言。Levinas讲"他者的面容"：他者在伦理上优先于自我，他者的面容本身就在向你要求承担责任，这份责任先于你们之间发生过什么、先于任何互惠。Buber讲"我与你"：人和人之间要么是"我-你"式的相互确认，要么退化成"我-它"式的工具关系。这两条线，跟道德律第一定理——把具体的他者认作目的、不可还原为手段——在本体层面几乎是同一件事的不同说法。不同的地方也要如实说：Levinas把这份优先性讲成不对称的，你对他者的责任先于对等；道德律讲的不能不，是结构上对称的构成性条件，不是单方面欠下的一笔债。这不是谁更深刻，是两条从不同起点出发的阐述，走到了重叠但不完全相同的地方。

祖鲁语里有一句谚语：Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu——我是因为我们在。德斯蒙德·图图和其他Ubuntu哲学的阐述者，把这句话用进了真相与和解委员会的具体实践里：一个人之所以成为人，是通过其他人的承认才成为的，不是与生俱来的属性。这跟第六篇讲的利尚邦——集体15DD是共同体层面涌现出来的，不是个体美德的加总——是同一个方向上的深度共振。不同的地方也值得说清楚：Ubuntu讲的人格，是共同体养成的一个过程，需要时间，是发展性的；道德律讲的15DD，是运作模式的一个切片，随时可以进入，也随时可能退出，不是一次修成就终身持有的身份。一个讲发展，一个讲切片，表面上不一样，其实指向同一个更深的事实：人格从来不是一个人关起门来能独自长成的东西。

说回康德——不是第二篇已经讲过的那次分歧，是另一处，第二篇没提的。《道德形而上学奠基》第三条公式里，康德讲了一个"目的王国"：所有理性存在者，作为目的本身，构成的一个整体。这个概念的对面，是"手段王国"——一切以强制和交换为纽带的配置。道德律继承的正是这一对概念的骨架，往下拓展了康德没有具体展开的部分：目的王国不是理性存在者的一个抽象总和，是多个15DD立法主体在具体共同体里彼此承认之后，实际运作出来的一种配置；它和手段王国不是历史先后的两个阶段，是混合现实里同时存在、此消彼长的两层。上一篇讲的道德庭与14DD法庭的路由，就是这两个王国在利尚邦那种具体处境里，怎么分工的一个切片。

这一篇能碰到的传统，只是很小一部分——道家、佛教、康德、Levinas、Buber、Ubuntu，加上前面几支否定之路。亚里士多德的德性伦理、早期基督教哲学、藏传佛教、印度教内部的各个流派、神道、拉美的解放哲学、京都学派——没点到的，比点到的多得多。这不是疏漏之后的补记，是纪律本身的一部分：跨文明对话到这里先停下，阐述的分量来自对具体关系的诚实描述，不来自覆盖了多大范围。没被谈到的传统，不代表被判定为不相关，只是这一篇、这一次，没有走到那里。

第一篇说过，这个系列以第零篇收尾，而不是第十篇，因为第零篇是元层反思。现在可以把这句话说得更具体：元层反思具体做的事，就是这一篇正在做的这件事——把系列自己走过的路，摆到别人也走过的路旁边，不判谁的路更对，只说清楚哪里重合、哪里分岔。这个姿态本身，就是第一定理和第四定理的一次具体示范：把每一个不同的传统都当作目的本身，而不是拿来证明自己正确的手段；让自己的框架接受质询，而不是躲开质询、自证清白。写道德律，写到这一篇，就是在活出道德律。

道德律系列到这里收尾了。不是因为所有问题都有了答案，是因为这个系列能做的元层动作，做完了。汇合而不消融——大同有余项，这份余项从一开始就没打算被填满，也不该被填满。它留给下一个读到这里、并且愿意把自己的具体处境摆进去的人。

A series that spends nine papers building its own architecture eventually has to turn and face every other tradition that has asked the same questions — Daoism, Kant, Levinas, Ubuntu, Buddhism, Islam — and at that point there are really only two moves available. Paper Zero of the Moral Law series makes the other one: no critique, only the mapping of structural similarity and difference. It never says "this tradition is wrong, SAE is right" — only what it has inherited, what it extends, and where the frameworks diverge. Evaluation and choice are left to the reader, in their own concrete situation. This is not diplomatic courtesy. It is the cross-civilizational version of a discipline the first essay already stated: what 14DD (self-purposing subject) does within its own era is structurally fitting for that era, not a moral failure to be retroactively condemned. Here the same discipline stretches wider. Kant's categorical imperative, Confucian ritual and benevolence, the Buddhist path of suffering and cessation, Islamic kalām and Sufism — each is precise and adequate within its own civilization and scope. A framework that starts narrating itself as the corrected, final version of all of these has, in that instant, already slid from cultivation into colonization — exactly the line this series has been guarding since the sixth essay.

There is something this series has done for seven essays without ever saying it aloud: the four theorems, since the second essay, have all been written as cannot-nots — not "you should do X" but "you cannot not do X," a negative construction. This is not a stylistic choice. Daoism opens with a negation: the dao that can be spoken is not the eternal dao. Buddhist Madhyamaka speaks of emptiness through fourfold negation: not existent, not nonexistent, not both, not neither. The Upanishads approach Brahman through neti neti — not this, not that. In the Sufi tradition, Ibn 'Arabi speaks of fanā, the self's dissolution in the divine — sayable only as what the self is not, never as what it is. Western mysticism, in Pseudo-Dionysius and Eckhart, walks the same path of negative theology. In the twentieth century, Wittgenstein compressed it into one line: whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent. Most of these traditions never touched each other, yet they converge on the same move: the deepest structure cannot be stated positively — it can only be approached by way of what would be missing without it. The moral law's cannot-not is one more concrete instance of this same old intuition.

The fourth essay spoke of the inexhaustibility of the thing-in-itself — Confucius, after meeting Laozi, said "today I saw Laozi; he is like a dragon" — a documented instance of genuine mutual chiseling. That thread runs further back than the fourth essay let on. Kant distinguishes appearance from the thing-in-itself: human articulation can grasp appearance, but of the thing-in-itself it can say only that it exists, never what it contains. Schopenhauer gives that unreachable thing-in-itself a name: will. The Upanishads call it Brahman — something a lifetime of speech could never finish describing. Buddhist Madhyamaka takes a different road entirely: it is not that some inexhaustible "thing" hangs out there waiting — the category of "thing," the assumption of inherent nature itself, is what needs dismantling from the root. These are not the same answer. For Kant and Schopenhauer, the thing-in-itself exists, merely out of reach. For Madhyamaka, the question of existence is the wrong question to ask. The similarities and differences both deserve stating plainly, with no forced synthesis. But there is one thing these four lines arrive at without ever consulting each other: a shared refusal to let the deepest thing collapse into a sentence any single mind could finish. Two 15DD subjects (self-legislating subjects) genuinely chiseling each other carve out faces that are never the whole — and Confucius, Kant, Schopenhauer, and the authors of the Upanishads turn out to be describing different silhouettes of the same thing.

Two thinkers in twentieth-century Western ethics said something that is almost another language for the moral law's first theorem. Levinas speaks of the face of the Other: the Other is ethically prior to the self, and the Other's face itself calls forth a responsibility that precedes whatever has passed between the two of you, prior to any reciprocity. Buber speaks of I-Thou: between people, the relation is either the mutual affirmation of I-Thou or the degraded instrumentality of I-It. Both lines are, at the ontological level, nearly the same statement as the moral law's first theorem — recognizing the specific other as an end, never reducible to a means. The differences deserve honesty too: Levinas frames this priority as asymmetric — your responsibility to the Other precedes equality itself. The moral law's cannot-not is a structurally symmetric constitutive condition, not a debt owed one-sidedly. Neither is the deeper one. They are two articulations, starting from different points, arriving somewhere that overlaps without fully coinciding.

There is a Zulu saying: Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu — I am because we are. Desmond Tutu and other articulators of Ubuntu philosophy carried this into the concrete practice of truth and reconciliation: a person becomes a person through the recognition of other people, not as an attribute they are simply born with. This resonates deeply with what the sixth essay said about Le Chambon — collective 15DD as a community-level emergent property, not the sum of individual virtues. The difference is worth stating too: Ubuntu describes personhood as something cultivated by community over time, a developmental process. The moral law's 15DD is a slice of an operating mode — one can enter it at any moment, and just as easily step back from it. It is never a status earned once and held for life. One account speaks in terms of development, the other in terms of a slice — different on the surface, but pointing at the same deeper fact: personhood was never something a person could grow alone, behind a closed door.

Back to Kant — not the divergence the second essay already covered, but somewhere else, something the second essay never mentioned. In the third formulation of the Groundwork, Kant describes a Kingdom of Ends: the totality of all rational beings, each as an end in themselves. Set against this is a Kingdom of Means — any configuration bound together by coercion and exchange. The moral law series inherits the skeleton of this pairing, then extends what Kant left undeveloped: the Kingdom of Ends is not an abstract sum of rational beings — it is a configuration that actually operates once multiple 15DD subjects, within a real community, recognize one another. It is not a historical stage that comes after the Kingdom of Means. In mixed reality the two coexist, each waxing as the other wanes. The routing between the moral court and the 14DD court described in the previous essay is one concrete slice of how these two kingdoms divide the work, in a situation as specific as Le Chambon's.

The traditions this essay manages to touch are a small fraction — Daoism, Buddhism, Kant, Levinas, Buber, Ubuntu, and the handful of via-negativa threads above. Aristotelian virtue ethics beyond communitarianism, early Christian philosophy, Tibetan Buddhist philosophy, the various schools within Hinduism, Shinto, Latin American liberation philosophy, the Kyoto School — what goes untouched far outnumbers what gets named. This is not an oversight to be apologized for. It is part of the discipline itself: the cross-civilizational dialogue stops here, and its force comes from the honesty of the concrete relations it does describe, not from how much ground it covers. A tradition left undiscussed is not thereby judged irrelevant — this essay, this time, simply did not get there.

The first essay said that this series closes with Paper Zero, not Paper Ten, because Paper Zero is the meta-layer reflection. Now that claim can be made more concrete: what the meta-layer reflection actually does is exactly what this essay has just done — laying the path the series has walked next to paths others have walked, judging neither as more correct, only stating plainly where they overlap and where they part. This posture is itself a concrete demonstration of the first and fourth theorems: treating every different tradition as an end in itself, not as a means for proving oneself right; letting one's own framework stand under interrogation rather than dodging it to declare itself innocent. Writing the moral law, by the time it reaches this essay, has become living it.

The Moral Law series closes here. Not because every question has an answer, but because the meta-layer move this series was capable of making has now been made. Convergence without absorption — great convergence, with remainder — and that remainder was never meant to be filled in, and should not be. It is left for whoever reads this next, and is willing to bring their own concrete situation into it.
