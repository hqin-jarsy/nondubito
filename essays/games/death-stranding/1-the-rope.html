The Rope

绳

Sep 4, 2026Han Qin (秦汉)Independent English edition中文原作 · 简 / 繁

Edition note: An independent English rewriting of the Chinese argument for English-language readers. Plot claims have been checked and interpretive claims are marked as such.

Death Stranding begins with a man walking.

Sam Porter Bridges carries a tower of cargo across a country that no longer functions as a country. There are mountains, rivers, ruined highways, and rain that ages whatever it touches. Somewhere beyond them is a person he may never meet, waiting underground for medicine, tools, or a box of old photographs.

Most heroic games ask what you can defeat. This one asks whether you can deliver what someone else needs without dropping it on the way.

A world made dangerous by death

After the Death Stranding, the world of the living overlaps with the world of the dead. An unattended corpse undergoes necrosis and becomes a Beached Thing. A BT that consumes a living person can produce a voidout, obliterating the surrounding area. Timefall makes exposure itself dangerous by accelerating age and decay.

The sequence matters. A corpse is not simply a bomb waiting to explode, but death has acquired a public afterlife. The dead must be collected and incinerated quickly, not because grief has vanished, but because survivors are denied the time to grieve first.

Under these conditions, retreat becomes sensible. BTs, timefall, and repeated voidouts drive people into isolated underground settlements. No single villain has to persuade them to distrust proximity. Each person can make the reasonable choice to stay safe, and the accumulation of reasonable choices produces a shattered society.

Death Stranding does have villains. Yet its loneliness precedes them. The first antagonist is a world in which being together has become materially risky.

A hero whose work is carrying

Sam’s primary verb is not shooting. It is bearing.

The player studies contour lines, distributes weight, tests a river’s current, searches for a foothold, and grabs a shoulder strap when the cargo begins to pull Sam sideways. The destination does not move closer because the story has become urgent. The load does not become lighter because Sam is important. He has to carry it there.

Combat exists, but lethal force creates an unusual obligation: a dead enemy leaves a corpse that must be transported to an incinerator. Ignore that responsibility and necrosis may lead to catastrophe, including a game-over voidout. Death cannot be converted into a momentary reward and then cleared from the map. It remains as work someone has to do.

That is one reason the game can feel so strange. It treats consequence not as a cutscene after the action, but as additional weight.

Stick and rope

Hideo Kojima often invokes a metaphor from Kobo Abe’s short story “The Rope.” The stick keeps bad things away; the rope holds good things close.

Games are naturally good at sticks. A blow lands, a health bar falls, an enemy disappears, and success is immediately legible. Ropes are harder to design. Their effects are delayed and distributed. You may build a bridge without ever seeing the person who later crosses it.

Death Stranding sets itself the difficult task of making the rope playable.

This does not mean that every stick is evil. Sam carries tools for restraint as well as ropes for climbing and securing cargo. The sharper question is whether a medium built around eliminating obstacles can make room for another pleasure: leaving a path for someone you do not know.

The cost of slowness

The familiar complaint that Death Stranding is a delivery simulator is not evidence that the critic failed to understand it. The game is slow, repetitive, and often lonely. For long stretches, a player really is watching one man struggle up a slope.

Its slowness nevertheless has a purpose. If traversing a mountain were a button press, arriving would possess no weight. The awkward stumble, the river that proves too strong, and the exhausting last climb restore distance to a landscape that most games turn into scenery.

The game pays for that commitment. Some players leave; those who remain can feel genuine relief when the cargo terminal finally comes into view. Neither response invalidates the other.

What strangers leave behind

Then, in the middle of an empty region, Sam finds a ladder precisely where he needs one. A climbing anchor hangs from the dangerous side of a ridge. A bridge crosses the river. A charged vehicle waits beside the road.

These objects often come from other players. Death Stranding’s asynchronous system allows structures built in one world to appear in another. You cannot meet their makers, seize their territory, or damage the builders’ worlds. You can remove a structure from your own world, contribute materials, or offer the game’s simplest currency: a like.

Assistance becomes a remarkably light relationship. No stranger must prove worthy of help. The builder does not need to know who benefited. Two extra minutes in one person’s journey can save twenty in another’s.

Nor does every generous object begin in pure altruism. A player may place a ladder for personal use, only for it to become part of somebody else’s route. Cooperation does not require saints. It requires a world in which private acts can leave usable remainders for strangers.

The two faces of a network

Sam’s national mission is to connect isolated settlements to the chiral network. Communication returns; knowledge can be shared; scattered people can cooperate again.

But Death Stranding refuses to make connection an automatically virtuous word. The network has another foundation, hidden beneath its promise of unity. Some bridges are freely left by strangers. Others have someone built into the support.

Who is being connected? What makes the connection possible? Who receives new power, and who has been reduced to infrastructure?

The next essay turns to the small pod carried against Sam’s chest—the one everyone has taught him to call equipment.

一个男人在走路。

他背上的货摞得比人还高，走在山石、河流和时间雨之间。他要把这些东西送到几十公里外，那里也许只有一个住在地下、从未与他见过面的人。

整个游戏，他反复做的就是这件事。

一、这个世界为什么散了

死亡搁浅以后，生者与死者的世界碰到了一起。尸体若未及时焚化，会亡人化，成为搁浅者；搁浅者吞噬活人，才会造成虚空噬灭。天空还落下时间雨，使接触到的事物迅速老化。

所以一个人的死亡不再只属于他自己。遗体必须被立刻运走，不是因为剩下的人不悲伤，而是因为他们没有时间先悲伤。

这套危险，加上搁浅者、时间雨和一次次虚空噬灭，把人赶进地下。聚居不再天然意味着安全，公路与城市也不再当然存在。每个人选择躲起来都很合理；这些合理选择叠在一起，世界就散了。

这不是说游戏没有反派。它当然有。但在任何反派出现之前，隔绝已经写进了日常：别人可能带来帮助，也可能带来一具来不及处理的尸体。人不必仇恨彼此，也会越来越远。

二、他的本行是背东西

山姆是送货员。他看地形、选路线、分配重量，过河时试水流，爬坡时找落脚点；货摞高了，他还要用双手把失去的重心拉回来。

许多游戏让主角靠清除障碍前进。《死亡搁浅》却把“承担”做成了最基本的动作：目的地不会因为你厉害就靠近，货物也不会因为情节紧张就变轻。你只能把它背到那里。

山姆也会战斗，但杀人会制造新的遗体。玩家若用致命手段，就要承担运尸焚化的麻烦；若放着不管，亡人化可能招来虚空噬灭，乃至游戏结束。这里的杀戮不是按一下按钮便从世界上抹去一个敌人，而是一笔留下来的后果。

三、棒和绳

小岛秀夫常借用安部公房《绳》里的比喻：棒把坏东西赶开，绳把好东西留住。

“棒”很适合游戏。击中、掉血、倒下，反馈清楚，胜负即时可见。“绳”难得多：你搭一座桥，可能永远不知道后来谁从上面走过。

《死亡搁浅》偏偏想做绳。

这并不表示它否定棒。山姆身上既有攀登与固定货物的绳索，也有用来制服敌人的武器。真正的问题是：当世界已经习惯靠排除危险维持秩序，还有没有一种玩法，能让“给陌生人留下一条路”也成为值得做的事？

四、慢不是免罪牌

很多人玩不下去，说它是送货模拟器，说前十个小时无聊。这批评不能用“你没看懂”打发。游戏确实慢，确实反复，也确实让玩家花很长时间独自赶路。

但慢在这里不是装饰。若翻山越岭只需按一下按钮，“送到了”就不会有重量。一次狼狈的跌倒、一条冲不过去的河、一段在雪坡上的挪动，把路重新变成了路。

作品为自己的选择付了代价：它可能让一部分玩家离开，也让留下的人在抵达时真正松一口气。两件事同时成立。

五、别人放在那里的东西

路上偶尔会出现一架正好够你爬上悬崖的梯子，一根垂在险坡边的绳，一座跨过河的桥，或者一辆还有电的车。

它们往往来自别的玩家。你们不会见面，也不能在同一个画面里并肩走，但他在自己的世界里留下的东西，可能被传到你的世界。你可以点赞，也可以把那件设施从自己的世界移除；你却不能借此闯进他的世界、摧毁他的劳动。

于是帮助变成了一种很轻的关系。对方不必知道你是谁，你也不必先证明自己值得被帮。陌生人多花的两分钟，可能让你少绕二十分钟。

更有意思的是，许多设施起初是为自己造的。善意不必纯粹到毫无私心：一架为自己搭的梯子，也可以顺便成为别人的路。连接不是要求人先变成圣人，而是让各自的行动留下可被别人接住的部分。

六、网的两面

山姆的任务，是把一个个地下结点接入开罗尔网络。通信恢复，资料可以共享，分散的人重新有了协作的可能。

可是这张网并不只由梯子、桥和陌生人的点赞构成。它还有一层被藏在基础设施里面的代价。

《死亡搁浅》最好的地方，正是不让“连接”成为一个自动正确的词。谁在连接？靠什么连接？连接给了谁力量，又把谁留在桥墩里面？

下一篇写那件被挂在山姆胸前、被所有人称作设备的东西。
