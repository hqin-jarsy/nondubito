代价就是余项

Cost Is Remainder

Han Qin (秦汉)

一次行动在自己账上留下什么

《第一批判》问每一次划界：管到哪里，漏下什么。它的回答是一张账：每一刀都有偏差，偏差不属于界的哪一侧，“属于这一刀”。第二批判把同样的问题搬到实践一侧来问：一个人照自己的法做了一件事，这件事在他自己的账上留下了什么？

回答先说在前头：留下的是代价，而代价就是余项——这一次落刀没有收纳进去的那一部分。代价是自己的代价；接受一个权力就有代价；人们常常忽视它，不是因为它小，是因为它不出声。

为什么就是余项？照自己的法做一件事，就是在一个处境里落一刀：做这个，不做那些；为这个，不为那些。处境里摆着的，总比一刀收得进的多。行动论把一次判断之后剩下的东西分成三种，说“只有第三种是这次判断的余项”——与这次要做的事相关、却不在这次处理之内的那一种；两次划线在一件事上相撞，“没有走的那一条不因此取消”。代价，是这些剩余落在立法者自己账上的那一面：他的法里有的东西，这一刀没有收进去——有的是没去处理，有的是掂量过、让掉了。

一次失约

举一个例子。一个人为了赶一件要紧的事，失了与朋友的约。朋友的失望是真的；那作为朋友那里的事，是一件后果，有多重由朋友自己说。

他自己账上记的是另一样：他的法里有一条，答应了的事要做到，这一次没有收进去。他掂量过，让掉了它。同一次失约，若他的法里本没有这一条，朋友的失望照样是后果，却不是他的代价。

代价跟着法走，所以它只在立法者自己的账上；两个人各有各的法，也就没有一本共同的账，可以拿来比谁的代价更重。《第一批判》早有一句定义级的话：“分支由法定义，代价按目的结算”。

一笔可以了，总账不清零

道歉，补偿，改日再约，可以回应这一笔——朋友那里，这件事也许就过去了。可每一样回应都是新的一刀，各有它收不进的东西。《第一批判》说过：“消灭余项的动作，恰恰在生产余项。”没有一刀能宣称自己什么都没留下；谁这样说，幻相论里有一宗等着他，叫冒领免费。

这一点决定了这部书怎样说话。代价若只是预告后果——你这样做，会有什么落到你头上——说出它就可能变成从外面施压。代价若是价格，说出代价就是拿一把公共的尺去量它；价值论判过，余项不受这样的量：“凡标价皆预设公共尺与市场”，“无价不是价格的极大值，是不在定价域内”。所以，说出代价，只是指给一个人看：这一刀，在你自己的法里有哪一处没有收进去。对不对，只有他拿自己的法去对；担不担，也只有他自己定。

所以，不强制、只说代价，不是一种软一点的强制。余项不是惩罚，也不是勋章；它是缺口，也是把手。《第一批判》说，余项“给下一轮以条件，不施迫使”；行动论说得一样准：“余项给的是可能，不是推力。”下一刀从哪里落，常常就看上一刀没收进的是什么。

四种入账

《第一批判》说，一次划界的偏差以四种基本方式入账；第二批判借这四个名目来分读实践一侧。收纳之亏：行动收纳不尽法。一条法说的，比任何一串行动做到的都多；没有人能时时刻刻合于 15DD。道德律写过同一件事的另一面：“完美其实比不完美更可疑。”只有这一格不能由同类的处理消去：“相可移，总账不空。”哪一次做对了，法与行之间那道缝也不会合上。

错置之亏：把外来的认作己出，或把己出的认作外来。前一半，价值论写作自我殖民：一份外部的担保被当作自己的构成已经完成，它的判定只能回头看。后一半反过来：一个人以为自己只是照规矩办，回头才看出那正是他自己的法在做事。两种错放，代价一样：那一段日子里，他没有看清自己在为谁付出。

两属：两项要求在同一处不能共同做成——同一个下午，两件都该做的事做不成两件。补一个条件、补一条规则，都消不掉它，“缺口只是搬到了清单的下一格”。让掉一项，不等于放弃那一项：“让是行动次序上的事，退出是构成上的事。”判外：自己的法没有管到的地方——“判外不是判错，是无判。”可以改法去管到它，也可以照实记下：这件事，我的法不判；判外的代价，就是那一段无判，在那里没有自己的法可以凭。

代价与后果，分两栏

《第一批判》把余项与反面分开：余项属于这一刀，反面属于界的另一侧。实践一侧照此分开代价与后果。代价，是这一刀在自己账上的亏欠；后果，是世界与别人对这件事的回应。价值论给过这一分的形状：裁断，他实际选的那件事，他把它做完，以及这件事在世上留下的后果，是“四件事”，“各有各的时间，各有各的主词”。

行动论说得最直接。一个人没做到的那件事，“它也不抹掉后果：那个团队多花的那一个季度，是真的”。所以，那个改了数字的人，离开的价记在他自己的账上；那个团队多花的一个季度，是后果，不由他那一笔代价结清，也不因为他付了代价就一笔勾销。

还有一点：主体在移动。《第一批判》写过，每一凿之后，“凿者的所处已非凿前的所处”。代价因此常常落在后来的自己身上：今天说出口的一句话，明天的自己才付它的价。这里藏着一种冒领的镜像：今天的我替明天的我签下代价，好像明天的那个人不会变。今天的承诺延伸到明天，并不自动冒领；把它当成无需再问的永久授权，才是幻相论要查的情形。

What a Deed Leaves on One’s Own Books

The First Critique asked of every demarcation how far it reaches and what it lets slip, and answered with a ledger: every cut deviates, and the deviation belongs to neither side of the line — it “belongs to this cut.” The second critique asks the same question on the practical side. A person has done something by his own law. What has that deed left on his own books?

The answer is stated up front: it leaves cost, and cost is remainder — the part this cut did not take in. Cost is one’s own cost. Accepting a power has a cost. People often overlook it, not because it is small, but because it makes no sound.

Why remainder? To act by one’s law is to cut a situation: this, not those; for this, not for those. A situation always holds more than one cut can take in. Action Theory sorted what is left after a judgment into three kinds and said “only the third kind is the remainder of this judgment” — what bears on the thing to be done yet falls outside what is handled this time. And when two lines collide on one matter, “the one not followed is not cancelled by that.” Cost is the face these leftovers show on the lawgiver’s own books: something his law holds that this cut did not take in — some never handled, some weighed and yielded.

A Broken Appointment

An example. To get an urgent thing done, a man breaks an appointment with a friend. The friend’s disappointment is real; as something on the friend’s side, it is a consequence, and how heavy it is the friend alone can say.

His own books record something else. His law contains the rule that a promise made is a promise kept, and this time that rule was not taken in. He weighed it and yielded it. Take the same broken appointment where his law held no such rule: the friend is just as disappointed, and that is still a consequence — but it is not his cost.

So cost follows the law, and stands only on the lawgiver’s own books. Two people each have their own law, so there is no common ledger in which to compare whose cost is heavier. The First Critique had already put it in a line with the weight of a definition: “branches are defined by laws, costs are settled against ends.”

One Entry Settles; the Ledger Never Empties

An apology, amends, another appointment can answer this entry, and on the friend’s side the matter may well be over. But every answer is a new cut, with things of its own it cannot take in. The First Critique: “The act of eliminating the remainder is precisely an act that produces remainder.” No cut can claim to have left nothing behind; whoever claims it will meet a case in the book’s chapter on illusion, the false claim of costlessness.

This settles how the book speaks. If cost were a forecast of consequences — do this, and here is what lands on you — stating it could become pressure from outside. If it were a price, stating it would mean measuring it against a common yardstick; but Value Theory had already ruled that remainder submits to no such measure: “every pricing presupposes a common measure and a market”; “to be without price is not to be the maximum of price; it is to be outside the domain of pricing.” So to state a cost is only to point: here is where, in your own law, this cut did not take something in. Whether that is right only he can check, against his law; whether to bear it only he can decide.

That is why no coercion, only the cost stated is not a softer form of coercion. Remainder is neither punishment nor medal; it is a gap, and also a handle. The First Critique: it “gives the next round its conditions, and exerts no compulsion.” Action Theory: “A remainder gives possibility, not a push.” Where the next cut falls often depends on what the last one did not take in.

Four Ways of Entering the Ledger

The First Critique said a demarcation’s deviation enters the ledger in four basic modes, and the second critique borrows the four names for the practical side. The intake-deficit: action never takes in the whole of the law. A law says more than any run of deeds; no one accords with 15DD at every moment. Moral Law put the other face of it: “Perfection, indeed, would be more suspicious than imperfection.” This deficit alone cannot be cleared by treatment of its own kind: “the modes can shift; the ledger does not empty.” Getting it right once does not close the seam between law and deed.

The misplacement-deficit: taking the foreign for one’s own, or one’s own for the foreign. The first half is what Value Theory calls self-colonization — an outside warrant taken as constitution already completed — and its verdict can only look back. The other half runs the reverse way: a man believes he is only following the rules, and sees afterwards that it was his own law at work. Either way the cost is the same: through that stretch, he did not see clearly for whom he was paying.

The double-hit: two demands that cannot both be met in one place — one afternoon, two things that both ought to be done, and only one can be. Adding a condition or a rule does not remove it: “the gap has merely moved to the next line of the list.” Yielding one item is not giving it up: “Yielding belongs to the ordering of action, exit to constitution.” Out-of-field: where one’s own law does not reach — “out-of-field is not error but no-verdict.” One can revise the law to reach it, or record that on this matter one’s law is silent; the cost is that stretch with no law of one’s own to go by.

Cost and Consequence: Two Columns

The First Critique kept the remainder apart from the reverse side of the line; the practical side keeps cost apart from consequence in the same way. Cost is what this cut owes on one’s own books; consequence is how the world and other people respond to the deed. Value Theory gives the split its shape: the adjudication, the thing chosen, its doing, and what followed in the world “are four things. They have their own times, their own subjects, and their own settlements.”

Action Theory puts it most plainly. Of the thing a man failed to do: “Nor does it erase the consequences: the extra quarter the other team spent on the problem is real.” So for the man who changed the number, the cost of leaving goes on his own books; the team’s extra quarter is consequence — his entry of cost does not settle it, and his having paid the cost does not write it off.

One more feature: the subject is moving. After each stroke, the First Critique said, “where the chiseler stands is no longer where it stood before the stroke.” So cost often falls on one’s later self: a word said today is paid for by tomorrow’s self. The mirror of a false claim lurks here — today’s self signing for a cost on behalf of tomorrow’s, as though tomorrow’s person would not change. A promise reaching into tomorrow is not thereby a false claim; treating it as a permanent authorization that never needs asking again is a case the book examines among its false claims (Essay 07).
