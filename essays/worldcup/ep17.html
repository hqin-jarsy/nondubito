第十七篇　柏林:写好了的结局,与一个不肯照着演的人

Essay 17: Berlin — An Ending Already Written, and the Man Who Wouldn't Play Along

（切入点:2006年德国世界杯）
(Entry point: the 2006 Germany World Cup)
Han Qin (秦汉)

一、写好了的结局

2006年的世界杯,本该有一个写好了的结局。

那一年,齐达内三十四岁,早已宣布这届世界杯打完就退役。八年前,1998年,他在巴黎用两个头球,为法国捧起了第一座世界杯。八年后,他从退役的边缘被请回国家队,带着一支赛前并不被看好的法国,一轮一轮往上走,淘汰西班牙,淘汰巴西,淘汰葡萄牙,一直走到了柏林的决赛。一个天才的最后一届,以一座冠军收尾,然后退回传奇里去,这个故事太完美了,完美到还没等球踢完,那个结局就已经被人写好了,摆在那里,只等他上场去把它兑现。

讲述总想抢在事实前面。一件大事还没发生,关于它的故事,往往已经先一步写好了。齐达内那届世界杯,几乎从一开始,就被安放进一个早已备好的模子里,一个老英雄归来,善始善终,把自己的生涯画上一个圆满句号的模子。所有人都在等那个句号落下。

这种抢先,不是哪一个人的心血来潮,而是讲述这件事天生的脾性。一个故事,只有当它有头有尾,能收成一个漂亮的形状时,才最让人踏实。而一个老英雄归来,在生涯的尽头再赢一次,然后功成身退,退回众人的记忆里去,是所有形状里最讨喜的一种。它太顺了,顺到人们几乎是迫不及待地,要把那个还没踢出来的结局,提前填进这个现成的模子里去。于是齐达内那一整届,几乎是被这样一个模子罩着走完的。人们在看台上看的,在评论席上说的,在报纸上写的,与其说是他这一场究竟踢得怎么样,不如说是在盯着他,等他把那个早已备好的句号,一笔一划,亲手描出来。剧本已经写完,只等主角走完最后一步。

可事实,偏偏不肯照着写好的剧本演。

2006年7月9日,柏林奥林匹克体育场,决赛,意大利对法国。第七分钟,齐达内主罚点球,皮球击中横梁下沿,弹进网内,那是一记挑到极致的勺子点球,轻巧,傲慢,是只有他那种级别的球员才敢在决赛场上开的玩笑。第十九分钟,马特拉齐接皮尔洛的角球,头球扳平。此后九十分钟里,双方再没能分出高下,一比一,拖进加时。加时赛里,齐达内有一记头球攻门,被布冯奋力托出横梁,那一下,几乎就是那个完美结局的临门一脚。

然后,是第一百一十分钟。

齐达内在远离球的地方,忽然转身,用头猛地撞向马特拉齐的胸口。马特拉齐倒地。主裁走过来,出示了一张红牌。齐达内被直接罚下,低着头,从奖杯旁边走过,走进球员通道,消失了。那是他职业生涯的最后一场球,也是最后一幕。终场,双方仍是一比一,点球大战,意大利五比三胜出。法国唯一罚丢的是特雷泽盖,他的点球,也击中了横梁。格罗索罚进最后一个,意大利捧起了大力神杯。

那个被写好的完美结局,在离终点只有几步的地方,被写它的那个人,亲手撞得粉碎。

二、第一百一十分钟

先把那一下红牌本身说清楚。

一个球员在球场上对另一个球员施暴,该被红牌罚下,这是清清楚楚的。齐达内那记头槌,动作就在那里,几亿人看得一清二楚,它是一次暴力行为,按规则,就该是一张红牌。构在这里,干净利落地闭合了那个看得见的动作。

可紧接着,一个说不清的问题冒了出来:主裁到底是怎么知道的。

那次头槌,发生在远离皮球的地方,主裁埃利松多当时未必看得见。转播画面反复回放了那一下,于是许多观众,许多评论员,连法国主帅多梅内克,都起了疑心,怀疑裁判组是先看了场边的电视回放,才回过头来补出这张红牌的。里皮赛后也提到,法国方面质疑过这里有没有视频介入。这个疑问要紧,因为它触到了一条当时的红线,那届世界杯,视频只允许用于赛后的纪律处理,绝不允许在比赛中介入裁判的当场决定。如果那张红牌是看了回放才补的,它就越了界。

国际足联赛后给出的正式说法,是另一个版本。国际足联在7月13日关于纪律程序的声明里说,齐达内第一百一十分钟那次暴力行为,是由第四官员在场边的位置直接观察到的,声明还特别注明,未使用监视器,第四官员随后通过通信系统,通知了主裁。国际足联也在第一时间否认了比赛中动用视频回放辅助判罚的说法,并解释,场边那位掌握电视监视器的官员虽然看得见画面,却无权介入,第四官员也无权接触回放。按当届的规则条文,第四官员本就有一项职责,是向主裁指出裁判和助理裁判视线之外发生的暴力行为,最终的决定权,仍在主裁手里。所以只要那次施暴确实是第四官员亲眼所见,再经耳机通报给主裁,这张红牌,在规则的框架内,是有依据的。

于是这里,又出现了那个熟悉的裂缝。红牌罚得对,这一点没有争议,齐达内确实施了暴。可这张红牌是怎么来的,是第四官员的肉眼直击,还是有人看了回放,这中间,隔着一层外人无论如何也钻不进去的耳机通信。国际足联的案卷说是直击,可决赛现场那副耳机里,当时到底传了些什么,外界没有办法独立地重放一遍。就正式的规则解释而言,国际足联并未认定存在违规使用视频,案子到此为止;可公众心里那点怀疑,也就一直悬在那里,谁也消不掉。看得见那一记头槌,却定不了那张红牌背后,究竟发生过什么。

这一层够不着,和从前那些又不太一样。1966年温布利那个球,是皮球太快,人眼跟不上,看不清;而这一回,画面清清楚楚,头槌被拍到了,红牌被拍到了,连第四官员在场边站的位置也被拍到了,唯独那副耳机里,那几秒钟到底传了句什么话,是没有任何一台摄像机拍得到的。它不藏在黑暗里,也不是快得看不清,而是发生在一条只有当事的几个人才听得见,事后谁也没法再按一遍重放键的线路上。画面堆得越来越满,可就在这堆得满满当当的影像正中央,偏偏留着这么一小块,任你怎么放大,怎么慢放,也补不齐的空白。技术能拍下越来越多看得见的东西,却拍不下一句只在耳机里响过一次的话。

而更要紧的是,这张红牌,无论它是怎么来的,都已经太迟了。它能把施暴的人罚下场,却没法把那个被撞碎的结局,重新拼回去。构可以对一个动作做出裁决,却没法让那个动作,当作没有发生过。谢幕已经毁了,红牌追不回它。

这里头还藏着一个说不清是残酷还是别的什么的巧合。八年前在巴黎,齐达内正是用他的头,顶进两个球,替法国赢下了第一座世界杯,那颗头,是那一夜整个法兰西的英雄。八年后在柏林,还是那颗头,一记撞向对手的胸口,把他自己撞出了场,也把那个本该圆满的结局,撞出了一道谁也没料到的裂痕。同一颗头,一次成就了他,一次葬送了他;先把他写进了一个最辉煌的故事,又在另一个故事的最后一页,亲手划破了那份辉煌。构可以记下这颗头做过的每一件事,那两个头球,那一记头槌,全都留着清清楚楚的影像,却没有办法告诉你,同样一颗头,为什么会在两个最大的舞台上,走向两个正好相反的结局。

三、那句听不真的话

齐达内为什么要撞人。这个问题,牵出了这一届最需要小心分层的一条线。

能确证的,先是齐达内自己的说法。他在赛后第一次系统地接受法国电视采访时说,让他做出那个反应的,是对方对他母亲和姐姐反复的侮辱,但他没有当场复述那句原话。几个月后,马特拉齐在2006年9月接受采访时,承认了事情的经过:是他先去拉拽齐达内的球衣,齐达内对他说,你要是想要这件球衣,赛后我给你,而他回了一句,说自己更想要的是齐达内的姐姐。马特拉齐当时也承认,把人家姐姐扯进来,不是一件好事。到2007年8月,他在一家意大利电视杂志的采访里,第一次公开给出了他所称的那句准确的话,内容涉及齐达内的姐姐。又过了些年,到2016年,他再一次概括说,自己当时提到了对方的姐姐,但没有提到他的母亲。

把这些叠起来,最稳妥的写法只能是这样:马特拉齐本人最终承认,他的言语涉及齐达内的姐姐;而齐达内本人说,对方反复侮辱了他的母亲和姐姐。两个人的说法,在到底有没有牵扯到母亲这一点上,并不一致。公开的材料里,没有一条足以压倒一切的同步录音,被公之于众。

再往细里追,那一句当场的原话,究竟是哪一句,就成了争议本身。2006年7月,好几家报纸掀起过一场读唇的竞赛,请人对着画面,去还原马特拉齐当时到底说了什么。可这些还原互相打架,而且绝大多数,并不是基于一段可以公开复核的完整影像声轨。一直到很晚,当事人自己的说法,也只能坐实那一句涉及姐姐的侮辱,没有办法把当年的每一个字,都抬升成无可争议的史实。

这里头有一个绕不过去的死结。要还原那句话,拢共只有两条路,一条是听当事人自己讲,一条是找出那段能对口型,能听声音的完整记录。可当事人是这桩事的一方,他讲的,永远是他愿意讲的那个版本,别人没法拿它当铁证;而那段完整的影像声轨,又从头到尾没有被公开过。两条路都走不到头,那句话于是永远卡在了一个尴尬的位置上:它一定被说出来过,几亿人都亲眼看见那两片嘴唇在动,可它具体是哪几个字,却谁也没有办法把它钉死成一句史实。看得见两片嘴唇在动,和听得清它们究竟吐出了什么,原来中间也隔着一道跨不过去的坎。

而在这条线的最外一层,漂着一个流传最广的传说,说马特拉齐当场骂的,是一句种族主义或者恐怖主义的侮辱。2006年之后,一些英国的小报,把诸如恐怖分子之子这样的版本,当成读唇的结论去传播。马特拉齐否认了。到2008年,路透社报道,《太阳报》已经就那句所谓种族主义辱骂的说法,向马特拉齐道了歉。就今天能核实的史实而言,这类恐怖分子或种族主义的定句,不该被写成已经坐实的事情。

这又是那台叙事的机器,在做它最擅长的事。一个天才,在生涯的最后一场球里,做出了一件震惊世界的失常举动,人们急于知道,到底是什么话,能把这样一个人逼到那个地步。因为那句话,决定了这件事该怎么定性:如果那是一句不堪入耳的侮辱,齐达内的暴怒就多了几分可以理解;如果那句话没那么重,他这一撞,就更像是一次无法辩解的冲动。人们太想给这个失常的举动,配上一个足够分量的理由了,于是当画面没法给出答案时,读唇的竞赛就上了场,一个个越传越具体的版本被造了出来,直到有人不得不为它道歉。头槌那一下,几亿人看得清清楚楚;可撞出这一下的那句话,到最后,谁也没能真正听真。看得见,依旧不等于听得清。

四、他不肯说后悔

那个撞碎了自己完美结局的人,在赛后说的话,比那一撞还要难以塞进任何一个模子。

齐达内在决赛后三天公开受访,说了两句最要紧,也最能准确回溯的话。第一句,是向孩子们道歉,他说,他想请求所有看了这场比赛的孩子原谅。第二句,是他拒绝了后悔这个词,他说,如果他后悔,那就等于承认对方说得对。他同时说,自己没有任何借口,但对方那些关于母亲和姐姐的话,反复说了好几次。他没有在采访里复述那句原话,也没有向马特拉齐本人致歉。

这两句话,是一个活人,对着一个替他写好的剧本,给出的回答。那个剧本要的,是一个善始善终的英雄,是一座举过头顶的奖杯,是一个可以让所有人心满意足地合上书本的圆满句号。而他偏偏不肯给。他为那些孩子道歉,是因为他知道自己在球场上做了一个坏的示范;可他拒绝为那一撞本身后悔,因为在他心里,后悔就意味着,那些侮辱他母亲和姐姐的话是对的,而这一点,他无论如何不肯认。他宁可毁掉那个完美的句号,也要守住这个。剧本需要他做一件工具,一件用来把故事送到圆满结局的工具;他偏要做一个人,一个有母亲有姐姐,一个到了那个当口宁可掀翻棋盘的人。

这一点,恰恰是那台想把一切都写成故事的机器,最无可奈何的地方。它可以预先写好结局,可以提前收拢选票,可以把一个人捏塑成它需要的任何形状,英雄也罢,莽夫也罢。可它算漏了一件事,被写进故事里的那一个,不是一枚可以随意摆布的符号,而是一个有血有肉,有自己的痛处,有自己咽不下的那口气的人。当剧本要求他含笑谢幕时,他偏偏想起了母亲和姐姐;当所有人都眼巴巴盼着那个圆满的句号落下时,他心里那个更要紧的东西,盖过了头顶那座唾手可得的奖杯。故事是可以被提前写好的,可写故事的人,总算漏了同一件事:故事里的那个人,是活的,是会自己动的,他随时可能在最后一页,做出一件谁也没替他写进去的事。

而在球场之外,还有一件事,把那个被写好的结局的荒诞,衬得更清楚。

那一届世界杯的金球奖,评的是本届赛事的最佳球员,由报道这届世界杯的记者投票产生。结果是,齐达内得了两千零一十二分,卡纳瓦罗得了一千九百七十七分,皮尔洛得了七百一十五分。齐达内,那个刚刚在决赛里因为暴力行为被红牌罚下的人,以三十五分的微弱优势,压过了最终夺冠的意大利队长,拿到了这届世界杯的最佳球员。

而这里,有一层证据要老实交代。这个奖的投票时间,材料并不完全整齐。一路由路透社分发的赛后报道明确写着,这个奖由媒体投票决定,而这些票,是在齐达内被出示红牌之前就已经投出的。另一条同期的报道则说,正式的截止时间在决赛之后,但很多记者会提前交票,好腾出手来专心看球和赛后写稿。这两条并不互相排斥,它们合起来能支持的最稳妥的结论是,相当数量的选票,是在决赛结束之前,至少是在那张红牌被人们完全消化之前,就已经交上去了。至于是不是大多数票都在终场哨响前投出,公开的材料里没有一张一张的时间戳,这个更强的说法,证据并不完整。所以只能说,多数已投,是当时媒体广泛报道的一个解释,而不是一件可以逐票审计的程序事实。

可即便只坐实到这个程度,那幅画面已经足够刺眼了。评选把齐达内选成最佳,靠的是他整届赛事的表现,是那些记者们在最后一场球踢完之前,就已经写好并递交的判断。等到第一百一十分钟那一撞真的发生,那些票早已交出,收不回来了。于是那座金球,成了一个被提前写好的结论的化石,一个在事实反超它之前,就已经凝固下来的判决。讲述抢在了事实的前头,把最佳球员这个句号提前落了下去,然后事实一头撞了上来,可那个句号,已经擦不掉了。

而投出这些票的,恰恰就是讲故事的那些人本身。这座金球,不是球员投的,不是教练投的,是报道这届世界杯的记者们投的,是那一整届都在为老英雄的完美谢幕铺陈文字的人。他们手里的那支笔,一边写着归来与善终的稿子,一边在选票上,提前把最佳球员的名字填成了他。等到第一百一十分钟,那支笔下的主角,当着全世界的面,把自己的稿子撕了。可选票已经交出,墨迹早已干透。于是那些写故事的人,用一场正式的投票,给自己笔下的那个结局,盖上了一枚公章;等到主角当着全世界的面把稿子撕了,那枚公章却早已盖死,再也揭不下来。

一座金球,一张红牌,同一场球,同一个人。构最想做的,是给一个人一个干净的定性,是英雄,还是莽夫,选一个。可齐达内偏偏两样都是,他既是那届公认的最佳球员,也是那个在决赛里施暴被罚下的人,这两样,谁也盖不过谁,谁也化不掉谁。想把他钉成一张脸,是钉不住的。就连法国国内和国际舆论,当时都给不出一个一致的说法。国际上,对头槌这个暴力行为,是普遍的批评;可在法国本土,情绪并不完全跟着走,总统希拉克在巴黎接见了齐达内,表达了支持,《队报》的读者投票里,有百分之六十一的人认为,这件事不会损害齐达内的声誉。同一个动作,在国界的两边,得到的是两种截然不同的裁断。一个人,到底该被记成什么,连这一点,都闭合不了。

五、污泥里开出的冠军

那座被法国人亲手放走的奖杯,落到了意大利手里。而意大利捧起它的时候,国内正陷在一场叫电话门的风暴中心。

2006年5月,世界杯开赛前不久,意大利足坛的一批窃听内容被曝了出来,矛头直指俱乐部高层和联赛裁判指定体系之间的关系。风暴一起,连国家队主帅里皮都受了牵连,《卫报》在5月下旬就写到,里皮的帅位,正因为这桩丑闻而受到质疑。意大利就是顶着这样一片乌云,去打的世界杯。

而夺冠之后,乌云并没有散。7月14日,离决赛还不到一周,意大利体育法庭作出初审:尤文图斯被降入乙级,还被剥夺了最近两次的联赛冠军;佛罗伦萨和拉齐奥,也在初审里被一并判降;米兰虽然没有降级,却被扣了分,还在欧战层面吃了处分。几天之后,意大利足协的负责人圭多·罗西公开表示,世界杯夺了冠,反而更要求国内足坛把该清理的清理干净,不存在因为夺了冠,就一笔勾销的道理。

于是一个太诱人的故事,又一次找上门来:一支从污泥里开出花来的冠军之师,在祖国足坛最肮脏的时刻,用世界杯的荣耀,完成了一场救赎。可这个故事,必须收缩到它的事实边界里去。要顶住的,是这样两句话:电话门是真的,冠军也是真的,而这两件事,不能揉成一件。被那场审判处理的,是俱乐部,是俱乐部的官员,是联赛裁判指定里的门道,而不是意大利国家队在世界杯上的哪一场比赛。国家队的夺冠,和电话门的裁决,几乎是同时发生的,国家队的阵容里,也确实有大量来自那些涉案俱乐部的球员;可以把这两件事并排放在一起看,却不能因此就自动写出一个结论,说这座世界杯冠军本身,带着司法的污点。丑闻有据,是一回事;那座冠军被这桩案子玷污了,是另一回事,后者,并没有被任何一纸裁决坐实过。

要把这两件真事分开摆着,是很难受的,因为人心天然地想把它们合成一件。合成向上的一件,就是一支蒙冤受辱的球队,在祖国足坛最黑的时刻,用一座冠军替自己正了名,污泥里开出一朵花来,这个版本让人热血沸腾;合成向下的一件,就是一摊从头烂到脚的足球,连世界杯都赢得不干不净,这个版本让人义愤填膺。两个版本都痛快,都能一口气讲到底,唯独都不老实。老实的讲法,反而是最不痛快的那一种:窃听是真的,降级是真的,那座奖杯也是真的,可要把俱乐部的那笔账,径直算到国家队这十一个人在柏林踢的那场球头上,中间缺着一环谁也没能补上的证据。丑闻有据,是一句话;冠军蒙污,是另一句话,把前一句直接当成后一句的证明,又是一次图省事的合并。

意大利那条晋级路上,也不是没有看着刺眼的判罚。十六强对澳大利亚,第五十三分钟,马特拉齐先被罚下,而在伤停补时的最后时刻,格罗索在禁区里和对方的卢卡斯·尼尔有了接触后倒地,当值的西班牙主裁判给意大利一个点球,托蒂主罚命中,意大利一比零涉险过关。这个点球,当时英国媒体就把它写成一次有争议的判罚,到2006年10月,连布拉特都曾就这粒点球引发的争议表示过歉意。这又是一次看得见判罚,却说不清那背后有几分是误判,几分是别的什么。它够不上任何定论,却也一样,消不掉。

到了年底,意大利队长卡纳瓦罗又拿到了那一年的金球奖,那是评整个年度的欧洲足球先生,和世界杯上齐达内那座是两码事;布冯则拿到了本届世界杯的最佳门将。荣誉,一样一样,都落到了这支冠军队的头上。而那片始终没有散尽的乌云,也一样,悬在这些荣誉的上方,谁也没能替它们,彻底洗清。

六、一个夏天的童话

把镜头从赛场移开,那一年的德国,还有另一个故事,一个关于东道主自己的故事。

先看那个夏天本身。克林斯曼带领的德国队,拿到了第三名。东道主一共打进了十四个球,是那届赛事进球最多的球队。可比成绩更被记住的,是那个夏天德国社会的整个气氛。国际足联后来回顾这届世界杯时也强调,德国队和东道主的社会氛围加在一起,才解释了德国2006为什么会留下那样长久的记忆。

那支德国队,原也不在夺冠的热门之列。克林斯曼接手时,德国足球正处在一段被普遍看衰的低谷里,他起用了一批年轻人,踢法比人们记忆中那支刻板的德国队要奔放得多。一支赛前不被看好的东道主,踢出了赛事最多的进球,一路杀进四强,光是这一条,就够让主场沸腾了。球队的意外之喜,和看台的忘情释放,是彼此点着彼此的:越踢越好的球队,把街头的人潮越煽越旺,而漫山遍野的红旗,又反过来把球场里的声浪推得更高。那个夏天之所以被记成一个童话,不只是因为一部纪录片替它起了名字,更因为在它发生的当下,几百万人是真的,把自己整个地投了进去。

夏日童话这个名字,和一部同名的纪录片紧紧连在一起。德国足协后来的官方文字,直接把2006年的世界杯,称作那个夏天的童话,并且明确提到了那部由索恩克·沃特曼拍摄的同名电影。也就是说,夏日童话这个说法,并不是许多年后才被史家追贴上去的标签,而是在2006年之后,很快就由电影和官方叙述共同固定下来的一个公共称呼。这个夏天,在它刚刚过去的时候,就已经开始被讲成一个童话了。

那个夏天最显眼的社会景象之一,是大规模的挂旗和公共观赛。《卫报》在2006年6月下旬就写到,德国全国出现了一种黑红金三色爆发式的可见,汽车上,阳台上,花园里,到处是国旗。德国的历史文献平台收录的当年报纸文章,把这种现象讲成一场关于心的爱国主义的讨论,有人把它理解成,战后的德国,对国旗和民族象征长期的那种拘谨,终于松动了。路透社的一家研究所关于英国媒体如何看待德国2006的研究也指出,那届世界杯,显著改善了德国在英国媒体里的形象,而其中一个重要的原因,就是德国人表现出了一种更舒适,更自然的爱国主义。

这些,都是真实存在的,有着大量当时的记录。几百万人真的走上街头,真的把国旗挂满了阳台,那种松弛的,发自内心的喜悦,和一个国家在自己土地上办赛的自豪,是真真切切的。一个曾经因为历史,而对民族象征格外小心的国家,在那个夏天,难得地放松了一次。那份放松,那份喜悦,不该被后来发生的任何事,一笔抹掉。童话的这一面,是真的。

七、停在时效上的那笔账

可童话的底下,压着一笔一直没能算清的账。

这个夏天的故事,后来被2015年之后的一桩调查,持续地冲击着。要追这桩账,得先回到这届世界杯的起点,那场决定它花落谁家的申办投票。2000年7月,德国以十二比十一的最终票数,击败南非,拿到了2006年世界杯的主办权。而那关键的一票,是大洋洲代表查尔斯·登普西投出的弃权。当时多家媒体的记录都显示,如果登普西按照大洋洲足联既定的倾向,把票投给南非,结果就会是十二比十二,而外界普遍认为,时任国际足联主席的布拉特,会用他手里的决定票,支持南非。登普西后来说,自己当时承受了无法容忍的压力,还提到,曾经遭遇过行贿的企图。

整届世界杯的归属,原来就悬在这样一票弃权上。倘若那一票按着大洋洲原本的心意,投给了南非,历史很可能就是另一个样子。这届被后来讲成童话的世界杯,它的起点,原本就立在一个说不清道不明的悬念之上,一个承受着无法容忍的压力的人,在最后关头,把手缩了回去。这个起点上的疑问,和多年以后那笔六百七十万欧元的疑问,是同一条线上前后相隔的两个结:一个关于那一票为什么会那样投,一个关于那笔钱为什么会那样走,两个结,到今天,都没有谁能真正把它解开。

到了2015年,《明镜》抛出了核心的指控:德国2006的申办委员会,曾经有过一笔约六百七十万欧元的资金安排,来源被指向阿迪达斯的一位前高管的一笔贷款,疑似和争取2000年那场执委会投票有关。路透社当时记录下了两件并存的事实:一件是,德国足协强烈否认存在黑金,也否认买过票;另一件是,德国足协的主席随后承认,他自己没有办法完整地解释,那笔六百七十万欧元,为什么会在2005年被打到国际足联去。一个说没有,一个说不清,并排立在那里。

2016年,德国足协委托的一项调查,给出了一条重要,但并没有封口的解释:这笔钱,被说成是经由国际足联,返还给那位前高管的一笔旧贷款。可是,《卫报》根据那项调查的公开结论写道,德国买票夺标的可能性,不能被排除。于是最稳妥的写法只能是这样:资金回到那位前高管手里,这条路径,是被调查指认过的;但这笔钱最初为什么会出现,是不是真的和申办投票的交换有关,并没有被这项调查,彻底查清。

而到了2020年,这桩账迎来了它最后,也最奇特的一个结局。瑞士方面的刑事程序,没有给出任何实体的判决,而是因为诉讼时效届满,终止了。国际足联当时公开表示失望,说这个案子就这样没有任何结果地结束,令人担忧。这一句,恰好点在了这桩案子今天最要紧的那条定性边界上:它终结于时效届满,而不是终结于查清。此后在德国继续推进的程序,更多地集中在那笔六百七十万欧元的税务和会计表述上;哪怕到很晚,德国法院处理的焦点,也是税务申报和交易的表述,而不是对2000年那场申办到底有没有买票,作出一个已经坐实的刑事判定。

这就又是一种,构关不住自己的样子,而且是一种新的样子。1966年温布利那个球,说不清,是因为看不清,真相失落在人眼的极限之外。2006年这笔申办的账,不一样,它不是没人去查,恰恰相反,《明镜》查了,足协委托的调查查了,瑞士的检方也立了案。可查到最后,那个最要紧的问题,那笔钱到底是不是买票的钱,依旧悬在半空。而这一次,让它悬住的,既不是黑暗,也不是那个够不着的动机,而是一样更冷的东西,时间。构里那套最讲证据的机器,它自己有一口钟,钟走到了头,时效一到,程序就自动停摆了,案子既没被证实,也没被证伪,就那样被时间接了过去,永远地悬在了那里。最想闭合的那台机器,这一回,不是没能给出答案,而是压根没来得及给出答案,它的钟,先响了。

这样一种停法,比查出个真相,或者查明是清白,都要更让人不安。查实了,余项落定,坏就坏个明白;查清是清白,疑云也就散了。可时效届满这种停法,两样都不给。它不宣布你有罪,也不宣布你无辜,它只是宣布,时间到了,不问了。于是那个问题,那笔钱到底是不是买票的钱,就从一个等着被回答的问题,变成了一个永远不会再被回答的问题,悬在那里,既没有被证实,也没有被证伪。它没有被解决,只是被搁置了。构里那套本该去逼近真相的程序,最后逼近的,不是真相,而是它自己的截止日期,到了点,就自动收了工,把那个还没够着的答案,连同所有的疑问,一起原封不动地留在了那里,再没有人回去动它。

于是德国2006,就成了一个可以并排放,却不能揉在一起写的东西。那个东道主夏天真实的社会氛围,那份被改善的国家形象,是真的,有大量当时的记录;而2015年之后,那团缠着申办资金的疑云,也是真的,长期地缠着,并且在2020年,因为时效届满,被永远地悬置了起来。两样都是真实的史实,只是后面那一样,到今天为止,始终没有被司法机关,用一纸实体的裁判,彻底地澄清过。童话是真的,童话底下那笔算不清的账,也是真的。

八、收束

把这一切收束起来。

2006年的世界杯,是一届关于讲述,如何抢在事实前面,而事实,又如何一次次不肯照着讲述去走的世界杯。

齐达内的生涯,被预先写好了一个完美的结局,一个老英雄善始善终的句号。可到了第一百一十分钟,那个句号,被写它的那个活人,亲手撞碎。他拒绝当一件把故事送到圆满的工具,他宁可毁掉那个结局,也要守住自己作为一个人的那点东西。而那座在他施暴之前,就已经被投票选定的金球,成了一个凝固的证据,证明讲述确实曾经抢在了事实的前面,把最佳球员的结论提前写下,然后被事实迎面撞上,却再也收不回来。一座金球,一张红牌,落在同一个人身上,谁也化不掉谁,构想给他一个干净的定性,到最后也没能给成。

意大利在祖国足坛最肮脏的时刻捧起了冠军,一个从污泥里开花的故事,已经等在那里。可丑闻是真的,冠军也是真的,那桩电话门处理的是俱乐部和联赛,不是世界杯的赛场,两件真事只能并排放着,谁也不许替谁,写下一个痛快的结论。而德国那个夏天的童话,被电影和官方,在它刚过去时就讲成了童话;童话底下那笔申办的账,却查了很多年也没能查清,末了,不是被证实,也不是被证伪,而是被一口叫时效的钟,接了过去,永远地停在了那里。

这三样,是构关不住自己的三副面孔。一副,是那个活人,自己动手,把写好的结局掀了;一副,是两件都为真的事,死活不肯塌成一个痛快的定论;还有一副,是那口时效的钟,抢在真相前面,先一步敲响,把问题永远地悬了起来。讲述想要的,永远是一个能合上的圆圈,一个有始有终,严丝合缝的故事。可这三副面孔合起来,说的是同一件事:总有些东西,是这个圆圈合不拢的。有的,是它压根不肯进圈;有的,是它一个偏要顶俩,你把它当英雄它是,你把它当莽夫它也是;还有的,是圈还没画完,画圈的那只手,自己先停了下来。

看得见的,越来越多。齐达内那一撞,几亿人一帧一帧看了个够;那两个被吹掉又被吹响的判罚,那些挂满阳台的国旗,那笔打到国际足联的六百七十万欧元,全都留着记录,摊在明处。可看得见,从来不等于看得清,反倒是那些看得见的画面堆得越满,几处真正够不着的地方,越发被衬得扎眼。撞出那一下的那句话,到底是哪一句,听不真;那张红牌背后的耳机里传了什么,复不了;那笔钱到底是不是买票的钱,查不清。讲述总想把一件事,提前写成一个圆满收口的故事,可总有一个活人,一桩悬案,一笔算不清的账,不肯被那样收口,顽固地留在故事的边上,戳破那份圆满。循环没有停下,它仍旧在转动。

1. The Ending Already Written

The 2006 World Cup was supposed to have an ending that had already been written.

Zinedine Zidane was thirty-four that year, and had already announced that he would retire the moment the tournament ended. Eight years earlier, in 1998, he had scored two headers in Paris and lifted France's first World Cup. Eight years later, coaxed back from the edge of international retirement, he led a France that nobody had fancied before the tournament began, climbing round by round, eliminating Spain, eliminating Brazil, eliminating Portugal, all the way to the final in Berlin. A genius's last tournament, closed out with a title, retiring into legend — the story was too perfect, so perfect that long before the ball stopped rolling, its ending had already been written down and set aside, waiting only for him to walk onto the pitch and cash it in.

Narration always wants to get ahead of the facts. A great event has not yet happened, and already the story about it has, more often than not, been written first. Zidane's tournament, almost from its first match, was set inside a mold prepared long in advance: an old hero returning, finishing well, drawing a perfect circle around his own career. Everyone was simply waiting for that circle to close.

This getting-ahead is not any one person's whim. It is narration's own native temperament. A story feels most secure exactly when it has a head and a tail, when it can be gathered into a pleasing shape, and an old hero who comes home, wins one more time at the very end of his career, and then retires gracefully into everyone's memory, is the most agreeable shape of all. It fit too well — so well that people could hardly wait to pour the ending, which hadn't been played out yet, into that ready-made mold, and Zidane's entire tournament was watched almost entirely through it. What people watched from the stands, what commentators said, what the newspapers wrote, was less a question of how he actually played that day than a matter of waiting for him to trace out, stroke by stroke, with his own hand, the period that had already been prepared for him. The script was already finished. It only awaited the lead actor's final step.

But the facts refused to follow the script that had been written for them.

On July 9, 2006, at the Olympiastadion in Berlin, the final, Italy against France. In the seventh minute, Zidane took a penalty, chipping it off the underside of the crossbar and into the net — a Panenka pulled off at the very limit of audacity, light, arrogant, the kind of joke only a player of his stature would dare crack on the stage of a final. In the nineteenth minute, Materazzi met a Pirlo corner with a header and leveled the score. For the ninety minutes that followed, neither side could separate itself from the other: 1-1, into extra time. In extra time, Zidane rose for a header that Buffon somehow clawed off the crossbar — a save that came within inches of being the final stroke on that perfect ending.

And then came the hundred and tenth minute.

Far from the ball, Zidane suddenly turned and drove his head into Materazzi's chest. Materazzi went down. The referee walked over and showed a red card. Zidane was sent off directly, walked past the trophy with his head down, and disappeared down the tunnel. That was the last match of his career, and its last scene. At full time the score still stood at 1-1; in the penalty shootout, Italy won 5-3. France's only miss belonged to Trezeguet, whose penalty also struck the crossbar. Grosso converted the winning kick, and Italy lifted the trophy.

The perfect ending that had already been written, a few steps from the finish line, was smashed to pieces by the very hand that had written it.

2. The Hundred and Tenth Minute

Start by settling the red card itself.

That a player who assaults another player on the pitch should be sent off is perfectly clear. Zidane's headbutt is there for anyone to see; hundreds of millions of people saw it with total clarity. It was an act of violence, and by the letter of the rule, it earned a red card. Here, the construct closed cleanly around a visible act.

But right behind that clean closure, an unclear question surfaces: how, exactly, did the referee know?

The headbutt happened far from the ball, and the referee on the pitch, Horacio Elizondo, may well not have seen it directly. The broadcast replayed it again and again, and so a great many viewers, a great many commentators, even France's coach Raymond Domenech, grew suspicious that the officiating crew had first watched a pitchside television replay and only then circled back to issue the card. Italy's coach, Marcello Lippi, mentioned after the match that the French side had questioned whether video had been involved. The question mattered, because it touched a line that could not be crossed that year: video, under that tournament's rules, could be used only for post-match disciplinary proceedings, never to intervene in a referee's decision during play. If that red card had been added only after someone watched a replay, it had crossed that line.

FIFA's official account, issued afterward, told a different story. Its July 13 statement on the disciplinary proceeding said the fourth official had directly observed Zidane's violent conduct from his position at the side of the pitch — specifically noting that no monitor had been used — and had then relayed it to the referee over the communications system. FIFA also moved quickly to deny that television replay had assisted any decision during the match: the official stationed beside the pitchside monitor, though he could see the picture, had no authority to intervene, and the fourth official had no access to replay either. Under that year's rules, the fourth official carried a specific duty, to flag violent conduct occurring outside the sight of the referee and his assistants, with final authority remaining the referee's own. So as long as the violence really had been witnessed directly and relayed by headset, the red card had a basis within the rules as written.

And here, once again, that familiar crack opens up. That the red card was correctly given is beyond dispute — Zidane did commit the violence. But how the card actually came about, whether by the fourth official's own eyes or by way of somebody's replay, sits behind a layer of headset traffic that no outsider can ever get inside. FIFA's file says direct observation; but what that headset actually carried, in those seconds, on the night of a World Cup final, is something the public has no way to play back independently. As a matter of the official rule and its interpretation, FIFA found no violation involving video, and the file was closed there. But the public's doubt has simply stayed suspended, unresolved by anyone. Seeing the headbutt with perfect clarity is not the same as settling what happened behind the card that followed it.

This particular kind of unreachability is not quite like the earlier ones. The disputed goal at Wembley in 1966 was unclear because it was unseeable — the ball moved faster than the eye, and the truth was lost beyond the limit of human vision. This time the picture is entirely clear: the headbutt was caught on camera, the red card was caught on camera, even where the fourth official stood was caught on camera. The only thing no camera in the stadium could capture was whatever those few seconds of headset traffic actually carried — not hidden in darkness, not too fast to see, but happening on a line only the men wearing headsets could hear, a line nobody can press play on again afterward. Footage keeps piling up, fuller and fuller, and yet dead center in the middle of all that fullness sits one small blank that no amount of zooming, no amount of slow motion, will ever fill in. Technology can capture more and more of what is visible. It cannot capture a single sentence spoken once, inside a headset, and never again.

And more to the point: whatever produced it, that red card arrived too late. It could send the man who committed the violence off the pitch. It could not put the shattered ending back together. A construct can rule on an act. It cannot make that act not have happened. The curtain call was already ruined; the red card could not retrieve it.

There is a coincidence buried in all this, and it is hard to say whether it is cruel or something else entirely. Eight years earlier, in Paris, it was Zidane's head that scored two goals and won France its first World Cup — that head was the hero of all of France that night. Eight years later, in Berlin, it was the same head, driven once into an opponent's chest, that sent him off the pitch and cracked open the ending that was supposed to have been perfect, a crack nobody had foreseen. The same head made him, once, and ruined him, once — writing him first into the most glorious story football had to offer, and then, on the final page of another story, tearing that glory open with its own hand. The construct can record every single thing that head ever did — the two headers, the one headbutt, all of it preserved in footage sharp enough to freeze — but it cannot tell you why the same head, on the two largest stages it was ever given, arrived at two exactly opposite endings.

3. The Sentence No One Ever Heard Clearly

Why did Zidane headbutt him? That question pulls out a thread that needs to be handled, in this whole episode, more carefully than any other.

What can be confirmed starts with Zidane's own account. In his first extended interview on French television after the match, he said that what provoked his reaction was a repeated insult directed at his mother and his sister, though he did not repeat the exact words on camera. Months later, in September 2006, Materazzi gave an interview admitting the sequence of events: he had first tugged at Zidane's shirt; Zidane told him that if he wanted the shirt, he could have it after the match; Materazzi answered that he would rather have Zidane's sister. Materazzi acknowledged at the time that bringing someone's sister into it had not been a good thing to do. By August 2007, in an interview with an Italian television magazine, he gave, for the first time publicly, what he called the precise words — words concerning Zidane's sister. Some years further on, in 2016, he summarized the exchange once more, saying he had mentioned the sister but not the mother.

Stack these accounts together, and the only safe formulation is this: Materazzi himself eventually admitted that his words concerned Zidane's sister; Zidane himself said the insults, repeated several times, concerned both his mother and his sister. The two accounts do not agree on whether the mother was involved at all. No synchronized recording strong enough to settle the matter has ever been made public.

Press further into the detail, and the exact sentence spoken in that instant becomes a controversy in its own right. In July 2006, several newspapers ran what amounted to a lip-reading contest, asking people to reconstruct from the footage what Materazzi had actually said. These reconstructions contradict one another, and the great majority of them were never based on any complete, publicly reviewable audio track synced to the picture. Even much later, the parties' own statements can confirm only the one insult concerning the sister; they cannot elevate every word spoken that day into undisputed historical fact.

There is a knot here that cannot be untied. To reconstruct that sentence, there are only two roads available: take the parties' own word for it, or find a complete record that can be matched, lip to sound, in full. But the parties are exactly that — parties — and what each says will always be the version he is willing to say, a version no one else can treat as conclusive; and the complete audio-visual record has never, start to finish, been made public. Neither road reaches an end, so that sentence stays permanently stuck in an awkward place: it was certainly spoken — hundreds of millions watched those two lips move — but which exact words they formed, no one has ever nailed down as settled fact. Watching lips move and hearing clearly what they said turn out to be separated by a gap that cannot be crossed either.

Floating on the outermost edge of this whole thread is the most widely circulated legend of all: that what Materazzi said on the spot was a racist or terrorism-tinged insult. After 2006, some British tabloids spread versions along the lines of "son of a terrorist" as though they were the settled conclusion of lip-reading. Materazzi denied it. By 2008, Reuters reported that The Sun had apologized to Materazzi over its claim of a racist slur. As far as what can be verified as historical fact today, this kind of terrorism- or race-based sentence should not be written down as an established thing.

This, too, is the narrative machine doing exactly what it does best. A genius, in the last match of his career, does something that shocks the world, and everyone is desperate to know what words could possibly drive a man like that to a moment like that. Because that sentence decides how the whole event gets classified: if it truly was an insult beyond bearing, Zidane's fury gains a measure of understandability; if the words carried less weight than that, the headbutt looks more like an impulse with no defense at all. People wanted, too badly, to attach a reason weighty enough to this aberration, and so when the footage itself offered no answer, the lip-reading contest stepped in, each version more specific than the last, invented and re-invented, until somebody had to apologize for one of them. The headbutt itself, hundreds of millions of people saw with total clarity. The sentence that provoked it, in the end, nobody ever really heard clearly. Seeing clearly, once again, is not the same thing as hearing clearly.

4. He Would Not Call It Regret

The man who shattered his own perfect ending said things, after the match, that were even harder to fit into any mold than the headbutt itself.

Three days after the final, Zidane gave a public interview and said two things that matter most, and that can be traced back most precisely. The first was an apology to children: he said he wanted to ask forgiveness from every child who had watched the match. The second was a refusal of the word regret: he said that if he regretted it, that would amount to admitting the other man had been right. In the same breath he said he had no excuse whatsoever, but that the other man's words about his mother and his sister had been repeated, several times over. He did not repeat the exact words in the interview, and he never apologized to Materazzi personally.

These two sentences are a living man's answer to a script that had been written for him. That script wanted a hero who finishes well, a trophy held overhead, a satisfying period that lets everyone close the book with a full heart. He simply refused to supply it. He apologized to the children because he knew he had set a bad example on the pitch. But he refused to regret the headbutt itself, because in his own accounting, regret would mean conceding that those insults about his mother and his sister had been justified — and that was a concession he would not make under any circumstance. He would rather destroy the perfect ending than give up that one thing. The script needed him to function as an instrument, a device for delivering the story to its satisfying close. He insisted, instead, on being a person — someone with a mother, someone with a sister, someone who, when it came down to it, would rather overturn the whole board.

This is exactly where the machine that wants to write everything into a story finds itself with no recourse at all. It can write the ending in advance, gather its votes early, mold a man into whatever shape it needs, hero or brute, whichever is called for. But it miscalculates one thing every time: the person written into the story is not a symbol to be arranged at will. He is flesh and blood, with his own wounds, with something of his own he cannot swallow. When the script demanded he bow out smiling, he thought instead of his mother and his sister; when everyone stood there, eyes fixed hungrily on that satisfying period about to fall into place, the thing that mattered more to him outweighed a trophy sitting within arm's reach. A story can be written ahead of time. What the writer always misses is the same thing every time: the person inside it is alive, capable of moving under his own power, and can, on the very last page, do something nobody wrote in for him.

Off the pitch, one more fact makes the absurdity of that pre-written ending even sharper.

That tournament's Golden Ball, awarded to the best player of the World Cup, is decided by a vote among the journalists covering the tournament. The result: Zidane took 2,012 points, Cannavaro 1,977, Pirlo 715. Zidane — the man who had just been sent off for an act of violence in the final — beat the captain of the champion Italian side by a margin of only thirty-five points, and carried off that tournament's award for its best player.

Here, one layer of evidence has to be laid out honestly. The exact timing of this vote is not entirely tidy in the record. One post-match dispatch distributed by Reuters states plainly that the award is decided by media vote, and that these particular votes had been cast before Zidane was shown the red card. Another report from the same period says the official deadline fell after the final, but that many journalists submit early anyway, to free themselves up to watch the match and write their post-match copy. The two accounts are not mutually exclusive, and together the most solid conclusion they support is that a considerable number of ballots had already been submitted before the final ended, or at least before the red card had been fully absorbed. Whether the majority were cast before the final whistle is a stronger claim the public record cannot fully support — no ballot-by-ballot timestamp exists. So the fairest statement is this: "most ballots already in" is an explanation that circulated widely in the media at the time, not a fact that can be audited vote by vote.

Even confirmed only to this degree, the picture is already glaring enough. The selection made Zidane the best player on the strength of his performance across the entire tournament — on judgments those journalists had already written and filed before the last match had even finished. By the time the collision in the hundred and tenth minute actually happened, those ballots were already turned in, and there was no calling them back. So that Golden Ball became the fossil of a conclusion written in advance, a verdict already set hard before the facts could overtake it. Narration got ahead of the facts, dropping the period on "best player" early, and then the facts came crashing straight into it — and that period, once dropped, could not be erased.

And the people who cast those votes were exactly the storytellers themselves. This award was not voted by players or coaches — it was voted by the journalists covering the tournament, the very people who, for the whole of it, had been laying down prose about the old hero's perfect farewell. The same pen that was writing copy about return and closure was, at the same time, already filling in his name on the ballot as the tournament's best player. Then, in the hundred and tenth minute, the man under that pen tore up their draft in front of the entire world. But the ballots had already gone in, the ink already dried. The storytellers, through a formal vote, had stamped an official seal onto the ending they themselves had written, and by the time the protagonist tore up the draft in front of everyone, that seal could no longer be peeled back off.

One Golden Ball, one red card, the same match, the same man. What a construct wants most is to hand a person one clean characterization — hero, or brute, pick one. Zidane, infuriatingly, was both at once: he was that tournament's recognized best player, and he was the man sent off for violence in its final, and neither fact could cover the other, neither could dissolve the other. Trying to nail him to a single face simply does not work. Even opinion within France and abroad could not settle on one verdict at the time. Internationally, criticism of the headbutt as an act of violence was close to universal. Inside France itself, sentiment did not fully follow suit — President Chirac received Zidane in Paris and voiced his support, and in a reader poll run by L'Équipe, sixty-one percent said the incident would not damage Zidane's reputation. The same act, on the two sides of one border, received two starkly different judgments. Even the question of what, exactly, a man should be remembered as — even that would not close.

5. A Champion Blooming Out of the Mud

The trophy that France let go of with its own hand landed in Italy's. And when Italy lifted it, Italian football at home was standing at the center of a storm known as Calciopoli.

In May 2006, shortly before the World Cup began, a batch of wiretapped conversations from inside Italian football was made public, pointing directly at ties between club executives and the system that assigned referees to league matches. Once the storm broke, even the national team's coach, Marcello Lippi, was drawn into it — The Guardian wrote in late May that his position was already in question because of the scandal. Italy went into the World Cup under exactly this cloud.

And once Italy had won, the cloud did not lift. On July 14, less than a week after the final, Italy's sporting tribunal issued its first-instance ruling: Juventus was relegated to Serie B and stripped of its two most recent league titles; Fiorentina and Lazio were also relegated in that same first ruling; Milan, though spared relegation, was docked points and penalized in European competition as well. A few days later, Guido Rossi, head of the Italian football federation, said publicly that winning the World Cup made cleaning up the domestic game more necessary, not less — there would be no wiping the slate clean simply because a title had been won.

And so an all-too-tempting story presents itself once again: a champion side blooming out of the mud, delivering, through the glory of a World Cup, a redemption for a domestic football scene at its filthiest hour. But this story has to be pulled back inside its actual factual boundary. Two things have to be held at once: Calciopoli was real, and the championship was real, and the two cannot be kneaded into a single thing. What that tribunal's ruling dealt with was clubs, club officials, the mechanics of referee assignment within the league — not any match Italy's national team played at the World Cup. The national team's title and Calciopoli's verdict landed almost simultaneously, and the national squad genuinely did include a large number of players drawn from the implicated clubs. The two can be set side by side. That does not license, on its own, the conclusion that the World Cup title itself carries a judicial stain. That the scandal is documented is one statement. That the title was tainted by it is another. The second has never been substantiated by any ruling at all.

Holding these two true things apart, side by side, is uncomfortable, because the mind naturally wants to fuse them into one. Fused upward, it becomes a wrongly maligned team vindicating itself at its homeland's darkest football hour by winning a title, a flower blooming out of the mud — a version that stirs the blood. Fused downward, it becomes a football culture rotten from top to bottom, unable even to win a World Cup cleanly — a version that stirs righteous anger. Both versions are satisfying, both tellable start to finish in a single breath, and both, for exactly that reason, dishonest. The honest account is the least satisfying one of all: the wiretaps were real, the relegations were real, the trophy was real too, but tracing a straight line from the clubs' ledger to the eleven men who played that match in Berlin leaves a gap in evidence nobody has ever filled. "The scandal is documented" is one sentence. "The title is tainted" is another. Treating the first as proof of the second is, once again, a convenient fusion of two things that were never one.

Italy's own road to the final was not without its own glaring calls. In the round of sixteen against Australia, Materazzi was sent off in the fifty-third minute, and in stoppage time Grosso went down in the penalty area after contact with Australia's Lucas Neill; the Spanish referee on duty awarded Italy a penalty, which Totti converted, sending Italy through 1-0. British media at the time already wrote that penalty up as controversial, and by October 2006 even Sepp Blatter had expressed something close to regret over the dispute it caused. This, too, is a case where the call itself is visible, but how much of what lay behind it was misjudgment, and how much was something else, cannot be said with any certainty. It never rises to a verdict. It never disappears either.

By the end of the year, Italy's captain Fabio Cannavaro won that year's Ballon d'Or, the separate annual award for Europe's — and effectively the world's — footballer of the year, a different thing entirely from the World Cup award Zidane had won; Buffon, meanwhile, took home that tournament's Golden Glove. Honor after honor landed on this championship side. And the cloud that never fully dispersed hung over all of those honors too, in exactly the same way, with no one ever managing to wash it entirely clean.

6. A Summer's Fairy Tale

Pull the camera away from the pitch, and that year's Germany had another story to tell — a story about the host nation itself.

Start with the summer on its own terms. Jürgen Klinsmann's Germany finished third. The hosts scored fourteen goals in total, more than any other team in the tournament. But more than the results, what stayed in memory was the mood of German society across that entire summer. FIFA, looking back on the tournament years later, would stress that it was the German team together with the atmosphere of the host society, taken together, that explains why Germany 2006 left such a lasting memory.

That German team had not even ranked among the favorites going in. When Klinsmann took charge, German football was mired in a slump that was widely written off, and he brought in a wave of young players who played a game far more expansive than the rigid, mechanical Germany that people remembered. A host team nobody had fancied before the tournament produced its most goals and marched all the way to the semifinals — that fact alone was enough to set the home crowd alight. The team's unexpected success and the stands' unrestrained release fed off each other: the better the team played, the more it fanned the crowds in the streets, and the flags covering every hillside, in turn, pushed the roar inside the stadiums even louder. That summer is remembered as a fairy tale not merely because a film later gave it that name, but because, while it was actually happening, millions of people genuinely threw themselves into it, completely.

The name Sommermärchen — Summer's Fairy Tale — is tied tightly to a documentary film carrying the same title. The German football federation's own later, official writing calls the 2006 World Cup that summer's fairy tale outright, explicitly naming the film, directed by Sönke Wortmann, that carries the identical title. In other words, Sommermärchen was not a label historians attached years afterward; it was a public name that the film and the official narrative, together, fixed in place almost immediately after 2006. This summer, even while it was still passing, had already begun to be told as a fairy tale.

One of the most visible social scenes of that summer was the mass display of flags and public viewing on a huge scale. The Guardian wrote, in late June 2006, of an explosive visibility of black, red, and gold breaking out across the whole country — flags on cars, on balconies, in gardens, everywhere at once. German archival newspaper coverage from that year treated the phenomenon as a debate over what was called a patriotism of the heart, with some reading it as a sign that postwar Germany's long-held wariness around the flag and national symbols had, at last, begun to loosen. A study by a Reuters-affiliated institute on how British media covered Germany 2006 likewise found that the tournament notably improved Germany's image in the British press, one significant reason being that Germans displayed what was read as a more comfortable, more natural patriotism.

All of this is real, and extensively documented at the time. Millions of people really did take to the streets, really did hang flags from their balconies, and that relaxed, heartfelt joy, together with the pride of a nation hosting the world on its own soil, was entirely genuine. A country that, for historical reasons, had long kept an unusually careful distance from its own national symbols loosened, for once, that summer. That relaxation, that joy, should not be erased by anything that happened afterward. This face of the fairy tale is real.

7. A Ledger Closed by the Clock, Not by the Truth

But underneath the fairy tale sits a ledger that was never actually settled.

This summer's story was, later, repeatedly shaken by an investigation that began in 2015 and kept unfolding for years afterward. To trace that ledger back to its source, you have to return to the very origin of this World Cup — the vote that decided who would host it in the first place.

In July 2000, Germany beat South Africa for the right to host the 2006 World Cup, by a final vote of twelve to eleven. The decisive vote was an abstention, cast by Oceania's representative, Charles Dempsey. Multiple contemporaneous media accounts recorded that had Dempsey voted according to the Oceania confederation's stated position and given his vote to South Africa, the result would have been twelve to twelve, and it was widely believed that Sepp Blatter, FIFA's president at the time, would have used his own casting vote in South Africa's favor. Dempsey later said he had come under pressure he described as intolerable, and also mentioned that he had faced an attempted bribe.

The whole tournament's fate, in other words, once hung on a single abstention. Had that vote followed Oceania's original intention and gone to South Africa, history very possibly reads differently. This tournament, later told as a fairy tale, had, at its very origin, an unresolved suspense of its own: a man under intolerable pressure pulling his hand back at the last possible moment. That question at the origin, and the question of the 6.7 million euros that would surface years afterward, are two knots on the same length of thread — one about why that vote fell the way it fell, one about why that money moved the way it moved — and neither, to this day, has ever truly been untied.

By 2015, Der Spiegel had raised the central allegation: Germany's 2006 bid committee, at some point, had arranged roughly 6.7 million euros in funds, traced to a loan from a former Adidas executive, allegedly connected to securing votes in that 2000 executive-committee ballot. Reuters, reporting at the time, recorded two facts standing side by side: first, that the German football federation strongly denied any slush fund and denied buying any votes; second, that the federation's own president subsequently admitted he could not fully explain why that 6.7 million euros had been wired to FIFA in 2005. One side says there was nothing to it. The other side says it cannot explain it. Both statements simply stand there, side by side.

In 2016, an investigation commissioned by the German federation itself produced an important explanation, though not one that closed the case: the money, it said, was a repayment, routed through FIFA, of an old loan owed to that former executive. But The Guardian, writing on the basis of that investigation's own published findings, reported that the possibility Germany had bought its way to hosting rights could not be ruled out. The safest formulation, then, is this: that the money's path back to the former executive is what the investigation identified; but why that money existed in the first place, and whether it truly had anything to do with a vote-buying exchange, was never fully resolved by that investigation.

Then, in 2020, this ledger reached its final, and strangest, conclusion of all. Criminal proceedings in Switzerland delivered no substantive ruling whatsoever — the case was simply terminated once the statute of limitations had expired. FIFA, at the time, publicly expressed disappointment, saying it was troubling that the case should end this way, with no outcome at all. That single sentence lands precisely on the boundary that defines this case's status today: it ended because time ran out, not because the matter was resolved. The proceedings that continued afterward in Germany focused more narrowly on the tax and accounting treatment of that 6.7 million euros. Even very late in the process, what German courts actually dealt with was the filing and representation of a transaction, not any settled criminal finding on whether votes had actually been bought in 2000.

This, again, is a new shape taken by the construct's inability to close on itself. The disputed goal at Wembley in 1966 stayed unclear because it was unseeable, the truth lost beyond the limit of human vision. This ledger over Germany's hosting bid is different. It is not that no one investigated it — quite the opposite. Der Spiegel investigated it, the federation's own commissioned inquiry investigated it, Swiss prosecutors opened a formal case. But at the end of all that investigating, the one question that actually mattered, whether that money truly was money spent to buy votes, still hangs in midair. And this time, what leaves it hanging is neither darkness nor an unreachable motive but something colder still: time itself. That whole apparatus built to run on evidence carries its own clock, and once the statute of limitations arrives, the proceeding halts automatically, the case neither proven nor disproven, simply taken over by time and left suspended there, permanently. The machine that wanted closure more than anything, this time, did not fail to produce an answer. It never got the chance to. Its clock rang first.

This particular way of stopping is more unsettling than either finding out the truth or finding out someone was innocent. Prove it, and the remainder settles — bad, but plainly, legibly bad. Prove innocence, and the cloud of suspicion disperses. But stopping because a statute of limitations expired gives you neither. It does not declare guilt. It does not declare innocence. It declares only that time is up, and that no one will ask again. So the question — whether that money truly was money spent to buy votes — slides from a question waiting to be answered into a question that will never be answered at all, suspended there, neither proven nor disproven. It was not resolved. It was only shelved. The procedure inside the construct that was supposed to close in on the truth ended up closing in, in the end, not on the truth but on its own deadline. When the date arrived, it simply clocked out, leaving the answer it never reached, together with every question attached to it, exactly where they stood, untouched, for no one to return to.

So Germany 2006 becomes something that can only be set side by side, never kneaded into one single thing. The host nation's genuine social mood that summer, its improved standing in the world's eyes — real, extensively documented at the time. And the cloud of suspicion tangled around the bid's funding since 2015 — equally real, tangled for years, and in 2020, suspended forever by the statute of limitations. Both are genuine historical facts. It is only the second of the two that has never, to this day, been fully clarified by any judicial body through a substantive ruling. The fairy tale is real. The unsettled ledger underneath the fairy tale is real too.

8. Closure

Bring all of this together.

The 2006 World Cup was a tournament about how narration tries to get ahead of the facts, and about how the facts, again and again, refuse to follow narration's script.

Zidane's career had a perfect ending written for it in advance — an old hero's period, closing well. But in the hundred and tenth minute, that period was shattered by the very living man who had written it, with his own hand. He refused to serve as the instrument that delivers a story to its satisfying close; he would rather destroy that ending than surrender the one piece of himself that made him a person and not a device. And the Golden Ball, already decided by vote before he committed that act of violence, became a solidified piece of evidence that narration really had, once, gotten ahead of the facts — dropping the period on "best player" early, only to be run head-on into by the facts themselves, unable, ever afterward, to take it back. One Golden Ball, one red card, landing on the same man; neither could dissolve the other. A construct wanted to hand him one clean characterization. In the end, it never managed to.

Italy lifted the title at its domestic football's filthiest hour, a story of blooming out of the mud already waiting to be told. But the scandal was real, and the title was real too; Calciopoli dealt with clubs and a league, not a World Cup pitch — the two true things can only be set side by side, and neither is permitted to write, on the other's behalf, one satisfying verdict. And Germany's summer fairy tale, told as a fairy tale by a film and an official narrative almost as soon as it had passed, sat above a ledger of bid funds that was investigated for years without ever being cleared up, and that was, in the end, neither proven nor disproven, but taken over by a clock called the statute of limitations and stopped there forever.

These three are the construct's three separate faces of failing to close on itself. One: a living man, with his own hand, overturning the ending that had been written for him. One: two things, both true, that simply refuse to collapse into one satisfying verdict. And one more: the clock of the statute of limitations, striking before the truth could arrive, suspending the question forever. What narration always wants is a circle that closes — a story with a beginning and an end, fitted seamlessly together. But these three faces, taken together, say the same thing: there is always something this circle cannot close around. Some of it simply refuses to enter the circle at all. Some of it insists on being two things at once — call it a hero, and it is one; call it a brute, and it is that too. And some of it is a circle still being drawn, when the hand doing the drawing stops first, on its own, before the line ever meets itself.

What is visible keeps growing. Zidane's headbutt — hundreds of millions of people watched it, frame by frame, to their heart's content. The two controversial calls, one waved off and one waved through; the flags draped over every balcony; the 6.7 million euros wired to FIFA — all of it stayed on the record, laid out in plain sight. But being visible was never the same thing as being settled. If anything, the fuller the visible footage piled up, the more glaringly the few genuinely unreachable spots stood out against it. The exact sentence behind the headbutt was never heard clearly. Whatever passed through the headset behind that red card can never be replayed. Whether that money truly was money spent to buy votes was never fully traced to the end. Narration always wants to write a thing, in advance, into a story that closes neatly shut. But there is always a living man, an unsettled case, an unbalanced ledger, refusing to be closed that way, staying stubbornly at the edge of the story, puncturing its neatness. The cycle has not stopped. It is still turning.
