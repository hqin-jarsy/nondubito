禅让——把天下交出去之后

Yao, Shun, and Yu — What a Handoff Cannot Settle

Han Qin (秦汉)

三皇五帝的故事有个奇怪的转折。伏羲画卦、女娲补天、神农尝百草，讲的是人怎样有了一个可以生活的世界。到了尧舜禹，问题忽然变得很具体：一个人老了，他手里的权力交给谁？

他有儿子，却把另一个人推到了前面。

后来的人把这种传位叫作禅让。它的吸引力不难理解：天下不是一家人的财产，做得好的人来做，不能因为父亲坐在那个位置上，儿子就天然有资格坐下去。尧选舜，舜荐禹，仿佛同一个办法既回答了“凭什么是你”，也回答了“你之后是谁”。

不过，我们读到的是后世整理、争论、反复讲述的古代故事，不是一份保存完好的史前宪法。这不妨碍它提出真正的政治问题。恰恰因为有人愿意把它当理想，我们才值得追问：这份美好的交接，靠什么完成？交接中的人如果不再意见一致，又怎么办？

一、为什么是舜

《史记》写尧向四岳询问人选。被推荐的舜出身不显赫，在历山耕作。他父亲和异母弟对他怀有恶意，他却没有让家庭关系彻底变成一场报复。这段通常被当作孝的故事，也可以读成一种识人的尝试：一个人在不公平的关系里怎样行动？到了有权力的时候，他会不会只会把不公平倒过来施加给别人？

尧没有听完推荐就交出天下。他把两个女儿嫁给舜，让这个人进入自己的家，也让他参与公共事务。家庭关系、地位变化、实际工作，成了不同的观察入口。舜不是到职位空出来时才突然出现的，他已经被看见做了很久的事。1

传世故事给了这段共同执政很长的时间：二十八年。读者很容易为这个数字所打动。不是一次谈话，不是一句口碑，是接近一代人的相处。识人的困难，似乎被时间慢慢克服了。

禹的考验则是水。治水需要安排人手、疏通河道，让许多彼此不相识的人能够重新生活。他经过家门而不入的形象，把公共责任压进了一个人人都能感到的痛处：家就在眼前，事情还没有做完。《史记》说十三年在外，《孟子》说八年、三过其门；两种讲法都把漫长的付出放在传位之前。2

这套叙事并不幼稚。它没有说“找个好人就行”。它安排推荐、观察、工作、关系变化，让一个人的德行经受不同处境。它甚至没有把最后一票全交给前任。《孟子》直接说：“天子不能以天下与人。”推荐之外，还要有天的授予、政事的治理和人们的接受。朝觐的人、求断案的人、讴歌的人，转向了舜，故事里的继位才真正完成。3

这不是现代选举。但它让我们看见：交接不只是两个人之间的交钥匙。其他人是否愿意继续来办事，是否承认这个人能主持共同生活，也在其中。

二、大家不再同意的时候

问题恰恰从这里开始。一个成功的故事，可以让推荐、能力、天命与认可都指向同一个人。政治最难的时候，却是它们不指向同一个人。

前任说这个人合适，诸侯不认；政务做得不错，继承资格却有争议；有人愿意接受，有人觉得自己被排除在外。那时，由谁说明理由，由谁判断，又由谁承担判断错了的后果？

长期观察确实有用，但它观察的是一个人在已有关系中的表现。有人在前任身边能够听取意见，独自掌权后却未必还听得见。原先能够纠正他的那个人离开了，原先敢说话的人也可能渐渐沉默。二十八年能积累了解，不能替下一种处境预先给出保证。

“德”也需要解释。治水的功劳、调解冲突的耐心、战时的决断，未必总落在同一个人身上。谁的能力更重要，取决于接下来要面对什么。共同赞成一个词，并不等于已经共同赞成所有具体选择。

还有退出。掌权者愿不愿意离开？围绕他建立了生活和地位的人，愿不愿意让他离开？有时不是他一个人舍不得那个位置，而是一整圈人都在告诉他：没有你不行。这里的风险不需要假定所有人都会腐化；只要真实反馈变得困难，最重要的决定就可能越来越难作出。

所以，真正值得保留的批评，不是古人完全没有制度，只会赌圣人。而是：一个安排把多少重量放在了卓越人格之上？当推荐者判断错了、继任者改变了、不同群体不肯接受同一个结果时，它有没有给普通人留下继续处理分歧的办法？

好人值得期待。但人们不能只有等到好人出现，才有机会过上不被任意支配的生活。

三、血缘、强制与看不见的远处

在这套安排旁边，有三件事始终没有消失。

首先是自己的孩子。尧有丹朱，舜有商均，禹有启。把位置交给外人，不只是执行一个抽象原则，也是在既有亲情和公共职责之间作选择。故事说丹朱、商均不适合继位，似乎让冲突容易了一点：为了天下，只好不传给儿子。

那么，儿子如果也能干呢？古典并非没有回答。《孟子》讲启贤，也讲天可以授予贤者、可以授予儿子。问题因而更有意思了：非世袭与世袭都可以获得正当化，那么在结果尚未发生时，人们靠什么分辨应走哪一条路？解释一个已经被接受的继承者，与解决两个仍在争夺的继承者，不是一件事。3

其次是强制。关于上古传位，一直存在不那么和气的说法。《韩非子·说疑》中就出现了舜逼尧、禹逼舜的说辞。那段文字是在讨论危险的夺权劝说，不是交接现场的记录；但它足以提醒读者，同一个“让”字，可以被用来讲自愿，也可以被用来遮住胁迫。4

这时不能说真假不重要。一个人主动离开与被迫签字，对他本人、对受其影响的人，区别非常大。我们既要辨认叙事的作用，也要继续追问发生了什么。否则，对漂亮语言的怀疑就会变成另一种任意断言。

最后是距离。人与人相识，可以提供书面材料没有的信息；但这种了解也有边界。治理的范围变大，更多人的生活只能通过别人的转述抵达决策者。谁能够被推荐，谁的付出能够被看见，谁连进入候选名单的机会都没有，就成了新的问题。

选出一个人，不等于已经听见所有人。即使交接者真诚，仍有亲情、力量、信息与被遗漏的要求，不能仅靠“择贤”两个字安顿。这些安排尚未接住、却继续发生作用的东西，就是本系列所说的余项。

四、为什么人们去了启那里

禹死后，故事没有照前两次继续。被推荐的益退开，诸侯转向禹的儿子启。

如果只留下“他是禹的儿子”，这件事很好解释，也解释得太快。《史记》同时写启贤、天下属意，又写益辅佐禹时间较短，恩泽和关系尚未充分展开。最后，人们说：这是我们君主禹的儿子。能力、熟悉、积累的关系与血缘，挤在同一个选择里。2

益并不是因为没有一纸推荐才失败。问题是推荐本身不能把支持、信任和实际合作一并交给他。禹能推出一个人，不能替所有其他人完成接受他的过程。

启的血缘也不只是一种私人感情。对追随者来说，这个名字可能意味着原有承诺还算数、熟悉的人还能找到、与禹建立的关系不必从头解释。家族连续性提供了一种可以辨认的未来。这也解释了世袭为什么能有真正的吸引力，而不只是贪心战胜公心。

代价同样在这里：当关系都沿着一家人延续，公共职位就容易被看作一家人的东西。先前为免除私人占有而提出的理想，被一种能够稳定合作的办法挤到了旁边。解决一个真实困难，并不会让别的困难自动消失。

在《史记》这一路叙事里，益退让；《晋书》转录竹书内容时，则记下启杀益的异说。两种故事不能拼成同一份确定经过。4 不过，它们都把我们带回同一个问题：不同根据支持不同人时，交接需要的不只是一个更动听的理由。

我愿意把这一转折理解为安排的边界显露了出来。它不证明一切努力都是徒劳，而是说明一个方案不能靠自己的成功故事，免除自己面对失败情形的责任。

五、交接办法之外的生命

尧舜的故事并没有随着启的出现退出政治。相反，后来的人不断回来借它说话。

一种用法是批评。既然天下可以交给更合适的人，那么坐在位置上的人就不能只拿出父亲的名字。禅让使“你应当怎样对待天下”成为一个可以追问的问题。《孟子》对天与人的讨论，正是在给统治者划界，而不只是劝他多修养。3

另一种用法是为夺取权力披上一件旧衣服。曹丕受汉献帝禅位的文书援引尧舜，把朝代更换放进古老的传位语言中。5 强者既然已经有力量，为什么还要借这套话？因为能够迫使人服从，与能够解释自己为何有资格，并不是同一件事。

仪式不能单独告诉我们参与者是否自愿。正因为禅让的语言有吸引力，它才可能被与其理想相反的行动使用。读者不能看见一个庄严形式，就停止追问形式里面的人。

王莽又展示了援古的另一条路。周公摄政、古礼、符命，为他的政治行动提供了语言和先例。6 但引用古代，和重新建立古代的关系条件，相距很远。一个名称可以拿回来，支撑它的信任、分工与日常生活却不能随名字一起搬来。

这里最值得注意的，不是某个办法从此只能失败，而是一个未实现的要求可以改变存在方式。它不再直接决定谁接位，却仍然追问接位的人：天下究竟是你的财产，还是你必须向他人负责的事务？

理想也可能被遗忘、被歪用，需要人反复把它说清楚。它的持续，不是语言自己拥有不死之身，而是仍有人不肯把“已经如此”当作最后答案。

六、家里的人，然后呢

让同一个家族继续执掌，缩小了候选范围，却没有完成继承。

家里的谁？年长者还是更有能力的人？母亲身份不同的儿子怎样排序？父亲死后传给儿子，还是先交给成年兄弟？选定的人做不好时，谁能提出异议，谁又能使异议发生作用？

太康失国、少康再兴、桀的结局，将把这些问题一个个推到前面。这里不必先把夏王朝想成一套已经完备、只等坏人来破坏的制度。家族身份能提供支持，支持怎样变成持续的共同生活，还需要许多工作。7

这就是下一篇的起点。一个安排确实可以解决旧问题；真正的危险，是把这份成功误认成以后再也不必问。

A ruler has a son. He recommends somebody else.

There is an unmistakable attraction in that gesture. Public responsibility should not be a family possession. The person who can undertake it should succeed, not the person who happens to have been born in the right room.

In the Chinese stories of Yao, Shun, and Yu, this principle seems to answer two questions at once. Why should you govern? Because you are worthy. Who should govern after you? The person whose worth has become apparent in turn. Succession need not begin with a battle or end at the boundary of a bloodline.

The traditional name for this transfer is shanrang, usually translated as abdication or yielding the throne. We encounter it in accounts shaped long after the world they describe. They are not a surviving prehistoric constitution. They are something political thought also needs: stories about what authority ought to look like, and what might make people accept it.

The difficult question begins after the admirable gesture. What does recommending a successor actually transfer? And what happens when the people involved do not agree?

I. More than a good reputation

The Shiji, Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, does not have Yao meet a promising stranger and immediately hand him the realm. He asks advisers for candidates. Shun is recommended from outside an eminent ruling family. He works at Mount Li, and his first distinction concerns conduct in a hostile household.1

His father and half-brother mean him harm. He nevertheless maintains relationships without turning his life into a campaign of revenge. Read as a story about selecting a ruler, this is an unusual reference check. What does a person do with injustice when it comes from people close to him? If he later acquires power, will he know any use for it besides reversing the direction of the injury?

Yao brings Shun into his own family by marrying two daughters to him. He also gives him public work. A change in status, difficult relationships, and administrative responsibilities become different ways to observe the same person. Shun does not first appear when the office becomes vacant. Others have already seen him at work.

The narrative gives this association a memorable duration: twenty-eight years. Almost a generation. Such a span makes selection feel less like a wager on charm. A person may perform well at an interview; it is harder to perform an invented character across decades of ordinary demands.

Yu's test involves floodwater. His task requires people, knowledge, coordination, and sustained work that will let others live again. The image of him passing his home without entering places public responsibility beside a private cost anyone can understand. The Shiji gives thirteen years away; the Mengzi gives eight years and three passages by his door. Both make enduring service part of the case for succession.2

There is more here than an incumbent's personal confidence. The Mengzi, the text associated with Mencius, explicitly denies that the Son of Heaven can simply give the realm to another person. It describes Heaven's acceptance, successful public affairs, and people's acceptance. Lords come to Shun, litigants seek him, and songs praise him. The transfer acquires meaning through what others do.3

This is not a modern election. But neither is it a private exchange of keys. A public office cannot be fully transferred inside a conversation between its former and future occupants.

II. When the reasons point in different directions

A successful story can bring virtue, recommendation, Heaven, and public acceptance together in one person. Politics becomes difficult when they separate.

The incumbent recommends someone whom important groups refuse to follow. A capable administrator has a disputed claim. Different communities experience the same candidate differently. Who hears those disagreements? Who decides which reasons count? What can someone do after being overruled?

Long observation helps, but it observes a person within particular relationships. Someone willing to hear criticism beside a senior ruler may become harder to reach when the senior ruler is gone. An adviser who once spoke freely may fall silent. Twenty-eight years of knowledge cannot settle in advance what a new distribution of power will do to that knowledge.

The standard itself needs interpretation. Skill in managing water, patience in settling disputes, and decisiveness in a crisis may belong to different people. Their relative importance depends on what comes next. Agreeing that the ruler should possess virtue does not remove disagreement over which conduct demonstrates it.

Departure presents a further difficulty. An incumbent must be willing to leave, and people whose positions depend on him may have reasons to discourage it. They need not think themselves corrupt. They may sincerely believe that an unfamiliar successor threatens everything they have helped build. Gradually, the ruler hears less about a future without him.

The point is not that ancient people possessed no institutions and merely gambled on saints. It is that an arrangement can place too much weight on exceptional judgment and restraint. What happens when the recommending ruler is mistaken, the successor changes, or the groups expected to accept the result disagree?

Good people matter. But ordinary people should not have to wait for an extraordinary person before they can live without arbitrary domination.

III. Three things the ideal does not absorb

The ruler still has a family. Yao has Danzhu, Shun has Shangjun, and Yu has Qi. Yielding to someone outside the family means choosing across an existing attachment, not merely applying an abstract principle.

The stories describe Yao's and Shun's sons as unsuitable. This makes the sacrifice easier to explain: the father puts public responsibility before an unworthy child's claim. But suppose the son is capable too?

The classical tradition does answer. The Mengzi describes Qi as worthy and allows for Heaven's giving authority either to a worthy outsider or to a son. That answer sharpens rather than abolishes the institutional question. If either outcome can be justified, what distinguishes them before the contest has been settled? Explaining an accepted successor is different from adjudicating between two people still seeking acceptance.3

A second difficulty is coercion. A transfer described as yielding may also be told as forcing someone out. The Han Feizi includes such accounts in a discussion of dangerous persuasion and usurpation. Its embedded speeches are not eyewitness records, but they show that the agreeable language of abdication was open to a darker reading.4

Whether a person voluntarily leaves or is compelled to sign matters enormously. Suspicion of ceremonial language does not make facts irrelevant. Otherwise, exposing one legitimizing story would merely license us to invent another.

A third difficulty is distance. Familiarity can provide knowledge that a dossier cannot. It also has limits. As the field of responsibility widens, more lives reach the decision-maker through other people's reports. Who gets recommended? Whose work is visible? Who never enters the conversation at all?

Selecting one capable person is not the same as hearing everyone affected by the selection. Family attachment, force, information, and unrepresented claims continue to operate beside the ideal. In the vocabulary of this series, these are remainders: matters an arrangement has not adequately taken up, although they still shape what happens. The word does not classify some people as leftovers.

IV. Why people went to Qi

After Yu's death, the narrative takes a different turn. Yi, his recommended successor, withdraws. The lords go instead to Yu's son Qi.

“Because he was the son” is an intelligible explanation, but an incomplete reading of the Shiji. The text also calls Qi worthy and describes people favoring him. Yi, it says, had assisted Yu for a shorter time; his standing and benefits had not spread as widely. Finally comes the language of descent: Qi is the son of their ruler Yu.2

Competence, familiarity, accumulated relationships, and bloodline meet in one decision. Yi's problem is not simply the absence of a nomination. He has that. What a nomination cannot supply by itself is the network of confidence and cooperation needed to make it effective.

Qi's descent offers more than private affection. To followers, a recognizable family may promise that existing commitments will still be understood, familiar contacts will remain available, and relationships will not have to begin again. Hereditary continuity can answer a real anxiety about the future. Its appeal should not be dismissed as greed defeating virtue.

Its cost lies close to its strength. When cooperation travels through one household, responsibility for public affairs can come to resemble that household's property. A workable answer to the uncertainty of succession creates another question about ownership.

In the Shiji account, Yi yields. The later Jinshu, reporting contents of the Bamboo Annals, preserves a contrary tradition in which Qi kills him. These should remain distinct accounts, not be assembled into a single confident reconstruction.4 Either way, disagreement among rival grounds of authority demands more than an eloquent account of why one's own candidate deserves to win.

This is how I read the turning point: an arrangement encounters a situation its picture of success has not settled. That does not make the arrangement worthless. It makes its limits consequential for the people who must live through them.

V. An ideal after the handoff

Yao and Shun did not disappear from politics when Qi entered the story. Later generations repeatedly returned to them for different purposes.

One use was criticism. If authority could rightly pass to someone better suited to it, an incumbent could not answer every objection by naming his father. The account kept a question alive: how should the realm be treated? The Mengzi discussion of Heaven and people places limits on what a ruler may call his own.3

Another use was legitimation. Documents surrounding Cao Pi's receipt of Emperor Xian's abdication invoked Yao and Shun, clothing a dynastic change in the language of an ancient transfer.5 Why would someone already strong enough to prevail need that language? Because compelling obedience and explaining one's entitlement are different achievements.

Ceremony alone cannot tell us whether its participants consented. Indeed, a compelling ideal is especially available for use by actions that contradict it. The reader needs to keep looking at the people inside the form.

Wang Mang offers another version of the relationship with antiquity. Ancient ritual, the precedent of the Duke of Zhou's regency, and auspicious signs supplied resources for his political claims.6 Yet citing an ancient arrangement is not the same as rebuilding its conditions. A name can be restored more easily than the trust, work, and relationships that gave it meaning.

An unrealized demand can therefore change its mode of existence. It may cease to determine succession directly while continuing to question successors: is the realm your possession, or a responsibility for which you must answer to others?

Such demands do not survive without people. They can be forgotten or appropriated. Their persistence depends on people restating them and refusing to treat what already exists as the final answer. The ideal remains powerful not because language is magically immortal, but because the difficulty to which it speaks keeps being encountered.

VI. Someone in the family—and then?

Keeping authority within a family narrows the candidate pool. It does not finish the work of succession.

Which member? An eldest son or an abler one? How do sons born to mothers of different standing rank? Does a deceased ruler's adult brother precede his child? If the selected heir governs badly, who can object, and who can make an objection effective?

The traditional stories of Taikang's loss, Shaokang's restoration, and Jie's defeat bring those questions forward. We need not imagine the Xia as a complete institutional machine awaiting a bad operator. Family identity can supply support; making that support sustain a common life requires further work.7

That is the next essay's starting point. An arrangement may genuinely solve an inherited problem. The danger begins when that success is mistaken for permission to stop asking questions.
