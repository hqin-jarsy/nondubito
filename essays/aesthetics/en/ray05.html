The Wood Still Holds That Dry Year

Rings, worn steps and repairs bring a past into view. Reading what remains does not excuse everything that caused it.

Han Qin · 2026-09-28

In this essay

A piece of wood lies on the table, its cut face turned toward us. One person notices the color. Another touches it. Someone starts counting the bands.

A finger stops at a particularly narrow ring.

A difficult year? Perhaps. But a narrow ring does not authorize us to invent a drought. Species, location, temperature, moisture and disturbances all matter. There may be missing rings or growth changes that make a simple count misleading. Reading wood is not attaching a plausible story to every line.

What is remarkable is that some of its past can be read with care.

The year itself did not stay

Where moisture limits growth, drought can leave a narrow annual increment. Later growth adds layers outside it. The weather of that year is long gone, but subsequent rain does not go back and make the earlier ring wider.

This is not a recording of the summer, stored intact and waiting to be played. It is an effect of conditions on growth. We work back from that effect to ask what happened.

One tree is often insufficient. Researchers compare sequences across trees and timbers, establishing dependable matches. One sequence connects with another not because the marks look vaguely alike, but because the correspondence withstands scrutiny.

The past is not being summoned by nostalgia. Something that remains was actually affected by it.

“That is over” can mean two different things. The drought has ended: that is one. Therefore it has nothing to do with the present: that is another. The wood gives us reason to separate them. Its present form still includes that year.

A beam acquires a date

In 1929, a timber recovered from a ruin at Show Low, Arizona, was given the research designation HH-39. It became an important piece in the history of tree-ring dating.

A. E. Douglass and his colleagues already had sequences of different kinds. Some could be traced back from living trees and assigned calendar years. Other sequences came from old building timbers and could be arranged relative to one another without yet being securely joined to the calendar. They were like two readable passages whose connection had not been established.

HH-39 helped join them. Many archaeological timbers could consequently be dated. A further distinction remains necessary: the formation or felling date of wood is not invariably the construction date of a building. Timber may be reused; outer rings may be absent. Material and context must do the work together. A satisfying discovery story cannot settle every inference for the researcher.

Even without the drama of a mystery suddenly solved, the beam remains worth looking at.

It knew nothing of archaeology and did not grow with the intention of recording history for us. It grew, became part of a building, and survived in material form. Researchers read in its grain a checkable relation between different times.

The past did not occur again. But it ceased to be merely “long ago.” It acquired a position in something that could still be touched.

The steps need no famous owner

A similar encounter is possible at a worn stone stair.

The middle is lower than the sides. People who passed had their own destinations. Some hurried; others waited by the entrance. Nobody had the task of making this particular hollow. Weather and the properties of stone can contribute too, so we should not attribute every depression to feet. But where repeated passage can be identified, dispersed journeys have become visible together.

We may know almost none of the travelers' names. The stair need not first be authenticated as the route of a famous person before it deserves attention.

Sometimes the impressive label obstructs the view. We are busy confirming who lived here and what the object is worth, and stop looking at how the stone has been worn. Reputation may itself be part of a history. It cannot perform the looking for us.

Neither must we conclude that old things are better. A new chair can have beautiful lines. An old one may be illuminated largely by our habitual fondness for the past. Age does not automatically confer beauty.

Recognizing a history and preferring an old appearance are different activities.

A trace does not excuse a harm

At a ruin or a site of suffering, the distinction becomes more urgent.

Why a wall broke there, how an event marked an object: these may present forms that can be examined closely. Recognizing them is not approving the violence that produced them. It certainly does not entitle us to tell someone who was hurt that the injury made them who they are.

What endures need not have been worth causing. What participates in the present need not have been right to impose.

Beauty has no authority to decide the further questions by itself. Whether to repair, what to preserve and who gets to tell the story concern lives and rights. They cannot be settled by declaring that ruins look better when left broken. We may read the history of a harm while wishing it had never occurred. We may preserve testimony without making another person's pain into material for our own enjoyment.

Someone standing at a memorial may want only to mourn. Not attending to the lines of a broken wall is not a failure to complete an aesthetic assignment. No such assignment has been issued.

Even an ordinary repair need not erase a past. A patch may leave another subsequent event legible: this broke, and somebody chose not to throw it away. The issue is not simply whether a repair was made. It is which history we are actually seeing, and whether we have inserted a story the material does not support.

The wood on the table

Return to the cut piece and perhaps the eye will move more slowly.

There is no need to race toward an impressive age, or supply storms and sunshine for every ring. We can begin by distinguishing marks of growth from marks of cutting, what the evidence supports from what another sample would be needed to establish.

Knowing less can be a reason to say less. Looking carefully does not oblige us to narrate the object's entire life.

The beauty proposed here is not simply the sadness of passing time. If all we say is that everything passes, wood, stairs and letters become interchangeable decorations for the same thought. What makes them worth attention is precisely their different ways of retaining something: how that year affected growth, how passage contributed to wear, how a folded and postmarked letter arrived in the present.

An object becomes more than its current outline. It has a provenance that does not depend entirely on our telling.

The hand withdraws from the wood. The narrow ring remains, with many later rings outside it, some wide and some narrow.

Better years did not remove it. Nor did it dictate everything the tree would subsequently become.
