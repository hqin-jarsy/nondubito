“I Was Mistaken.”

A name called in error brings a judgment back to its maker. The world is not wholly wrong. Neither is the person who made the mistake.

Han Qin · 2026-09-28

In this essay

Someone walking ahead looks like a friend you have not seen in years.

You call his name. He does not turn, so you call again, a little louder.

He turns. It is not your friend.

“Sorry. I thought you were someone else.”

He smiles and walks on. The incident could end there. Little has been lost; even the apology need not be solemn. Yet alongside the question of who was ahead of you, something else has briefly become apparent.

The confident judgment was yours.

From “not him” to “I was mistaken”

The two statements are close, but they do different work.

“Not him” changes an answer. The person ahead is a stranger, not the bearer of that name. “I was mistaken” brings the correction back to someone who looked, recognized and, until a moment ago, felt quite certain.

When recognition succeeds, this can go unnoticed. You recognize a friend as though the world had supplied his name directly. Error reveals that the name was not written across a stranger's back. You brought it to what you saw.

There need be no mysterious object inside the body assigned to make judgments. Nor must we first settle what a self ultimately is or whether it remains unchanged forever. The incident offers a smaller distinction: replacing an answer is not quite the same as the person who gave it acknowledging, “That was how I saw it.”

The account of self begins here, with very little.

It does not begin with a sovereign who controls everything. You did not even control all the reasons you mistook the stranger for someone else. Yet a bystander cannot entirely perform this acknowledgment on your behalf. They can tell you that you were mistaken. Recognizing it as your mistake remains something else.

The mistake came from somewhere

Why that particular figure?

Your friend used to carry one shoulder slightly lower and wore that sort of coat in winter. You remember him ten years ago, not as he has looked recently. You were also standing some distance behind the stranger. His back was visible; his face was not.

The error did not emerge from a blank space. It brought with it someone you had known and the position from which you were looking.

Another passerby might never have paused. They did not know your friend. From a position facing the stranger, you might not have called out either.

Acknowledging a judgment as yours does not mean declaring that you created all its conditions. Poor light may be a fact. The years since you last saw your friend may matter. We can describe those causes and still acknowledge who called the name.

Having reasons does not remove the “I.” The “I” does not absorb all the reasons into a solitary person's guilt.

That distinction matters much more in serious situations. Someone may have been deceived, denied relevant information, or forced to decide under pressure. Responsibility must be examined in its actual circumstances. A small mistake on a street cannot establish that everyone ought to accept blame for everything that happens. Nor should a coerced “It is all my fault” be celebrated as a beautiful emergence of self.

We are attending to how a judgment returns to its maker, not looking for someone who can be made to carry all the blame.

The stranger is looking too

Embarrassment can hide another small change.

As you see the stranger's face, he is looking at you. Someone has called a name twice; he has turned and is waiting for an explanation.

At first you were occupied with identifying him. Now it becomes evident that he has his own side of the encounter. He is not a picture placed on the street for you to recognize. While you are looking at him, you have also entered his view.

Perhaps he notices your embarrassment. Perhaps he is merely puzzled. A smile does not authorize us to write out all his thoughts. Even “He must think I am an idiot” is another judgment of yours, not necessarily more reliable than the first.

This return to yourself has not granted you the power to read someone else completely.

It has only brought an easily overlooked position into view: the person looking can also be seen. The giver of judgments can become the object of another person's incomplete judgment.

You need not respond by monitoring yourself at every step. Constantly checking whether others approve is another occupation, not necessarily a clearer encounter with yourself.

Recognition is not approval

Further along, you pass a shop whose lights are still off. In its window there is someone walking quickly, shoulders a little dropped, carrying a bag. You notice the figure before recognizing it as yourself.

Not necessarily attractive or unattractive. Just slightly different from the habitual picture in your head.

We may imagine ourselves at a fixed age, or as we appeared when something went particularly well. The glass returns today's posture instead. The years did not wait for permission to pass. Changing your clothes this morning did not exchange the history that brought you here.

Recognizing that history need not amount to the verdict that you can never change. Being here today is one thing; how to live from here is another. Acknowledging where you came from does not require treating it as an order.

Nor must a program of self-improvement begin immediately. Stand straighter, lose weight, appear more confident, become a better version: these may be things you choose to pursue. They need not get ahead of every recognition and hand you a scorecard before you can see who is there.

For a moment it may be enough to recognize this person, not every possible person. Someone who still cannot do or understand many things does not first need to acquire unlimited powers to have a self.

Completeness here is not the absence of faults, much less certification as a good person. An ordinary, morally unremarkable person can recognize their mistaken judgment. An admirable person may not always see themselves clearly.

Finding something worth attending to in that return does not pronounce a favorable verdict on an entire life. We owe nobody indiscriminate praise.

A sentence not yet finished

Sometimes the return takes longer than it did on the street.

Two people are arguing. One suddenly stops. They want to continue along the sentence already underway, but an example the other person gave does not fit what they have been saying. They are searching for words and deciding whether to defend the sentence or acknowledge that they did not see enough.

A witness may not know what the pause contains. Silence has many causes; a lowered head is not proof of an awakening. But from within such a moment you may recognize the change: some part of the “You are wrong” directed outward has turned back.

No complete answer need follow. Knowing that what you said was mistaken does not immediately explain why you were so insistent. It certainly does not mean that you now understand yourself for good.

The self need not resolve into a clear portrait on every occasion. Sometimes a familiar portrait loosens, and only then can we ask what the person making the judgment has brought to it.

Back on the street, the stranger is already far away. The incident has not suddenly made you a different person.

But an easily omitted place remains in the sentence.

Not “The world got it wrong.” Not “Everything about me is wrong,” either.

I mistook someone for my friend.

About this essay and its source

Based on The Beauty of Self, developing its mistaken-identity and shop-window scenes. The people, dialogue and argument are illustrative. Self here has the minimal sense of a judgment returning to its maker, not virtue, shame or an immutable entity. Acknowledging one's judgment does not mean bearing all causal responsibility, and coerced confessions are excluded. Interpretations of silence and smiles remain fallible.
