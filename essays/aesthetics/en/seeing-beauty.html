I Don’t Like It. But I Can See Its Beauty.

A novel we won’t read again. A cup that is finished. Liking, beauty and judgment need not all fit inside the same verdict.

Han Qin · 2026-09-28

In this essay

She puts the novel on the table. She won't be reading it again.

Her friend is surprised. For the past ten minutes she has been talking about its final pages: the man who has spent the book avoiding the truth finally speaks, and the novelist leaves out his words. We see only the person across from him putting down a cup. Suddenly all those earlier silences matter.

“So you didn't think it was good?”

“It was very good. I just don't want to live in that house again.”

There are books we admire without wanting to return to them. There are songs we know aren't particularly remarkable that we play whenever we're alone in the car. What a work does to us and what we see in it are related, but they aren't identical. Make us answer only “Did you like it?” and something worth discussing disappears.

What liking doesn't have to prove

A song belongs to a summer in your life. Your friend had a different summer. You play it for him; nothing much happens. He owes you no corresponding emotion. Nor do you have to manufacture a universal argument to justify keeping the song.

But imagine another conversation. You drag the recording back a few seconds. Listen here, you say. That unsettled sound finally finds somewhere to go. Your friend listens again and looks up. This time he hears it.

He may still have no desire to hear the whole piece. Yet something has passed between you that isn't simply a shared mood. You didn't install your preference in him. The relation between those sounds was there to be heard; now he has heard it too.

SAE aesthetics starts from the possibility of encounters like this. It distinguishes our personal tastes—what moves us, where we choose to linger—from what different people can see for themselves and acknowledge together. The paper calls that shared part the overlap. There is a great deal of it, and still more that does not overlap.

This is a stronger claim than “everyone has their own taste.” A judgment of beauty points beyond the person making it, toward something another person might genuinely see. But it isn't an instruction to acquire the same favorites. You can hear why that musical phrase works and leave the recording behind.

Nobody can look on your behalf

An obvious difficulty follows. How do we distinguish seeing something from believing that we see it?

A museum label can identify a material or explain a process. A teacher can draw your attention to the way a line travels across a painting. These can help. They cannot turn the teacher's assent into your own encounter with the painting.

A high price doesn't settle the matter. Neither does a crowd, or the absence of one. Being in the minority is no more a guarantee of judgment than being in the majority.

Still, looking can be learned. A passage you couldn't follow becomes audible after months at an instrument. Brushwork you dismissed as a mess begins to hold together. Learning from someone needn't mean borrowing their verdict forever. Good teaching gradually makes the teacher's presence unnecessary.

“You disagree because you don't understand” cannot be the last word. The person claiming beauty must remain answerable to a question: what, exactly, are you pointing to? If every objection is ruled out as a failure to see, the claim has secured itself against ever being wrong. The revised SAE paper explicitly leaves room for its claims to fail. A public essay shouldn't quietly close that opening.

Beauty isn't a promise of comfort

In a film, a daughter enters the room where her father used to live. The camera stays back. No music supplies the grief. We wait for her to say something. She closes a window.

The scene may hurt. We are not enjoying her loss. Yet we might see why the film holds this distance, why it doesn't finish her grief for her or explain those years away. Sadness isn't evidence of beauty. But discomfort doesn't disqualify it either.

The reverse matters too. A tune can soothe us without that feeling settling whether it is beautiful. There is nothing shameful about wanting relief. It simply answers a different question.

And a further distinction is essential: the beauty of a tragic work does not justify the injury it depicts. Finding something in the traces of the past doesn't mean the suffering that left them was worth causing. Beauty cannot make out a receipt declaring someone else's pain a worthwhile expense.

The finished cup

At a pottery class someone makes six cups. The sixth finally sits well in the hand. Its rim, curve and weight belong together. He puts it by a window and spends a while looking at it.

At the next table, another potter is still working. A small pressure on the inside requires a change to the outside. The shape hasn't settled. Her hands are still finding it.

There is something to look at in both places. We can be absorbed by a form coming into being, or by the quiet sufficiency of something that has arrived. Finished does not mean dead. Unfinished does not automatically mean alive.

Non Dubito's daily discoveries tend toward work still finding its way: a piece of code, an improvised tool, an experiment that went somewhere its maker didn't expect. That is an editorial interest, not a verdict on all beauty. A sentence read by millions, or music played for centuries, may still let someone see something for the first time today.

Nor does a crack automatically improve a cup. We have to see what that trace means in this particular object, rather than awarding it a prize for imperfection. Newness, age, finish and incompleteness cannot do our looking for us.

More than one way of looking

Return to the cup at the window. One person notices how it catches the light. Another follows the changes left by firing. Someone else can see why the maker left a small irregularity alone. The object is the same. The question being asked of it is not.

The thirteen rays of SAE aesthetics are directions of attention, not thirteen display cases. Stars don't have to go in one cabinet and music in another, with human beings in the last and highest. A single thing can be approached along several rays.

A question that lets a stalled argument become intelligible opens one direction. A familiar melody whose next note is already being awaited opens another. So does the sight of a person who knows how they must proceed without taking over someone else's decision.

These directions aren't a proficiency ladder. Seeing beauty in a proof doesn't promote you above someone absorbed in a patch of color. Nobody has to master all thirteen before being entitled to respond to anything.

Mathematics and physics belong here as encounters, not examinations. Why can two methods give the right answer, while only one makes the necessity visible? Why must an elegant idea wait for an answer from the world? Those are questions about beauty too. The essays won't require readers to work through technical derivations to reach them.

A favorite is not a final verdict

“This is my favorite book” is a perfectly good sentence.

“This book settles what beauty can ever be” is a different one.

When SAE says there is no supreme beauty, it challenges the second sentence. It doesn't confiscate our favorites or require a restless appetite for novelty. It denies any work, taste or theory the authority to settle in advance what everyone may see hereafter.

You return to a book after twenty years and find a paragraph you had hardly noticed. Or a sentence that once moved you now seems to speak too readily on someone else's behalf. The book needn't be written off. Your younger self needn't be ridiculed. You have seen something else this time.

Noticing something unresolved doesn't oblige you to resolve it. One reader follows the question; another closes the book. An opening shows where someone might continue. What actually carries a person forward is their own inability to leave it alone. If you stop reading tonight, you don't owe aesthetics an assignment.

At the table, her friend opens the novel and finds the passage with the cup.

“Here?”

She nods. He reads for a while.

She doesn't ask whether he likes it now.
