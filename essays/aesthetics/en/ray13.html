After Talking, We Still Took Different Paths

One returned to class. The other did not. A question reached each person without delivering either to the other's intended destination.

Han Qin · 2026-09-28

In this essay

Two friends have been going to a pottery class for two months. Over tea, one says he is not going on Thursday.

“Give it a few more weeks. Your last cup was much better than the first.”

“That's not why.”

He turns his cup and pauses. Whenever he finishes something, he explains, he takes it to the teacher. Without a nod he cannot decide whether to continue. Lately he even asks before leaving a rim slightly uneven. He does not want to keep going like that.

His friend almost says that the teacher never required permission at every step. Instead, she asks something else.

“Do you want to stop making pots, or stop waiting like that?”

“The waiting. I haven't decided about the pots.”

She leaves the rest of his answer unfinished.

Later she says, “But I was genuinely pleased when he praised mine.”

“So you still think I should go back?”

“No. I'm talking about me. He noticed why I'd left that crease instead of smoothing it out. I want to keep going.”

Now he pauses too.

“Were you pleased because he said it was good, or because he saw what you wanted to keep?”

“Both. I don't want to pretend the praise didn't matter.”

They finish their tea. On Thursday, she goes to class and he does not.

No common answer, but something happened

Retell this as successful communication and it becomes tempting to supply a tidier ending. They discover that both should stay, or finally understand that both should leave.

Neither happens.

But nothing happening would be an inaccurate account too. Not wanting to make pots and not wanting to await permission had overlapped. Asked about the difference, he separates them himself. He does not thereby agree to return, or reject the question by saying that she understands nothing about him.

She, in turn, does not let his question force a choice between two kinds of pleasure. She acknowledges both being understood and being praised. His distinction is useful without deciding which feeling she is entitled to have.

Each has reached the other without settling the other's case.

Neither conceals an examination in the next sentence: my question was so good, why haven't you thanked me? If you really listened, why hasn't your conclusion become mine?

A question can genuinely enter someone's thinking without returning them to the place its questioner expected.

A change need not resemble its source

Later, at home, he makes a cup and leaves its rim uneven. He puts it on the table to look at it on his own, without immediately asking for an assessment.

At class, she finishes a bowl and pauses before the teacher reaches her. What, in this particular bowl, does she want to preserve?

Both developments may owe something to their conversation. They are not one shared program of improvement. He has not followed her back to class. She has not adopted his absence as proof of independence.

A friend opened a question. What happens within that opening remains each person's work.

Change is not a required result, either. Someone can consider a question seriously and remain unpersuaded. If every conversation demands a visible improvement to prove that the advice was not wasted, the place of the approving teacher has merely acquired a new occupant.

Looking back later, each might ask: without this friend beside me, do I still acknowledge the decision as mine? If I see something different, can I revise it?

These questions cannot conclusively test a person. We can misunderstand our motives and describe compliance in the language of independence. They do at least remind us that not everything left in someone by an encounter has been genuinely taken up by them.

Loud disagreement proves very little

Some friends agree about almost everything and enjoy each other's company. Others argue energetically over details while never touching a conviction they share.

Neither atmosphere establishes that the mutual questioning considered here has occurred.

Look somewhere more specific. When a question reaches a belief precious to both people, is there still room for “We may be wrong here”? Or can they criticize everyone outside, while the word “we” brings further questioning to an end?

Mutual acknowledgment is not a safe-deposit box for a shared prejudice.

It need not manufacture disagreement constantly, either. Tea can simply be tea. Someone who is tired can decline another discussion. Frankness is not a license to exceed another person's limits. Driving someone into silence and then saying “I trusted you to withstand it” does not rename the harm.

A person can question, reject a way of being questioned, or leave. Silence need not indicate understanding. Someone who has changed no stated opinion may inwardly be occupied only with pleasing the other person.

A witness who overhears two elegant exchanges therefore cannot certify the relationship. What is available is this particular encounter, with the possibility that the witness has misread it.

This time does not guarantee the next

The friends leaving the café separately have not demonstrated that they will always recognize one another as people with directions of their own.

Next time someone may demand repayment. A question may reach a genuinely painful place and awaken the wish to decide for the other person. Making room for each other once does not prove that they have done so in every aspect of their lives.

There is no need to diminish the occasion. Nor must it guarantee a lifetime.

If such encounters become familiar, another kind of quietness becomes possible. Someone plans to exhibit their work; a friend asks what they want people to see. The person can answer seriously or say they have not yet worked it out. They need not establish in advance that the friend will like the answer before daring to speak.

Familiarity still offers no permanent exemption from scrutiny. The ease does not come from agreeing never to disturb each other. A question can arrive, a refusal can be spoken, and any change can still wait for the person themselves to see its point.

Neither becomes the other person's self on their behalf.

The final essay is not the highest step

A new friend joining them one day would change the situation again.

The understanding between the original pair might make it difficult for the newcomer to speak. They might have thought themselves open to different views when they had only made room for familiar differences. Does an unfamiliar voice receive the space they leave for each other?

Beyond that table are colleagues, occasional acquaintances and people who will never become close. A further direction of inquiry opens here: none of them should be reduced to a position in somebody else's undertaking.

How that direction becomes an arrangement for living together has not been answered by a conversation over tea. Nor can people first be treated as material for a better world and receive their standing as persons once the construction is finished.

The thirteen essays end here. But colors, tree rings, proofs and individual commitments have not all been preliminary exercises leading toward this cup of tea.

The garden's blue has not become a lower kind of beauty. A task someone carefully completes alone need not await another person's recognition before offering anything worth attention.

This is simply the final direction: after talking, we still took different paths. During the conversation, neither person disappeared from the other's view because of it.

About this essay and its source

Based on The Beauty of Mutual Non-doubt, developing the pottery-class conversation. The people and later events are illustrative. Mutual acknowledgment on one occasion does not certify an entire relationship or prove that both people recognize each other as ends in every matter. Agreement, a third solution or an agreeable encounter alone does not establish such acknowledgment. Questioning a judgment does not license harm or require constant argument or a particular relationship. The thirteen essays offer directions, not ascending ranks.
