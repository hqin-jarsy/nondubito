Before the Book Went to Press, He Put the Word Back

The smoother version made sense. Why did he call again? A direction of one's own need not make a person uncorrectable.

Han Qin · 2026-09-28

In this essay

The book is about to go to press. That afternoon, the editor calls the translator about a word on the final page.

His word is a little unfamiliar. Her replacement reads more smoothly and makes sense. She reads both versions aloud. He listens and agrees.

The call ends. The matter should be settled. He starts collecting what he needs for tomorrow, then returns to his desk. The final page lies open. The smoother word is not an obvious mistake. Still, when he reaches it, he stops.

That evening he calls again.

“Could we put the word back?”

She asks why. He tries to explain, then reads the whole paragraph. He has reasons, but no single sentence that defeats all the reasons she gave that afternoon. Readers may indeed notice no difference.

He has spent three years translating this book. At this point on the page, he knows he is not yet ready to let it go.

Not one last chance to win

Seen only through the second call, he might look like someone unwilling to concede.

Most of us have encountered this at work. A matter has been agreed, someone goes away feeling dissatisfied, and the argument reopens. Sometimes the disputed word matters less than who will finally have the last word.

Is the translator doing that? His persistence alone cannot certify that he is being true to himself.

We need to look further back. During those three years, the last page has changed many times. Some changes came from the editor. Others followed a friend's discovery of a misunderstanding, or his own return months later to a passage he had made too explicit. When he genuinely saw a problem, he revised. Having labored over a sentence did not give it permanent immunity.

This time too, he read before deciding that the smoother sentence did not preserve the pause he had been hearing. He remains open to questions and must check the original. If the original does not support his word, “This is my choice” cannot excuse a mistranslation.

What deserves attention is therefore not simply his refusal to move. He has not stopped looking. He can revise and has repeatedly done so. His present insistence has grown through those revisions.

It is not a means of making himself uncorrectable.

Direction need not begin as a plan

We are accustomed to asking people about their goals.

Finish this year, publish next year, sell a certain number of copies, receive a certain kind of review. All can enter a plan and matter in practical terms. None determines which word belongs on the final page.

Sometimes a direction first appears as “Not like this, not yet.” Someone cannot describe the eventual destination but already knows they cannot call the work finished. Nobody need be standing nearby insisting. Something in what they have been attending to has not let the passage settle.

Another reader might move through the same page without stopping. Not everyone would make the second call. The direction comes through the book, and through years of reading and listening to language. In this person it meets this particular response.

That makes it neither arbitrary nor a command issued by the book. The page cannot pick up the telephone. The editor may disagree; readers may not care. He still has to decide whether to accept the trouble of asking again.

Something being his own does not mean that he invented it from nothing.

His language was learned. Teachers and other writers are present in his ear. If every contribution from another person had to be stripped away before a direction could count as one's own, there might be no sentence left to speak. The question is not whether it has a history, but whether he can still attend and judge when something different becomes apparent.

After nobody applauds

Let the story continue a little.

The book is published and does not sell particularly well. Nobody writes to say that the final word brought the whole work to life. The editor's estimate of what readers would notice may have been entirely accurate.

Has the evening call become ridiculous?

If later approval alone decides whether the translator's insistence was his own, other people's agreement is still making the decision. It has merely arrived a few months late.

This does not make failure noble, or recommend greater persistence in proportion to public indifference. Time, livelihood and other people's labor carry real costs. A project can be renegotiated or stopped. The outcome of the work and the way someone takes up a direction nevertheless require separate attention.

Suffering is no better a certificate.

Someone may repeatedly do something they feel unable to stop, without endorsing what they are doing and while wishing to be free of it. “I cannot let it go” must not automatically turn distress into a calling. No assessment of anyone's mental or physical condition follows from that sentence alone.

The translator's case invites attention to another connection: he sees a difference, acknowledges it and is willing to speak for it. If he later sees something he previously missed, he can reconsider.

This is not a test that conclusively reveals a true self. Observers can misread the situation; so can the translator. It is an additional question, meant to prevent every refusal to turn back from being celebrated as direction.

The word restored

The editor puts the word back. The next day the file goes out.

There need be no feeling of triumph. She may remain unconvinced, and he may not suddenly feel happy. He can simply read past that point without repeatedly reaching to bring the page back.

Sometimes we notice a direction while a person still hesitates. The telephone is beside him; whether he will pick it up remains unsettled. At other times, direction is quietly present in the activity itself. A line is checked, a tool put away, and the person does not have to interrogate every action to establish whether it is really theirs.

Quietness need not be shallower than hesitation. A life need not remain a struggle in order to count as one's own.

Yet the story has not finished speaking for everyone involved.

The editor's concern for fluency may have little to do with sales. She too may have read the page many times and sincerely want the reader to move through it without stumbling. Her concession does not prove that her own concern was a disposable opinion.

The translator has kept his word. Can he still see her?

If he says that because he cannot let something go, she must find it equally important; that because this is his path, she must always move aside, then the direction he has just acknowledged becomes a demand imposed on another person.

Having an insistence of one's own does not automatically establish that boundary.

The next essay begins there. I may know why I cannot write a sentence that way. That does not decide how you must write yours.

The book remains on the table, the restored word on its final page. It need not become everyone's word for this to be the page he has chosen to send out.

About this essay and its source

Based on The Beauty of Purpose, developing the paper's translator-and-editor scene. The people, dialogue and publication outcome are illustrative, not a report about an actual translation. No particular word is adjudicated. Persistence, distress, success and praise do not certify a direction as one's own. The essay neither assesses anyone's health from an inability to let go nor excuses mistranslation or control over others in the name of fidelity to oneself.
