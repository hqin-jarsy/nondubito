胶片已死，我们拍纸

Film Is Dead. We Shoot Paper.

Han Qin (秦汉)

Jon Schiereck 的 YouTube 频道简介只有两个词：Shoots paper（拍纸）。他的 Ko-fi 页面横幅写着「✨ Perforator goes brrrrr」，正文一行：「胶片已死，我们拍纸。」他造的东西是一台 3D 打印的打孔机：一条照相纸从机器里过，两支冲头在纸的两边打出齿孔，冲头由一组齿轮带动的曲柄上下驱动，手摇可以，接一把电钻也可以，一只橡胶滚轮把纸往前拽。打完的纸条卷进 135 暗盒，装进任何一台 35mm 相机，按快门，拿去暗房冲。九月十一日 Jenny List 在 Hackaday 上写它，用了一句很准的话：它是胶片，但它又不完全是胶片。图纸他免费给，通过 Ko-fi——那不是一个发文件的地方，那是一个打赏页。他还在做一台同样 3D 打印的分切机，用来把整张相纸裁成 35mm 宽的长条。

先把已构摆清楚。一九〇九年巴黎的国际会议把 35mm 定成了全球标准：每格四个齿孔，采用 Bell & Howell 齿孔。而齿孔最早的形状是圆的——之所以改掉，是因为圆孔容易磨损变形。一九二四年柯达又推出更大的 KS 齿孔，底边是带圆角的矩形，理由写得很清楚：稳定性、定位精度、耐久、寿命。后来 BH1866 成了底片的标准，KS1870 成了拷贝的标准。请注意这一整串理由指向的是同一件事：一条片子要反复过机器——底片要被印很多次，拷贝要被放映成百上千场。可是一条照相纸，曝光一次，显影一次，一辈子不会经过任何一台放映机。切口当年的理由已经过期了，切口还留在标准里。Schiereck 磨了两支钻头当冲头，于是他打出来的是圆孔——正是一九〇九年被凿掉的那个形状——而且它们工作正常。他不是在复古，也不是在「还原」什么。他只是待在一个理由死了、刀还在那里，因此一直空着的房间里。SAE 说的余项正是这个：不是切下去扔掉的那部分，是切口的根据失效之后、切口本身却没有跟着失效，于是留下来的那片没人认领的空地。

第二重余项在纸本身。Hackaday 那条评论区里有人写得很专业：相纸不适合做负片基底，因为它的光学密度不均匀，出来的像是花的。这话完全正确，而且正是全部要点所在。在一个以「忠实呈现」为目的的系统里，密度不均匀就是缺陷，是要被消掉的东西。但它同时是整张照片里唯一没有作者的部分——不是镜头给的，不是被摄物给的，也不是拍照的人给的；它是纸自己的履历，是造纸时的浆料分布在七十年后突然被一束光量了一遍。再往下两条，同一个评论区已经在给它起名字了：有人把「花的像」当成一支乐队的首张专辑名，另一个人回了两个字，faux bokeh，假散景。命名开始了，而且和绝大多数命名一样，是从玩笑开始的。

更要紧的是，这不是一个人的怪癖。另一个人叫 Jackson Bohm，网名 TheAlbinoGiraffe，一月十日在 Printables 上传了一台完全不同路线的 35mm 打孔机：核心是一台 Neutaper 电影拷贝接片机——那东西原本是电影院用来接片的，自带一副能一次冲六对齿孔的冲模——外面套上 2040 铝型材架子、两台 NEMA 17 步进、一块从旧 3D 打印机上拆下来的主板；一百英尺纸要冲大约一千个循环，跑三个小时；他的暗房是衣柜里四张宜家 Lack 桌摞成两层，四周钉一圈皮子当裙边。原料是 eBay 上四十美元买来的一卷六英寸宽、六百一十英尺长的富士 Crystal Archive 光面彩色相纸，自己裁成 35mm。实测感光度户外 ISO 8–10，室内 1.5–2；纸基太厚，一个暗盒只塞得下 24 张，而同样的暗盒装某些缩微胶片能装近 80 张。然后是颜色：整卷片子压着一层浓重的青。原因他自己查清楚了——Crystal Archive 这种纸，是为了抵消 C-41 负片那层橙色片基而设计的。你把它直接塞进相机拍，前面没有橙色片基了，那层抵消就没有东西可抵消。于是它不再是一次补偿，它变成了一种颜色。这大概是我能找到的关于余项最干净的一张图：一个补偿活得比它所补偿的对象更久，因此第一次被看见。

为什么现在看比以后看重要，这一条有确切日期。二〇二〇年四月一日，胶片社区最大的站点之一 EMULSIVE 发了一则产品新闻：「Paper Blues」，预先卷好的蓝晒相纸 35mm 暗盒，三卷一包，三档感光度 ISO 1.5 / 5 / 12，三十五美元，七月发货。那是个愚人节玩笑。它之所以好笑，正因为当时所有人都知道这事做不成。而玩笑的评论区里，愿望就明晃晃写在那儿：一个读者承认自己被骗到了最后一行，然后说，我仍然觉得做出蓝晒 35mm「胶片」是有路的，只是 ISO 1 实在太梦幻。编辑回他：既然大家这么失望，我觉得我们应该真把它做出来，算是赔罪。六年过去，两个互不相识的人各自把机器造出来了，而那个数字不是 1，是 8 到 10。一个领域的愚人节玩笑和它的实际操作之间隔着多远，是衡量余项最干净的尺子之一；这把尺子刚刚被两个有 3D 打印机的人量到了零。

还有一件事值得记下来。Bohm 那篇写得极详尽的构建记录，如今已经不在他自己写它的地方了：他的网站关了，原因他大方写在页面上——「胶片销量不足以支付托管费」。文章现在只以一段引文的形式，贴在一个免费 3D 模型页面的最下方，配图全部指向 Internet Archive。关于余项的记录，本身也成了余项。而页面上同时挂着一个 Etsy 店。这就是窗口的形状：这件事一旦拿到名字——纸片、一个 Lomography 的货号、一门「替代工艺」工作坊——花斑就会变成一个预设，那层青就会变成一枚滤镜，剩下的是又一个可以欣赏的完成品。此刻它还只是一个人磨了两支钻头，还没有人决定它是什么。

Han Qin (秦汉) · @hqinjarsy · SAE 判断力与美学

Jon Schiereck's YouTube channel description is two words long: Shoots paper. The banner on his Ko-fi page reads "✨ Perforator goes brrrrr," and underneath it, one line: "Film is dead. We shoot paper!" What he has built is a 3D-printed perforator. A strip of photographic paper feeds through it; a pair of punches drops sprocket holes down both edges; the punches are driven up and down by a crank off a gear set, turned by hand or by a cordless drill; a rubber roller pulls the strip forward. The perforated strip goes into a 135 cassette, into any 35mm camera ever made, and afterwards into a darkroom tray. Jenny List wrote it up on Hackaday on September 11th and got the sentence exactly right: it's film, but it's not exactly film. He gives the files away through Ko-fi — not a file host, a tip jar. A 3D-printable slitter, for cutting sheet paper down to 35mm-wide ribbons, is in progress.

Start with what is already construct. In 1909 an international convention in Paris fixed 35mm as the global standard: four perforations per frame, the Bell & Howell perforation. The detail that matters is that perforations were originally round, and the shape was changed because round holes wore and deformed. Kodak introduced the larger KS perforation in 1924 — rectangular base, rounded corners — and stated the reasons plainly: steadiness, registration, durability, longevity. BH1866 became the standard for negative stock, KS1870 for release prints. Every one of those reasons points at the same fact: a strip of film passes through machinery over and over. A negative gets printed many times; a release print gets projected for years. A strip of darkroom paper is exposed once and developed once and never meets a projector in its life. The reason for the cut expired. The cut stayed in the standard. Schiereck ground two drill bits into punches, so what comes out of his machine is round holes — the very shape the standard severed in 1909 — and they run fine. He is not doing retro and he is not restoring anything. He is standing in a room that has been empty since the reason for the wall died and the wall did not. This is precisely what SAE means by remainder: not the piece the chisel threw away, but the unclaimed ground that stays open once the grounds for the cut have lapsed and the cut has not.

The second remainder is the paper. Someone in the Hackaday comments puts it with real expertise: paper makes a poor substrate for photographic negatives because its optical density is not uniform, so the results come out blotchy. That is entirely correct, and it is the whole point. Inside a system whose purpose is faithful rendering, uneven density is a defect, something to be engineered out. It is also the only part of the picture nobody authored — not the lens, not the subject, not the photographer. It is the paper's own biography: the distribution of pulp on a machine decades ago, suddenly measured by a beam of light. Two comments further down, the same thread is already naming it. One reader turns "blotchy images" into the title of a band's first album; another replies with two words — faux bokeh. Naming has begun, and as usual it has begun as a joke.

More to the point, this is not one man's quirk. A second person, Jackson Bohm — TheAlbinoGiraffe — posted a completely different 35mm perforator to Printables on January 10th. Its heart is a Neutaper film splicer, the sort of thing a cinema used to join prints with, which comes with a punch and die that cuts six pairs of perforations at a stroke. Around it he built a frame of 2040 aluminium extrusion, two NEMA 17 steppers, and the control board out of a dead 3D printer bought off Facebook. A hundred feet of stock takes about a thousand punch cycles and roughly three hours. His darkroom is four IKEA Lack tables stacked two by two in a closet with a leather skirt stapled around the top. His stock is a 610-foot roll of six-inch Fuji Crystal Archive glossy colour paper, forty dollars on eBay, slit down to 35mm by hand. Measured speed: ISO 8–10 outdoors, 1.5–2 indoors. The base is thick enough that only 24 exposures fit a cassette where some microfilm takes nearly 80. And then the colour: every frame carries a heavy cyan cast. He worked out why himself. Crystal Archive is engineered to cancel the orange mask of C-41 negative film. Load it straight into a camera and there is no orange mask in front of it, so the cancellation has nothing left to cancel. It stops being a correction and becomes a colour. That is about as clean a picture of a remainder as I have found: a compensation that has outlived the thing it compensated, and is therefore visible for the first time.

Why seeing this now matters more than seeing it later has a date attached. On April 1st, 2020, EMULSIVE — one of the largest film-photography sites there is — ran a product announcement: "Paper Blues," pre-rolled cyanotype paper in 35mm cassettes, three to a pack, three speeds at ISO 1.5, 5 and 12, thirty-five dollars, shipping in July. It was an April Fools joke. It worked because everyone knew the thing could not be done. And the wish is sitting right there in the comment thread of the joke: one reader, admitting he was taken in to the last line, writes that he still thinks there could be a way to make cyanotype 35mm "film," but that ISO 1 is too much of a dream. The editor replies that given the general disappointment, he feels they should make it just to make amends. Six years on, two men who have never met have each built the machine, and the number turns out not to be 1 but 8 to 10. The distance between a field's April Fools joke and its actual practice is one of the cleanest rulers for a remainder there is — and it has just been measured to zero by two guys with 3D printers.

One last thing worth recording. Bohm's build log no longer lives where he wrote it. His site is down, and he says why on the page: insufficient film sales to maintain hosting fees. The article now survives only as a block quote pasted into the bottom of a free 3D-model listing, its illustrations all pointing at the Internet Archive. The record of the remainder has itself become a remainder. There is also an Etsy shop linked from the same page. That is the shape of the window. The moment this gets a name — paper stock, a Lomography SKU, an "alt process" weekend workshop — the blotch becomes a preset, the cyan becomes a filter, and what is left is one more finished surface to admire. Right now it is still a man who ground two drill bits, and nobody has decided yet what it is.
