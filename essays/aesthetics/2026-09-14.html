没有人再试图改良它们

No One Tries to Improve Them Anymore

Han Qin (秦汉)

Ian Summers 住在英国埃克塞特。他的第二台击弦古钢琴二○二五年完工，建造说明写于二○二六年一月，是一份挂在他自己网站上的 PDF。琴全长 940 毫米。二十六个音，八根弦——大部分音区一弦三音，最高的两根弦一弦四音。音域两个半八度，C3 到 F5；最低那个八度做成「短八度」，看上去是 E、升 F、升 G 的三个键，实际发出的是 C、D、E。律制取基尔恩贝格尔第二律。每个音的有效弦长算到半毫米，一个不落地印在文件里：625、611.5、555.5，一路到 171。调弦不用弦轴，用八个吉他卷弦器。弦是 0.33 毫米黄铜丝，第八根是两股同样的铜丝绞成的，每厘米约一圈。切弦片是门形钉，用定位键子剩下的同一批 2 毫米铜条弯出来。穿在弦间止音的那条布，是裁缝用的斜纹滚边条。键子出自一块放了五十年的巴拉那松——一块旧搁板：先整块钻孔，用锪孔钻把远端削薄 9 毫米，再用曲线锯锯成二十六根，1.5 毫米的锯路正好成为键与键之间的间隙。总共大约五百小时——「大约」，因为他说他不知道该怎么把各道工序之间的思考时间算进去。

先把已构摆在哪里说清楚。钢琴是已构。钢琴的动作是这样的：槌击弦，然后必须离开弦；擒纵机构存在的全部理由，就是让槌在触弦的那一瞬松开。击弦古钢琴没有槌，也没有擒纵。键的远端立着一枚金属切弦片，抬起来撞上弦，然后留在那里——顶住弦，同时充当发声段的一端，直到你松手为止。也就是说，只要那个音还在响，演奏者的手指就和正在振动的弦保持着机械上的连续。手上加一点力，音高就被顶上去一点，这就是压弦颤音。钢琴用来换音量的那一凿，凿掉的正是这段连续；而音量换来了音乐厅，音乐厅换来了曲目、学院、市场。今天钢琴当然是美的，但那是一轮凿构循环走完之后沉淀下来的美：它已经是构，不再是活着的余项。击弦古钢琴的美，是被凿掉的那一侧的美——手指还在弦上。

不过这台琴真正的余项不在乐器上，在那份文件上。早期音乐本身也是一座已构。Ian 的开篇第一段是一句警告：早期音乐的演奏者普遍看重本真，所以现代造的击弦古钢琴几乎清一色是历史原件的复制品——我要描述的不是那个。这句话有多重，要看 Hackaday 那条报道底下一位职业羽管键琴制作者把规矩说得多死：二十世纪「复兴期」那批想用现代钢琴技术改良早期键盘乐器的琴，被认真的业余者和职业者一致鄙夷；今天没有人再试图改良它们，只做好原件的复制品。于是这个领域对物件有一整套完备的命名法——「仿 Hubert，一七八四」——却根本没有一格留给一台既不是复制品、也不是复兴期改良品的新琴。那个空格就是余项。所以这份文件的体裁不是图纸，是选择与理由的记录：高琴马还是抬高一段音板（他选后者，因为轻的琴马也许能响一点）；单弦还是双弦（第一台上两种都试过，音量几乎没差，于是单弦，以减轻结构受力）；沉重的黄铜切弦片还是轻的门形钉（第一台沉，这台轻，抄的是乌尔比诺那台只靠一幅十五世纪图像存世的琴）。这些岔路口，复制的方法一个也走不到。

判断力最集中的地方是一弦多音。他为此写了一张 Excel 表，用来试各种弦与切弦片的排布。因为一根弦要担三到四个音，律制在这台琴上不是调出来的，是做进木头里的：同一根弦上的两个音，切弦片到琴马的距离之比，必须是频率比的倒数。也就是说，他得先定律制才能开钻。他定的是基尔恩贝格尔第二律，理由不是它通行，而是它接近弗罗茨瓦夫一份约一四七○年的手稿里为击弦古钢琴记下的那一套。紧接着他写了一句最要紧的限定：如果两枚切弦片不在同一根弦上，这些比值就不构成要求。这是拒绝收口——他不让一条整齐的规则去吞掉它其实管不着的情形。同样的克制也在别处。第一台琴二十六个音只用七根弦，以四音一弦为主，缺点是有些相隔小三度的音因共用一根弦而无法同时奏响；他没有抹掉这一条，而是把它写成这一台改用八根弦的理由。文件里还有一次失败留在图注里：第五个键（E/升 G）的切弦片离止音布太远，会从那根绞弦上弹开，他在切弦片底下加了配重才治住。一份复制图纸里没有地方放这样一句话。而最像凿构循环本身的，是一句附带的交代：键面的比例取自埃克塞特皇家阿尔伯特纪念博物馆（RAM）里一台十七世纪的维吉那琴，作者 Charles Rewallin——「Rewallin 工作的地方，和我工作的地方在埃克塞特的同一片。」

现在看比以后看重要，有三个具体理由。第一，这份文件还没有被归档。写于今年一月，九月六日被 Hackaday 捞出来，底下十二条评论。它是一个用 EasySiteWizard 8.7 搭起来的私人网站上的一份 PDF，而那个网站是一座一个人的「乐器博物馆」：十六个房间，三百五十件藏品，导航就是左边一列 Room 1 到 Room 16。站点的 TLS 早已和当代浏览器谈不拢，报道底下的读者发现，要读到这份文件，得把 https 的 s 去掉，或者干脆用 wget。一份要把浏览器降级才够得着的余项。第二，它所依赖的那条链全部是业余者的，而且全部易碎：一个叫 Renaissance Keyboards 的博客、别人代为托管在 dustyfeet.com 上的 Owen Daly 的造琴笔记、tabulatura.com、几篇关于乌尔比诺那台琴的论文，以及和制作者 Alan Grove 的一次长途电话。这些没有一样在图书馆里。让这台琴成为可能的整条链，是一串个人网站，而这正是最容易一夜之间变黑的那种链。第三，格子在等着。造琴这件事旁边就有一座运转良好的已构：现成的套件、博物馆藏品的图纸。这份 PDF 一旦被概括成「一份击弦古钢琴制作指南」，被丢掉的恰好是还活着的那部分——理由。而最深的那条理由，就摆在同一个站点的隔壁页面上：这位制作者是埃克塞特大学研究触觉的学者，一辈子在研究皮肤如何读取振动，如今花了五百小时，造出那件唯一一种手指自始至终没有离开弦的键盘乐器。这条连线还没有人命名过。它还是余项。趁那些理由还是理由，去把它们读掉。

Ian Summers lives in Exeter. His second clavichord was finished in 2025; the document describing how he built it is dated January 2026 and sits as a PDF on his own website. The instrument is 940 mm long. Twenty-six notes on eight strings — three notes per string over most of the range, four on the top two. Two and a half octaves, C3 to F5, and the lowest octave is a "short octave," so the keys that look like E, F♯ and G♯ sound C, D and E instead. The temperament is Kirnberger II. The sounding length of every note is calculated to the half-millimetre and printed out in full: 625, 611.5, 555.5, all the way down to 171. There are no wrest pins; he tunes it with eight guitar machine heads. The strings are 0.33 mm yellow brass, and the eighth is two of those wires twisted together at about one turn per centimetre. The tangents are staples, bent from the same 2 mm brass rod used to locate the keys. The listing woven between the strings is dressmaker's bias binding. The keys came out of a fifty-year-old piece of parana pine — an old shelf — drilled as a single block, thinned 9 mm at the far end with a Forstner bit, then cut into twenty-six keys with a jigsaw, the 1.5 mm kerf becoming the clearance between them. About five hundred hours: "about," because he says he is not sure how to allow for the thinking time between stages.

Put the construct where it belongs first. The piano is the construct. A piano hammer strikes the string and must then leave it; escapement exists for no other reason than to make the hammer let go at the moment of contact. A clavichord has no hammer and no escapement. The far end of the key carries a metal tangent that rises, hits the string, and stays there — holding it, acting as one end of the sounding length — until you release the key. Which means that for as long as the note lasts, the player's finger is mechanically continuous with the vibrating string. Press a little harder and the pitch rises: that is Bebung. That continuity is exactly what the piano's chisel cut away in exchange for volume; volume bought the concert hall, and the hall bought the repertoire, the conservatory, the market. The piano is beautiful, but it is beautiful the way sediment is beautiful — it is what one completed chisel-construct cycle left behind. It is construct now, not a living remainder. The clavichord's beauty is the beauty of the side that got cut: the finger still on the string.

But this instrument's real remainder is not the instrument. It is the document. Early Music is a construct of its own. Summers opens with a warning: players of early music are generally interested in authenticity, so modern clavichords are almost always built as replicas of historic instruments — that is not what he is describing here. How much that sentence weighs can be read under the Hackaday piece, where a professional harpsichord maker states the rule flatly: the twentieth-century "revival" instruments, the ones that tried to improve early keyboards with modern piano technique, are despised by serious amateurs and professionals alike; nobody now tries to improve them, and only replicas of good original models get made. So the field has a complete naming system for objects — "after Hubert, 1784" — and no slot at all for a new clavichord that is neither replica nor revival. That absent slot is the remainder. Which is why the document is not a plan but a record of choices and reasons: tall bridge or raised soundboard (he took the raised soundboard, because a lighter bridge might be louder); single or double stringing (he tried both on the first instrument, found almost no difference in volume, and chose single to reduce the load on the structure); heavy brass tangents or light staples (heavy on the first, light on this one, copied from the Urbino clavichord, which survives only as a fifteenth-century picture). A replica never has to arrive at any of those forks.

The fretting is where the judgment is. He wrote an Excel spreadsheet to try arrangements of strings and tangents. Because one string has to carry three or four notes, the temperament on this instrument is not tuned in, it is built into the wood: for two notes on the same string, the ratio of the tangent-to-bridge distances has to be the inverse of the frequency ratio. He had to settle the temperament before he could drill. He settled on Kirnberger II — not because it is standard, but because it is close to a system written down for the clavichord in a Wrocław manuscript of around 1470. And then comes the qualifier that matters most: these ratios are not a requirement if the tangents in question are on different strings. That is a refusal to close — he will not let a tidy rule swallow the cases it does not actually govern. The same restraint runs elsewhere. His first clavichord put twenty-six notes on seven strings, mostly four to a string, with the defect that some notes a minor third apart could not be played together because they shared a string; he does not bury that, he reports it as the reason this one has eight. And there is a failure left standing in a figure caption: the tangent on the fifth key, E/G♯, sits too far from the listing and kept bouncing off the twined string, so he added weight underneath it. A replica drawing has nowhere to put a sentence like that. The line that is most exactly the chisel-construct cycle, though, is an aside: the key-plate proportions come from a seventeenth-century virginals by Charles Rewallin in the Royal Albert Memorial Museum in Exeter — "Rewallin worked in the same part of Exeter as I do."

Three reasons seeing this now matters more than seeing it later. First, the document has not been filed yet. Written in January, surfaced by Hackaday on 6 September, twelve comments underneath. It is a PDF on a personal site built with EasySiteWizard 8.7, and that site is a one-man Musical Instrument Museum: sixteen rooms, three hundred and fifty instruments, the navigation a left-hand column reading Room 1 through Room 16. The site's TLS stopped negotiating with current browsers some time ago, and readers under the Hackaday post found that to read the thing at all they had to drop the s from https, or reach for wget. A remainder you have to downgrade your browser to touch. Second, every debt the document names is to an amateur, and every one of them is perishable: a blog called Renaissance Keyboards, Owen Daly's building notes hosted for him on someone else's dustyfeet.com, tabulatura.com, a few papers on the Urbino clavichord, and one long telephone conversation with the maker Alan Grove. None of that is in a library. The chain that made this instrument possible is a chain of personal websites, which is the kind of chain that goes dark overnight. Third, a slot is waiting. Clavichord building has a well-run construct right next door — the kit, the museum drawing. The moment this PDF is summarized into "a clavichord build guide," what gets dropped is precisely the living part: the reasons. And the deepest reason is sitting on the neighbouring page of the same site. The maker is a touch and haptics researcher at the University of Exeter who has spent a career on how skin reads vibration, and who has now spent five hundred hours building the one keyboard instrument in which the finger never stops touching the string. Nobody has named that connection yet. It is still a remainder. Go and read the reasons while they are still reasons.
