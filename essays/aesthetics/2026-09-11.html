她把自己的用户画像喂坏了

She Fed Her Own Portrait Until It Broke

Han Qin (秦汉)

Mika Ben Amar 生于二零零四年，在柏林工作。她做的东西是两个 Chrome 扩展。第一个叫 internet.flowers。扩展本身是 vibe coding 写的，这一点她自己讲得很直白；真正花力气的是跑在旁边的那个物体检测模型——她拿好几套数据集混着自己训，目标只有一个，让它认出各种各样的花，「尤其是那些藏在随手拍的照片后排里的花」。模型训好之后，对象是她自己的全部浏览记录：从里面把上百张含花的图片捞出来，扎成数字花束。呈现上没有任何加工：「我就照它们在我文件夹里的样子摆出来，一个网格。」第二个扩展做的是减法——打开任何网页，把内容整个删掉，只留广告。她用它连续冲浪两个月，尽量覆盖最多的站点类型和最多的广告版式，每一个站的 cookie 都点同意。成果是一百一十一张构图，二零二五年十二月十日以《Ad Space》的名义上线。她在推特上对这件事的全部说明是一句：为了做这个，我把谷歌训练成只给我看奇怪的广告。

问她怎么做审美判断，她的回答是没有审美判断：「几乎从来不是审美决定，都是很技术性的决定，然后它在审美上也就自然说得通了。」唯一的策展动作是保证输出的种类齐全——各种版式的广告都要有，各种花都要有——其余一律原样。这不是谦虚，是一条硬规矩，而且是全部关键所在。她挑的两样材料都是网页的废料：浏览记录是使用留下的沉渣，人人被教着定期清空；广告位是页面里唯一不属于内容的那块，人人被教着视而不见。一个该删，一个该滤，她两样都当正文收。

但更要紧的是那个检测模型。物体检测这门技术从设计上就是对着主体的：它被造出来是为了找到照片要拍的那个东西。她把它掉转过来，训练它去找没人打算拍的那朵花。那朵花从一开始就在框里，只是从来没被算进去过——这是余项最严格的一种形态：不是遗漏在外面，是在里面而未被计数。要把它数出来，现成的模型一个都不能用，因为没有一个是朝那个方向架的。她只能自己造一台。

《Ad Space》那一层还要更狠。中文广告业管定向系统给一个人建的那个模型叫「用户画像」，这个词是准的：那确实是一幅像，由一台机器画你，而它的精度就是形式化对一个人所能达到的程度。她的工作方法却要求她走进几千个这辈子都不会去的网站，并且在每一个门口点同意。做到一半，画像开始垮：「我的广告不再是投给我的了，变成了一堆随机的垃圾广告。」到最后阶段，系统能给她的只剩一种——智商测试广告。这就是那台机器在再也画不出她之后吐出来的东西。它不是她的像，它是那幅像的余项。而她拒绝对这批余项做任何构图：一百一十一张原样印出，连一看就是骗局的那几张也不挑掉。构图会是第一凿，她不凿。

把她放进已构旁边看，差别就很清楚。她讲过，是 Rafaël Rozendaal 的《Abstract Browsing》让她意识到浏览器扩展可以是作品。那件作品今天已经被织成挂毯、进了收藏、写进了课程——它当然是美的，但那个美是上一轮凿构循环收尾之后沉下来的沉积，它已经彻底有名字了。她这两件还没有。九月二十一日到二十七日，ARTXCODE 与 Rhizome 在纽约 Great Jones 街三十九号办一场叫《WEBSITES WEBSITES WEBSITES!》的群展，纪念 Rhizome 三十周年，二十位艺术家，名单上有 Vuk Ćosić、Petra Cortright、Rafaël Rozendaal、Auriea Harvey、Molly Soda、Kim Asendorf、LIA、EXONEMO——半张网络艺术的正典。她也在上面。展览给每位艺术家一行字，给她的那行是：「Mika Ben Amar 工作在数字基础设施对我们的了解与我们对自己的了解之间的那道缝里。」缝不是名字。点「了解更多」跳到她自己的网站，那上面总共一句话，说的是同一件事。

所以现在看比以后看重要，有三个具体理由。第一，展览十天后开幕，一旦挂进那个房间，命名就开始了——而那个房间里恰好站着 Vuk Ćosić。近三十年来他一直在纠正一个关于他的传说：网络艺术之所以叫 net.art，据说是因为他一九九五年打开一封乱码邮件，满屏字符里只有这一串可读。这个故事出自 Alexei Shulgin 一九九七年的一封信，后来被认定是个玩笑，Ćosić 本人把这个词归给 Pit Schultz。一个领域连自己的偶然都能收编成正典，然后再花几十年把它拆回去。第二，她的材料会过期。广告位和用户画像是有季节的，二零二五年底那批智商测试广告，今天已经收不到第二次；这种余项不能补采。第三，两个扩展至今都没有公开发布，而她说她想让它们公开，好让每个人都能种自己的「数据花园」。那一天到来，作品就变成工具，工具是最快被命名的东西。眼下还不是那一天。

internet.flowers ↗

Han Qin (秦汉) · @hqinjarsy · SAE 判断力与美学

Mika Ben Amar was born in 2004 and works in Berlin. What she makes are Chrome extensions. The first was internet.flowers. She is candid that the extension itself was vibe-coded; the part that took real work was the object-detection model running alongside it, which she trained herself on a mix of datasets with a single goal — that it recognize every kind of flower, "especially ones hiding in the back of random pictures you find online." Once it worked she pointed it at her own browser history in full, pulled out the hundreds of images with a flower somewhere in them, and arranged those into digital bouquets. Nothing was styled: "I just presented the images the same way they appeared in my files, as a grid." The second extension subtracts. Open any page and it deletes the content, leaving only the ads. She surfed with it for two months straight, chasing the widest possible range of sites and ad layouts, accepting cookies at every one. The result was 111 compositions, released on December 10, 2025 as Ad Space. Her entire account of the process, posted on X: I trained Google to only show me weird ads for this.

Asked how she makes aesthetic decisions, she answers that she doesn't: "It's usually never about aesthetic decisions. It's always just very technical decisions, then it all makes sense aesthetically as well." The one curatorial act is coverage — every ad layout represented, every kind of flower — and everything else stands as it arrived. That isn't modesty, it's a hard rule, and it is the whole of the matter. Both of her materials are waste. Browser history is the sediment left by use, the thing everyone is taught to clear. The ad slot is the one region of a page that isn't the page, the thing everyone is taught not to see. One you delete, one you filter; she files both as the text.

The detector is the sharper move. Object detection is built facing the subject — it exists to find the thing the photograph was taken of. She turned it around and trained it to find the flower nobody meant to photograph. That flower was inside the frame the whole time and was never counted, which is the remainder in its strictest form: not left outside, but present and untallied. To count it she could not use anything off the shelf, because nothing off the shelf is aimed that way. She had to build the instrument first.

Ad Space goes further. The industry calls the model a targeting system builds of a person a profile; the Chinese term, 用户画像, says it plainer — user portrait. It is a picture, drawn of you by a machine, and its accuracy is the measure of how far formalization can get with a person. Her method required her to walk into thousands of sites she would never otherwise open and consent at each door. Partway through, the portrait began to fail: "my ads stopped being targeted to me and just became random selection of slop ads." By the end the system had one thing left to offer her — ads for IQ tests. That is what the machine emits once it can no longer draw her. It is not her picture; it is the remainder of her picture. And she refuses to compose the remainder: 111 frames printed as they arrived, the obvious scams left in. Composing would be the first chisel. She does not make it.

Set her beside an already-construct and the difference is easy to see. She has said it was Rafaël Rozendaal's Abstract Browsing that showed her a browser extension could be an artwork. That piece has since been woven into tapestries, collected, taught — it is beautiful, but its beauty is sediment, what settled after a chisel-construct cycle closed. It is thoroughly named. Hers are not. From September 21 to 27, ARTXCODE and Rhizome open WEBSITES WEBSITES WEBSITES! at 39 Great Jones Street in New York, marking Rhizome's thirtieth year: twenty artists, among them Vuk Ćosić, Petra Cortright, Rafaël Rozendaal, Auriea Harvey, Molly Soda, Kim Asendorf, LIA, EXONEMO — half the canon of net art. She is on the list. The exhibition gives each artist one line, and hers reads: "Mika Ben Amar is working in the gap between what digital infrastructures knows about us and what we know about ourselves." A gap is not a name. The Learn More link goes to her own site, which holds one sentence, saying the same thing.

Three reasons seeing this now matters more than seeing it later. First, the show opens in ten days, and once she is hanging in that room the naming starts — in a room that happens to contain Vuk Ćosić, who has spent most of thirty years correcting the story that his field got its name from a corrupted email he opened in 1995, the only legible string on a screen of garbage. The story came from a 1997 message by Alexei Shulgin, was later identified as a prank, and Ćosić himself credits the term to Pit Schultz. A field can absorb even its own accident into canon, then spend decades prying it back out. Second, her material expires. Ad slots and user portraits have a season; the IQ-test ads of late 2025 cannot be collected a second time. This kind of remainder does not wait to be gathered. Third, neither extension has been released publicly, and she has said she wants them out so that anyone can grow their own "data gardens." On the day that happens the work becomes a tool, and tools are named fastest of all. That day has not come.
