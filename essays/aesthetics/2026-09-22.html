让云母去算

Let the Mica Do the Math

Han Qin (秦汉)

Visual Thinker 在一支新视频里做了一件看起来像是倒退的事：不跑流体仿真，改用流变显示液（rheoscopic fluid）。配方简单到近乎敷衍——化妆品里加闪的云母粉，兑水，必要时再滴一滴洗洁精防止结团。云母是扁平的反光薄片，悬浮在水里时会顺着局部剪切方向排好，于是每一片都成了一枚小小的指针，把它所在那一点的流动朝向反射给你看。

他先做的是一件玩具：把两片激光切割的亚克力夹出一层薄薄的流体，在一只 Benchy 的船体截面里埋上磁铁，从外面拖着它穿过这层液体。船身两侧的剥离、尾迹里的涡街，全部在你眼前出现，不需要网格，不需要求解器，不需要 GPU。后面还有一座用激光切割亚克力和 3D 打印件拼成的小风洞——他自己也提醒，要想让它有一点定量上的用处，得认真对待尺度和流速，也就是雷诺数。

计算流体力学是当代最彻底的已构之一。它曾经是一次活的凿削：把连续的流体切成网格，把 Navier–Stokes 方程离散成可以一步一步推进的算术。这一刀切得极成功，以至于今天“看流场”几乎等于“跑仿真”。但每一次离散都留下余项——小于网格尺度的湍流、需要额外闭合模型去猜的那部分——而这些余项在彩色云图里是看不见的。云图给你的是一张被算过、被平滑过、已经命名好的图。

云母做的是相反的事。它不求解，它不离散，它不替流体做任何决定。流体自己算，自己算完，云母只负责让结果反光。这里没有被删掉的余项：网格底下那些本来要被模型化的小尺度结构，就在那里闪。这不是更精确——它连雷诺数都对不上，是定性的——但它诚实地不假装自己是别的东西。它的美恰好来自这种拒绝：拒绝把流动翻译成数字之后再翻译回图像。

流变显示液本身不新，六十年代就有人把它做成了会动的艺术品。新的是它此刻出现的位置：在一个“仿真”默认意味着算力的年代，一个做 Benchy 的人，用化妆品原料，回头去让物质自己显示自己。他随口提了一句，想把它和沙画桌的下挂机构合起来做成一张咖啡桌。那一刻它还没有定型，还没有变成解压视频的一个品类，也还没有变成网店里二十九块九的桌面摆件。现在看它，它还是一个问题：我们究竟是在看流体，还是在看我们对流体的计算？

Han Qin (秦汉) · @hqinjarsy · SAE 判断力与美学

In a recent video, Visual Thinker does something that looks like a step backward: instead of running a fluid simulation, he reaches for rheoscopic fluid. The recipe is almost insultingly simple — the mica powder sold to put sparkle in cosmetics, stirred into water, with a drop of soap if the flakes want to clump. Mica comes as flat reflective platelets, and in suspension they line up with the local shear. Each flake becomes a tiny needle, throwing back at you the direction of the flow at exactly the point where it sits.

The first thing he builds is a toy. Two sheets of laser-cut acrylic sandwich a thin layer of the fluid; a cross-section of a Benchy hull, with magnets buried in it, gets dragged through from outside. Separation along the hull, the vortex street in the wake — all of it shows up in front of you, with no mesh, no solver, no GPU. Later there's a small wind tunnel of laser-cut acrylic and printed parts, and he's careful to say that if you want it to mean anything quantitative you'd better respect scale and flow speed — the Reynolds number.

Computational fluid dynamics is one of the most thoroughly settled constructs of our time. It began as a living cut: slice the continuum into cells, discretise Navier–Stokes into arithmetic you can march forward step by step. The cut worked so well that today "seeing a flow" means, almost by default, "running a simulation". But every discretisation leaves a remainder — the turbulence below grid scale, the part a closure model has to guess — and that remainder is invisible in the colour plot. The plot hands you a picture that has already been computed, smoothed and named.

The mica does the opposite. It solves nothing, discretises nothing, decides nothing on the fluid's behalf. The water does its own computing, all of it, and the mica only makes the answer catch the light. Nothing has been deleted here: the small-scale structure that would have been modelled away under the grid is right there, glittering. It isn't more accurate — it can't even match the Reynolds number, it's qualitative — but it doesn't pretend to be something else. Its beauty comes from exactly that refusal: the refusal to translate flow into numbers and then translate the numbers back into a picture.

Rheoscopic fluid isn't new; people were making kinetic art out of it in the sixties. What's new is where it turns up now — in a moment when "simulation" means compute by default, someone who prints Benchies reaches for cosmetic supplies and lets matter display itself. He mentions, almost in passing, wanting to combine it with the under-slung mechanism of a sand-drawing table and make a coffee table. At this point it hasn't settled into anything: not yet a genre of satisfying videos, not yet a desk gadget on a shopping site. Seen now, it is still a question — are we looking at the fluid, or at our computation of it?
