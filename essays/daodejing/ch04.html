第四章
Chapter 4 of 81

道冲

The Dao Is Empty

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、换挡

读到第四章，你会感觉到一个明显的换挡。

前三章每一章都有具体的演示。第一章讲"有和无是同一刀"，第二章讲立"美"则立"丑"，第三章讲圣人怎么治民。每一章都从一个具体的场景立起来。

第四章一上来：

"道冲，而用之有弗盈也。"

没有具体场景了。读者突然面对的是纯抽象。

如果你跟着读到这里觉得有点跟不上——这是正常的。

第四章在做一件之前三章没做的事——把前三章演示的所有内容，往上抽一次。

主语从"圣人"换成了"道"。

问题从"圣人怎么做"换成了"道是什么样的"。

具体变成了抽象，但讲的是同一件事。

二、用了又不盈

第一句：

"道冲，而用之有弗盈也。"

"冲"是虚而涌。像井口——看起来空，但用起来有水不断涌出。

"用之有弗盈也"——"有"通"又"，反复义。用了又不盈，用了又不盈。

通行本写的是"或不盈"。"或"是不确定语气——也许有点空余。

帛书"有（又）弗盈"是反复肯定。每次用都不盈。

两个本子，强度差一层。通行本读起来像"道用了之后好像总是不会满"，帛书读起来像"道用了又不盈，用了又不盈，永远不饱和"。

字面深度完全不一样。

为什么道用了不会满？

回到第一章那个核心：道是普遍余项。每一次凿一下，构立了。但构怎么立余项都在。每一次凿用了余项，余项永远还在——永远还可以再凿。

"用之有弗盈"——就是这件事。

道是一口井，但这口井的水不是有限的库存。每打一桶水上来，井里还是满的。永远是满的。

这件事——你怎么用都不空——就是凿构循环的不饱和性。

这是第四章对"道"这个字的第一次深化。第一章说道是余项。第四章说这个余项是无限的。

三、似万物之宗

第二句：

"渊呵，似万物之宗。"

渊是深水。看起来静，但底下深不可测。

"万物之宗"——万物的根源。

这里有一个字要注意。

不是"是万物之宗"，是"似万物之宗"。

一字之差。

如果说道"是"万物的根源——那道就成了一个最高的构。最高的生产者，最高的源头，最高的主宰。

老子用"似"——道只是看起来像万物的根源。

为什么？

因为所有的构都从余项里凿出来——所以万物看起来像是从道出来的。但道不拥有万物，不立万物，不作为一个主体去产生万物。

道只是那个让万物得以涌现的条件。

这一字之差，就是殖民和涵育之差。

"是万物之宗"——道是高高在上的主宰，万物是它的产物。这是殖民的姿态。
"似万物之宗"——道是让万物得以涌现的条件，不占有万物。这是涵育的姿态。

后来的注家很多把"似"读成"是"——把老子的道建成了一个最高的形而上学实体。其实老子说得清楚：似，不是是。

四、四个动作

接下来四句是这一章的核心：

"锉其兑，解其纷，和其光，同其尘。"

四句节奏一样，每句三个字加一个动作。

读起来像诗。但拆开来看，是非常精确的结构操作。

锉其兑：兑是"锐"的本字。把锐利的地方挫下去。

什么是锐？锐是一个构要去切别的层的那个尖端。一切立构的动作都有锐——上贤是锐，定美是锐，立标签是锐。

锉兑就是：在立构动作刚要生出殖民压力的时候，把切过去的力道收回来。

解其纷：纷是缠结。把缠结解开。

什么是纷？构与构之间互相纠缠、互相压迫的状态。立一个贤又要立更贤，立一个法又要立律令——立一个构就得立无数个构来维护它。

解纷就是：在这些构的缠结中松开结，让每个构各回各的位。

和其光：光是刺眼。把刺眼调和。

什么是光？一个构闪耀出来、要照进别人里的那种"我看见了真理"的姿态——所有立构的人都容易这样。自己的构一立，就觉得自己光芒万丈要去照亮别人。

和光就是：把这种光收回来，调和下来，不刺眼。

同其尘：尘是已经落下来的东西。和尘土在一起。

什么是同尘？不把自己立出来，不做那个"在上的"、"在外的"、"先进的"、"超越的"位置。

同尘就是：不自立为顶构。

这四个动作合起来，是第二章和第三章讲的"涵育"在抽象层的版本。

第二章圣人对自己——无为、不言、不占位。
第三章圣人对民和知者——不上贤、虚心实腹、不许撤场。
第四章道自己——锉、解、和、同。

主语换了，结构相同。

这四个动作还有更深一层。

道不消灭构，不对抗构，不取代构。道——

锉、解、和、同。

道不是站在构对面的东西。道是在一切构里、与一切构共在、但不被任何一个构收走的那个余。

不锉兑，就会与构对立——一对立就变成了反构派，又成了一个新的构。
不解纷，就会陷入构的缠结——一陷入就被构化。
不和光，就会成为刺眼的异物——一刺眼就被排斥出去。
不同尘，就会自己立成一个新的顶构——一自立就违反了道自己。

四面同时做，道才能在一切构之中而不被构收。

五、位置，不是主体

但这里有一个必须挡住的误读。

读完上面四句，你很容易理解成：道像一个有意图的主体那样，主动去锉、去解、去和、去同。

如果这样读，道就成了一个有主体意图的执行者——一个最高的主体——

又变成了帝。

老子在这一章的结尾就要把这个理解堵死（"象帝之先"），所以这里要先把这个区分立清楚。

真正的意思要读到两层。

第一层：道本身不做动作。道只是处于那个位置。

"锉、解、和、同"不是道主动做出来的——是构在撞上道的时候，自己被锉、被解、被和、被同。

想象一下：石头砍在水上，石头自己被磨平了。光照进深渊，光自己漫散了。

水什么都没做。深渊什么都没做。但凡是想撞它们的——撞上来就自己被改变了。

道也是这样。道什么都不做，但凡是想撞它的构——想把自己立成顶构、想覆盖一切的构——撞上来就自己锉了兑、自己解了纷、自己和了光、自己同了尘。

道是位置性的，不是主体性的。

第二层：但学道的涵育者不是道。涵育者是有主体的。

涵育者要做的事不是模仿道的"做"——因为道根本不"做"。

涵育者要做的事是：让自己成为那种位置。

具体怎么做？锉自己的兑，解自己和他人的纷，和自己的光，同于尘。

涵育者做的不是对抗别人的构，是把自己从"发出殖民压力的位置"转到"让他人自立的位置"上。

主体的操作模仿道的位置，但不冒充道。

两层合起来——

道不冒充主体（否则又成了帝）。
涵育者不冒充道（否则又成了立构者）。

道作为位置，涵育者作为学位置的主体。

这就是第四章四个动作的真正字面深度。

六、在与不在

接下来一句：

"湛呵，似或存。"

湛是清深而静。"似或存"——好像在，好像不在。

道不是一个实在之物——站在那里让人看。也不是一个虚无之物——根本不存在。

道是存与不存之间的那种状态。

什么时候在？什么时候不在？

构越紧，余项越逃。
构越松，余项越现。

当你守着一个具体的构去找道——你说"我要找到道，道在哪里"——道立刻跑掉。因为你已经把道当成一个目标构来追了，而道是所有目标构之外的那个余项。

当你不再守任何一个构去找道——你不立"我要找到道"——道就在那儿。

这其实就是第一章那两种工夫的另一种说法："恒无欲也，以观其眇"——把"我要"放下，眇就现；"恒有欲也，以观其所徼"——带方向去做，徼就出。

恒无欲时，你看见道在。
"我要找到道"那种有欲——道就跑掉。

"似或存"不是含糊其辞。是余项对构的反应机理。

七、我不知道

读完前面这几句，老子说了一句你不会预期他说的话：

"吾不知其谁之子。"

我不知道它是谁的孩子。

一个写哲学经典的人，在自己的书里说"我不知道"。

而且不是承认局限——不是"我也搞不清楚"——是结构上必须的"我不知道"。

为什么必须？

"道是谁之子"这个问题——表面是问道从哪里来——但这个问题本身就把道当成一个构来问它的来源。

而道是余项，不是构。

余项不是某个构的产物。余项是一切构的共同条件。

问余项的来源——就像问"一切问题的前提是由哪个问题产生的"——前提先于问题，所以没法问。

如果老子答"道生于某某"——他立刻违反了自己在第一章立的规矩："道可道也，非恒道也。"

所以他只能说："吾不知。"

这三个字是老子对自己第一章规矩的严守。

这里还要注意一个细节——

老子用的是"吾"，不是"我"。

整本书里，老子区分这两个字。

"我"是在关系里说话的代词——"我无为而民自化"里的"我"，站在"我和民"的对照里。
"吾"是觉知本身站出来发声——"吾不知"里的"吾"，不在任何对照结构里。

庄子后来把这一区分做成了一个操作——"吾丧我"。

吾，把我丧掉。

不是失去自己，是把所有"我是X、我是Y"的关系位置一个个放下。位置一放下，吾就在了。

老子在这里用"吾"——是觉知本身在说话。这一声"我不知"的份量从这里来。

八、象帝之先

最后一句：

"象帝之先。"

比帝还早。

帝是什么？帝是一切构之中最高的那个——最高的立构者，最高的主宰，最高的秩序。

老子说：道比这个最高的构还要先。

但"先"不是时间上的先。是结构上的先。

任何一个立构者、任何一个主宰、任何一个秩序，一旦立起来，它就已经是构了——而余项是在它立成之前就在的那个东西，也是它立成之后永远逃出来的那个东西。

余项先于一切立构。也后于一切立构。

它不在时间的链条上。

它在整个时间链条的每一点之外。

所以——不要把道理解为"一个更高的帝"。

那样就把道又变成构了。

道是帝这个构之所以可能的那个余项。

第四章是这本书的第一次抽象。

它把前三章演示的所有涵育操作往上抽一次，落到"道"自身的运作上。

它给出"道是什么样的"的字面定义——冲（虚而涌），渊（深而静），似（不是），锉解和同（四面动作），湛（在与不在之间）。

它堵死了一个最容易的误读——把道理解为最高的主宰。

它给出觉知者的发声方式——吾，不是我。

整章的实质是一个定位：

道作为位置，涵育者作为学位置的主体。

道不冒充主体，涵育者不冒充道。

两者各居其位——这是这本书后面所有讲修道、讲圣人、讲治民的章节都要回到的那个分位。

I. Shifting Gears

Reaching Chapter Four, you will notice a distinct gear change.

Each of the first three chapters demonstrated something concrete. Chapter One showed "being and non-being are the same cut." Chapter Two showed that erecting "the beautiful" erects "ugly." Chapter Three showed how the sage governs. Each one opened from a concrete scene.

Chapter Four opens with:

"The Dao is empty — yet used repeatedly, it never fills."

No concrete scene. The reader is suddenly facing pure abstraction.

If you feel a little lost here — that's normal.

Chapter Four is doing something the first three chapters did not — it takes everything demonstrated in those chapters and abstracts it one level up.

The subject shifts from "the sage" to "the Dao."

The question shifts from "how does the sage act?" to "what is the Dao like?"

Concrete becomes abstract — but it's still the same thing.

II. Used and Used, Never Full

The first line:

"The Dao is empty — yet used repeatedly, it never fills."

"Empty" here means empty yet surging. Like a well's mouth — looks empty, but draw from it and water keeps surging up.

"Used repeatedly, never fills" — the character for "repeatedly" functions here as "again and again." Used again and it still isn't full. Used again, still not full.

The received text has "perhaps not full" — an uncertain register. Perhaps a little space left over.

The silk manuscript's "used again, never fills" is repeated affirmation. Every use, never full.

Two versions, a full grade of difference in force. The received text reads like "the Dao seems to never quite get filled when used." The silk manuscript reads like "used and not full, used again and not full — permanently unsaturated."

Completely different depths.

Why isn't the Dao filled when used?

Back to Chapter One's core: the Dao is the universal remainder. Every cut, a construct is erected. But no matter how the construct is erected, the remainder is there. Every time the Dao is used — the remainder is drawn on — the remainder is still there forever, always available for another cut.

"Used repeatedly, never fills" — that is exactly this.

The Dao is a well, but this well's water is not a finite reserve. Draw a bucket and the well is still full. Always full.

This — that you can never exhaust it — is the unsaturability of the chisel-construct cycle.

This is Chapter Four's first deepening of the character "Dao." Chapter One said the Dao is the remainder. Chapter Four says this remainder is infinite.

III. Resembles the Ancestor of the Ten Thousand Things

The second line:

"Fathomless — it resembles the ancestor of the ten thousand things."

"Fathomless" is deep water that looks still but has immeasurable depth below.

"The ancestor of the ten thousand things" — the origin of all things.

There is one character to pay attention to here.

Not "it is the ancestor of the ten thousand things." "Resembles the ancestor of the ten thousand things."

One character's difference.

If the Dao "is" the origin of all things — then the Dao becomes the highest construct. The supreme producer, the supreme source, the supreme ruler.

Laozi uses "resembles" — the Dao only looks like the origin of the ten thousand things.

Why?

Because all constructs are chiseled out of the remainder — so the ten thousand things look as though they emerge from the Dao. But the Dao does not possess the ten thousand things, does not erect them, does not act as a subject to produce them.

The Dao is only the condition that allows the ten thousand things to emerge.

This one character's difference is the difference between colonizing and cultivating.

"Is the ancestor" — the Dao is a supreme ruler on high, the ten thousand things its products. The colonizing posture.
"Resembles the ancestor" — the Dao is the condition allowing the ten thousand things to emerge; it does not own them. The cultivating posture.

Many later commentators read "resembles" as "is" — building Laozi's Dao into a supreme metaphysical entity. But Laozi is explicit: resembles. Not is.

IV. Four Actions

The next four lines are the chapter's core:

"Blunt its edge; untangle its knots; harmonize its glare; merge with the dust."

Four lines with the same rhythm, each three characters plus an action.

It reads like poetry. Unpacked, it is very precise structural operation.

Blunt its edge: "edge" here is the original character for "sharp." Blunt the sharp places down.

What is the edge? The tip of a construct as it moves to cut into another layer. Every act of erecting a construct has an edge — elevating the worthy is an edge, defining the beautiful is an edge, erecting any label is an edge.

Blunting the edge means: at the moment a construct-erecting action is about to generate colonizing pressure, pulling the cutting force back.

Untangle its knots: "knots" is entanglement. Undo the entanglement.

What are the knots? The state of constructs entangled with and pressing on each other. Erect "the worthy" and you must erect "the even more worthy"; erect a law and you must erect regulations — every construct erected demands countless other constructs to maintain it.

Untangling the knots means: loosening the entanglement among these constructs, letting each construct return to its own position.

Harmonize its glare: "glare" means dazzlingly bright. Tone down the dazzle.

What is the glare? The "I have seen the truth" posture of a construct as it shines forth wanting to illuminate everyone else — everyone who erects constructs is prone to this. The moment your own construct is erected, you feel yourself radiating light and wanting to enlighten others.

Harmonizing the glare means: pulling this light back, toning it down, making it non-blinding.

Merge with the dust: "dust" is what has already settled. Be with the dust.

What is merging with the dust? Not placing yourself apart, not taking up the position of "above," "outside," "advanced," or "transcendent."

Merging with the dust means: not self-erecting as the top construct.

All four actions together are the cultivation described in Chapters Two and Three — abstracted one level up.

Chapter Two — the sage toward himself: wu wei, teaching without words, not occupying positions.
Chapter Three — the sage toward the people and the knowing-class: don't elevate the worthy, empty hearts and fill bellies, don't allow absence.
Chapter Four — the Dao itself: blunt, untangle, harmonize, merge.

The subject changes; the structure is the same.

There is one more layer to these four actions.

The Dao does not eliminate constructs, does not oppose constructs, does not replace constructs. The Dao —

Blunts, untangles, harmonizes, merges.

The Dao is not something standing opposite constructs. The Dao is the remainder that is inside all constructs, coexisting with all constructs, but never collected away by any one construct.

Without blunting its edge, the Dao would oppose constructs — oppose them and it becomes anti-construct, another new construct.
Without untangling the knots, the Dao would fall into the entanglement of constructs — fall in and it gets constructified.
Without harmonizing the glare, the Dao would become a blinding aberration — blind them and it gets expelled.
Without merging with the dust, the Dao would self-erect as a new top construct — self-erect and it violates itself.

All four done simultaneously — only then can the Dao remain within all constructs without being collected by any.

V. Position, Not Subject

There is a misreading that must be blocked here.

After reading the four lines above, it's easy to understand them as: the Dao, like an intentional subject, actively blunts, untangles, harmonizes, and merges.

If you read it that way, the Dao becomes an executor with subjective intent — a supreme subject —

And turns back into Lord-on-High.

Laozi will seal off this reading at the end of the chapter ("prior to the Lord-on-High"), so the distinction needs to be established clearly first.

The real meaning has two layers.

First layer: The Dao itself performs no actions. The Dao simply occupies that position.

"Blunting, untangling, harmonizing, merging" are not performed actively by the Dao — they happen to constructs when they collide with the Dao. The constructs blunt themselves, untangle themselves, harmonize themselves, merge themselves.

Imagine: a stone striking water — the stone wears smooth on its own. Light entering a deep abyss — the light diffuses on its own.

Water does nothing. The abyss does nothing. But anything that tries to strike them — strikes and is itself changed.

The Dao is the same. The Dao does nothing, but any construct that tries to strike it — any construct that tries to erect itself as the top construct, tries to cover everything — strikes and its edge blunts itself, its knots untangle themselves, its glare harmonizes itself, its separateness merges with the dust on its own.

The Dao is positional, not subjective.

Second layer: But the cultivator who studies the Dao is not the Dao. The cultivator is a subject.

What the cultivator must do is not imitate the Dao's "doing" — because the Dao doesn't "do."

What the cultivator must do is: make themselves into that kind of position.

Practically: blunt your own edge, untangle the knots between yourself and others, harmonize your own glare, merge with the dust.

What the cultivator does is not oppose others' constructs — it is shifting oneself from "the position that generates colonizing pressure" to "the position that allows others to stand on their own."

The subject's operations imitate the Dao's position, but do not impersonate the Dao.

Both layers together:

The Dao does not impersonate a subject (or it becomes Lord-on-High again).
The cultivator does not impersonate the Dao (or they become a construct-erector again).

The Dao as position. The cultivator as the subject learning to occupy a position.

This is the real literal depth of Chapter Four's four actions.

VI. Present and Absent

The next line:

"Limpid — as though it were there or not."

"Limpid" is deep and clear and still. "As though it were there or not" — as though present, as though absent.

The Dao is not a substantial thing — standing there to be seen. Nor is it nothing — entirely non-existent.

The Dao is that state between present and absent.

When is it present? When is it absent?

The tighter the construct, the more the remainder retreats.
The looser the construct, the more the remainder appears.

When you cling to a specific construct and go looking for the Dao — "I want to find the Dao, where is it?" — the Dao immediately vanishes. Because you've already treated the Dao as a target-construct to pursue, while the Dao is the remainder outside all target-constructs.

When you stop clinging to any construct to find the Dao — when you don't erect "I want to find the Dao" — the Dao is there.

This is just another way of stating Chapter One's two practices: "Enduringly without desire, observe the minute origin" — set "I want" down and the minute origin appears. "Enduringly with desire, observe the boundary it reaches" — act with direction and the boundary appears.

When enduringly without desire, you see the Dao present.
When in the "I want to find the Dao" mode of desire — the Dao vanishes.

"As though it were there or not" is not vagueness. It is the mechanism by which the remainder responds to constructs.

VII. I Don't Know

After these lines, Laozi says something you wouldn't expect:

"I (wu) do not know whose child it is."

A writer of philosophical classics, saying "I don't know" in his own book.

And not admitting a limitation — not "I'm not sure either" — but a structurally necessary "I don't know."

Why necessary?

The question "whose child is the Dao?" — on the surface a question about where the Dao comes from — but the question itself treats the Dao as a construct and asks for its origin.

The Dao is the remainder, not a construct.

The remainder is not the product of any construct. The remainder is the common condition of all constructs.

Asking for the origin of the remainder is like asking "which question produced the premise of all questions?" — the premise is prior to all questions and therefore cannot be questioned.

If Laozi answered "the Dao arose from such-and-such" — he would immediately violate the rule he set in Chapter One: "The Dao that can be told is not the enduring Dao."

So he can only say: "I don't know."

These three characters are Laozi strictly keeping the rule he set in Chapter One.

One more detail to note —

Laozi uses wu (吾), not wo (我).

Throughout the book, Laozi distinguishes these two characters.

Wo is a relational pronoun — "I act, and the people transform on their own" — standing in contrast with "the people."
Wu is awareness itself stepping forward to speak — "I don't know" — not inside any contrasting structure.

Zhuangzi later made this distinction into an operation: "wu sang wo" — the aware self sheds the relational self.

Not losing yourself — but setting down, one by one, every relational position of "I am X, I am Y." Once the positions are set down, wu is there.

Laozi uses wu here — awareness itself is speaking. This is where the weight of this "I don't know" comes from.

VIII. Prior to the Lord-on-High

The final line:

"Prior to the Lord-on-High."

Earlier than even the Lord-on-High.

What is the Lord-on-High? The highest of all constructs — the supreme construct-erector, the supreme ruler, the supreme order.

Laozi says: the Dao is prior even to this highest construct.

But "prior" is not prior in time. It is prior in structure.

Any construct-erector, any ruler, any order — the moment it is erected, it is already a construct. The remainder is what was there before it was erected, and what eternally escapes out of it after it is erected.

The remainder is prior to all construct-erecting. And after all construct-erecting.

It is not on the chain of time.

It is outside every point on the entire chain of time.

Therefore — do not understand the Dao as "a higher Lord-on-High."

That would turn the Dao back into a construct.

The Dao is the remainder that makes the construct "Lord-on-High" possible.

Chapter Four is this book's first abstraction.

It takes all the cultivation operations demonstrated in the first three chapters and abstracts them one level up, landing on the Dao's own mode of operation.

It gives the literal definition of "what the Dao is like": empty yet surging (chōng), fathomlessly deep and still (yuān), resembles (not is), four actions (blunt, untangle, harmonize, merge), limpid (between present and absent).

It blocks the easiest misreading — understanding the Dao as a supreme ruler.

It gives the mode of utterance for the aware practitioner: wu, not wo.

The substance of the whole chapter is a positioning:

The Dao as position. The cultivator as the subject learning position.

The Dao does not impersonate a subject. The cultivator does not impersonate the Dao.

Each in their position — this is the division of positions that every later chapter on cultivation, sages, and governance will return to.
