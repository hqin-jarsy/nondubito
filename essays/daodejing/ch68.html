# 真正的高手，赢得很安静

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 7 月

一、为什么最能赢的人，往往最安静

我们总觉得,"赢"应该是很响的:亮出实力、气势压人、正面刚上去、当那个说一不二的老大。

可你留心看看那些真正一次次赢下来的人——谈判桌上的、冲突里的、带团队的——他们常常安静得出奇。

他们不逞强、不发火、不跟人硬碰硬,也不摆一副"我是老大"的架子。可最后赢的,偏偏是他们。

安静的,凭什么赢过响的?

老子在《道德经》第六十八章,把这件事讲透了。这一章,是他讲"不争之德"的地方。

二、四种"真正会的人"

老子一口气,列了四种"真正会的人"——

"善为士者不武,善战者不怒,善胜敌者弗与,善用人者为之下。"

— 善为士者不武——真正会带兵打仗的,不逞武力;

— 善战者不怒——真正会打的,不带着怒气;

— 善胜敌者弗与——真正会赢的,不跟对方正面硬碰;

— 善用人者为之下——真正会用人的,把自己放在对方之下。

四句话,其实是同一件事的四个面:真正会的人,都不靠"逞"。不逞武、不逞怒、不逞强、不逞高。

"逞",往往是心里不稳的人才需要的东西。你越是底气不足,越要靠外头那点声势撑着;越是里头有货,越不必往外抖。这四种"会",说到底都是一句话:里子足了,面子就不重要了。

我们一样一样看。

三、不武、不怒：越厉害，越不用逞、越不动气

前两句,是一对——都是"不逞"。

"善为士者不武"——真正的强者,不需要老是把"我很强、我很横"摆在脸上。

那种时时刻刻要证明自己厉害的人,恰恰多半没那么厉害。真有底气的,反而松弛、不张扬——他不必逞,因为他不慌。（这也接着上一章说的:逞出来的勇会塌,真的强不用逞。）

你身边多半有这样的对比:一个人,张口就是"我认识谁谁"、"我当年如何如何",生怕别人不知道他厉害;另一个人,什么都不说,可你跟他共事几次,自然就服了。前一个,是在用嗓门补底气;后一个,是底气足到,不用出声。

"善战者不怒"——这一句,今天尤其用得上。

真正会打的人,从不带着怒气上场。为什么?

因为你一动怒,就不是你在做决定了,是那口气在替你做决定。怒火一上来,人就从"看清楚再出手"变成"凭一股火乱出手"——而这,恰恰是对手最希望你做的。

这在谈判里,几乎是一种套路:有人会故意说一句气你的话、踩一下你的痛处——不一定真为了羞辱你,是想看你会不会因此乱了阵脚。你要是当场炸了,就等于把主动权,亲手交给了他。而那个能笑一笑、把话接住、继续往下谈的人,才始终握着方向盘。

放到今天看:谈判桌上、争执里、竞争中——谁先被激怒,谁就先输了一半。沉得住气的那个,看得清全局;沉不住气的那个,只看得见眼前那口气。

而"沉得住气",不是天生的性格,是能练的:就在那口火要冲上来的一瞬,给自己半秒——先别回那句话、先别发那条消息、先别拍那下桌子。常常就是这半秒,隔开了"赢"和"输"。

我在硅谷这些年,越来越信一件事:很多关键的胜负,不在谁更聪明、谁更强,而在谁在最要紧的那一刻,没有失控。

四、善胜敌者弗与：真正的赢，是让对方无从下手

第三句,最微妙——

"善胜敌者弗与。"

这里的"与",不是"给"、也不是"参与"。它的意思是"正面交锋、对着干"。"弗与",就是不跟对方正面硬碰。

真正会赢的人,赢得往往不靠一场你死我活的正面对撞。他不在对方最强的地方跟他撞,不接对方递过来的那一招,不在对方划好的战场上跟他打。

这不是"怕事、躲着走"。是像水一样——水从不跟石头较劲,它绕过去、漫过去,最后石头还是泡在水里。

这跟上一章（ch66)说的"无争"是一路的:最高明的赢,不是把对手打趴下,是让这场"对撞",根本没必要发生。

放到今天看:两家公司,一家拼命跟对手比价格、比谁功能堆得多——打得头破血流,利润越打越薄;另一家干脆不接这个茬,去做一件对手做不了、也不屑做的事。等对手回过神来,战场早换了,他那套打法,一拳打在了空处。

真正的高手,很少靠正面的价格战、蛮力的对撞去赢——那是两败俱伤。他们赢,常常是不在对方的地盘、按对方的规则跟他打;而是换一个战场,做一件让对方根本无从跟你较劲的事。你不接招,他那一身力气,就没处使。

说到底,"弗与"不是"躲",而是一种更高的主动:你不让对方把你拖进他设定好的那场架里,你自己决定"在哪儿打、打什么"。能选择战场的人,常常还没开打,就已经赢了一半。

五、善用人者为之下：会用人的，把自己放在最低

第四句,又落回了"处下"——

"善用人者为之下。"

真正会用人的人,把自己放在被用的人之下。

这一句,你应该眼熟——它和 ch66"把自己放低"、ch65"不把自己的懂塞给别人"是同一股劲。

为什么"放低"反而是最会用人的方式?

因为人不是机器。你越是在上头压着、盯着、指挥着,他越是只会照着做、不敢多想;你把自己放下来、把空间让出去,他才敢把自己真正的判断、真正的劲,使出来。你占的位置越小,他能施展的地方越大。

这里还藏着一处很关键的字差。

紧接着这四句,老子有一句总结。帛书写的是"是谓用人";通行本（后世流传最广的版本）却改成了"是谓用人之力"。

多了"之力"两个字,意思就变味了。

— 用人（帛书)——是真正地"成全一个人":你处下、让位,他自己长起来、自己使出劲。重点在"人"。

— 用人之力（通行本)——是"利用一个人的力气":把他当成一份可榨取的产出。重点变成了"力"。

这两者,差的正是那条最要紧的线:一个是成全对方长出他自己（把他当一个人),一个是榨取对方的产出（把他当一件工具)。

放到今天看:那个把自己放在团队之下、替他们扫清障碍、把功劳让给他们的管理者——团队会长起来,会为他拼命;那个只想"用足每个人的产能"、把人当资源榨的管理者——人会被耗干,他反而得到得更少。

这里有个很朴素的检验:一件事做成了,你的第一反应,是"这是我带出来的",还是"这是他们干成的"?——把成事归给人的,人会越来越强;把人的功劳归到自己身上的,人会越来越缩。

最好的那种管理者,常常像个"扫雷的":他不站在队伍最前头发号施令,而是走在前面,把路上的坑、上头的压力、外面的干扰,一个个替团队挡掉、清掉。他把自己活成了团队的"下位"——托着大家,而不是压着大家。

六、不争之德，配天，古之极

讲完这四种,老子连下三个"这就叫"——

"是谓不争之德,是谓用人,是谓配天,古之极也。"

这就叫"不争之德"。不武、不怒、弗与、为之下——这四个"不"加起来,就是"不争"的德。不跟人争强、争气、争对撞、争高下。

这就叫真正的"用人"（前面已说)。

这就叫"配天"。这个"配",不是"配得上老天"、更不是"得了老天的偏爱"。它的意思是:这套做法,跟天地运行的方式,是合上拍的。

天不跟谁争,四季照样轮转;水不跟谁抢,百川照样归海。世界上最大的那些力量,没有一个是靠"逞"来的。你把自己调到这个频道上,借的,是天地本来就有的那股劲——所以老子说,这叫"配天"。

最后四个字,最有意思:"古之极也"——这是自古以来的极致。

注意,老子没说"这是我老子想出来的最高明的道理"。他说的是:这套东西,不是我的,是上古就一直如此、早已是古人的极致了。

你看——连讲这套"不争之德"的老子自己,都不肯往自己身上贴"这是我立的"那块招牌。他把它,推给了更早的古人。这本身,就是最好的一次"不争"。

（想想看,今天多少人反过来:一点小成绩,恨不得满世界贴上"这是我的";而老子握着这么深的一套东西,却轻轻说一句"这是古人的"。谁高谁低,一目了然。）

七、给读者的

这一章,给我们几件当下就能用的事——

第一:别急着"逞"。

真正强的人,不需要不停证明自己强（善为士者不武）。越要时时刻刻显得厉害,往往越是心里不稳。

第二:别带着怒气上场。

一动怒,你就开始按对方希望的方式出招了（善战者不怒）。先把火压下去,把事看清楚,再动手。谁先失控,谁先输。

第三:别一味硬碰硬。

赢,不一定要正面撞上去;常常是不在对方的地盘、按对方的规则跟他打（善胜敌者弗与）。你不接招,他的力气就没处使。

第四:会用人的,把自己放最低。

不是榨对方的"力",是给对方长起来的空间（善用人者为之下 / 用人,不是用人之力)。你把人当人,人才为你长;你把人当工具,工具很快就耗坏了。

第五:最厉害的赢,是安静的赢。

不动声色、不靠蛮力、让对方根本无从较劲——这才是老子说的"不争之德"。

这一章的核心,可以收成一句话——

真正的高手,不靠更响、更横、更硬去赢;靠的是安静、克制、处下——赢得让对方,根本无从跟他较劲。

八、承上启下

第六十八章,是上一章的"落地"——上一章（ch67)讲"慈"、讲"不争",这一章把它们放进了最实在的场景:带兵、打仗、胜敌、用人。而"善用人者为之下",又直接接上了 ch66 的"善下"。

"不争之德"这四个字,是老子讲了一整本书的"不争"里,又一个落点——到全书快结尾时,他会把它收在"为而弗争"上。

下一章（ch69),老子会接着往下讲用兵最深的一层:"吾不敢为主而为客,不敢进寸而退尺"——真正用兵的,不抢着当主动进攻的一方;甚至,"哀者胜矣"——心怀不忍的一方,反而胜。还是这同一股:不逞、不争、以退为进。

I. Why the People Who Win the Most Are Often the Quietest

We tend to assume that "winning" should be loud: displaying your strength, overwhelming with force, going head-to-head, being the one who says what goes.

But look carefully at the people who actually win again and again — at the negotiating table, in conflicts, leading teams — they are often remarkably quiet.

They don't assert dominance, don't lose their temper, don't clash head-on, and don't put on a "I'm the boss" air. And yet they are the ones who end up winning.

How does quiet beat loud?

Chapter 68 of the Daodejing makes this transparent. This is the chapter where Laozi speaks of the virtue of non-contention.

II. Four Kinds of "Those Who Are Truly Good"

Laozi names four kinds of "those who are truly good" in a single breath:

"Those who are truly good as warriors do not assert force. Those who are truly good in battle do not act in anger. Those who are truly good at defeating opponents do not clash with them directly. Those who are truly good at using people place themselves below the people they use."

Four statements, all faces of the same thing: those who are truly good at anything do not assert (cheng). They don't assert force, don't assert anger, don't assert dominance, don't assert superiority.

Assertion is something people need when they are unstable inside. The less grounded you are, the more you need external bluster to hold yourself up. The more you have inside, the less you need to display outside. All four of these "truly goods" come down to one sentence: when the substance is solid, the surface does not matter.

Let us look at each.

III. Not Asserting Force, Not Acting in Anger: The More Capable, the Less Need to Prove It

The first two lines are a matched pair — both about not asserting.

"Those who are truly good as warriors do not assert force" — a genuinely strong person does not need to constantly display "I am strong, I am formidable."

The person who needs to prove their capability at every moment is usually not that capable. The person who is genuinely grounded is relaxed and unassuming — they don't need to assert, because they are not anxious. (This connects to the previous chapter: performed courage collapses; real strength needs no performance.)

You have probably seen the comparison yourself: one person, the moment they open their mouth, it's "I know so-and-so," "back when I did such-and-such" — terrified that people might not know how capable they are. Another person says nothing, and after working with them a few times you simply respect them. The first is using volume to compensate for groundedness. The second is grounded enough not to need to speak.

"Those who are truly good in battle do not act in anger" — this one is especially relevant today.

Those who are truly skilled never bring anger onto the field. Why?

Because the moment you become angry, you are no longer making decisions — the anger is making decisions for you. Once the fire rises, you go from "see clearly, then act" to "act on the fire blindly" — and that is exactly what your opponent is hoping for.

At the negotiating table, this is almost standard practice: someone deliberately says something to provoke you, to press on a sore point — not necessarily to humiliate you, but to see whether you will lose your footing. If you blow up in the moment, you have handed them the wheel. The one who can receive the remark with a slight smile and continue the conversation is the one who always holds the steering wheel.

Seen from today: in a negotiation, a dispute, a competition — whoever is provoked first has already lost half. The one who stays composed sees the whole picture. The one who cannot sees only that immediate flash of anger.

"Staying composed" is not an innate personality trait — it is trainable: in the very instant that fire is rising, give yourself half a second — don't say that thing yet, don't send that message yet, don't slam your hand on the table yet. Often it is this half-second that separates winning from losing.

Years in Silicon Valley have convinced me of one thing: many of the pivotal wins and losses come down not to who is smarter or stronger, but to who did not lose control at the critical moment.

IV. The Truly Winning Does Not Clash Head-On

The third line is the subtlest:

"Those who are truly good at defeating opponents do not clash with them directly (fu yu)."

The word yu here does not mean "give" or "participate." It means "direct confrontation, going head-to-head." "Not yu" means: not clashing with the opponent directly.

Those who truly win do not usually win through a head-on collision where one side destroys the other. They don't meet the opponent at their strongest point, don't receive the strike being offered, don't fight on the field the opponent has mapped out.

This is not "being afraid, staying out of the way." It is like water — water never fights a stone; it flows around it, flows over it, until in the end the stone is sitting in water.

This connects to the previous chapter's (ch. 66) "no contention": the highest form of winning is not defeating the opponent — it is making the "clash" unnecessary in the first place.

Seen from today: two companies, one fighting furiously on price and feature count — head-to-head attrition, margins shrinking with every blow. The other simply declines to engage, and goes to do something the opponent cannot do and would not stoop to do. By the time the opponent looks up, the field has shifted; their whole strategy connects with empty air.

Real masters rarely win through frontal price wars or brute-force confrontation — those are mutually destructive. They win by not fighting on the opponent's ground, by the opponent's rules — by changing the field and doing something that gives the opponent nothing to grip. Decline the strike and their strength has nowhere to land.

At the deepest level, "not clashing directly" is not avoidance — it is a higher form of initiative: you refuse to let the opponent drag you into the fight they designed; you decide "where to fight and what to fight." Choosing the field often means you have already won half before any fighting begins.

V. Those Who Truly Use People Put Themselves Lowest

The fourth line returns to "being below":

"Those who are truly good at using people place themselves below the people they use."

This should look familiar — it is the same force as ch. 66's "placing yourself low" and ch. 65's "not forcing your knowing onto others."

Why is "placing yourself below" the truest form of using people well?

Because people are not machines. The more you press from above, watch over, and direct, the more they simply execute orders without thinking. Place yourself lower, open up the space, and they dare to bring out their real judgment and their real effort. The smaller the position you occupy, the larger the space they have to act.

There is a crucial textual difference here.

Right after these four lines, Laozi offers a summary. The silk manuscript reads "this is called using people"; the received text changed it to "this is called using the strength of people."

Two extra words, and the meaning goes stale.

— "Using people" (silk text) — genuinely "completing a person": you yield and step back; they grow and bring out their own effort. The emphasis is on "person."

— "Using the strength of people" (received text) — "exploiting a person's labor": treating them as an extractable output. The emphasis shifts to "strength."

The difference is exactly the most important line: one is completing the other person's growth as themselves (treating them as a person); the other is extracting the other person's output (treating them as a tool).

Seen from today: the manager who places themselves below the team, clears obstacles for them, and gives them the credit — the team grows, and they give everything for that manager. The one who only wants to "maximize each person's output" and treats people as resources to exploit — people get exhausted, and they end up getting less.

There is a simple test: when something succeeds, your first reaction — "this is what I developed in them," or "this is what they achieved"? — those who attribute success to their people find their people growing stronger; those who claim their people's credit for themselves find their people shrinking.

The best kind of manager often operates like a "minesweeper": not standing at the front of the column issuing commands, but going ahead and clearing out the pitfalls, the pressure from above, and the outside interference — one by one — before the team reaches them. He makes himself the "below-position" of the team — holding them up, not pressing down on them.

VI. The Virtue of Non-Contention: Matching Heaven, the Ancient Extreme

Having described these four, Laozi uses three "this is called" in succession:

"This is called the virtue of non-contention. This is called truly using people. This is called matching heaven. It is the extreme of the ancients."

This is called "the virtue of non-contention." Not asserting force, not acting in anger, not clashing directly, placing oneself below — these four "nots" together constitute the virtue of non-contention. Not competing for dominance, for satisfaction of anger, for head-on collision, for the higher position.

This is called truly "using people" (as explained above).

This is called "matching heaven." "Matching" here is not "being worthy of heaven" or "being heaven's favorite." It means: this way of operating is in tune with the way heaven and earth move.

Heaven contends with no one, and the seasons still turn; water fights no one, and the hundred streams still reach the sea. None of the greatest forces in the world operate through assertion. Tune yourself to this frequency and you are borrowing the force that heaven and earth already carry — that is why Laozi calls it "matching heaven."

The final four words are the most interesting: "the extreme of the ancients."

Note that Laozi does not say "this is the wisest teaching I, Laozi, have come up with." He says: this is not mine — it has always been so since the most ancient times; it was already the extreme of the ancients long ago.

Even Laozi, while teaching the virtue of non-contention, refuses to hang the sign "this is mine" on his own chest. He attributes it to even earlier ancients. This itself is the finest instance of non-contention.

(Consider: how many people today do the opposite — even a small achievement and they want to label it "mine" everywhere. While Laozi, holding something this deep, says lightly "this belongs to the ancients." Who is truly great here is obvious.)

VII. To the Reader

This chapter offers several things you can put to use right now:

First: don't rush to assert.

The genuinely strong don't need to constantly prove they are strong (not asserting force). The more you need to seem capable at every moment, the more likely you are unstable inside.

Second: don't bring anger onto the field.

The moment you become angry, you are acting the way your opponent wants you to act (not acting in anger). Press the fire down first; see the situation clearly; then act. Whoever loses control first, loses first.

Third: don't always go head-to-head.

Winning doesn't always require a direct collision; often it means not fighting on the opponent's ground, by the opponent's rules (not clashing directly). Decline the strike and their force has nowhere to land.

Fourth: those who truly use people put themselves lowest.

Not extracting their "strength" — giving them space to grow (placing oneself below / "using people," not "using the strength of people"). Treat people as people and they grow for you; treat people as tools and the tools wear out.

Fifth: the most powerful winning is the quietest winning.

No drama, no brute force, leaving the opponent with nothing to grip — that is Laozi's "virtue of non-contention."

This chapter's core can be summed in one line —

The true master does not win by being louder, harder, or more forceful — but by being quiet, restrained, and below — winning in a way that leaves the opponent with nothing to contend against.
