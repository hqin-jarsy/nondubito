第九章
Chapter 9 of 81

功遂身退

Withdraw When the Work Is Done

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、青牛出函谷关

老子写完《道德经》就走了。

骑着青牛，出了函谷关。司马迁后来写到这一刻——"莫知其所终。"不知道他最后去了哪里。

老子用实际行动示范演示了他写的第九章。

第九章的最后一句是：

"功遂身退，天之道也。"

事情做完了，撤下来——这是天的运行方式。

老子写完五千字，就撤下来。

不留下来当老师。
不建立学派。
不收门徒。
不让自己变成一个权威。

不是因为他不爱讲学。不是因为他想隐居。

而是因为他在第九章里讲的就是这件事——涵育者必须知道怎么离场。如果他自己写完了书还留下来——他立刻违反了他刚刚写下的话。

整个 Paper 1 到这一章收束。前八章铺好了所有工具——余项、凿构循环、涵育、不言之教、双向涵育、不自生、上善似水。

第九章用这些工具，回答一个非常具体的问题：

如果你想做涵育者，你最容易犯什么错，最后该怎么离场？

涵育不是填鸭式的教育，那就成了殖民了。

所以老子这是给了一份涵育者操作手册。

二、四个陷阱

第九章前四句都是"不要做什么"——

"持而盈之，不若其已。
揣而锐之，不可长葆之。
金玉盈室，莫之守也。
贵富而骄，自遗咎也。"

四句话讲四个陷阱。这四个陷阱是涵育者最容易掉进去的四个坑。

不是抽象的伦理告诫，是非常具体的操作错误。

前两个陷阱——持/揣——是对被育者的错误操作：涵育者想替代被育者完成本来该由被育者自己完成的事。

后两个陷阱——守/骄——是对涵育者自己的错误立构：涵育者在自己身上立了"我是成功的涵育者"这个自。

四个陷阱合起来，是涵育这件事最常出问题的全部姿态。

我们来一个一个的看。

三、持而盈：让别人盈不了

"持而盈之，不若其已。"

持是把某样东西握在手里控制。盈是让它满。

在涵育场景里——

涵育者看着被育者尚未饱满，最自然的姿态是什么？

把他拽过来，反复往他里面填。

填知识。填技能。填价值观。填经验。一切都往被育者里面塞，直到他盈。

这是几乎所有"教育者"的默认姿态。也是几乎所有公司里 senior 对 junior 的默认姿态。

老子说：不若其已——不如停下来。

为什么？

因为被育者的"盈"不可能通过你的持来实现。

你持着他反复灌，他盈的是你灌进去的东西——这是殖民。

被育者自身的盈，只能在他自己的余项运作到那个位置时自然发生。

你越持着他塞，他自己的那个涌出机制越被堵住。

停下你的持，让他自己盈——这就是"不若其已"的真义。

不是让你放弃涵育他。是放弃"通过持来让他盈"这个默认方法。

四、揣而锐：磨出来的锐保不住

"揣而锐之，不可长葆之。"

揣是反复捶打、打磨。锐是让刀刃锋利。

在涵育场景里——

涵育者想让被育者锐。让他思想敏锐、能力突出、见解独到。

默认姿态是什么？

揣而锐之——反复打磨他、训练他、批评他、修正他，直到他锋利。

老子说：不可长葆之——这样磨出来的锐保不住。

为什么？

因为被持续打磨出来的锐不是那个人的锐——是打磨者塞给他的。

一旦离开打磨者，这个锐立刻就钝了。

你的学生在你身边很锐利，一离开你回到自己的生活里就钝下去——这就是"揣而锐之，不可长葆之"的具体结果。

真正的锐是他自己凿出来的。

你越持续磨他，他越依赖你的磨，他自己凿的能力越弱。

这是训练和涵育的分水岭——

训练者揣而锐之，磨出的锐保不住。
涵育者知道这样做没用，所以停下揣的动作，让被育者自己磨出自己的锐。

持和揣是同一个错的两个版本——涵育者想替代被育者完成被育者自己该完成的涌出。

老子连着说两句，挡住涵育者最容易犯的这两个错。

五、金玉盈室：开始守，涵育就死

"金玉盈室，莫之守也。"

涵育者涵育了一段时间之后，会积累一些涵育的成果——名望、影响、学生、地位、被认可的方法论。

这些是涵育者的"金玉"。

帛书写"金玉盈室"——这个"盈"字跟第一句"持而盈之"的"盈"字是同一个字，前后呼应。

老子在这里做了一个内部对偶——

你不让别人盈，自己倒积成盈室。

通行本把第二个"盈"改成"满"，前后字脉就断了。

帛书把这个字留着——字面冲突一目了然。

默认的姿态是什么？

守住这些金玉——保护自己的声望，维护自己的方法论，防止别人质疑、动摇、挑战。

老子说：莫之守也——守不住。

为什么守不住？

因为一开始动用能量去守，你就已经不在涵育的位置上了。

涵育是让被育者自己涌出。

你守自己的金玉这件事本身就是立了"我有这些东西要保护"的自——开始消耗。

消耗一开始，涵育的能量就下降。

涵育能量下降，你就真的守不住那些金玉——因为金玉本来就不是靠守来的，是靠涵育过程中自然涌现的。

一旦你开始守，源头就干了。

真正好的涵育者不守他的涵育成果——他把那些"金玉"让给接下来的涌出。

守的那一刻，涵育就死了。

六、贵富而骄：定住当前的自己

"贵富而骄，自遗咎也。"

帛书的字面顺序是——贵在前，富在后。

通行本写"富贵"是颠倒的——把"富"放前面。

这一字之差很关键。

涵育者积累的成果里，地位/尊贵的诱惑实际大于财富诱惑。

一个涵育者比一个商人更容易因"我是受尊敬的老师"立构，而不是因"我有钱了"立构。

想想现在硅谷的思想领袖、网红创始人、独立咨询者、各种"KOL"——他们立的几乎都是"贵"的构（我是有影响力的人、我是被尊敬的人、我是被引用的人），而不是"富"的构。

贵的诱惑比富的诱惑大得多。

帛书"贵富"字面把这一层挑明，通行本"富贵"反而把它埋了。

贵富本身不是问题。

骄是问题——骄是涵育者在自己涵育有成之后，把"我是一个成功的涵育者"立成一个自我边界去守护。

"自遗咎也"——自己给自己留下祸患。

注意这里是"自"——不是别人给你带来的，是你自己立了那个"我是成功涵育者"的构在那里，咎从这里来。

咎从两个方向来。

对外——骄开始吸引敌意。

任何一个立起来要展示优越的构，都会在周围引发对抗。你本来在涵育他人，一旦立"骄"的构，你就从涵育者的位置掉到了对抗者的位置——被育者开始防你、争你、反你。

对内更严重——骄的本质是定住当前的自己。

我已经贵富了。我已经成功了。我已经对了。

一定住，就不再长。

你自己的涌出被你冻住了。

你本来还可以继续凿、继续涌、继续成为新的自己——你在守过去的那个自己的位置上停下了。

你亏的是你自己本来还可以长成的那个样子。

这正是第七章"不自生"在涵育者身上的反例——涵育者"自生"起"我是成功涵育者"的自我边界，立刻开始消耗，然后停滞，然后出事。

七、功遂身退

前四句都是"不要这样做"。

最后一句是正面告诉你应该怎样——

"功遂身退，天之道也。"

帛书写"功遂身退"。通行本写"功成身退"。

一字之差。

遂是"完成所应完成"——中性陈述。
成是"成功"——带成就感的味道。

帛书的"遂"字让退场的动机变得不一样——

不是"得到了成就之后骄傲地退场"。
是"事情完成后自然到了下一步"。

身退有两个动力同时推着涵育者走。

动力一：你自己的余项还要涌出，还要涌现。

余项是要涌的——这是这本书的核心。涌到哪里？涌到新的位置上。

如果你一直占着"他们的涵育者"这个旧位置，你自己的余项就没有空间涌到新的地方。

身退是为了自己的余项继续涌出——让它们涌到下一个该涌的地方去。

留在同一个位置上，就是在堵自己的涌出。

动力二：防止涵育关系变成闭环。

这是更深的一层。

涵育关系如果不退——涵育者一直在那，被育者一直参照涵育者——会慢慢变成一个闭合的系统。

闭合的系统会开始积累内部张力：

被育者长到某一刻会发现，自己始终活在涵育者的影子里——他自立的位置被涵育者占着，他想走出来却没地方走。
涵育者长到某一刻会发现，自己始终被绑在"他们的老师"这个身份上——他自己的下一步涌出没有空间出来。

两边都在堵，张力持续积累。

这个张力不会一直静静累着——到某一刻它会裂开。

身退有两层同时发生——

对自己，让自己的余项涌到新位置。
对被育者，让他在原位置上自立，而不必通过反叛你来获得自己。

两件事是同一个动作。

身退不是为了别人退——那是道德化的读法。

但也不仅仅是为了自己退——它同时是防止涵育关系积累到闭环的必须动作。

退，是保持整个涵育系统处于开放状态的结构性必然。

八、为什么师徒反目

回到一个非常常见的现实现象——

师徒背离这个故事在历史上反复重演。

孔子和某些弟子的几次冲突。柏拉图离开苏格拉底的方向。亚里士多德最后跟柏拉图分道扬镳。无数学派的师承关系最后变成相互背离。

这无关个人品质问题。

是涵育者身退的必然。

涵育关系积累到某个点——闭合系统张力到了顶——只有通过"裂"才能继续往下。被育者反对涵育者，是这个系统能继续下去的唯一通路。

如果涵育者身退的更早，更快，更决绝——在功遂的那一刻自己撤下来——这个背离的冲突就不需要直接发生了。不是说弟子的方向不会与涵育者相离，那几乎是必然的且必须的，正确与错误，好与坏，幻觉与涌现，本来就都是一体两面的事情。但是如果涵育者可以更早做到冰雪女王 Elsa 的 Let it go，那就大家就都会轻松很多。

被育者不需要靠反对你来证明自己长大了。他在原本的位置上自然就站起来——因为你已经退了，那个位置空着。

涵育关系不需要靠决裂来终结——可以靠自然的更替来更替。

老子写完五千字就走，是这件事的演示。

他没有留下来当孔门弟子那样的师承体系。他没有让自己变成一个权威。

他骑着青牛走了。

让这本书自己去运作。让读这本书的人自己去理解、去争论、去发展、去离开。

两千四百年后——这本书依然在被读、被讨论、被译。

没有"老子学派"。

只有一本书在一代一代人手里运作。

这就是"功遂身退"在最高尺度上的演示。

功遂了，撤下来。

天就是这样运行的。每一个节气做完它该做的事就退场，没有一个节气赖着不走。

老子也是。

第九章是这本书第一个九章的最后一章。

它把前八章的所有工具——余项、凿构循环、涵育三面、双向涵育、不自生、上善似水——收到一份操作手册里：

如果你想做涵育者，这是四个陷阱和一条原则。

老子自己先做。

写完，就走。

不立学派。不留权威。不当老师。

骑青牛出函谷关，莫知其所终。

I. The Green Ox Through Hangu Pass

When Laozi finished the Daodejing, he left.

Riding his green ox, he passed through Hangu Pass. Sima Qian, writing of this moment, said: "None knew where he ended up."

Laozi demonstrated Chapter Nine in practice.

The last line of Chapter Nine:

"Withdraw when the work is accomplished — this is the Way of heaven."

When the thing is done, step back — this is how heaven operates.

Laozi wrote five thousand words, then stepped back.

He did not stay on as a teacher.
He did not found a school.
He did not take disciples.
He did not let himself become an authority.

Not because he didn't enjoy teaching. Not because he wanted to live as a hermit.

But because what Chapter Nine says is exactly this — a cultivator must know how to exit. If he had stayed on after writing the book, he would have immediately violated what he had just written.

The whole of Paper 1 closes at this chapter. The first eight chapters laid out all the tools — remainder, chisel-construct cycle, cultivation, teaching without words, two-directional cultivation, not self-living, the highest adeptness like water.

Chapter Nine uses these tools to answer a very specific question:

If you want to be a cultivator, what mistakes are you most likely to make — and how do you exit?

Cultivation is not force-feeding — that becomes colonization.

So Laozi is providing an operator's manual for cultivators.

II. Four Traps

The first four lines of Chapter Nine are all "do not do this" —

"Hold and fill it — better to stop.
Hammer and sharpen it — it cannot be kept long.
Gold and jade fill the house — no one can guard it.
Noble and wealthy and arrogant — one leaves oneself the blame."

Four sentences, four traps. These are the four pits cultivators are most likely to fall into.

Not abstract ethical warnings — very specific operational errors.

The first two traps — hold / hammer — are wrong operations directed at those being cultivated: the cultivator tries to substitute for those being cultivated in completing what they themselves should complete.

The last two traps — guarding / arrogance — are wrong constructs erected within the cultivator: the cultivator has erected in themselves the self "I am a successful cultivator."

Together, the four traps cover every posture that most commonly goes wrong in cultivation.

Let's look at them one by one.

III. Hold and Fill: Preventing the Other from Filling

"Hold and fill it — better to stop."

Hold means gripping something and controlling it. Fill means making it full.

In a cultivation context —

When a cultivator sees those being cultivated not yet full, what is the most natural posture?

Pull them over, and pour into them repeatedly.

Pour in knowledge. Skills. Values. Experience. Stuff everything into those being cultivated until they are full.

This is the default posture of nearly every "educator." It is also the default posture of nearly every senior toward a junior in any organization.

Laozi says: better to stop.

Why?

Because those being cultivated cannot be filled through your holding.

Hold them and pour repeatedly — what they fill with is what you poured in. That is colonization.

The cultivated person's own fullness can only occur naturally when their own remainder operates to that position.

The more you hold them and stuff, the more their own surge mechanism gets blocked.

Stop your holding and let them fill themselves — that is the true meaning of "better to stop."

Not telling you to abandon cultivating them. It is abandoning "filling through holding" as the default method.

IV. Hammer and Sharpen: Sharpness That Cannot Be Kept

"Hammer and sharpen it — it cannot be kept long."

Hammer means repeatedly striking and grinding. Sharp means making the blade keen.

In a cultivation context —

A cultivator wants those being cultivated to be sharp. Keen in thought, outstanding in capacity, distinctive in insight.

What is the default posture?

Hammer and sharpen — repeatedly refine them, train them, critique them, correct them, until they are keen.

Laozi says: it cannot be kept long — the sharpness ground out this way cannot be sustained.

Why?

Because sharpness that is continuously ground in by another is not that person's sharpness — it was inserted by the grinder.

The moment they leave the grinder, the sharpness dulls.

Your student is very sharp at your side; step away and return to their own life and they dull down — this is the specific result of "hammer and sharpen; it cannot be kept long."

True sharpness is chiseled out by themselves.

The more you continuously grind them, the more they depend on your grinding, and the weaker their capacity to chisel their own becomes.

This is the watershed between training and cultivation —

The trainer hammers and sharpens; the sharpness cannot be kept.
The cultivator knows this is futile and stops the hammering — letting those being cultivated grind out their own sharpness.

Hold and hammer are two versions of the same mistake — the cultivator trying to substitute for those being cultivated in completing the surge that they themselves should complete.

Laozi says two sentences in sequence to block these two most common errors of the cultivator.

V. Gold and Jade Fill the House: Guarding Kills Cultivation

"Gold and jade fill the house — no one can guard it."

After cultivating for a period, a cultivator accumulates some fruits of cultivation — reputation, influence, students, status, an accepted methodology.

These are the cultivator's "gold and jade."

The silk manuscript has "gold and jade fill the house" — this "fill" (盈) is the same character as "hold and fill it" in the first sentence, echoing across the chapter.

Laozi is making an internal pairing here —

You don't let others fill, yet you yourself accumulate a houseful.

The received text changes the second "fill" to "full/満," breaking the textual thread between the two sentences.

The silk manuscript keeps the character — the literal clash is immediately visible.

What is the default posture?

Guard this gold and jade — protect your reputation, maintain your methodology, prevent others from questioning, unsettling, challenging.

Laozi says: no one can guard it.

Why can't it be guarded?

Because the moment you deploy energy to guard it, you are already no longer in the cultivator's position.

Cultivation is letting those being cultivated surge out themselves.

The act of guarding your gold and jade erects in you "I have things to protect" — that self begins consuming.

Once consumption starts, the energy available for cultivation declines.

When cultivation energy declines, you truly cannot guard that gold and jade — because gold and jade were never obtained through guarding; they naturally emerged through the cultivation process.

Once you start guarding, the source dries up.

A truly good cultivator does not guard their cultivation fruits — they let that "gold and jade" flow to the next surge.

The moment guarding begins, cultivation dies.

VI. Noble and Arrogant: Freezing the Present Self

"Noble and wealthy and arrogant — one leaves oneself the blame."

The silk manuscript's character order: noble first, wealthy after.

The received text has "wealthy and noble" — inverted, wealth first.

This variant is significant.

Among the fruits a cultivator accumulates, the temptation of status and distinction is actually greater than the temptation of wealth.

A cultivator is more likely to erect a construct around "I am a respected teacher" than around "I have become wealthy."

Think of today's Silicon Valley thought leaders, influencer founders, independent consultants, various "KOLs" — what they erect is almost always a "noble" construct (I am influential, I am respected, I am cited), not a "wealthy" construct.

The temptation of distinction is much greater than the temptation of wealth.

The silk manuscript's "noble and wealthy" foregrounds this layer; the received text's "wealthy and noble" buries it.

Being noble and wealthy is not itself the problem.

Arrogance is the problem — arrogance is when the cultivator, having achieved cultivation success, erects "I am a successful cultivator" as a self-boundary to guard.

"One leaves oneself the blame" — the blame you leave yourself.

Note this is "oneself" — not brought upon you by others. You erected that construct of "I am a successful cultivator"; the blame comes from there.

The blame arrives from two directions.

Outwardly — arrogance begins attracting hostility.

Any construct erected to display superiority generates opposition around it. You were cultivating others; once you erect an "arrogance" construct, you fall from the cultivator's position into the contestant's position — those being cultivated begin guarding against you, competing with you, opposing you.

Inwardly even more seriously — the essence of arrogance is freezing the present self.

I have already become noble and wealthy. I have already succeeded. I am already right.

Freeze — and you stop growing.

Your own surge has been frozen by you.

You could have continued chiseling, continued surging, continued becoming a new self — but you stopped at the position of guarding your past self.

What you lose is the shape you could still have grown into.

This is exactly the counterexample of Chapter Seven's "not self-living" applied to the cultivator — the cultivator "self-lives" a boundary of "I am a successful cultivator," immediately begins consuming, then stagnates, then breaks down.

VII. Withdraw When the Work Is Accomplished

The first four sentences are all "don't do this."

The final sentence is a positive statement of what to do —

"Withdraw when the work is accomplished — this is the Way of heaven."

The silk manuscript has "when the work is accomplished" (功遂). The received text has "when the work succeeds" (功成).

One character's difference.

Accomplished (遂) — "having completed what was due" — a neutral statement.
Succeeded (成) — "success" — carrying a flavor of achievement.

The silk manuscript's "accomplished" changes the motivation for withdrawal —

Not "departing proudly after obtaining achievement."
But "having finished the thing, naturally reaching the next step."

Withdrawal has two driving forces simultaneously pushing the cultivator forward.

First force: your own remainder still needs to surge — still needs to emerge.

The remainder must surge — this is the core of this whole book. Surge to where? To a new position.

If you keep occupying the old position of "their cultivator," your own remainder has no space to surge to new places.

Withdrawal is so your own remainder can continue surging — letting it surge to the next place it should surge to.

Staying in the same position is blocking your own surge.

Second force: preventing the cultivation relationship from becoming a closed loop.

This is the deeper layer.

If the cultivator doesn't withdraw — the cultivator perpetually there, those being cultivated perpetually referencing the cultivator — the relationship gradually becomes a closed system.

A closed system begins accumulating internal tension:

Those being cultivated, at a certain moment, realize they have always lived in the cultivator's shadow — the position where they would stand independently is occupied by the cultivator; they want to step out but have nowhere to go.
The cultivator, at a certain moment, realizes they have always been bound to the identity of "their teacher" — their own next surge has no space to emerge.

Both sides blocked, tension continuously building.

This tension won't keep accumulating quietly — at a certain moment it will crack.

Withdrawal has two layers occurring simultaneously —

For yourself: letting your remainder surge to new positions.
For those being cultivated: letting them stand independently in their original position — without having to oppose you to get themselves.

The two things are the same action.

Withdrawal is not stepping back for the sake of others — that is the moralized reading.

But it is also not merely stepping back for yourself — it is simultaneously the necessary action to prevent the cultivation relationship from accumulating into a closed loop.

Withdrawal is the structural necessity of keeping the entire cultivation system in an open state.

VIII. Why Master-Disciple Relations Break Down

Return to a very common real-world phenomenon —

The story of teacher and student parting ways has played out again and again throughout history.

Several conflicts between Confucius and certain disciples. Plato's divergence from the direction of Socrates. Aristotle ultimately parting ways with Plato. Countless schools whose succession relationships eventually became mutual departures.

This is not a matter of individual character.

It is the structural necessity of the cultivator's withdrawal.

When a cultivation relationship accumulates to a certain point — the closed system's tension at its limit — continuation is only possible through "cracking." The cultivated opposing the cultivator is the only channel through which the system can continue.

If the cultivator had withdrawn earlier, faster, more decisively — stepping back at the moment the work was accomplished — this conflict of departure would not need to occur directly. That's not to say the disciple's direction wouldn't diverge from the cultivator's — that is almost inevitable and necessary. Right and wrong, good and bad, illusion and emergence — they have always been two sides of one thing. But if the cultivator could have achieved Elsa's "Let it go" earlier, things would be much lighter for everyone.

Those being cultivated need not prove they've grown up by opposing you. They naturally stand where they are — because you have already withdrawn, the position is open.

The cultivation relationship need not end through rupture — it can be replaced through natural succession.

Laozi's leaving as soon as he finished five thousand words is the demonstration of this.

He did not stay to build a succession system like Confucius's disciples. He did not let himself become an authority.

He rode his ox away.

Letting the book operate on its own. Letting whoever reads it understand on their own, argue on their own, develop on their own, depart on their own.

Twenty-four hundred years later — this book is still being read, discussed, translated.

No "School of Laozi."

Just one book operating in the hands of one generation after another.

This is the demonstration of "withdraw when the work is accomplished" at the highest scale.

Accomplish what was due — then step back.

Heaven operates this way. Every season does what it must do and then exits; no season hangs around past its time.

So did Laozi.

Chapter Nine is the last chapter of the first nine chapters of this book.

It takes all the tools from the first eight chapters — remainder, chisel-construct cycle, the three faces of cultivation, two-directional cultivation, not self-living, the highest adeptness like water — and gathers them into an operator's manual:

If you want to be a cultivator, here are four traps and one principle.

Laozi does it himself first.

Finishes writing. Leaves.

No school founded. No authority left behind. No teacher role claimed.

Rode his green ox through Hangu Pass. None knew where he ended up.
