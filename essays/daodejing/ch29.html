Chapter 29 of 81

做事，不把天下据为己有

Act Without Owning the World

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

本章经文本章經文Chinese text for this chapter

帛书整理文本 · 本注采用帛書整理文本 · 本注採用Silk-manuscript text · the author’s edited Chinese reading

将欲取天下而为之，吾见其弗得已。夫天下神器也，非可为者也。为者败之，执者失之。物或行或随，或嘘或吹，或强或羸，或培或隳，是以圣人去甚、去大、去奢。

一、想"拿下"什么的人

一个 founder 说："我们要把这个市场 take down。"

一个 PM 说："我们要把用户体验完全 own 起来。"

一个父母说："我要让孩子成为最好的版本。"

一个政治家说："我要让国家变得伟大。"

这些愿望里，有些确实出于善意。但善意不能替我们回答另一个问题：我们究竟在帮助一件事发生，还是要求它只能按我们的意思发生？

第二十九章从这里提出警告。“吾见其弗得已”语气很重，却不必把“弗”排成一个普遍强于“不”的等级，更不能据此推出一条保证每次应验的历史定律。这里要读的是占有的限度，不是一张预测成败的公式表。

"吾见其弗得已。"

二、天下是神器

老子的核心立据在下一句：

"夫天下神器也，非可为者也。"

“神器”先让人停一下：天下不是摆在面前、任我取用的一件家具。“神”带着神妙、不可轻慢的意味；这里据此读出对整体的敬畏，不把一个古字当成现代系统理论的证明。

在上一章，器有具体的用途。这一章却把“天下”叫作“神器”。沿着这个对照想下去：一把椅子可以按我的需要摆放，一座城市却不能只按市长的一张图纸生活。街道上有会回答、会拒绝、会另作打算的人。

并不是城市因此不该规划。规划是必要的，但地图要能被实际生活纠正。真正被质疑的，是把自己的图纸当成城市全部的那一刻。

三、为者败之，执者失之

老子接下来一句：

"为者败之，执者失之。"

这里把“为”放在“取天下而为之”的语境里读：想把整个天下拿过来，照自己的意志加工。它批评的是这种包办，不是任何行动。第二章的“为而弗志”也没有取消那个“为”。

修一条路、制止暴力、组织公共卫生应对，都可能是必须承担的工作。不能因为“不可执”就撤掉责任。区别在于行动是否容许反馈，是否承认受影响的人有意见，是否知道哪里该停。

握住方向盘是为了驾驶，不是为了让道路从此再无弯曲。手不能离开，也不能僵死。

四、变化的四组形象

接下来，老子让这些不同的节奏出现在眼前——

"物或行或随，或嘘或吹，或强或羸，或培或隳。"

四组形象把不同的节奏摆到眼前：行与随，嘘与吹，强与羸，培与隳。它们不是把宇宙所有运动穷尽的一张表，而是提醒我们，一张计划表容不下事物全部的变化。

有人往前走，有人暂时跟随。前面的人也会疲倦，跟随的人可能在另一段路上认出方向。强与弱同样不是永久身份。昨天最能承担的人，今天也可能需要别人接手。

“嘘”“吹”可联系缓吐暖气与急吹凉气的经验来体会；这是古人的身体意象，不能据此把呼气方式当成判断生死的生理规则。重要的是，同样叫作气息，动作和感受却可以不同。

至于“培”“隳”，可以沿培固与毁坏的对照读。修堤、加固房子、维持可靠的制度，并不因此成了错误。问题是把一次加固误认为从此不必检查：河水会变，材料会老，人也会提出新的需要。

项目计划留出修订窗口，照料病人的安排允许交班，一项制度容许申诉——这些不是放弃秩序，而是让秩序继续接触它所照顾的现实。

五、圣人去甚、去大、去奢

最后老子给反向操作——

"是以圣人去甚、去大、去奢。"

“去甚”可以提醒我们，不把一件事推到别人再无回旋处。“去大”警惕把整个局面收进自己的尺度。“去奢”则问：哪些多出来的要求，其实只是为了证明我能要求？

这里采用帛书的“大”来展开。传世本作“泰”，古书中大、泰有相通的用法；不能仅凭这一处差异，就断定后人有意把原文改造成道德训诫。两种字形的讨论应当留有余地。

这三句话可以落到同一个日常问题上：我能不能完成自己该做的，同时不把一切都变成我的作品？

做事，不把天下据为己有

放回今天，这一章不是要创始人不做决策、父母不照顾孩子，也不是要公共事务无人负责。

创始人仍要选择产品的方向，但用户的反馈不只是等待被消除的噪音。项目负责人仍要定计划，但计划落后于现实的时候，修计划不等于承认自己无能。父母仍要保护孩子，但保护不能无限延长成替他决定一生。

孩子有一天可能选择与你不同的职业，也可能暂时没有答案。不能拿一句“我都是为你好”，把他的不同都解释成错误。你不必同意每一个选择，却可以让谈话继续发生。

这里没有反向的保证：不控制，就一定不会失去；不占有，一切就会顺利。关系仍会变化，计划仍会失败。放下占有，首先是停止把别人的存在当成自己的权利，不是买一份成功保险。

“为者败之，执者失之”留给我的提醒是：做事的时候，别把手里的这一点控制，误认成对整体的所有权。

I. People Who Want to "Take Down"

A founder says: "We're going to take down this market."

A PM says: "We're going to completely own the user experience."

A parent says: "I want my child to become the best version of themselves."

A politician says: "I'm going to make the country great."

Some of these ambitions begin in genuine care. Care, however, does not answer a different question: are we helping something happen, or insisting it happen only on our terms?

Chapter 29 issues a warning here. Its emphatic refusal need not make 弗 universally stronger than 不, much less establish a law that predicts every outcome. The subject is the limit of possession, not a formula for forecasting success.

"I see it will not be obtained."

II. The World Is a Numinous Vessel

Laozi's core premise in the next line:

"The world is a numinous vessel. It cannot be forcibly mastered."

“Sacred vessel” asks us to pause: the world is not a piece of furniture placed before us for our use. 神 carries a sense of the numinous, something not to be treated lightly. I read it as inviting respect for a whole, not as an ancient proof of modern systems theory.

The previous chapter gave vessels particular uses. This one calls the world a sacred vessel. Follow that contrast: I can arrange a chair to suit me, but a city cannot live solely by its mayor’s drawing. Its streets contain people who answer, refuse and make other plans.

That does not make planning wrong. Plans are necessary; they must also be corrected by the lives they affect. The danger arrives when the drawing claims to be the whole city.

III. Those Who Act on It Ruin It; Those Who Hold It Lose It

The next line:

"Those who act on it ruin it; those who hold it lose it."

I read “acting” within the opening ambition to seize the world and remake it. The target is that takeover, not every action. Chapter 2’s 為而弗志 still retains the act of doing.

Building a road, stopping violence or coordinating a public-health response can be necessary responsibilities. Non-grasping does not cancel them. The question is whether action admits feedback, hears those affected and recognizes a stopping point.

We hold the steering wheel to drive, not to make the road stop bending. The hand must neither abandon its task nor become rigid.

IV. Four Images of Change

Next, Laozi puts different rhythms before us.

"Things either lead or follow; breathe warmly or coldly; are strong or frail; rise or fall."

Four pairs bring different rhythms into view: leading and following, warming and cooling breaths, strength and weakness, building up and breaking down. They do not inventory every movement in the universe. They remind us that no schedule contains all that changes.

One person leads; another follows for a while. The leader tires, and the follower may recognize the next stretch of road. Strength and weakness are no permanent identities either. Yesterday’s most dependable person may need someone to take over today.

The breath images can be approached through the experience of gently exhaling warmth or blowing a cooling stream. They are bodily images, not a physiological test distinguishing life from death. Even something called by one name can have different gestures and effects.

Read 培 and 隳 through building up and breaking down. Repairing a levee, reinforcing a house or sustaining reliable institutions is not wrong. The error is treating one repair as the end of inspection: rivers change, materials age, people develop new needs.

A project has revision points; a care arrangement allows relief; an institution hears appeals. These do not abandon order. They keep order in contact with the reality it serves.

V. The Sage Removes the Extreme, the Excessive, the Extravagant

At last Laozi gives the reverse operation —

"Therefore the sage removes the extreme, removes the excessive, removes the extravagant."

“Remove excess” asks whether we are pushing something until others have no room left. Reading 大, “great,” I hear a warning against fitting the whole situation to our own scale. “Remove extravagance” asks which extra demands exist only to demonstrate our power to demand.

This essay follows the silk text’s 大. The received text has 泰; the graphs can overlap in older usage. That difference alone does not establish a deliberate moralizing alteration. The textual question deserves room to remain open.

All three can become one everyday question: can I do my part without turning everything into my creation?

Act Without Owning the World

Today, this chapter need not tell founders to stop deciding, parents to stop caring, or public officials to abandon responsibility.

A founder still chooses a direction, but user feedback is not merely noise to eliminate. A project leader still plans; revising a plan overtaken by reality is not proof of incompetence. Parents still protect, but protection cannot expand indefinitely into deciding another person’s life.

A child may choose a different profession, or have no answer yet. “It is for your own good” cannot make every difference a mistake. You need not approve every choice to allow the conversation to continue.

There is no reverse guarantee: relinquish control and you will never lose; cease possessing and everything will work. Relationships change and plans fail. Non-possession first means giving up a claim over another’s existence, not buying insurance against failure.

“Those who act upon it ruin it; those who grasp it lose it” leaves me this question: have I mistaken the little control in my hand for ownership of the whole?
