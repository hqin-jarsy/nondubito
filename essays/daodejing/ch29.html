第二十九章，共八十一章
Chapter 29 of 81

不取天下，才能不失天下

Not Grasping the World Means Never Losing It

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、想"拿下"什么的人

一个 founder 说："我们要把这个市场 take down。"

一个 PM 说："我们要把用户体验完全 own 起来。"

一个父母说："我要让孩子成为最好的版本。"

一个政治家说："我要让国家变得伟大。"

这些是 2026 年常听到的话。意图都不坏。

但老子在第二十九章一开口就给了一个断言——

"吾见其弗得已。"

绝不会得到。

不是劝、不是讨论、不是商量。是断言。帛书"弗"比通行本"不"更重——绝不，根本不会。

老子下完定局，然后用四对字面对偶证明这个断言为什么必然。这是老子很硬的一章。

二、天下是神器

老子的核心立据在下一句：

"夫天下神器也，非可为者也。"

天下是神器。绝不是可以被强行主宰的对象。

"神器"两个字要按字面精读——

神——有自己内在运动规律的（《说文》"天神，引出万物者也"）。
器——具体物件、有形之物。

神器 = 有自己内在运动规律的具体物。

天下不是没有结构的混沌一片，是有具体形态的。但这个具体形态有自己内在的运动规律，不是被外力主宰的器物。

这里有一个跟上一章（ch28）非常精彩的字面对照——

ch28 末段："圣人用之（器）则为官长"——这里的"器"是可被用的具体器（人的专业度）。这种器可以用。

ch29："天下神器"——这里的"器"前面加了"神"字，特指有自己内在运动规律的整体。这种器不可强行主宰。

同一个"器"字，两章用法完全相反。

合起来——可以用人的某个能力（器层面）。不能把整体（神器）当作可以主宰的对象。这是非常精确的区分——给所有想"控制"、"取得"、"主宰"的人立的边界。

三、为者败之，执者失之

老子接下来一句：

"为者败之，执者失之。"

"为"是强行主宰、动手干预。"执"是握住不放、想拿在手里。

为天下整体的人最终败掉天下。执天下整体的人最终失掉天下。

这一句接前面所有章的"做了不立位"线——第二章"为而弗恃"，第十章"生而弗有"，第二十四章"自伐者无功，自矜者不长"，ch28 末段"大制无割"。

但 ch29 比前面更重。

前面是"做了不立位"。ch29 是"对天下整体根本不能动手"。

不只是"做了不立位"，是对整体根本不能去为去执。可以做事，可以涵育，可以参与——但对天下作为一个整体，不能动主宰的手。

为者败之，执者失之。

四、物运动的四个维度

接下来这一段是老子在字面级做的具体证明——

"物或行或随，或嘘或吹，或强或羸，或培或隳。"

四对不是排比修辞，是结构性的论证。

第一对：行 / 随——空间位次。走在前 / 跟在后。这是物在和别物的关系中最基本的两面区分。

第二对：嘘 / 吹——气息生死。这两个字今天合用了，但在古汉语里是相反的。

钱锺书在《管锥编》里专门钩沉过这两字的相反义——

钱锺书引《淮南子》："呕（即嘘）之而生，吹之而死。"引张升《友论》："嘘枯则冬荣，吹生则夏落。"引《十六国春秋·刘琨遗石勒书》："吹之则寒，嘘之则温。"然后他下了断语："《老子》第二十九章是嘘吹二字相反义的最早出处。"（钱锺书：《管锥编》第二册，中华书局，1986 年，第 444 页。）

按这个考据读——嘘是缓出的暖气，使生。吹是急出的冷气，使死。气息的两面——生与死，温与寒，给与夺。

第三对：强 / 羸——身体力量。强是盛壮，羸是衰弱。物的力量在盛和衰之间往返。

前三对覆盖了物运动的三个不同维度——空间位次、气息生死、身体盛衰——物在每一个维度上都两面运动。不存在"只走不随"的物。不存在"只温不寒"的物。不存在"只强不衰"的物。

第四对：培 / 隳——这一对是最关键的收束。

培——按《说文》"培敦土田山川也"——在物体的根基处垒土，让根基稳固向上。隳——古字"墮"，毁坏、倒塌、垮下。

这一对和前三对不在同一层——前三对讲的是当下两面的对偶运动。但培 / 隳是有时间方向的——培是动作的"立起"姿态，隳是这个立起的最终命运（再怎么稳固也会塌）。

培 / 隳直接拎出"试图固定的姿态本身必然崩塌"——垒土稳固再多次，最终也会塌。试图让物固定在某个状态，物自己会按自己的规律继续运动。任何"求稳"的姿态本身就是埋了"塌"的种子。

四对合到一起——

前三对证明物在所有维度上都两面运动。第四对证明任何"试图固定"的姿态本身都会崩塌。

所以"为者败之，执者失之"是字面级的必然——既无法把物固定在任何维度的单面（前三对），更无法让"固定"这个动作本身不崩塌（第四对）。这就是老子立"非可为者也"的具体证据。

五、圣人去甚、去大、去奢

最后老子给反向操作——

"是以圣人去甚、去大、去奢。"

三个"去"对应前面四对的三层。

去甚——去掉过头的。不让任何对偶走到极端（针对前三对）：不让行没有随、不让嘘没有吹、不让强没有羸。

去大——去掉过大的。帛书是"去大"，通行本改成"去泰"（骄泰、傲慢）。一字之差，整章结构对应失了——帛书"去大"直接对应章首"将欲取天下"的"取"——不强行扩大主宰范围。通行本"去泰"是道德层，和章首的"取天下"对应不上。按帛书读，老子在用"去大"明确说——圣人不取天下。

去奢——去掉过分追求的。接 ch12"五色令人目盲"立的"外在堆砌带跑"线——不被追求姿态带跑。

三去合起来——不让任何维度推到极端 + 不强行扩张范围 + 不被追求带跑。

正是章首的反面：章首是"取（扩张）+ 为（强行作为）+ 求（堆砌姿态）"。结尾是"去大（不扩张）+ 不动手（去甚去奢）"。

六、不取天下，才能不失天下

整章一句话——

越求越求不得，不求天下才可以管理天下。

但这句话不是浮泛的"放下执着"——是字面级的结构论证：物在所有维度都两面运动 → 任何试图固定都必然崩塌 → 所以圣人不动手 → 不动手就是不失的方式。

放到 2026 年看——

那个想 take down market 的 founder——如果他用"取"的姿态运作，市场是神器（有自己的内在规律）。他越想抓得紧，市场越按自己的规律走。不是说不能做市场。是不能用"取"的姿态做。做事可以，但不立"我要拿下"的位。

那个想完全 own 用户体验的 PM——用户体验是用户自己的体验，不是 PM 可以"own"的。PM 可以做产品决策、做研究、做改进。但用户体验作为整体不是 PM 可以拿下的神器。试图完全 own，最后发现用户的反应永远在自己预测之外。

那个想让孩子成为最好版本的父母——孩子是有自己运动规律的神器。父母可以陪伴、扶持、引导。但孩子作为人的整体不是父母可以"做出来"的。试图把孩子做成某个版本，孩子最后要么垮掉、要么以反叛的形式回应。

那个想让国家变伟大的政治家——国家是几千万人共同运作的神器。政治家可以做政策、承担责任。但国家作为整体不是某一个人可以"变伟大"的。

每一个都是同一件事——做事可以，不立"取整体"的位。

ch29 比 ch28 末段更重——ch28 末段说"用器但守朴"；ch29 说"神器不可为"——对天下作为整体，根本不能动取的手。

合起来——对单个对象的"器"层面可以用（不殖民对方的朴）。对整体（神器）层面不能去"取"（任何取都必败）。

读到这里再看章首那一句——

"将欲取天下而为之，吾见其弗得已。"

老子说的"天下"不只是政治意义的国家——是所有"作为整体的神器"：市场、用户体验、孩子、伴侣、国家、注意力、健康、生活——每一个都是神器。对每一个，取者败之，执者失之。

不取天下，才能不失天下。

这是老子两千四百年前给所有想"拿下"什么的人留下的话。今天读，每一句都还成立。

I. People Who Want to "Take Down"

A founder says: "We're going to take down this market."

A PM says: "We're going to completely own the user experience."

A parent says: "I want my child to become the best version of themselves."

A politician says: "I'm going to make the country great."

These are words heard often in 2026. The intentions are not bad.

But Laozi opens Chapter 29 with a flat assertion —

"I see it will not be obtained."

Absolutely will not.

Not advice, not discussion, not negotiation. An assertion. The silk manuscript's "fu" (弗) is stronger than the received text's "bu" (不) — absolutely will not, fundamentally cannot. Laozi makes the verdict, then uses four pairs of verbal opposites to prove why it is inevitable. This is one of Laozi's hardest chapters.

II. The World Is a Numinous Vessel

Laozi's core premise in the next line:

"The world is a numinous vessel. It cannot be forcibly mastered."

Read "shen qi" (神器) precisely at face value —

Shen — having its own inner principle of movement (Shuowen: "heavenly spirit, that which draws out the ten thousand things"). Qi — concrete object, tangible thing.

Numinous vessel = a concrete thing with its own inner principle of movement.

The world is not formless chaos — it has concrete shape. But that shape has its own inner principle of movement. It is not a vessel that can be mastered by external force.

There is a striking verbal contrast with the previous chapter (ch. 28) —

Ch. 28's closing passage: "The sage uses them (qi)" — qi here is the usable concrete instrument (a person's professional skill). This kind of qi can be used.

Ch. 29: "The world as numinous vessel (shen qi)" — here "qi" is preceded by "shen," specifically referring to a whole that has its own inner principle of movement. This kind of qi cannot be forcibly mastered.

Same character, completely opposite usage in the two chapters.

Together: you can use a person's particular ability (instrument level). You cannot treat the whole (numinous vessel) as something to be mastered. This is a precise distinction — a boundary set for all who want to "control," "obtain," or "dominate."

III. Those Who Act on It Ruin It; Those Who Hold It Lose It

The next line:

"Those who act on it ruin it; those who hold it lose it."

"Wei" (为) = forcibly mastering, intervening by force. "Zhi" (执) = holding tight, wanting to keep in hand.

Those who act on the world's wholeness ultimately ruin it. Those who hold the world's wholeness ultimately lose it.

This continues all prior chapters' thread of "doing without staking a position" — ch. 2 "do but do not presume on it," ch. 10 "give birth but do not possess," ch. 24 "self-boasting gains no merit," ch. 28 "the great ordering does not cut." But ch. 29 is heavier than all of them.

The prior chapters say "do without staking a position." Ch. 29 says "for the whole, you cannot even begin to act or hold." Not just "do without staking a position" — for the whole, the acting hand cannot even be raised. You can do things, can nurture, can participate — but on the world as a whole, the mastering hand cannot be raised.

Those who act on it ruin it; those who hold it lose it.

IV. Four Dimensions of Things' Movement

The next passage is Laozi's concrete proof at the literal level —

"Things either lead or follow; breathe warmly or coldly; are strong or frail; rise or fall."

The four pairs are not rhetorical parallelism — they are structural argument.

First pair: lead / follow — spatial position. Leading ahead / following behind. The most basic two-sided distinction of a thing's relationship with other things.

Second pair: xu / chui — life and death of breath. These two characters merged in modern Chinese, but in classical Chinese they are opposite.

Qian Zhongshu in Guan Zhui Bian carefully examined their opposite meanings — citing the Huainanzi: "Xu (exhale warmly) and things live; chui (blow coldly) and things die." Citing Zhang Sheng: "Xu dried branches and winter blooms; chui living branches and summer falls." Citing Liu Kun: "Blow and it's cold; exhale warmly and it's warm." Then Qian concludes: "Chapter 29 of the Laozi is the earliest source of xu and chui's opposite meanings." (Qian Zhongshu, Guan Zhui Bian, Vol. 2, Zhonghua Press, 1986, p. 444.)

Reading from this textual research: xu = warm breath exhaled slowly, giving life. chui = cold breath blown sharply, causing death. The two sides of breath — life and death, warmth and cold, giving and taking.

Third pair: strong / frail — bodily power. Strong is vigorous; frail is weak. A thing's power moves back and forth between fullness and decline.

The first three pairs cover things' movement in three different dimensions — spatial position, the breath of life and death, bodily vigor and decline. Things in every dimension move both ways. There is no thing that only leads and never follows. No thing that only warms and never colds. No thing that only is strong and never frails.

Fourth pair: pei / hui — rise / ruin. This is the most critical closing pair.

Pei (培) — Shuowen: "to mound earth around fields, mountains, and rivers" — piling earth at a thing's root to stabilize and raise it. Hui (隳) — ancient character for collapse, topple, fall.

This pair is not at the same level as the first three. The first three describe simultaneous two-sided alternating movement. But pei / hui has a temporal direction — pei is the "rising" posture (mounding up, toward stability); hui is this rising's ultimate fate (no matter how stable, it will collapse). Pei / hui directly lifts out "the very posture of attempting to fix things necessarily collapses" — no matter how much earth you mound, it eventually falls. Any "seeking stability" posture itself plants the seed of collapse.

Four pairs together —

The first three prove things move both ways in all dimensions. The fourth proves any "attempting to fix" posture will itself collapse.

So "those who act on it ruin it, those who hold it lose it" is a literal-level necessity — impossible to fix things on any single side of any dimension (first three pairs), and impossible to prevent the "fixing" action itself from collapsing (fourth pair). This is Laozi's concrete evidence for "it cannot be forcibly mastered."

V. The Sage Removes the Extreme, the Excessive, the Extravagant

At last Laozi gives the reverse operation —

"Therefore the sage removes the extreme, removes the excessive, removes the extravagant."

Three "removes" corresponding to three levels of the four pairs.

Remove the extreme — remove what has gone too far. Don't let any opposition go to extremes: don't let leading have no following, don't let warmth have no cold, don't let strength have no frailty.

Remove the excessive — remove what is too large. The silk manuscript has "remove the excessive" (去大); the received text changed it to "remove the grand" (去泰, meaning arrogance). One character changes the whole chapter's structural correspondence. The silk manuscript's "remove the excessive" directly corresponds to the chapter's opening "wanting to take the world" — don't forcibly expand the domain of mastery. The received text's "remove the grand" is moral advice and doesn't correspond to the opening. Reading the silk manuscript: Laozi explicitly says — the sage does not take the world.

Remove the extravagant — remove excessive pursuit. Continuing ch. 12's thread of "being carried off by outer accumulation" — not being carried off by the posture of pursuit.

Three removals together: don't push any dimension to extremes + don't forcibly expand scope + don't be carried off by pursuit.

The exact reverse of the chapter's opening: opening is "take (expand) + act (force) + pursue (accumulate)." Closing is "remove the excessive (don't expand) + don't act (remove the extreme, remove the extravagant)." Laozi's arc is clear — those who seek must fail; those who do not seek are those who cannot lose.

VI. Not Grasping the World Means Never Losing It

The whole chapter in one sentence —

The more you seek, the less you get. Not seeking the world is how you can manage the world.

But this is not a vague "let go of attachment" — it is a literal-level structural argument: things move both ways in all dimensions → any attempt to fix necessarily collapses → therefore the sage does not act → not acting is the way not to lose.

Looking at this in 2026 —

The founder wanting to take down the market — if he operates with "taking" posture, the market is a numinous vessel (with its own inner principle). The tighter he grips, the more it moves by its own principle. Not that you cannot work in a market. Just not with the "taking" posture. Do the work; don't stake the position "I will take it down."

The PM wanting to own the user experience — user experience belongs to users; it is not something a PM can "own." A PM can make product decisions, do research, make improvements. But user experience as a whole is not a numinous vessel a PM can claim. Trying to fully own it, you find user responses always outside your predictions.

The parent wanting to make the child the best version — the child is a numinous vessel with its own inner movement. Parents can accompany, support, guide. But the child as a whole person cannot be "produced" by parents. Trying to shape the child into some version, the child either breaks or rebels.

The politician wanting to make the nation great — the nation is a numinous vessel run by tens of millions of people together. The nation as a whole cannot be "made great" by a single person.

Each is the same thing — do the work; don't stake the position of "taking the whole."

Ch. 29 is heavier than ch. 28's closing: ch. 28 says "use the instrument but keep the unhewn"; ch. 29 says "the numinous vessel cannot be acted on" — for the world as a whole, the taking hand simply cannot be raised.

Together: you can use a particular object's instrument level (without colonizing its unhewn). You cannot "take" the whole (any taking must fail).

Reading back to the chapter's opening —

"Wanting to take the world and act on it — I see this will not be obtained."

Laozi's "world" is not only the political state — it is any whole that is a numinous vessel: markets, user experience, children, partners, nations, attention, health, life itself. For each of these: those who take it ruin it; those who hold it lose it.

Not grasping the world means never losing the world. This is what Laozi left 2,400 years ago for all who want to "take" something. Today, every word still holds.
