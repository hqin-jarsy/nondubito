知道自己“不知道”，才是真本事

Knowing Where Your Knowledge Ends

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、回答来得太快的时候

会议上有人问起一个尚未查清的问题。一个人立刻给出答案，语气很稳，连下一步该怎么办都安排好了。另一个人却停下来，说要先确认两个条件。我们很容易把前者当成有本事，把后者当成底气不足。

过几天，事情露出另一面。那个没有犹豫的答案，恰好漏掉了最要紧的条件。我们才想起，慢一点的人不是没听懂问题，而是听见了问题里面尚未说清的部分。

这样的事当然也可能反过来。熟练的人可以答得又快又准；总说“看情况”的人，也可能是在回避责任。光凭一个人说话笃不笃定，判断不了他懂不懂。真正值得听的是：他知道自己的答案立在什么上面吗？换了条件，他还会不会坚持原来的话？

第七十一章把目光放在这个地方。它不要求我们把“我不懂”挂成新的招牌，而是让一个答案保留边缘：到这里，我有把握；再往外，我还没有看清。

二、地图上的空白，也是地图的一部分

知不知，尚矣。

知道自己的不知，是值得看重的知。本篇沿着这条读路，理解老子的“知不知”：不仅知道一些事情，也对自己尚不知道的部分保持清醒。

一张地图的用处，不只在画出了多少道路。标明“此处未探明”，有时和标明桥在哪里同样重要。空白被诚实地留在那里，使用地图的人才会多带一点准备，才知道这一段不能照着纸上的想象走。

反而是一张什么都画满的地图，最容易让人忘记地图和大地不是一回事。危险的不是空白本身，是它被一种完整的样子遮住了。

人的知识也有这种边缘。你可能很懂一个产品，却不懂另一类用户为什么不用它；熟悉一个行业，却不清楚换了规则以后，旧经验还剩多少效力。过去的判断有效，不等于它得到了无限使用的许可证。

第三十三章说“自知者明”。自知的一部分，就是能说出自己的判断在哪些地方还站得住。承认边界并没有把门关上；恰恰相反，它把门的位置显出来了。知道还缺什么，才有可能去问、去学，或者请另一双眼睛一起看。

三、最难看见的，是看不见本身

本站采用的文字接着说：“不知不知，病矣。”这里的“病”可以理解为缺失、毛病；是对认识处境的提醒，不是把身体患病拿来评判一个人的高下。

“不知不知”让我们注意到一种更麻烦的情形：我不仅不知道某件事，还没有意识到自己不知道。提出的疑问没有进入我的地图，我甚至不知道该向哪里求证。

通行本常作“不知知”。它可以理解为不知却自以为知，不必限定成心里明明不懂、嘴上故意装懂。自欺、误判和有意欺骗不是同一件事；不能凭一个字的差别，就把两个版本各自分配给两种固定的人。

现代心理学中的邓宁—克鲁格研究讨论过特定任务中低表现者高估自身表现的现象。但这不是“知道越少，信心一定越大”的普遍定律，更不是听一句发言便能给人贴的标签。

它给这篇散文的启发比较朴素：发现自己判断错了，也需要方法、反馈和练习。刚入行的人可能还不知道哪些地方容易出问题，做了多年的人可能已经见过那些坑；可是老手也会被熟悉感遮住，新人也可能提出所有人都忘了问的问题。

所以这面镜子最好先朝自己放。听见一个武断的答案，笑话对方并不难。难的是在自己最顺口的一句话之后，停一下：我凭什么这么确定？有没有一种情况，会让这个答案不再成立？

四、另一种传统里的那句“不知道”

谈到这里，很容易想起苏格拉底。那句流传很广的“我唯一知道的，就是我一无所知”，适合当作概括，却不宜不加说明地当作他的逐字原话。

在柏拉图的《申辩》21d里，苏格拉底比较自己和一位自以为有智慧的人：两人未必真正知道所讨论的事情，但对方以为自己知道，而他不把不知道当成知道。他所说的那一点优势，就在这里。

这和“知不知”之间，有一处可以互相照亮的地方：不知并不光彩，也不羞耻；关键是会不会用一种有把握的姿态，把它藏到连自己也看不见。

不过，我们不必把两者说成同一句话，更不必断言他们恰好同时、完全独立地得出了同一个结论。老子及其文本的形成有复杂的历史，柏拉图写下的苏格拉底也有具体的对话处境。跨文化的相似，可以帮助我们思考，不能代替证据，更不能因为两位古人都说过，就证明一件事必定正确。

保留这种距离，反而使相遇更有意思。我们不是寻找两枚相同的印章，而是发现：从不同的路走过来，人都可能遇到同一道门——我能不能先不假装自己已经走完了？

五、把那个毛病，真的当成毛病

是以圣人之不病，以其病病也，是以不病。

“病病”可以读作以这种毛病为病，对它有所警觉。前一个“病”不必硬释成“看清”；要紧的是，知道自己的认识可能出问题之后，不把这件事随手放过。

假如我总以为出错是别人偶尔发生的事，那么每一次相反的证据，都像是在冒犯我。假如我承认自己也会漏看，证据就还有另一种用处：它能提醒我修正，而不只是迫使我自卫。

这种态度不是一个永不出错的护身符。盲区不会因为被承认就全部显形，谨慎的人也会失败。区别在于，我们是否给纠错留了位置：记录当初的假设，听取知道得更多的人，在条件变化时重新检查，而不是一直替旧判断补理由。

我这些年在硅谷见过一些让人放心的工程师。他们可以很清楚地解释自己熟悉的部分，也能在另一处说：“这个我不知道，我去查一下。”让人信任的不是“不知道”三个字，而是他们真的会去查，回来时会说明查到了什么、还不能确认什么。

反过来，如果“不确定”永远不通向任何求证，它也可能只是体面的逃避。谦逊不是把所有答案都说虚一点，而是让答案的分量和它实际拥有的依据相称。

六、给判断留一个可以修改的地方

下次准备说“肯定如此”时，不妨先想一个具体的问题：如果我错了，什么迹象会让我发现？如果无论出现什么证据，我都能把它解释成自己原来就对，那就值得停一下了。

也可以把“我不懂”说得更具体。不知道价格，不等于不知道产品；不熟悉这个案例，不等于整个人没有能力。把缺口说清楚，求助才有方向，别人也更容易接得住。

向人请教时，不只听他的结论，还听他怎样处理例外。一个可靠的回答，可以有把握，也应当说明把握来自哪里。不要因为别人话说得响就立即跟随，也不要因为他说得谦虚就免掉核对。

如果你已经因过去的错误而不敢再判断，这一章也不必成为新的重负。看见边界，是为了更合适地行动，不是为了等到彻底没有盲区才开始。某些决定只能在不完整的信息下作出，那就把可回头的余地、复查的时间和求助的办法一起放进决定里。

知道自己不知道，并不是最后得到一个关于自己的负面结论。它意味着下一次学习还有地方落下。地图不用假装已经画完，人也不用。

七、明白里面，要留得下不明白

上一章谈从听懂一句话到把它放进生活的距离。走进生活以后，我们常会发现：原来以为理解的，比实际理解的多一点。

这不是阅读失败了。有时恰恰是开始尝试，才使“不知道”有了轮廓。你终于知道哪一步走不过去，哪一种情形会让自己犹豫，也就终于能提出真正属于自己的问题。

第七十一章与第三十三章的“自知”在这里相接。看清自己，不只是列出长处，还包括认出自己没有到过的地方。

下一章会把这份清醒带向另一处：“自知而不自见，自爱而不自贵。”知道自己的位置，不必靠把别人看低来守住它。一个能承认不懂的人，也完全可以好好尊重自己。

I. The answer that arrives too quickly

Someone asks a question in a meeting. One person answers immediately, with a confidence that seems to settle not only the question but what everyone should do next. Another hesitates. There are two conditions she would like to check first. It is easy to mistake the first response for competence and the second for uncertainty about having any competence at all.

A week later, the confident answer turns out to have missed something decisive. The hesitation looks different now. It was not necessarily a failure to understand the question. Perhaps it was an awareness of what the question had left unsaid.

Of course, the opposite happens too. Experienced people can be quick and right; people who endlessly say “it depends” can be avoiding responsibility. Confidence is not an ignorance detector, and hesitation is not a certificate of wisdom. The useful question is whether an answer has reasons behind it—and whether those reasons still hold when conditions change.

Chapter 71 directs our attention to that boundary. It does not ask us to acquire a modest-sounding verbal habit. It asks us to leave a visible edge around what we claim: this far, I have grounds; beyond this point, there is still something I need to find out.

II. A map needs to show its blank spaces

The chapter begins with zhi bu zhi: knowing that one does not know. In the reading followed here, this is not the absence of knowledge but an important part of it. Knowing a subject includes some awareness of where one's understanding of that subject runs out.

A map is useful not only because it shows roads. “Unsurveyed territory” can be valuable information too. A traveller who sees that warning can make preparations, ask someone who has been there, or choose another route. The blank becomes dangerous when someone fills it with an invented road merely to make the map look complete.

We do something similar with experience. Knowing a product well does not mean knowing why a different group of people refuses to use it. Years in an industry do not tell us automatically what will happen after its rules change. A judgment that worked yesterday has not thereby received a licence to operate everywhere.

Chapter 33 associates clarity with self-knowledge. One form of that clarity is being able to describe the limits of one's judgment without treating those limits as a personal disgrace. A boundary need not close a door. It can show us where the door is, and what would help us go through it.

This matters particularly when other people rely on our answers. An honestly marked gap gives them room to make their own preparations. A performance of completeness quietly transfers our uncertainty to them while denying them the information they would need to carry it.

III. The blind spot that hides itself

Our adopted text continues with bu zhi bu zhi: not knowing that one does not know. It calls this a bing, a fault or affliction. The language describes a difficulty in understanding; it should not be turned into a judgment about the worth of people who are physically ill.

The problem is more elusive than an unanswered question. An unanswered question is already on the map. Here the question has not occurred to us. We cannot seek an answer because we have not noticed that anything needs asking.

The familiar received wording is shorter, bu zhi zhi, often read as not knowing yet thinking one knows. That need not mean consciously pretending to possess knowledge. Deliberate deception, self-deception and honest misjudgment are different things. A textual variation does not conveniently divide humanity into those who lie and those who merely fail to notice.

Modern readers may think of the original Dunning–Kruger research, which examined low performers overestimating their performance on particular tasks. That is not a universal law that confidence rises whenever knowledge falls, and it is certainly not a diagnosis to deliver after hearing a stranger speak. Laozi need not be credited with anticipating an experimental result for the comparison to be useful.

The modest connection is this: recognising a mistake can itself require skills, feedback and practice. A novice may not yet know where mistakes tend to occur. A veteran may have seen those mistakes repeatedly—but may also have stopped questioning familiar assumptions. Sometimes the newcomer asks precisely what the experienced room has forgotten to ask.

It is therefore better to turn this passage first toward ourselves. After our most effortless pronouncement, can we pause long enough to ask what would make it false? Calling someone else overconfident is easy. Giving contrary evidence a route into our own judgment is harder.

IV. Socrates at another doorway

There is a familiar encounter waiting here with Socrates. “The only thing I know is that I know nothing” is a widely circulated summary, not a sentence we should casually present as his exact words.

In Plato's Apology, 21d, Socrates compares himself with someone reputed to be wise. Neither appears to know the matter in question; the other man thinks he does, whereas Socrates does not mistake his own lack of knowledge for knowledge. That is the limited advantage he identifies.

The resemblance to zhi bu zhi is illuminating. Ignorance is not improved by hiding it from oneself. But a resemblance need not become an assertion that two thinkers said exactly the same thing, at exactly the same time, in demonstrably complete independence. The formation of the Laozi text has a complicated history; Plato's Socrates belongs to a particular dramatic and philosophical setting too.

Nor would agreement between two ancient thinkers prove a proposition true. What it can do is give us another way into the question. We are not collecting two identical stamps of authority. We are seeing how different routes can bring a person to a similar threshold: can I stop behaving as though I have already reached the end?

Leaving the differences intact makes the encounter richer. Socrates does not have to become a Greek Laozi, or Laozi a Chinese Socrates, for either to disturb our habits of certainty.

V. Taking the fault seriously

The chapter then turns on the compact expression bing bing: treating the fault as a fault, or being troubled by it. The first word need not be forced to mean “see clearly.” The practical issue is what happens after we admit that our understanding can go wrong.

If mistakes are things other people make, contrary evidence arrives as an insult. If I accept that I can miss something, the same evidence has another possible role: it can help me repair a judgment. I do not have to enjoy being corrected to make room for correction.

This is not an amulet against error. Acknowledged blind spots do not all become visible, and careful people still fail. What changes is the provision we make: recording assumptions, asking someone with relevant experience, and returning to a conclusion when its conditions change instead of endlessly supplying it with fresh excuses.

In my years in Silicon Valley, some of the engineers I have found most reassuring could explain their own area with precision and then say, quite plainly, “I don't know that part. I'll check.” The reassuring part was not the phrase. They actually checked, and came back able to distinguish what they had established from what remained uncertain.

The reverse is possible as well. “We can't be sure” can become a respectable way never to investigate or decide. Humility does not mean weakening every sentence. It means letting a claim carry the weight its evidence can support—neither more nor less.

VI. Give your judgment somewhere to change

Before saying “definitely,” ask one specific question: if I were wrong, what might let me notice? If every conceivable result can be explained as confirmation of the original claim, the claim may have become difficult to learn from.

Make uncertainty specific. Not knowing the price is not the same as not understanding the product. Being unfamiliar with one case does not cancel a person's abilities. A clearly described gap makes asking for help easier, and makes it easier for someone else to offer help that actually fits.

When consulting another person, listen to how they handle exceptions, not merely to how confidently they announce conclusions. A reliable answer can be firm. It should also have some account of why firmness is justified. Neither a commanding tone nor a charmingly modest one should exempt the answer from checking.

If past mistakes have already made you afraid to judge anything, this chapter need not become another burden. The point of recognising limits is to act more appropriately, not to wait until all limits have disappeared. Some decisions have to be made with incomplete information. Where possible, build in a way to revisit them, a time to review them and someone to call when the picture changes.

Knowing that you do not know is not a final negative verdict on yourself. It means the next thing you learn still has somewhere to land. A map does not have to pretend to be finished. Neither does a person.

VII. Understanding must have room for what remains unclear

The previous chapter considered the distance between understanding a sentence and bringing it into life. Once we try to live with an idea, we often discover that we understood less than we thought. That is not necessarily a failure of reading. Practice has made the unanswered part visible.

You can now name the step you cannot take, or the situation in which an easy principle becomes difficult. You have acquired a question that is genuinely your own. Chapter 71 meets chapter 33's self-knowledge here: seeing oneself clearly includes recognising places one has not yet been.

Chapter 72 takes this clarity into self-respect: knowing oneself without making a display of oneself, valuing oneself without placing oneself above others. A person who can admit not knowing does not have to despise himself. There is no contradiction between keeping an open question and standing on one's own feet.
