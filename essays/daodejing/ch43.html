水穿石，不是靠硬

Water Pierces Stone — Not by Force

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、水怎么穿透石头

水滴石穿，是一个很容易被我们拿来讲坚持的故事。一滴不够，就再来一滴；今天不够，就再等一天。但除了耐心，这幅图景还可以让人想到另一件事：改变一个坚硬的东西，未必只能靠比它更硬。

水沿着地势流，遇到裂隙便可能进入，遇到挡路的石头也可能绕行。它没有先把自己做成另一块石头，才开始有所作用。

不过，比喻不等于物理定律。水并不是没有力量，也不能穿透任何材料。侵蚀与渗流有各自的条件，岩石的孔隙以及它们是否连通，都关系到水能否通过。美国地质调查局关于地下水的说明

第四十三章本身没有点名水。我们借这幅日常图景，走近它的开头：“天下之至柔，驰骋于天下之至坚。”它所打开的问题是：除了继续加硬、继续用力，我们是不是还漏看了别的作用方式？

二、驰骋，不只盯着战胜

“天下之至柔，驰骋于天下之至坚。”柔与坚，在一句话里撞到了一起；动起来的，却是那个看似不占优势的柔。

驰骋让人想到奔驰、往来、施展。本文愿意从它读出一种不只盯着输赢的可能：不必先把眼前的坚硬打败，才有地方可走。这与第三十六章“柔弱胜刚强”形成一个有意思的对照。那里容易把注意力引向胜负，这里则让我们留意行动的路径。

但这是一种读法，不能把驰骋的字义直接写成“绝对没有阻碍、完全没有对抗”。也不必把两章排成思想进步的台阶，说出现胜字的就浅，没有胜字的才高。老子的几个画面，可以分别照亮不同的处境。

在生活里，我们常常太早把问题认成一场比赛。对方声音大，我必须更大；流程不肯改，我只好更用力地催。好像目的已经不是办成事情，而是先证明自己的力度足够。

若把目光从“怎样赢过他”移到“事情究竟卡在哪里”，路有时才开始显出来。不是必定能走通，而是终于不再只认得撞门这一种动作。

三、无有入于无间

下一句更难：“无有入于无间。”无有、无间，可以引出不同的哲学解释；把它读作不以固定形态出现的作用，进入看似没有间隙的地方，是本文采用的入口。

它不能直接用水来证明。水能够进入连通的细小孔隙，不等于水能够进入真正不透水的任何东西。若把“没有缝隙”先说成极小的缝，再用水流进去证明，实际上已经偷偷换了问题。

我们仍然可以借它想一想人与人之间的僵硬。一个人带着已经写好的结论来找你，句句都在要求你就范，你很可能先关起门。另一个人愿意把尚不明白的地方问出来，允许答案改变自己的判断，谈话就有了不同的起点。

区别不在于后者没有立场。他也可以有清楚的目的和不能接受的事，只是不把预先想好的办法当成唯一形状。没有固定形状，在这里不是没有自己，而是不必每次都以同一种姿态出现。

这也不是“进入别人内心”的万能技术。别人仍可以拒绝，不愿交谈，也可以不与你达成共识。柔和若只是让人放下戒心、方便自己控制的手段，换了外表，目的并没有变。

真正可贵的，是谈话开始之后，对方的话有可能影响你。不是你变软了，于是终于可以穿过他的一切边界；而是两个人都不必先被打碎，事情才有机会改变。

四、由此知道：不硬来，可能更有用

“吾是以知无为之有益也。”这句话把前面的意象，带回一种行动判断：所以，我知道无为的益处。

这里的知，通行本同样保留。不能把它说成只有帛书才有的关键字。值得留意的是句子的推进：它没有单独摆出一条“你应该少做事”的命令，而是让我们先看，惯常的用力方式之外，还有什么可能发生。对照通行本第四十三章

无为不必理解成什么都不做。沿着本文的读路，它更像是不把自己的安排硬压到事情上，先看看已有的条件、节奏和参与其中的人。

一个项目迟迟推不动，连续催促也许只让每个人更忙于解释。若真正的问题是几个环节的交接不清，重新分工、减少一次重复确认，反而比再加一道命令有用。这仍然是行动，而且需要细心。

一段关系也一样。你反复要求对方立刻改变，对方可能越来越防备。先说清自己感到困难的地方，听听对方的限制，再商量下一步，往往比把音量升高更有可能使谈话继续。

可能，不是保证。遇到欺骗、暴力或持续侵害，必要的保护、求助和退出，不该被“无为”拦住。不硬来，不能变成让受伤的人继续替伤害者创造舒服的环境。

五、不言之教，不是让解释消失

“不言之教，无为之益，天下希能及之矣。”这句话把教与行放到一起：有些东西，不是靠不断重复口号，便能使人学会。

第二章说“行不言之教”。今天去看，一个成年人要求孩子耐心，却每次遇到麻烦都先发火；一个负责人提倡合作，却把每项功劳都归给自己。他们说的话并非听不懂，难的是，生活正在教另一套东西。

反过来，愿意承认错误、认真听完别人的话、在需要照顾时真的伸手，能够让人看见一种关系怎样成立。这不必随时附上“看，我正在给你作示范”的说明。

可是不言并不意味着，最好的老师永远沉默。知识需要讲解，危险需要提醒，边界也需要说清。示范与语言可以互相支持；要放下的是用训诫代替实践，不是所有言说。

“希能及之”，说出了这份分寸不容易。我们太容易把焦虑变成指挥，把帮助变成接管。它不是宣判多数人终身没有理解能力；想起第四十一章的三种听者，我们自己也可能在实践里慢慢听懂。

能看见自己又在硬推，愿意重新停一下，已经是学习。不是等到有了完美的境界，才有资格少强迫别人一点。

六、给读者的

遇到强硬的人，先不要急着把所有力气都用在比他更硬。若情形安全，也值得问问：他在保护什么，害怕失去什么，真正想达成什么？自己的要求背后，又是什么？

两个人可能一个坚持快，一个坚持慢。表面上只能有人认输；说开之后，才发现一个担心错过机会，一个担心出了错无人负责。也许可以先做一个可撤回的小步骤，既不无限拖延，也不一下子承担全部风险。办法成立，不在于谁把谁说服成了自己，而在于两个担心都被认真对待。

但不是所有利益都能兼容。有时确实没有双方都满意的方案，或者对方根本不愿尊重你的边界。那就需要拒绝、明确条件，甚至离开。理解一个人的来由，不等于替他的做法开脱。

遇到僵住的局面，可以试着退远一点。我们常在一个局部已经走到了头，却误以为整个世界都没有路。比如反复争一项任务究竟由谁接，不如先问这项任务是否仍然必要、能否换一种分法、期限能否重谈。局部看来是退让，放回更大的安排里，可能保护了原本想做的事。

这不是许诺你总能找到“全局最优”。人掌握的信息有限，有些选择确有损失。退一步的价值，是让选择多一点，不是保证选择从此没有代价。

遇到一道过不去的坎，也可以区分：是这件事做不到，还是眼下认准的这个方法走不通？水遇到石头，可能绕过去、积起来、沿着已有的缝走。人也可以换路径、等条件、找人搭把手，或者承认这条路不值得再付出。

等待不是总有一场雨会来，求助也不是一定有人能替你解决。柔软的分量，在于承认这些限制之后，仍然保有辨认和调整的余地，而不是把自己耗在“我只能这样”里面。

最后，再想一想那滴水。我们借它记住的，不是最弱的一定战胜最强的，而是不必先成为另一块石头，才配有所作为。有些门需要推开，有些地方需要绕行，有些界限则应当尊重。真正的力量，也包括分得清这几件事。

I. How Does Water Pierce Stone?

A drop falls on stone. Then another. We usually take this image as a lesson in persistence: if one attempt is not enough, keep going. But it can suggest something else. Changing something hard does not always require becoming harder than it.

Water follows the ground, travels through connected openings, gathers, and sometimes finds a way around what blocks it. Given time and suitable conditions, it can wear away rock. It does not first have to turn itself into a second stone.

“Not by force” here means not by meeting hardness with greater hardness. Water certainly exerts forces. Erosion and seepage depend on material conditions; water cannot pass through every substance. The connections between pores and cracks matter to whether it can move through rock. The US Geological Survey explains these conditions.

Chapter 43 does not itself name water. We borrow the image to approach its opening: “天下之至柔，驰骋于天下之至坚” — the softest in the world ranges through the hardest. Before we push again, the sentence asks us to notice what our familiar kind of effort may be missing.

II. Riding Through, Not Only Defeating

The sentence brings extreme softness and extreme hardness together. Yet it is softness that moves. In the reading developed here, that movement connects with the ability to change in chapter 42, rather than with the demand to remain permanently unyielding.

驰骋 evokes riding, ranging, moving freely. It is not the word for defeating. Chapter 36 says the soft and yielding overcome the hard and strong; this chapter lets us attend to the route an action takes, not only to which side wins.

That is a suggestive contrast, not a dictionary proof that 驰骋 means absolutely no resistance or conflict. Nor must we rank the chapters as successive discoveries, with overcoming an inferior idea and passing through a superior one. Different images can illuminate different circumstances.

We often decide too quickly that a problem is a contest. Someone raises their voice, so we raise ours. A process refuses to budge, so we demand more urgency. Soon the immediate objective is to demonstrate sufficient strength, while the original purpose waits outside the argument.

Moving the question from “How do I beat this?” to “What is preventing this from working?” may reveal a different route. It does not guarantee a route. It stops treating a harder collision as the only action available.

III. What Does Not Insist on One Shape

“无有入于无间” — what has no being enters where there is no gap. The terms permit different philosophical readings. Here I approach them through the image of an activity that does not insist on a fixed form reaching somewhere that appears closed.

Water cannot prove this paradox. Water entering tiny connected pores is not water entering a genuinely impermeable material. Quietly changing “no gap” into “a very small gap” would change the question rather than answer it. We can allow the philosophical image to remain strange without making it a physical mechanism.

In human encounters, however, we know something of the difficulty created by a predetermined shape. A person arrives with the verdict already written: who is unreasonable, what everyone must want, who must apologize. Whatever you say will either confirm that account or be dismissed. There is little room for an actual conversation.

Another person can have convictions, purposes, and firm boundaries while remaining willing to discover something. Their position is not an obstacle merely because it is clear. What matters is whether new information can affect how they respond.

Nor is gentleness a technique for getting past another person's defenses. People are not stones to penetrate. They may refuse a conversation, withhold intimacy, or decline agreement. Softness used to bypass consent merely gives domination a quieter manner.

The valuable possibility is mutual: neither person has to be broken before anything can change. Listening leaves room for their words to affect you, not merely for your preferred outcome to reach them.

IV. Therefore: Not Forcing Can Be Useful

“吾是以知无为之有益也” — from this I know the benefit of wu wei. The sentence moves from the preceding images toward a judgment about action. “Know,” 知, is also present in the received text; it is not a discovery lost from that version. Compare the received chapter.

The important movement is “from this.” Rather than simply commanding us to do less, the passage asks us to notice what can happen outside our habitual use of force.

In this reading, wu wei is not inactivity. It means not imposing a predetermined arrangement without attending to the conditions, timing, and people involved. It may require careful, practical work.

A project has stalled. Repeated demands for progress make everyone busier reporting the delay. If the real problem is an unclear handoff, clarifying responsibility or removing a duplicate approval may help more than another urgent instruction. This is action, but it addresses the obstruction rather than adding pressure around it.

A relationship has become tense. Insisting that the other person change immediately may make them more defensive. Describing the difficulty, acknowledging your part, and listening to their constraints may allow another conversation. Softening your approach does not mean declaring that their behavior is acceptable.

These are possibilities, not guarantees. In the presence of violence, deception, or sustained mistreatment, protection, outside help, and leaving may be necessary. Non-forcing cannot mean requiring an injured person to keep making the situation comfortable for someone who harms them. Useful action includes knowing what not to continue.

V. Teaching That Is More Than Words

“不言之教，无为之益，天下希能及之矣” — wordless teaching and the benefit of wu wei: few in the world reach them.

Chapter 2 also speaks of practicing wordless teaching. We recognize its relevance when a person's conduct teaches something different from their instructions. An adult demands patience but erupts at every inconvenience. A manager praises cooperation but claims every success. Their words are understandable; their practice supplies another lesson.

Admitting an error, listening without immediately taking over, and offering help when it is needed make another kind of relationship visible. They need not be accompanied by a running announcement that someone is setting an example.

But the best teacher is not necessarily silent. Knowledge needs explanation, danger needs warnings, and boundaries often need to be stated plainly. Words and example can support each other. The problem is instruction used in place of practice, not language itself.

“Few reach it” acknowledges the difficulty. Under pressure, becoming louder can feel easier than becoming more attentive. We turn anxiety into direction and assistance into control. This is not a verdict that most people belong to a class incapable of learning.

The three listeners in the earlier chapter 41 can be recognized in ourselves. At different moments we practice, waver, or laugh. Noticing that we are forcing things again, and becoming willing to pause, is already part of learning. We need not attain a perfect state before imposing a little less on someone else.

VI. For the Reader

The chapter becomes particularly concrete in three situations: a hard-line person, a stuck arrangement, and an obstacle you cannot get past.

With a hard-line person, it may help, where conversation is safe, to ask what lies behind the stance. What are they protecting? What do they fear losing? What are they trying to accomplish? Ask the same questions of your own position. Understanding a purpose is different from approving every means used to pursue it.

Two colleagues argue about speed. One insists that they must begin immediately; the other will not agree until every risk has been considered. They appear to want incompatible things. Further discussion reveals that one fears losing an opportunity, while the other fears being left responsible for an irreversible mistake. A small, reversible first step might address both concerns.

The solution, if it works, does not come from cracking somebody's shell. It comes from taking both concerns seriously enough to discover whether a shared arrangement is possible. Some interests really are incompatible. Someone may also refuse to respect your limits. Then clear conditions, refusal, mediation, or departure may be the appropriate response. Understanding is not a requirement to surrender.

In a stuck situation, try stepping back far enough to see what the present dispute leaves out. You may have reached the best available arrangement within one small part of the problem and mistaken that local limit for the edge of the world.

Suppose a team keeps arguing about who must take on an additional task. A wider view might ask whether the task is still necessary, whether it can be divided differently, or whether the deadline should change. Giving up the best-looking local arrangement may protect the larger purpose it was meant to serve.

The language of a “local optimum” is useful as an analogy, not as a mathematical promise. We have incomplete information. Some choices involve real losses. Stepping back may reveal more options; it does not guarantee that a global optimum exists within reach or that we will recognize it.

At an apparently impassable obstacle, distinguish the aim from the method. Is the endeavor impossible, or has this particular route failed? Water might flow around a stone, pass over it, enter an existing opening, gather until the level rises, or move differently after rain. A person might change methods, wait for better conditions, acquire a skill, or ask someone for help.

There is no obligation to keep trying forever. The rain may not come; help may not solve the problem. An aim can be unattainable, unsafe, or no longer worth its cost. Revising or abandoning it can be an exercise of judgment rather than a failure of courage. Flexibility includes refusing to spend your whole life proving that you can do something only one way.

These scenes develop the reading in the essay on chapters 37–45: the value of acting without making our current way of acting the world's only permitted form. Sometimes this requires less pressure and more work — the work of seeing what is actually there.

Return to the drop of water. It may wear a channel into stone under the right conditions. It may also evaporate or follow another course. It does not promise that the weakest always defeat the strongest. It reminds us that we do not have to become another stone before we can matter. Some doors need pushing, some obstacles can be approached differently, and some boundaries should be respected. Strength includes learning to tell them apart.
