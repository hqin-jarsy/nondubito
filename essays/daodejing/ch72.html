# 看得起自己，和把自己看得太高，是两回事

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 7 月

一、同样是自信，为什么一个舒服，一个别扭

你一定遇到过这两种人。

一种人很有底气:他知道自己几斤几两,遇事不慌,该说不的时候说不。可跟他相处,你一点都不累——他从不让你觉得,你矮他一头。

另一种人也很"自信":他也知道自己有本事,可他身上,总有一股说不出的味道——跟他说话,你总隐隐觉得自己被俯视着。

同样是"看重自己",为什么一个让人舒服,一个让人别扭?

老子在《道德经》第七十二章,用八个字,把这两者切得干干净净。

而这一章,是这一组九章（第六十四到七十二章）的最后一章;它收的,还不只是这九章——是老子讲了一整本书的那条大线。

二、靠"威"撑起来的关系，其实最脆

这一章开头,老子说了一句很重的话——

"民之不畏威,则大威将至矣。"

当人们已经不怕你的威压了,更大的反噬,就要来了。

老子说这话,面对的是他那个年代的君主和百姓。但这个道理,在任何一种"一方手里有权、一方没有"的关系里,都成立——管理者和团队,家长和孩子,老师和学生。

关键在于"已经不怕了"这四个字。

一段靠"威"撑起来的关系,表面上很稳:你一板脸,大家都听话。可这份"听话",不是因为认同,是因为怕。而"怕"这个东西,是会用完的。

一个只会靠发火、靠施压、靠"我说了算"来推动事情的人,他每用一次威,那份威就损耗一点。等到有一天,对方心里那句"其实你也拿我没办法"冒出来了——撑着这一切的那根柱子,就断了。 而这时候,他往往还想不明白,只会加倍地压回去，于是塌得更快。

这不是在说"威严不该有",是在说:把整段关系押在"威"上,本来就是脆的。 真正稳的关系,靠的是另外三个字:信得过。

三、别轻慢地看别人的处境

接着,老子讲了两个"不要"。第一个是——

"毋狎其所居。"

这里的"狎",历来容易读偏。 它常被读成"逼迫、压制";但它更准的意思是"轻慢、不端正地看待"。

"毋狎其所居",就是:不要轻慢地看待别人的处境。

这一条,位置越高的人,越容易犯。

在上面的人,看底下人的难处,常常是"扫一眼就过去了":那点通勤的辛苦、那点排期的压力、那点难开口的顾虑——在你眼里是小事,在他那儿,可能就是全部。

不是他不该跟你说,是你没有正眼看过它。

而"正视",是一切尊重的起点。 你不必立刻解决对方所有的难处,但你得先老老实实地,把它当回事。很多人真正难受的,不是问题没被解决,是问题没被看见。

四、"我觉得他们已经挺好了"——这句话的问题

第二个"不要",是这一章最深的一处,也是最容易被读丢的一处——

"毋厌其所生。"

这里的"厌",不是"讨厌"。 在古文里,"厌"的意思是"满足、够了"（跟"餍足"是一路的）。

所以"毋厌其所生",不是"不要厌弃别人的生活",而是:不要满足于"他们的日子已经够好了"。

这个区别，太大了。

紧接着老子说:"夫唯弗厌,是以不厌。"——唯独你不觉得"他们已经够好了",他们的日子,才能继续往更好里走。

为什么?

因为"够好了"这句话,一旦是由手里有权的那一方说出来的,它就变成了一个天花板。

一个管理者觉得"团队待遇已经不错了",于是这件事就到此为止;

一个家长觉得"孩子现在挺好的",于是孩子那些还没说出口的想法,就没了出口;

一个平台觉得"用户已经很满意了",于是它就停在了那里。

注意,他们未必有恶意——恰恰相反,他们常常是善意的、甚至是满意的。可"满意"这件事,不该由他们来替对方下结论。

这就是我们前面几章一直在讲的那条线: 那个"到底够不够"的判断,得留给对方自己去下。 你替他判了"够了",就等于替他封了顶——他往上长的那条路,是被你这句好心的"已经不错了",给堵住的。

所以老子说:唯独你不替他封顶,他才能继续往上长。

五、自知而不自见，自爱而不自贵

现在,到了这一章的核心。老子用了两组对子,每组都是"自X"——可一组是真的,一组是假的。

"是以圣人自知而不自见也,自爱而不自贵也。"

第一组:自知,而不自见。

— 自知——知道自己懂什么、有几分斤两（正是上一章"知不知"那份清醒）。

— 不自见——但不到处炫耀自己的见解和主张。

心里有数,是一回事;逢人就要显摆自己多有见地,是另一回事。 真正有判断的人,不必每次都抢着把自己的高见亮出来——该说的时候说,不必事事都要证明"我看得比你透"。

第二组:自爱,而不自贵。这一句,是整章、也是整本书的落点。

— 自爱——尊重自己,爱惜自己。

— 不自贵——但不把自己的身份,抬得比别人高。

这两个字,常常被人搅在一起,其实是完全不同的两件事:

自爱,是向内的: 我值得被好好对待,所以我不糟蹋自己,不为了讨好谁而作践自己,该守的底线我守住。

自贵,是向外的: 我比你高一等,你得敬着我。

你看——一个人完全可以极其尊重自己,同时完全不觉得自己高人一等。这两件事,根本不冲突。

而更值得琢磨的是反过来那一面:那些最急着把身份抬高、最需要别人敬着的人,心里往往恰恰缺的就是那份自爱。 正因为里头不稳,才需要外头那层"我很贵"的壳,来撑着。

真正自爱的人,不需要这层壳。

我在硅谷这些年,见过最让人服气的那种人,大抵都是这样:他从不拿职级、拿资历压人,跟谁说话都是平的;可你要是真越了界、真拿他不当回事,他会很清楚地告诉你"不行"。 前者是不自贵,后者是自爱。这两样,长在同一个人身上,一点都不别扭。

回到开头那个问题: 让人舒服的那种自信,是自爱;让人别扭的那种"自信",是自贵。差别不在他多看重自己,而在他有没有顺手,把你踩低一点。

这两组对子,还是老子讲了一整本书的那件事——

从（ch7）"把自己放到后面,反而站到了前面",到（ch22）"不自我彰显,反而明白",到（ch66）"江海处在最低,水自己就来",到（ch67）"不把自己刻成一个'我很大'的样子,才能真大"——一路讲下来的,都是同一句话:不立招牌,反而成。

而到了这最后一章,老子把它,收在了最贴身的地方——收在你怎么对待你自己上。

六、去彼取此：决定做什么，不做什么

全章最后四个字,轻轻落地——

"故去彼取此。"

所以,决定不做什么,做什么。

— 去彼——去掉的那部分:自见（炫耀见地）、自贵（抬高身份）。

— 取此——留下的那部分:自知（心里有数）、自爱（尊重自己）。

但这里有个很要紧的分别:"去彼取此",不是"我权衡了一下利弊,觉得这个更划算,所以选了这个"。

也不是"我心里其实很想显摆、很想被人敬着,但我忍住了"。

忍,是忍不了一辈子的。

老子说的这个"去",是接着上一章那份"看清"来的:当你真正看清了"自见、自贵"那条路通向哪里——看清它怎么一步步把人架空、把关系耗干——你就不再需要"忍"了。你压根就不想走了。

这才是最省力的活法:不是靠意志力天天跟自己较劲,是看透之后,那份诱惑自己散了。

而"取此",也不是费劲去够一个更高的目标——是你不往那条死路上走之后,自然而然,就站回了自己本来该在的位置上。

老子讲了七十二章,最后落到的,其实就是这么一个朴素的动作:每一天,决定做什么,不做什么。

七、给读者的

这一章,给我们几件当下就能用的事——

第一:别把一段关系,押在"威"上。

靠怕撑起来的听话,是有期限的（民之不畏威）。真正稳的,是"信得过",不是"惹不起"。

第二:正视别人的难处,别扫一眼就过去。

在你眼里的小事,在他那儿可能是全部（毋狎其所居）。很多人难受的不是问题没解决,是问题没被看见。

第三:别替别人下"已经够好了"这个结论。

那句好心的"你现在挺好的",常常就是替对方封的顶（毋厌其所生）。够不够,让他自己说。

第四:心里有数,不必逢人就亮。

有判断是好事,把它变成随时要显摆的东西,就变味了（自知而不自见）。

第五:尊重自己,但别抬高自己。

自爱是向内的（我不糟蹋自己）,自贵是向外的（我比你高一等）。你完全可以极其看重自己,同时一点都不看轻别人（自爱而不自贵）。

第六:看清了,就不必忍。

真正看透一条路通向哪里,你就不需要靠意志力躲开它了（去彼取此）。

这一章的核心,可以收成一句话——

看得起自己,和把自己看得比别人高,是两回事;前者让你站得稳,后者让你站得孤。

八、这一组，走完了

第七十二章,是这一组九章（第六十四到七十二章）的最后一篇。回头看这一路——

（ch64）在事情还没成形的时候用力,郑重到最后;

（ch65）不把自己的"懂"塞给别人,让人自己长;

（ch66）像江海一样处在最低,水自己就来;

（ch67）三样宝贝:慈、俭、不敢为天下先;

（ch68）真正的高手,赢得很安静;

（ch69）最大的祸,是觉得自己无敌;

（ch70）最简单的道理最难做到,粗布衣裳里揣着玉;

（ch71）知道自己不知道,才是真本事;

（ch72）尊重自己,但不抬高自己;然后,决定做什么,不做什么。

九章走下来,其实是同一件事:把那块写着"我"的招牌收起来——别人才长得起来,你自己,也才站得稳。

而老子把这一切,最后收成了一个再朴素不过的动作:去彼取此。

下一组（第七十三到八十一章),是《道德经》的最后九章。老子会讲到"天网恢恢,疏而不失",讲到"柔弱胜刚强",最后,把整本书收在一句话上——"圣人之道,为而弗争。"

做,但不争。 我们下一组见。

I. Same Confidence — Why Does One Feel Good and the Other Feel Off?

You have surely met both of these people.

One person has genuine groundedness: he knows his own capabilities, stays composed when things happen, says no when no is called for. And yet spending time with him is easy — he never makes you feel like you are a step below him.

The other person is also "confident": he too knows he has ability, but there is always an indefinable quality about him — when you talk with him, you faintly sense that you are being looked down upon.

Both "value themselves" — so why does one leave you at ease while the other leaves you vaguely uncomfortable?

In Chapter 72 of the Daodejing, Laozi separates the two with eight characters, precisely.

This chapter is the final chapter of this group of nine (chapters 64 through 72). What it draws together is not only these nine chapters — it gathers the main thread that has run through the entire book.

II. A Relationship Built on Authority Alone Is the Most Fragile

The chapter opens with a weighty statement:

"When the people no longer fear authority, the great reckoning arrives."

When people have stopped being afraid of your power over them, a far larger backlash is on its way.

Laozi was speaking to the rulers and people of his own time. But the principle holds in any relationship where one side has power and the other does not — manager and team, parent and child, teacher and student.

The key is in the phrase "no longer fear."

A relationship held together by authority appears stable on the surface: when you make a hard face, everyone complies. But that compliance does not come from agreement — it comes from fear. And fear has a finite supply.

A person who only moves things forward through anger, pressure, and "I decide" — each time they exercise that authority, some of it is spent. The day will come when the other person's quiet thought "you can't actually do much to me" rises to the surface — and the pillar holding everything up snaps. At that point they usually can't see what happened and just press harder — which only makes the collapse faster.

This is not saying authority shouldn't exist — it is saying that staking the whole relationship on authority is inherently fragile. What makes a relationship genuinely stable is a different three words: being trustworthy.

III. Don't Dismiss Other People's Circumstances with a Glance

Laozi then gives two "do nots." The first:

"Do not treat lightly the places where they dwell."

The word here is often misread. It is frequently understood as "oppress" or "coerce"; but its more accurate meaning is "to treat with lightness, to regard dismissively."

"Do not treat lightly the places where they dwell" means: don't be dismissive about other people's circumstances.

This failure is most common in people who hold the higher position.

Those on top tend to skim past the difficulties of those below: the exhaustion of the commute, the pressure of the deadline, the worry that is hard to put into words — these look like minor things to you, but they may be the whole of what he is carrying.

It is not that he shouldn't have told you — it is that you never truly looked at it.

Looking squarely at something is the starting point of all respect. You do not have to solve every difficulty someone faces — but you have to first give it honest weight. What many people find most painful is not that the problem went unsolved — it is that the problem went unseen.

IV. "I Think They're Already Doing Fine" — The Problem with That Statement

The second "do not" is the deepest point of this chapter — and the one most easily overlooked:

"Do not be satisfied with where their lives have arrived."

The word here, usually translated as "despise" or "resent," is not that. In classical Chinese, this word means "to be satisfied, to feel it is enough" (cognate with "satiated").

So "do not be satisfied with where their lives have arrived" does not mean "don't despise their way of living" — it means: don't conclude that their lives are already good enough.

That distinction is enormous.

Laozi immediately follows: "Only by not being satisfied are they not left stuck there" — only if you don't decide "they are already doing well enough" can their lives continue to get better.

Why?

Because the moment "they are already doing well enough" is said by the party that holds power, it becomes a ceiling.

A manager who decides "the team's compensation is already pretty good" puts an end to that question;

A parent who decides "the child is fine now" closes the door on thoughts the child hasn't yet found words for;

A platform that decides "users are already satisfied" stops there.

Note: none of these people necessarily have bad intentions — quite the opposite, they are often well-intentioned, even pleased. But "satisfied" is not a conclusion for them to reach on behalf of someone else.

This is the thread we have been following for several chapters: the judgment of "is this truly enough" must be left to the other person to make. When you conclude "enough" for them, you have put a ceiling above them — the path they could have grown along has been closed off by your well-meaning "it's already pretty good."

So Laozi says: only when you don't set their ceiling can they keep growing upward.

V. Knowing Yourself Without Displaying Yourself; Loving Yourself Without Elevating Yourself

Now we reach the heart of this chapter. Laozi uses two paired contrasts, each built on "self-X" — but one in each pair is genuine, and one is not.

"Therefore the sage knows himself but does not display himself; loves himself but does not elevate himself."

First pair: knowing oneself, yet not displaying oneself.

— Knowing oneself — knowing what one understands and what one's actual measure is (exactly the clarity of the previous chapter's "knowing that one does not know").

— Not displaying oneself — yet not parading one's insights and positions to everyone.

Having clear judgment is one thing; needing to perform your perspicacity to everyone you meet is another. The person with genuine judgment does not need to preemptively wave their superior view around — they speak when it is appropriate to speak, without needing to prove at every turn "I see more clearly than you."

Second pair: loving oneself, yet not elevating oneself. This line is the landing point of this chapter — and of the whole book.

— Loving oneself — respecting oneself, valuing oneself.

— Not elevating oneself — yet not placing one's status above others.

These two are routinely conflated, but they are entirely different things:

Loving oneself is inward: I deserve to be treated well, so I do not degrade myself, do not betray myself to please others, and I hold my own ground when I need to.

Elevating oneself is outward: I am a rank above you; you owe me deference.

See — a person can respect themselves completely and simultaneously feel not one bit superior to others. These two things are not in conflict at all.

More worth considering is the reverse: the people most urgently trying to elevate their status, most in need of others' deference, are often the ones who lack that inward self-love most of all. Because the inside is unsteady, they need the shell of "I am precious" on the outside to hold things up.

A person who truly loves themselves does not need that shell.

In my years in Silicon Valley, the people I have found most genuinely admirable are roughly like this: they never use rank or seniority to pressure others; they speak to everyone as an equal. But if you truly cross a line, truly treat them as nothing, they will tell you very clearly: "No." The first is not elevating oneself; the second is loving oneself. Both in the same person — and it isn't awkward at all.

Back to the opening question: the kind of confidence that puts people at ease is self-love. The kind of "confidence" that makes people uncomfortable is self-elevation. The difference is not in how highly they regard themselves — it is in whether they also, along the way, push you down a step.

These two pairs are the same thing Laozi has been saying throughout the entire book —

From ch. 7's "putting yourself in the back, you end up in front," to ch. 22's "not displaying yourself, you become clear," to ch. 66's "the sea stays lowest, and the water comes on its own," to ch. 67's "not carving yourself into the shape of 'I am great,' you can truly be great" — the whole thread has been one sentence: put up no sign, and you arrive.

And in this last chapter, Laozi brings it to the most intimate place of all — to how you treat yourself.

VI. Removing and Keeping: Deciding What to Do, and What Not To

The chapter's final four characters land lightly:

"Therefore: remove what belongs over there; keep what belongs here."

So: decide what to stop doing, and what to continue.

— What to remove: displaying oneself (parading one's views), elevating oneself (placing one's status above others).

— What to keep: knowing oneself (clear-eyed judgment), loving oneself (genuine self-respect).

But there is a crucial distinction here: "removing what belongs over there" is not "I weighed the pros and cons and decided this was more advantageous, so I chose it."

Nor is it "I really want to show off and be looked up to, but I am suppressing it."

Suppression cannot last a lifetime.

The "removing" Laozi speaks of flows from the clarity of the previous chapter: when you have truly seen where the path of displaying oneself and elevating oneself leads — seen how it hollows a person out step by step, drains relationships dry — you no longer need to suppress anything. You simply do not want to go there anymore.

This is the least-effort way of living: not fighting yourself with willpower every day, but having seen through it, watching the temptation dissolve on its own.

And "keeping what belongs here" is not straining toward a higher goal — it is that once you stop walking that dead-end road, you naturally stand back in the place you were always meant to be.

Laozi has spoken for seventy-two chapters, and what he finally lands on is as simple an act as this: each day, decide what to do and what not to do.

VII. To the Reader

This chapter offers several things you can put to use right now:

First: don't stake a relationship on authority alone.

Compliance born of fear has an expiration date (when people no longer fear authority). What is genuinely stable is "being trustworthy," not "being someone to avoid crossing."

Second: look squarely at other people's difficulties — don't skim past them.

What looks minor to you may be the whole of what they are carrying (do not dismiss where they dwell). Many people's real pain is not that the problem went unsolved — it is that it went unseen.

Third: don't conclude "they're already doing fine" on their behalf.

That well-meaning "you're in a good place now" often becomes the ceiling you set for them (do not be satisfied with where their lives have arrived). Whether it's enough — let them say so.

Fourth: have clear judgment — but don't need to show it off to everyone.

Having discernment is good; turning it into something you need to perform constantly is a different thing (knowing oneself without displaying oneself).

Fifth: respect yourself — but don't elevate yourself above others.

Self-love is inward (I do not degrade myself). Self-elevation is outward (I am a rank above you). You can hold yourself in the highest regard and simultaneously not look down on anyone (loving oneself without elevating oneself).

Sixth: once you have seen clearly, you don't need to suppress.

When you have truly seen where a road leads, you don't need willpower to avoid it (remove what belongs over there, keep what belongs here).

This chapter's core can be summed in one line —

Respecting yourself and placing yourself above others are two different things: the first lets you stand firmly; the second leaves you standing alone.

VIII. This Group Is Complete

Chapter 72 is the final essay in this group of nine (chapters 64 through 72). Looking back across the path:

(ch64) Put your care in while things are still forming; attend to the end as carefully as the beginning.

(ch65) Don't push your "understanding" into others — let people grow their own.

(ch66) Stay in the lowest place like the sea — and the water comes on its own.

(ch67) Three treasures: caring, restraint, not daring to go first in the world.

(ch68) The true master wins quietly.

(ch69) The greatest danger is thinking yourself invincible.

(ch70) The simplest truths are hardest to live; carry your jade inside a coarse cloth coat.

(ch71) Knowing you don't know — that's real skill.

(ch72) Respect yourself but don't elevate yourself; then decide what to do and what not to do.

Nine chapters, and underneath them all one single thing: put away the sign that says "I" — then others can grow, and you yourself can stand firm.

And Laozi, at the very end, gathers all of it into the plainest of acts: remove what belongs over there; keep what belongs here.

The next group (chapters 73 through 81) is the final nine chapters of the Daodejing. Laozi will speak of "heaven's net, vast and fine-meshed, missing nothing"; of "the soft and weak overcoming the hard and strong"; and finally, close the entire book on one sentence — "The way of the sage: act, but do not compete."

Do — but don't compete. See you in the next group.
