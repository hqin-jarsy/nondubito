# 你越往低处站，人越愿意跟着你

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 7 月

一、为什么有的领导让人想跟，有的让人想逃

我们通常以为,想让别人跟着你,你得站得更高、更强、更有存在感——你得让人觉得"这个人在我之上"。

可现实里,你多半见过相反的事:

有的人明明是领导,大家却打心底不服,能躲就躲;有的人位置不高、说话也不冲,大家却愿意跟着他,有事都来找他。

同样是"在上位",为什么一个让人想逃,一个让人想跟?

老子在《道德经》第六十六章,用最简单的一个东西,把这件事说透了——水。

二、江海为什么是"王"

老子说——

"江海之所以能为百谷王者,以其善下之也,故能为百谷王。"

江海之所以能成为"百谷之王",是因为它善于处在低处——所以能成为百谷之王。

先把这个"王"字读对。

这里的"王",不是"统治者"、不是"霸主"。它的意思是:众水归往的地方——所有的水,都往它那里流。

天下的溪流、山谷里的水,最后都汇进江海。不是江海把它们叫来的,是水本来就往低处流——而江海,恰好在最低的地方。

你看,这个"王",跟"发号施令"没有半点关系。它不指挥谁、不管束谁,甚至什么都没做——它只是在那儿,待在最低的地方。而所谓"成为王",也不是它去征服了百谷,是百谷自己流向了它、自己认了它。"归往"和"支配",是两件恰好相反的事。

所以"善下"在这里,根本不是一种道德姿态（"江海很谦虚,所以伟大"）。它是一个再朴素不过的机制:

水往低处流。谁在最低处,谁就成了万物汇聚的地方。

这件事,反直觉的地方就在这儿——

我们总以为"吸引人"要靠往上冲:更响的声音、更亮的招牌、更强的存在感。可江海告诉你,恰恰相反。

你越是拼命把自己举高、越是要所有人都看见你,人反而离你越远;你越是把自己放低、把姿态收下来,人反而愿意向你靠近。

高,是一个把人推开的姿势;低,才是一个把人聚拢的姿势。

放到今天看——

一个真正"大家都来找"的人,不是那个天天喊"快来我这儿"的人;是那个最没架子、最开放、最实在有用的人。你身边那个"谁有问题都愿意找他聊两句"的同事,那个所有人都愿意在上面搭东西的开放平台——他们不是把人招来的,是自己站得够低,人自然就聚过来了。

三、要在上，先把话放低；要在前，先把身放后

接着,老子把江海的道理,落到了"人"身上——

"是以圣人之欲上民也,必以其言下之;其欲先民也,必以其身后之。"

圣人想要居于众人之上,一定先把言辞放低;想要走在众人之前,一定先把自身放后。

注意,老子没说"圣人不当领导"。他说的是:圣人要居上位、要走在前——只是居上、在前的方式,是反过来的:

— 言下之——说话的姿态放低。不摆"我是老大、我说了算、我最懂"那块招牌。

— 身后之——把自己放在后面。让别人先,把功劳让出去,把担子接过来。

这听起来像退让,其实不是退让,是机制——

就像江海不是"谦虚"才容得下百川,是"处在低处"才容得下;人也不是"客气"才让人跟,是"把自己放低"了,人才聚得过来、才跟得起来。

老子在第七章早就说过同样的话:"退其身而身先。"——把身子往后一退,反而站到了前面。

为什么"放低"反而能"在上"?

因为让人难受的,从来不是"你在上",而是"你让人觉得被你压着"。一个把姿态放低的上位者,拿掉了那份"被压"的难受,人就不抵触了;人不抵触,才谈得上"愿意跟"。你抢的位置越少,别人反而越愿意把位置让给你。

放到今天:那个总说"你来定,我信你"、把功劳记在团队头上、有事自己先扛的管理者——大家愿意跟。那个永远站在聚光灯下、抢着表功、动不动"我是领导"的——大家躲着走。同样一件事做成了,一个把"我们"挂在嘴上,一个把"我"挂在嘴上——跟的人,天差地别。

这里有个特别小、却特别真的信号:出了成绩,他站到谁前面?把团队推到台前、自己退到后排的人,下一次,大家会更卖力;每次都抢先站到聚光灯正中的人,下一次,大家会留一手。你把别人往前推一次,别人就愿意把你往上抬一次;你把别人往后压一次,别人就在心里,悄悄把你往下记一次。

四、居上，却不让人觉得"重"

于是——

"故居前而民弗害也,居上而民弗重也,天下乐推而弗厌也。"

所以圣人走在前面,别人却不觉得被他挡、被他害;居于上位,别人却不觉得被他压、被他重;于是天下人乐意推举他,而且不厌烦他。

这里最妙的是"弗重"两个字——居上,却不让人觉得"重"。

一个"重"的领导,是什么样?——他的存在,你时时刻刻都感觉得到:事事要过他、处处要看他脸色、动不动就得想着"他会怎么看"。他压在你头上,像块石头。

而且这种"重",不一定是因为他凶、他坏。很多时候他很勤快、很负责、什么都亲力亲为——可正是这份"什么都要经我手",让底下每个人都动弹不得、都得时刻绷着。他越是"事事在场",别人越是喘不过气。

而一个"善下"的领导,你几乎感觉不到他在你之上。老子在第十七章说过:最好的在上者,"下知有之"——底下的人,只知道有这么个人,仅此而已。

不是他没在管,是他管得让你不觉得被管。所以大家"乐推而弗厌"——心甘情愿地拥护,而且不会有一天,盼着他赶紧走。

我在硅谷这些年,遇到过最好的几个管理者,有一个共同点:跟他们共事,你几乎会忘了他是你老板——直到你真正需要他的时候,他就在那儿。

五、他没在跟你争，所以你赢不了他

最后一句,是这一章最深的地方——

"以其无争,故天下莫能与之争。"

因为他不争,所以天下没有人能跟他争。

这句话里,藏着一个很关键的字差。

帛书写的是"以其无争";通行本（后世流传最广的版本）改成了"以其不争"。一字之差,深浅差了一层。

— 不争——是"我其实想争,但我忍着、我选择不去争"。你还在那个"争"的局里,只是按住了手。局还在,对手还在,那个要赢的东西还在。

— 无争——是根本没有"争"这件事。你压根不在那个局里。没有对手,是因为你不玩这个游戏。

为什么"无争"的人,天下没人争得过他?

因为"争",得有两个人对着争才成立。如果一个人根本没在争,你跟谁争?你赢谁?——你没法在一场"没有比赛"的比赛里,赢过一个"没上场"的人。

但要命的是:这不是一种手段。

不是"你装作不争,好让自己赢"——那还是在争,还在那个局里,只是换了个姿势。真正的"无争",是你真的不在那个你死我活的局里了——你在造一个东西,不在赢一个人。

再多说一句,因为这一层最容易被忽略——

待在"争"的局里,其实是很累的。累在哪儿?——累在你的方向,是被对手定的。

对手往哪走,你就得跟着往哪防;对手出什么招,你就得接什么招;对手降价,你也得降;对手上什么功能,你也得跟。你以为你在往前冲,其实你一直在被别人牵着走。

而一旦你走出这个局,去做那件只有你在做的事——你就把方向夺回来了。没有对手可追,因为路是你自己在往前开。

在硅谷,有两套截然不同的语言:一套叫"碾压对手"（Crush the Competition）,一套叫"在宇宙上留下一点凹痕"（Making a Dent in the Universe）。

前一套,眼睛盯着对手——总有个敌人要打败。后一套,眼睛盯着那个还不存在的东西——它跟谁都不在一个局里。

真正让人"争不过"的,往往是后一种人。不是因为他把所有对手都赢了,是因为他压根没在跟谁比。就像江海——它从不跟溪流争水;它只是待在最低处,水自己就来了。

而且,走出零和的局,不只是"没人跟你争"这么被动——它还让整件事变大了。争,是在一块固定的饼上,抢谁的那份更大;造,是把饼本身做大。江海不跟任何一条溪流抢水,可到最后,天下的水都因它而有了归处——它没从谁手里抢走什么,却成全了所有的水。

"莫能与之争"最深的意思,不是你赢了所有人;是这条路上,压根就没有"别人"。

六、给读者的

这一章,给我们几件当下就能用的事——

第一:想让人跟着你,先把话放低。

别把"我是老大、我最懂"摆在前面（言下之）。心里有数是一回事,把它当招牌去压人,是另一回事。

第二:想在前,先把身放后。

让别人先,把功劳让出去,把担子接过来（身后之）。退一步,常常反而站到了前面。

第三:让你的"在上"轻一点。

最好的在上者,让人几乎感觉不到他压在头上（居上而民弗重）。你越是让人时时刻刻感觉到你的存在,人越想逃。

第四:想成为"大家都来"的地方,别喊"来我这儿"。

去做那个最低、最开放、最实在有用的——水往低处流,人也一样（江海善下）。

第五:分清"不争"和"无争"。

不是"我忍着不争",是根本不在那个零和的局里——你在造东西,不在赢谁（无争）。你争不过的那个人,往往是没在跟你争的人。

这一章的核心,可以收成一句话——

江海从不招水,水却自己来;因为它待在最低处。想让人跟着你,不是把自己抬得更高,是把自己放得更低。

七、承上启下

第六十六章,接着上一章往下走——上一章（ch65)说"圣人不把自己的知强加给民,民才自长";这一章说"圣人善下,民才不被压、不感重,才乐意跟"。两章都是同一件事:圣人不抢别人的位,别人自然归往。

"江海"这个意象,老子从第八章"上善若水"、第三十二章"道之在天下犹小谷之与江海",一路讲到这里,收得最完整:处在最低,反而容得下最多。

而"无争"这个词,是老子讲了一整本书的"不争"里,最深的一处——到全书快结尾时,他会把它收在"为而弗争"上。

下一章（ch67),老子会亮出他的"三宝":慈、俭、还有一个"不敢为天下先"。还是这同一股劲:把自己放后,把位置让出来。

I. Why Some Leaders Make People Want to Follow — and Others Make Them Want to Flee

We tend to assume that making others follow you requires standing higher, being stronger, projecting a bigger presence — making people feel "this person is above me."

But in reality, you have probably seen the opposite:

Some leaders hold formal authority and yet no one truly respects them — people avoid them when they can. Others hold no particular rank, never speak sharply, and yet people are genuinely glad to follow them — and come to them whenever something comes up.

Both are "in a position above others." Why does one make people want to flee, the other make people want to follow?

In Chapter 66 of the Daodejing, Laozi uses the simplest possible thing to make this transparent — water.

II. Why the Rivers and Seas Are "King"

Laozi says:

"The reason rivers and seas can be the king of the hundred valleys is that they are good at being below them — and therefore can be the king of the hundred valleys."

First, read the word "king" correctly.

This "king" does not mean "ruler" or "overlord." It means: the place toward which all waters converge — the place everything flows to.

The streams of the world, the water of every mountain valley, all eventually flow into rivers and seas. Not because rivers and seas called them — but because water naturally flows downward, and rivers and seas happen to be in the lowest place.

This "kingship" has nothing to do with issuing commands. Rivers and seas direct no one, constrain no one — they don't even do anything. They are simply there, in the lowest place. And what it means to "become king" is not that they conquered the valleys — the valleys flowed toward them on their own, and recognized them on their own. "Being flowed toward" and "dominating" are two entirely opposite things.

So "being good at being below" here is not a moral stance ("rivers and seas are humble, therefore great"). It is the simplest possible mechanism:

Water flows downward. Whoever is in the lowest place becomes the place everything converges.

This is where the counterintuitive point lives:

We always assume "attracting people" requires charging upward — a louder voice, a brighter sign, a stronger presence. Rivers and seas show you the exact opposite.

The harder you try to elevate yourself, the more you demand to be seen, the further people retreat. The more you lower yourself, the more you bring your posture down, the more people are willing to draw near.

Height is a posture that pushes people away. Lowness is the posture that draws them in.

Seen from today: the person everyone genuinely comes to is not the one shouting "come to me" every day — it is the one who is the least pretentious, the most open, the most plainly useful. That colleague everyone is happy to talk to when something comes up, the open platform everyone is willing to build on — they didn't recruit people; they stood low enough that people naturally gathered.

III. To Be Above, First Lower Your Words; to Be in Front, First Put Yourself Behind

Laozi then brings the teaching of rivers and seas down to the level of people:

"Therefore when the sage wishes to be above the people, he must place himself below them in his words. When he wishes to be in front of the people, he must place himself behind them in his person."

Note that Laozi does not say "the sage refuses positions of leadership." He says: the sage does want to be above and in front — only the way of being above and in front is reversed:

— Lower himself in words — the posture of how he speaks is lowered. No sign that says "I am in charge, I decide, I know best."

— Put himself behind in his person — lets others go first; gives the credit away; takes on the burdens himself.

This sounds like giving ground — but it is not yielding, it is a mechanism:

Just as rivers and seas hold the hundred streams not from "humility" but from being in the low place — people gather around you not from "courtesy" but because you have placed yourself lower, making it possible for them to gather and follow.

Laozi said the same thing as early as Chapter 7: "Withdraw his person to the back — and he ends up in front."

Why does lowering yourself put you above?

Because what makes people chafe is never "you are above me" — it is "you make me feel I am being pressed down." An above-person who lowers their posture removes that sense of being pressed — people no longer resist; and without resistance, "willing to follow" becomes possible. The fewer positions you claim, the more readily others give them to you.

Seen from today: the manager who always says "you decide — I trust you," who puts the team's name on the success and carries the weight himself — people are willing to follow him. The one who is always in the spotlight, always claiming credit, always announcing "I'm the leader" — people avoid him. Same accomplishment: one has "we" on his lips; the other has "I." The difference in who follows is total.

There is a small signal that reveals everything: when something succeeds, who does he stand in front of? The one who pushes the team forward and steps to the back — next time, people give more. The one who rushes to the center of the spotlight every time — next time, people hold something in reserve. Every time you push someone forward, they are willing to lift you up once; every time you push someone back, they quietly register it.

IV. Be Above — Yet Let No One Feel the Weight

And so:

"Therefore though he is in front, the people do not feel harmed by him. Though he is above, the people do not feel his weight. The world is glad to support him and does not grow weary of him."

The most exquisite two words here are "does not feel his weight" — above, yet letting no one feel crushed.

What is a "heavy" leader? — His presence is felt every moment: everything must pass through him, everything requires reading his mood, constantly wondering "how will he take this?" He sits on top of you like a stone.

And this "heaviness" is not necessarily because he is mean or bad. Often he is diligent, conscientious, personally involved in everything — but precisely this "everything must pass through me" makes every person underneath immovable, always on edge. The more he is "present in everything," the less others can breathe.

A leader who is "good at being low," by contrast, you barely feel as being above you. Laozi says in Chapter 17: the best of those who are above is the one of whom the people below simply "know they exist" — that is all.

Not that he isn't leading — but that he leads without you feeling led. So people "are glad to support him and do not grow weary" — willing supporters, who will never reach the day they are hoping he leaves.

The best managers I have encountered in Silicon Valley all share one quality: working with them, you nearly forget they are your boss — until you truly need them, and they are there.

V. He Isn't Competing with You — So You Can't Beat Him

The final line is the deepest point in the chapter:

"Because he does not contend, nothing under heaven can contend with him."

This line conceals a crucial textual difference.

The silk manuscript reads "because he has no contention (wu zheng)"; the received text changed it to "because he does not contend (bu zheng)." One word's difference; one whole level of depth.

— Does not contend — "I want to compete but I am restraining myself, I am choosing not to." You are still inside the competition; you have only held back your hand. The game is still there, the opponent is still there, the need to win is still there.

— No contention — there is simply no such thing as "competition" in this person's world. You are not even in the game. There is no opponent because you are not playing that game.

Why can no one in the world outcompete the person with no contention?

Because competing requires two people competing against each other. If one person is simply not competing, who are you competing against? Who are you beating? — You cannot win a competition with no competition against someone who never entered it.

But here is the crucial point: this is not a strategy.

It is not "pretend not to compete, so you can win" — that is still competing, still in the game, just with a different posture. The real "no contention" means you are genuinely no longer in that zero-sum game — you are building something, not defeating someone.

One more observation, because this layer is most easily missed:

Staying in the game of competition is actually exhausting. Exhausting how? — Your direction is set by your opponent.

Wherever they move, you have to guard against it; whatever they do, you have to respond; they cut prices, you cut prices; they ship a feature, you follow. You think you are charging forward — but you have been on someone else's leash the whole time.

The moment you step out of that game and go do the thing only you are doing — you take the direction back. No opponent to chase because you are the one opening the path ahead.

In Silicon Valley there are two entirely different languages: one says "crush the competition"; the other says "make a dent in the universe."

The first keeps its eyes on the opponent — there is always an enemy to defeat. The second keeps its eyes on the thing that does not yet exist — it is not in the same game as anyone.

The people no one can truly outcompete are usually the second kind. Not because they beat all their opponents, but because they were never competing with anyone. Like the rivers and seas — they never compete with any stream for water; they simply stay in the lowest place, and the water comes.

And leaving the zero-sum game is not merely passive ("no one competes with you") — it makes the whole thing larger. Competition is fighting over a fixed pie, arguing over whose slice is bigger. Creation is making the pie itself bigger. Rivers and seas never fight any stream for water — and yet in the end, all the water in the world finds its home through them. They took nothing from anyone, and yet completed all the water.

The deepest meaning of "nothing can contend with him" is not that you beat everyone — it is that on your path, there simply is no "anyone else."

VI. To the Reader

This chapter offers several things you can put to use right now:

First: if you want people to follow you, lower your words first.

Don't put "I'm in charge, I know best" out front (lower in words). Knowing your own mind is one thing; using it as a sign to press others is another.

Second: if you want to be in front, put yourself behind first.

Let others go first; give the credit away; carry the burden yourself (put yourself behind). Stepping back often puts you in front.

Third: make your "being above" lighter.

The best of those who are above are barely felt as pressing on anyone (above, yet they do not feel the weight). The more you make people feel your presence every moment, the more they want to escape.

Fourth: to become the place everyone comes, don't shout "come to me."

Be the lowest, the most open, the most plainly useful — water flows downward, and so do people (rivers and seas, good at being below).

Fifth: know the difference between "choosing not to contend" and "no contention."

Not "I'm holding back from competing" — but simply not being in that zero-sum game at all. You are building something, not beating anyone (no contention). The person you cannot outcompete is usually the one who isn't competing with you.

This chapter's core can be summed in one line —

Rivers and seas never call the water to come — yet the water comes, because they stay in the lowest place. To make people follow, don't raise yourself higher; place yourself lower.
