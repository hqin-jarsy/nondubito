Chapter 1 of 81

道可道

The Sayable Dao

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、第一句

读《道德经》第一句最难受的事情是：你不知道老子在说什么。

"道可道，非常道。名可名，非常名。"

字都认识。每个字都认识。合起来好像在说一件正确但是说不清的事——道是个东西，但它不能被说；说出来的就不是它了。

这是读者两千年来的第一道槛。

很多人卡在这里就放弃了——觉得这是一本神秘主义的书。也有很多人没卡——他们把"道"理解成一个躲在语言背后的玄奥实体，然后接着往下读。

这是后来玄学家走的路。

这条路其实读偏了。

二、恒，不是常

先停在"非常道"这三个字上。

1973年，马王堆出土了帛书本《道德经》。比传世本早三百年。帛书写的不是"非常道"，是"非恒道"。

一字之差。

汉文帝叫刘恒。避讳，把"恒"字全改成了"常"字。两千年来流传的版本都是改过的。

"常"和"恒"听起来差不多，但字面深度不一样。

"非常道"读起来像在说："那个真道，不常出现。"
"非恒道"读起来像在说："那个真道，永远不灭。"

后面会看到，老子要立的是后一个意思。

但他要立的"永远不灭"，不是说有一个真道躲在某处永远不灭。是另一件事。

三、收不干净

"道可道也，非恒道也。"——一张嘴说出来的"道"，都不是那个永远的道。

最容易的读法是：那个真的道在语言之外。语言能描述的部分都是冰山一角，海面下藏着那个真的。

这个读法看起来很深，其实简化了老子。

老子说的不是"真道在语言之外"。他说的是"可道，非恒"——能说出来的，不恒。

注意这里的"恒"指的不是"道"。指的是"你说的那件事"。

是说：

你不管怎么说，说多深，说多细，总有一点东西收不干净。

收不干净的那部分，永远在。

不是有一个完整的"真道"藏在你说的"道"背后。是不管你怎么说、说多少、用什么方式说，这件事本身就有一部分会从你说的话里漏出来。

这个漏出来收不干净的东西——就是这本注后面会反复回来的那个词——

余项。

道，是普遍的余项。

"恒道"就是这件事——余项的永不灭。

四、道德两个字

把"余项"这个词立起来，《道德经》这本书的题目就可以读了。

两千年来，"道德"这两个字的意思已经被改了。现在说"道德"，大家想到的是伦理，规矩，做人的标准。所以读者翻开这本书，预期里是一本讲做人规矩的书。

但老子在这本书里没讲伦理。

第一章一句伦理都没讲。第二章一上来就把"美""善"这种伦理词拆掉——说你立一个"美"出来，"丑"同时就出来了。立"善"出来，"不善"同时就出来了。

这不是一本讲做人规矩的书的开篇。

那"道"和"德"两个字在老子那里是什么意思？

道是这本书要拆的第一个字。拆到最后是这件事：一个所有的构都收不干净的东西。它不是路，不是规矩，不是宇宙本体。它是永远在每一刀凿之后剩下来的那部分——普遍的余项。

德是这本书后面要反复回来的字。它讲的也不是美德。它讲的是每个具体的人身上，还没被他所进入的那些构收进去的那部分——个体的余项。

合起来，《道德经》这个题目，字面意思其实是：

论那个总是溢出构的东西——普遍的（道）和个体的（德）。

一本五千字的书，从头到尾在讲这一件事。

五、有和无是同一刀

第一章接下来这几句，结构最密：

"无，名万物之始也；有，名万物之母也。"

读这两句最容易的错是把它读成时间序列——先是一片"无"，然后冒出"有"，"有"再生出万物。

这样读不对。

因为接下来老子马上说：

"两者同出。"

有和无不是先后。是同一次凿的两面。

什么意思？

想象你在一张白纸上画一个圆。一刀下去，圆有了——纸上多了一个形状。但同时无也出来了——圆的外面那一大片"不是圆"的区域，也是这一刀切出来的。

没有圆，就没有"不是圆"。

圆和圆外，是同一刀产生的两面。

"无"是圆没画之前那个状态——浑沌一片，没有任何区分。这是万物之始。"始"不是时间的起点，是结构的起点——是所有区分得以可能的那个底。

"有"是圆画出来那一刹——区分起来了，事物有了形状。这是万物之母。

注意"母"不是"生产者"。

母是什么？母是使生产成为可能的那个位置。有了"有"这个起头位置，世界后面层层展开才有得发生。母是位置，不是手。

这一点很容易看错。后来的注家把"母"读成"道生万物"那个生产者，于是建出了一整套"道是宇宙本体"的玄学。但老子不是这么说的。他说的是：有一个位置，从这个位置起，世界开始一层一层地分化。这个位置叫"有"。它是母。但它不亲手生孩子。

六、两种工夫

"故恒无欲也，以观其眇；恒有欲也，以观其所徼。"

这句话讲怎么修。

恒无欲——不是压抑欲望，不是看破红尘。是不把自己立成那个发出欲的人。

正常人怎么想事？"我要这个。""我要那个。""我喜欢这个。""我讨厌那个。"每一句话里都有一个"我"在做主语。

这个"我"就是一个构——你给自己立起来的一个边界，说"这是我，那不是我"。

恒无欲——就是把这个"我"暂时放下。不立"我要什么"。

这时候你看见什么？

你看见眇。

眇是什么？是起头处的微妙。是凿正要落下又还没落下、构正要立起来又还没立起来的那个瞬间。是一个事物正要被命名、又还没被命名的那一刹。

只有不把自己摆在"我要"位置上的人，才看得见这个瞬间。

为什么？因为你只要立了"我要"，你就开始把眼前的东西往"我要的那个构"里塞。你看到的不是事物本身，是事物对你的用处。你看一个杯子，看到的是"能喝水的工具"；你看一个人，看到的是"对我有没有用"。

恒无欲——把"我要"放下——你才看见杯子在被命名为"杯子"之前的样子，看见那个人在被你贴上标签之前的样子。

帛书写的是"眇"。通行本是"妙"。两字读起来差不多，但"眇"字面更具体——眇是细到几乎不存在的那个状态。妙更抽象。老子说的是字面具体的那个东西。

"恒有欲也，以观其所徼。"

这是另一种工夫。

恒有欲——不是要你贪心。是带着一个具体方向去做事。

前面"恒无欲"是把"我要"放下，让觉知出场。但你不能一辈子只看不做。该做事的时候，你需要立起一个方向——我现在要做这件事。

这时候你看见什么？

你看见所徼。

徼是边界。所徼是你的方向所触及到的那个边界。

任何一件事你具体去做，做到一定程度都会撞墙——"再往这边推就推不动了""这里有个东西卡住了""这条路走不通"。这些卡住的地方就是徼。

注意"所徼"和"徼"不一样。帛书加了一个"所"字。一字之差。

"徼"是静态的边界。
"所徼"是你走到才显出来的边界。

你不走，边界不显。你越走，边界越显。

这是一个非常精准的字面。边界不是事先放在那里等你的——是你具体去做，才把它做出来。

通行本把这个"所"字删了。一删，整句的动作感就掉了。

老子在这里讲修道者的两种必要工夫：

恒无欲，看起头。
恒有欲，看尽头。

两者合起来才完整。

光看起头不行——你没办法做事。
光看尽头也不行——你看不见命名前的样子。

要两者都有。

七、玄

"两者同出，异名同谓。"

回到有和无这两个名。它们是同一次凿的两面。同出。名字不同，但所指其实是同一件事。

老子在这里故意没有点出所指的事情叫什么。他卖了个关子。

下一句才点出：

"玄之又玄，众眇之门。"

那件事叫——

玄。

玄就是凿构循环本身。

什么意思？就是这件事：

一刀下去，有和无同时出现。有出来，开始展开，长成一个结构。结构展到顶，又成了下一刀的底。下一刀又下去，新的有和无又出现。又展开。又展到顶。又成了再下一刀的底。

一层。一层。永不停。

"玄之又玄"——一层套一层，循环一直往上推。

这就是玄这个字想说的。它不是神秘玄妙的"玄"——是这个一层层套下去、一直跑下去的运动本身。

为什么这个循环能一直跑下去？

因为余项永远在。

每一刀下去，构总是收不干净。总有一点东西溢出来。这点溢出来的东西，就是下一刀的起点。如果一刀下去能把所有东西都收进构里，循环就停了。

但一刀下去从来收不干净。

所以下一刀总是有起点。

"众眇之门"——所有的眇都从这个门里出来。这个门，是余项不灭这件事所保证的那个生生机制。

只要余项不灭，眇就源源不断地涌出。
只要眇源源不断地涌出，世界就一层一层地展开。

这就是这一章想立的总框架。

八、第一章没讲任何具体的事

第一章其实没讲任何具体的事。

它讲的是所有具体的事之所以可能的那个前提。

后面八十章——讲圣人，讲水，讲战争，讲治国，讲修身，讲生死——每一件事都在这个前提下跑。这个前提就是：

凿构循环永远在跑。
余项永远不灭。
每一刀下去都有起头处和尽头处。
恒无欲的人看见起头。
恒有欲的人看见尽头。

整本书的总条件，老子在这第一章一句不漏地立完了。然后他没多说，转去讲第二章。

读《道德经》最容易出的错，是把它的语言当成神秘主义——觉得"道"是某种说不出来的玄奥东西，"玄之又玄"听起来很高深，所以一定深不可测。

不是。

老子在这一章里讲的是一件结构上非常清楚的事——只要你立一个名，就会有东西溢出来；这件事不会停；这件事就是玄。

他用了"玄"这个字，是因为这件事没有别的名字可以叫——你给它取任何一个名，那个名马上就被这件事本身溢出。所以只能叫——

玄。

玄不是神秘。玄是这本书没法不用的那个字。

I. The First Line

The most frustrating thing about reading the first line of the Daodejing is that you have no idea what Laozi is saying.

"The Dao that can be told is not the enduring Dao. The name that can be named is not the enduring name."

You know every character. Every single one. Put together, they seem to be saying something correct but indescribable — the Dao is something, but it can't be spoken; whatever gets said isn't it.

This has been the first obstacle for readers across two thousand years.

Many stop here and give up — deciding this is a book of mysticism. Many others push through by treating the Dao as a mysterious entity hiding behind language, and reading on.

That was the road the later xuanxue metaphysicians took.

It's a wrong turn.

II. Heng, Not Chang

Stop first at the three characters "non-chang-Dao."

In 1973, the silk manuscript Daodejing was unearthed at Mawangdui — three hundred years older than the received text. Where the received text has "non-chang," the silk manuscript has "non-heng."

One character's difference.

Emperor Wen of Han was named Liu Heng. To avoid the taboo on his personal name, every heng was changed to chang. Every version transmitted over the past two thousand years carries that substitution.

Chang and heng sound similar but differ in depth.

"Non-chang Dao" reads as: "That true Dao doesn't often appear."
"Non-heng Dao" reads as: "That true Dao never perishes."

As we'll see, it's the second meaning Laozi is establishing.

But the "never perishes" he's establishing does not mean there is a true Dao hiding somewhere that never perishes. It means something else.

III. What Cannot Be Fully Gathered

"The Dao that can be told — it is non-enduring." Whatever you say out loud about the Dao is not the enduring Dao.

The easiest reading: the true Dao lies beyond language. What language can describe is only the tip of the iceberg; the real thing hides below the surface.

That reading seems deep. It actually simplifies Laozi.

Laozi does not say "the true Dao is beyond language." He says "what can be told is non-enduring" — what can be said does not endure.

Notice that "enduring" here does not refer to "the Dao." It refers to "the thing you said."

What this says is:

No matter how you say it, how deep, how precise — there is always something that cannot be fully gathered in.

That part which cannot be gathered always remains.

There is no complete "true Dao" hiding behind what you say. Rather, no matter how you say it, how much you say, by whatever means — something about the thing itself will always leak out past what you've said.

That thing that leaks out and cannot be gathered — this is the term that will recur throughout this commentary —

The remainder.

The Dao is the universal remainder.

The "enduring Dao" is precisely this: the indestructibility of the remainder.

IV. The Two Characters Dao and De

With "remainder" established as a concept, the title of the Daodejing itself becomes readable.

Over two thousand years, "dao de" has had its meaning overwritten. Today the phrase evokes ethics, rules, moral standards. So readers open this book expecting a guide to moral conduct.

But Laozi does not discuss ethics in this book.

Chapter One says nothing ethical. Chapter Two immediately dismantles ethical terms like "beautiful" and "good" — saying that the moment you establish "the beautiful," "the ugly" simultaneously appears; establish "the good," and "the not-good" simultaneously appears.

This is not how a book of moral rules opens.

So what do dao and de mean for Laozi?

Dao is the first word this book takes apart. What it comes apart into is this: something that no chisel-construct can fully gather. Not a path, not a rule, not a cosmic substance. It is the part that always remains after every cut — the universal remainder.

De is the character this book returns to again and again. It too is not virtue. It is the part of each concrete person that has not yet been gathered into whatever constructs they have entered — the individual remainder.

Together, the title Daodejing literally means:

A treatise on what always overflows the construct — the universal kind (dao) and the individual kind (de).

Five thousand characters, from beginning to end, on this one thing.

V. Being and Non-Being Are the Same Cut

The next lines of Chapter One are the densest in structure:

"Non-being: the naming of the beginning of the ten thousand things. Being: the naming of the mother of the ten thousand things."

The easiest mistake when reading these two lines is to read them as a time sequence — first a span of "non-being," then "being" emerges, then being gives birth to the ten thousand things.

Wrong.

Because Laozi immediately says:

"Both come forth together."

Being and non-being are not sequential. They are two faces of the same single cut.

What does that mean?

Imagine drawing a circle on a blank sheet of paper. One stroke, and the circle is — a shape appears. But simultaneously non-being appears too — the large area outside the circle, "not-circle," is also produced by that same stroke.

No circle, no "not-circle."

The circle and its outside are two faces of the same stroke.

"Non-being" is the state before the circle was drawn — undifferentiated, no distinctions at all. This is the beginning of the ten thousand things. "Beginning" is not a point in time; it is the structural origin — the ground from which all distinction becomes possible.

"Being" is the instant the circle is drawn — distinction arises, things take shape. This is the mother of the ten thousand things.

Note: "mother" is not "producer."

What is a mother? A mother is the position that makes production possible. With "being" as this initiating position, the world can unfold layer after layer. Mother is a position, not a hand.

This is easy to misread. Later commentators read "mother" as the producer in "the Dao gives birth to the ten thousand things," constructing an elaborate metaphysics of the Dao as cosmic substance. But that's not what Laozi says. He says: there is a position; from this position, the world begins to differentiate layer by layer. That position is called "being." It is the mother. But it does not personally give birth.

VI. Two Kinds of Practice

"Therefore, enduringly without desire, observe the minute origin; enduringly with desire, observe the boundary it reaches."

This line is about practice.

Enduringly without desire — not suppressing desire, not renouncing the world. It means not placing yourself in the position of the one who issues desires.

How does an ordinary person think? "I want this." "I want that." "I like this." "I dislike that." Every sentence has an "I" as its subject.

This "I" is a construct — a boundary you have erected around yourself, saying "this is me, that is not me."

Enduringly without desire — temporarily set that "I" down. Don't establish "what I want."

Then what do you see?

You see the minute origin (miao).

What is the minute origin? It is the subtle moment of commencement — the instant when the chisel is just about to fall but hasn't yet fallen, the construct just about to be erected but not yet erected. The instant when something is just about to be named but hasn't been named yet.

Only those who are not standing in the "I want" position can see this instant.

Why? Because the moment you establish "I want," you start pressing everything in front of you into "the construct I want." What you see is not the thing itself — it's what the thing is good for. You look at a cup and see "a tool for drinking water." You look at a person and see "does this serve me?"

Enduringly without desire — setting "I want" down — you see the cup before it was named "cup," you see the person before you affixed any label.

The silk manuscript writes miao (minute, subtle). The received text writes miao (wondrous). Pronounced the same, but the silk manuscript character is more concrete — the character literally means something so fine it barely exists. The received text version is more abstract. Laozi is talking about the literally concrete thing.

"Enduringly with desire, observe the boundary it reaches."

This is the other kind of practice.

Enduringly with desire — not encouraging greed. It means acting with a concrete direction.

The previous "enduringly without desire" puts "I want" down, letting awareness emerge. But you can't spend your whole life only watching and never acting. When the time comes to act, you need to establish a direction — I am going to do this thing.

Then what do you see?

You see the boundary it reaches (suǒjiǎo).

Jiǎo is a limit, a boundary. Suǒjiǎo is the boundary that your direction encounters when it gets there.

In anything you actually try to do, at some point you hit a wall — "I can't push any further in this direction." "Something is stuck here." "This road dead-ends." These stuck places are the boundary.

Note that suǒjiǎo differs from jiǎo. The silk manuscript adds the character suǒ. One character's difference.

Jiǎo — a static boundary.
Suǒjiǎo — the boundary that appears only when you walk up to it.

Without walking, no boundary appears. The more you walk, the more boundary appears.

This is a very precise literal reading. Boundaries aren't placed there in advance waiting for you — they are brought into existence by your actual doing.

The received text deletes this suǒ character. Delete it and the whole line loses its sense of motion.

Laozi is describing the two necessary practices for anyone who seeks to know the Dao:

Enduringly without desire: see the start.
Enduringly with desire: see the end.

Neither is complete without the other.

Only seeing the start won't do — you can't act.
Only seeing the end won't do — you can't see things before naming.

You need both.

VII. Xuan

"Both come forth together, differently named but referring to the same thing."

Back to being and non-being. They are two faces of the same single cut. Same emergence. Different names, but pointing to the same thing.

Laozi deliberately does not name that thing here. He holds it back.

The next line names it:

"Xuan upon xuan — the gate of all minute origins."

That thing is called —

Xuan.

Xuan is the chisel-construct cycle itself.

What does that mean? It means this:

One cut, and being and non-being simultaneously appear. Being emerges, starts to unfold, grows into a structure. The structure reaches its apex and becomes the ground for the next cut. The next cut falls; new being and non-being appear again. They unfold again. Reach their apex again. Become the ground for the cut after that.

Layer. Layer. Never stopping.

"Xuan upon xuan" — layer within layer, the cycle perpetually advancing.

This is what the character xuan is meant to convey. Not "mysterious and profound" xuan — but the self-perpetuating motion itself, layer after layer, running on and on.

Why can this cycle keep running forever?

Because the remainder never perishes.

Every cut fails to fully gather everything into the construct. There is always a bit left over. That bit of remainder is the starting point for the next cut. If a cut could fully gather everything, the cycle would stop.

But no cut ever fully gathers.

So the next cut always has a starting point.

"The gate of all minute origins" — all minute origins come through this gate. This gate is the life-generating mechanism guaranteed by the indestructibility of the remainder.

As long as the remainder never perishes, minute origins keep surging forth.
As long as minute origins keep surging forth, the world keeps unfolding layer by layer.

This is the total framework Chapter One sets in place.

VIII. Chapter One Says Nothing Specific

Chapter One actually says nothing specific.

What it says is the prerequisite for all specific things to be possible.

The next eighty chapters — on sages, water, war, governance, self-cultivation, life and death — all operate within this prerequisite. The prerequisite is:

The chisel-construct cycle is always running.
The remainder never perishes.
Every cut produces both a starting-point and an ending-point.
Those enduringly without desire see the starting-point.
Those enduringly with desire see the ending-point.

The total conditions for the whole book — Laozi lays them all out in this first chapter without leaving anything out. Then, without further comment, he moves to Chapter Two.

The most common mistake in reading the Daodejing is to treat its language as mysticism — to feel that "the Dao" is some ineffable mysterious entity, that "xuan upon xuan" sounds very profound and must therefore be unfathomably deep.

It isn't.

What Laozi says in this chapter is something structurally very clear: the moment you establish a name, something overflows; this never stops; this is xuan.

He uses the character xuan because the thing has no other name that can hold it — give it any name and that name is immediately overflowed by the thing itself. So the only option is —

Xuan.

Xuan is not mystery. Xuan is the character this book cannot do without.
