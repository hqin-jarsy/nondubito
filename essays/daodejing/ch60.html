# 煎小鱼，别老翻

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 5 月

一、煎小鱼的道理

会做饭的人都知道,煎一条小鱼,最忌讳的是什么?

是老去翻动它。

你一会儿翻一下、一会儿戳一下、一会儿挪一挪——

小鱼很快就被翻得稀碎,不成样子。

正确的做法是:下锅之后,让它安安静静地煎,不要老去动它。火候到了,它自己就熟了,而且完整、漂亮。

老子在《道德经》第六十章里,用这个厨房里的小道理,讲了一个治理的大智慧——

"治大国若烹小鲜。"

治理一个大国,就像煎一条小鱼——不要老去翻动、老去折腾、老去插手。

这句话流传了两千多年,被无数人引用。

它的核心就一个:别折腾。

管一个国、带一个团队、养一个孩子、经营一段关系——

很多时候,你越是频繁地插手、翻动、折腾,越是把事情搞砸。

这一章讲的是——

为什么"别折腾"是最高的治理智慧。以及,一个更深的道理:当你不把自己摆在对抗的位置上,很多本来能伤你的东西,就伤不到你了。

二、治大国，像煎小鱼

第六十章开头——

"治大国若烹小鲜,以道莅天下。"

治理大国,像煎小鱼一样;用"道"来君临天下。

"若烹小鲜"——像煎小鱼。

这个比方的精髓,全在"不翻动"三个字——

小鱼下锅,你越是频繁翻动,它越碎;你让它安静地煎,它反而完整地熟。

治理也一样——

你越是频繁地立新规矩、改政策、插手干预、折腾来折腾去,系统越乱;你让它在一个稳定的环境里自己运作,反而运转得好。

这接前面几章一路讲下来的道理——

ch57:越管越乱(立的禁忌越多,反而越糟)。 ch58:管得越松反而越好(政闵闵则民屯屯)。 ch59:省着用、别透支(啬)。 ch60:别折腾(若烹小鲜)。

一条线下来,都是同一个智慧:不要过度干预,让事物在稳定中自己运作。

"以道莅天下"——用"道"来君临天下。

什么叫"以道"?

就是用前面讲的这种方式:不立招牌、不强为、不折腾、留有余地。

不是用强力去统治、用规矩去管控、用手段去驾驭——

而是像道一样,不折腾、不插手,让天下自己运转。

放到 2026 年看——

一个好的管理者,深谙"若烹小鲜"——

他定好方向、搭好环境,然后不去频繁插手、不去朝令夕改、不去事事干预——

让团队在一个稳定的环境里,自己把事情做好。

而那种天天改方向、天天出新规、天天插手细节的管理者——

把团队翻得稀碎,就像那条被反复翻动的小鱼。

别折腾,是最高的治理智慧。

三、那些本来能伤你的东西

接下来这一段,是这一章比较特别、也比较深的部分——

老子用了一个当时人们熟悉的说法:鬼神。

"其鬼不神?非其鬼不神也。其神不伤人也?非其神不伤人也,圣人弗伤也。"

难道是那些精怪没有神通吗?不是它们没有神通。难道是它们的神通不伤人吗?不是它们的神通不伤人——是圣人不(把自己摆在被)伤(的位置上)。

先说明——

老子这里说的"鬼""神",是当时人们对世界的一种理解:万物有各种看不见的、可能伤害人的力量。

我们今天不必执着于"鬼神"这个说法本身——可以把它理解为:那些潜在的、可能伤害你的力量。

不管是明枪暗箭、是意外的打击、是他人的恶意、是命运的无常——

那些本来"能伤你"的东西。

老子的推理很精妙,分三步——

第一步(承认): 那些力量,是有"神通"的(是真能起作用的)。老子不否认它们的存在和能力。

第二步(承认): 那些力量,本来是"能伤人"的。老子也不否认它们真能造成伤害。

第三步(关键): 但"圣人弗伤"——真正得道的人,不把自己摆在那个"能被伤害"的位置上。

这是什么意思?

不是说那些力量消失了,也不是说它们突然变善良了——

而是:真正得道的人,不与人为敌、不树立对手、不把自己摆在对抗和冲突的位置上——

所以那些"能伤人"的力量,找不到可以伤害他的地方。

这接前面讲过的两章——

ch50(无死地): 真正善于保全自己的人,不是能对抗猛兽,是根本不把自己置于猛兽能伤到的地方——"犀牛找不到地方用它的角,老虎找不到地方用它的爪"。

ch55(赤子): 婴儿"毒虫猛兽都不伤害他"——不是他有护身法术,是他不带攻击性、不与任何东西为敌,所以没有什么与他为敌。

ch60 讲的是同一个道理——

那些力量本来能伤人(承认),但当你不把自己摆在对抗、树敌、可被攻击的位置上,它们就伤不到你(圣人弗伤)。

放到 2026 年看——

一个四处树敌、处处争斗、时时把别人当对手的人——

浑身都是可以被攻击的破绽,危机四伏。

而一个不与人为敌、不把自己摆在对抗位置上的人——

别人的恶意找不到着力点,很多本来能伤他的东西,就伤不到他。

不是外面的威胁消失了,是你不再给它们可以伤害你的位置。

四、两边都不伤，德就回来了

整章的收束——

"夫两不相伤,故德交归焉。"

两边都不互相伤害,所以"德"就交相归回来了。

"两不相伤"——两边都不伤害对方。

那些"能伤人"的力量,伤不到圣人(因为圣人不给它们着力点);

圣人也不去伤害别人(圣人本就不立"伤人"的位)——

两边都不立"伤"的位置,于是"两不相伤"。

"故德交归焉"——所以"德"交相归回来了。

当没有伤害、没有对抗、没有冲突的时候——

"德"(那种和谐、滋养、生生不息的力量)就自然而然地回流、汇聚、归拢到这里来。

就像一个没有战争、没有内耗、没有互相伤害的地方——

各种好的东西(人才、资源、信任、生机)都自然地聚过来。

这接前面讲的"德"——

ch51:道生养万物却不占有(玄德)。 ch59:蓄养不透支,就是在积德(重积德)。 ch60:两不相伤,德就交相归来(德交归)。

一路下来:不折腾、不树敌、不伤害——德就自然地聚拢、回流、生生不息。

放到 2026 年看——

一个不折腾、不内耗、不互相伤害的组织——

人才愿意来,信任能建立,生机能生长——各种好的东西自然聚拢(德交归)。

一个天天折腾、处处对抗、互相伤害的组织——

再多的资源也留不住,再好的人才也会走。

不伤害,是让好东西聚拢的前提。

五、给读者的

这一章给读者两个层次的智慧——

第一层:别折腾。

在你管理的任何事情上(团队、项目、家庭、关系)——

你是不是习惯了频繁插手、频繁改动、频繁干预,总觉得"不管就会出问题"?

试试——像煎小鱼一样,别老翻动。

定好方向、搭好环境,然后给它稳定和空间,让它自己运作。

很多时候,你的"不折腾",比你的"折腾",效果好得多。

那条被反复翻动的小鱼会碎,那个被反复折腾的团队/孩子/关系,也会。

第二层:别树敌。

在你与世界相处的方式上——

你是不是习惯了把很多人当对手、把很多事当对抗、处处争斗、时时防备?

那样的话,你浑身都是可被攻击的破绽。

试试——不与人为敌,不把自己摆在对抗的位置上。

不是软弱、不是退让,而是不给那些"能伤你"的力量以着力点。

当你不树敌,很多本来能伤你的东西,就伤不到你了(圣人弗伤)。

这两层,其实是同一个智慧——

别对抗。

对内(治理),别频繁插手对抗事物自己的运作节奏(别折腾); 对外(相处),别把自己摆在与人对抗的位置上(别树敌)。

不对抗,反而——

事情自己运转得好(若烹小鲜); 伤害找不到你(圣人弗伤); 好东西自然聚拢(德交归)。

最后,回到那条小鱼。

煎它的秘诀,不是你多用心地翻动它、照料它、折腾它——

恰恰是:让它安静地煎,不去打扰它。

治大国如此,带团队如此,养孩子如此,过好自己的人生,也如此——

很多时候,最好的作为,是"不折腾"。

别翻动那条小鱼。 别与世界为敌。

然后你会发现——

事情自己会好,伤害找不到你,好的东西,自然会回到你身边。

I. The Wisdom of the Small Fish

Anyone who cooks knows what you must never do when frying a small fish.

Keep flipping it.

Flip it every minute, poke at it, nudge it around — and the small fish quickly falls apart into pieces, a ruined mess.

The right approach: once it's in the pan, let it fry in peace. Don't keep disturbing it. When the heat is right, it's done — whole, beautiful, perfect.

In Chapter 60 of the Daodejing, Laozi takes this small lesson from the kitchen and makes it a great wisdom about governance —

"Governing a great state is like frying a small fish."

Don't keep flipping, don't keep stirring, don't keep meddling.

This sentence has been quoted for over two thousand years. Its core is one thing: don't interfere needlessly.

Governing a state, leading a team, raising a child, managing a relationship — often, the more frequently you meddle, flip, and interfere, the more you ruin things.

This chapter is about —

Why "don't interfere needlessly" is the highest wisdom of governance. And a deeper point: when you don't place yourself in an adversarial position, many things that could have harmed you simply can't.

II. Governing a Great State — Like Frying a Small Fish

The chapter opens —

"Governing a great state is like frying a small fish. When the Dao presides over the world —"

"Like frying a small fish" — the entire art is in "don't keep flipping."

Put the fish in the pan — the more often you flip it, the more it falls apart. Let it fry in peace and it cooks whole.

Governance is the same — the more often you issue new rules, change policies, meddle in details, interfere this way and that, the more the system falls apart. Let it operate stably in a settled environment, and it runs well on its own.

This connects the chain that runs through the preceding chapters —

Ch. 57: the more you control, the more chaos (more prohibitions, the worse it gets). Ch. 58: the looser the governance, the better (dim governance produces wholesome people). Ch. 59: save something, don't burn through (啬). Ch. 60: don't interfere needlessly (like frying a small fish).

One line running through them all: don't over-interfere. Let things operate stably on their own.

"When the Dao presides over the world" — what does "the Dao presiding" mean?

Exactly this approach: not hanging up signs, not forcing things, not stirring up trouble, keeping margin.

Not ruling through power, controlling through rules, managing through stratagems — but like the Dao itself: not interfering, not meddling, letting the world run on its own.

Seen from 2026 —

A good manager knows "like frying a small fish" — sets the direction, builds the environment, then does not constantly meddle, does not chop and change policy, does not interfere in every detail — lets the team, in a stable environment, do the work well on its own.

The manager who changes direction every day, issues new rules every day, pokes at the details every day — breaks the team apart like a repeatedly flipped small fish.

Don't interfere needlessly — this is the highest wisdom of governance.

III. The Things That Could Have Harmed You

The next passage is the more unusual, more profound part of this chapter —

Laozi uses a reference familiar to his contemporaries: ghosts and spirits.

"Is it that the spirits lose their power? Not that they lose their power. Is it that their power does not harm people? Not that their power does not harm people — it is that the sage does not place himself where he can be harmed."

A clarification first —

When Laozi speaks of "ghosts" and "spirits" here, he is using the conceptual framework of his time — the understanding that the world contains unseen forces that may harm people.

We today need not take the "ghosts and spirits" literally — we can understand it as: those latent forces that could potentially harm you.

Whether it's hidden hostility, unexpected blows, others' malice, the capriciousness of fate — those things that could harm you.

Laozi's reasoning is exquisite — three steps —

Step one (acknowledging): those forces do have "spirit-power" — they are genuinely operative. Laozi doesn't deny their existence or capacity.

Step two (acknowledging): those forces are genuinely capable of harming people. Laozi doesn't deny they can cause real harm.

Step three (the key): But "the sage does not place himself where he can be harmed" — the truly enlightened person does not place himself in the position of being reachable by harm.

What does this mean?

Not that those forces have vanished, or that they've suddenly turned benevolent —

But that the truly enlightened person does not make enemies, does not stir up opposition, does not place himself in the position of confrontation and conflict —

So those "forces capable of harming" find no place to gain purchase.

This connects the thread running through earlier chapters —

Ch. 50 (no place of death): those truly skilled at self-preservation don't oppose fierce beasts — they never place themselves where the fierce beasts can reach them. "Rhinoceroses find no place to thrust their horn; tigers find no place to use their claws."

Ch. 55 (the infant): venomous creatures and fierce beasts don't harm the infant — not because the infant has protective magic, but because the infant carries no aggression, is enemy to nothing, so nothing is its enemy.

Ch. 60 is the same principle —

Those forces can harm (acknowledged), but when you don't place yourself in a position of confrontation, don't make enemies, they cannot harm you (the sage is not harmed).

Seen from 2026 —

Someone who makes enemies everywhere, who is in constant combat, who treats everything as confrontation — they are full of places where attack can land. Crises surround them.

Someone who makes no enemies, who does not place himself in an adversarial position — others' hostility finds no purchase; many things that could have harmed him simply can't.

It's not that external threats disappear — it's that you no longer give them a position from which to harm you.

IV. Neither Side Harms — And Virtue Returns

The chapter closes —

"Both sides do not harm each other — therefore virtue (德) flows back to meet them both."

"Both sides do not harm each other" —

Those "harmful forces" cannot harm the sage (because the sage gives them no purchase); the sage does not go out to harm others (the sage never held a "harming" position) — neither side is in a position of harm, so neither harms the other.

"Therefore virtue flows back to meet them both" —

When there is no harm, no opposition, no conflict — virtue (that harmonious, nourishing, generative force) naturally flows back, gathers, and returns here.

Like a place without war, without internal exhaustion, without mutual harm — all good things (talent, resources, trust, vitality) naturally flow in.

This connects the recurring thread of "virtue" (德) —

Ch. 51: the Dao nurtures all things without possessing them (xuan de, dark virtue). Ch. 59: not burning through is accumulating virtue (layered virtue). Ch. 60: neither side harms the other, and virtue flows back to meet them (virtue flows back).

Running through: don't interfere, don't make enemies, don't harm — and virtue naturally gathers and flows, generative and unbroken.

Seen from 2026 —

An organization that doesn't stir things up, doesn't exhaust itself internally, doesn't harm one another — talent wants to come, trust can be built, vitality can grow — all good things naturally gather (virtue flows back).

An organization that stirs things up constantly, is in constant opposition, constantly harms — no amount of resources can be retained; the best people will leave.

Not harming is the prerequisite for good things to gather.

V. To the Reader

This chapter offers two layers of wisdom —

First layer: don't interfere needlessly.

In anything you manage (team, project, family, relationship) —

Are you accustomed to constantly meddling, constantly changing things, constantly intervening, always feeling that "if I don't manage this, something will go wrong"?

Try — like frying a small fish, don't keep flipping it.

Set the direction, build the environment, then give it stability and space — let it run on its own.

Often, your "not interfering" produces far better results than your "interfering."

That repeatedly flipped small fish falls apart. That repeatedly disturbed team, child, relationship — so does it.

Second layer: don't make enemies.

In the way you relate to the world —

Are you accustomed to treating many people as adversaries, treating many things as confrontation, constantly in combat, constantly on guard?

Then you are full of places where attack can land.

Try — don't make enemies; don't place yourself in an adversarial position.

Not weakness, not yielding — but not giving those "forces capable of harming you" a position from which to reach you.

When you stop making enemies, many things that could have harmed you simply can't (the sage is not harmed).

These two layers are the same wisdom —

Don't be adversarial.

Inwardly (governance): don't constantly meddle against the natural rhythm of things (don't interfere); outwardly (relating): don't place yourself against people (don't make enemies).

Without adversarial posture —

Things run well on their own (like frying a small fish); harm can't reach you (the sage is not harmed); good things naturally gather (virtue flows back).

Finally, back to that small fish.

The secret of frying it well is not how attentively you flip it, tend it, and fuss over it —

It is precisely: let it fry in peace. Don't disturb it.

Governing a great state, leading a team, raising a child, living your own life —

Often the best action is "not interfering needlessly."

Don't flip that small fish. Don't make enemies of the world.

Then you will find — things will be fine on their own, harm can't reach you, and good things will naturally return to your side.
