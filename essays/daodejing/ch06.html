第六章
Chapter 6 of 81

溪谷和母亲

The Valley and the Mother

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、玄学化的两千年

老子第六章在说的，其实是一件非常朴素的事——溪谷的水永远在流，母亲永远在生孩子。

但两千年来这一章被读成了一首玄学颂歌。

注家们一看到"谷神"两个字就开始往深奥里跑——什么"谷中央的虚空"、"虚灵之神"、"养神不死之法"——一代比一代解得玄。

老子用的明明是他那个时代每个人每天都能看见的东西。山间的溪谷，村里的母牛。

他不在讲什么神秘的修炼法门。

他在讲一件非常朴素的事：

这件事不会停。

二、浴，不是谷

帛书第六章第一个字是——

浴。

通行本写的是"谷"——"谷神不死"。

这是个重要的字差。

"浴"字从水从谷，本义是"含水的山谷"——一条山谷里有水从中间流出来。这个字字面上就带着水。

"谷"字本义也是"泉出通川为谷"——山间流水之处。两字在"山谷"这一层是通假的。

那字差在哪里？

在于——

"谷"字后世引申出了另一支字义：谷物。五谷的谷。

"浴"字没有这一支。

一字之差，意象立刻发生分歧。

读"谷神不死"——读者可以把"谷"理解成"山谷"，也可以理解成"谷物"。意象散开了。

读"浴神不死"——意象被字本身收紧。只能是水从溪谷里流出来这一支。

整章的字脉因此而紧——

浴神不死：溪谷的水流不会停。
玄牝之门：母体涌出新生命的入口。
绵绵呵其若存：水流连绵不断。
用之不堇：用不尽，水不竭。

全是水和涌出的字脉。一以贯之。

通行本的"谷"字把这条字脉散开了一格。注家们读到"谷"就有人往"谷物丰盛"那一支跑，整章意象一散，第一句到第四句的字脉就接不上。

帛书的"浴"字把字脉收住。

意象明确——一条溪谷里的水从来不停地流。

这就是老子要立的那个画面。

三、玄牝之门，天地之根

接下来一句：

"浴神不死，是谓玄牝。"

溪谷的水流这件事不会停。这就叫"玄牝"。

什么是玄牝？

玄是什么——第一章讲过。玄是凿构循环本身。

牝是雌性。但不是后来文化里"阴柔之美"那种抽象的"阴性原理"——在老子那个时代，牝最朴素的意思就是——

会生孩子的母体。

所以玄牝合起来——

作为循环运作的生育机制。

不是"神秘的阴性原理"。是循环在生育层面的具体呈现。

水从溪谷源源流出。
孩子从母体里生出来。

老子在这里把两件事并到一起。

为什么？

因为它们是同一件事在不同尺度上的运作。

天地尺度上，余项作为不可遏止的涌出，表现为水流。
生物尺度上，余项作为不可遏止的涌出，表现为生育。

不同的尺度，同一的结构。

第四章讲过：道像井，越用越涌。用的是人造物的意象。
第六章讲：道像溪谷，像母体。用的是生命本身的意象。

意象越来越根本。从人造的井到自然的溪谷再到生命的母体——一步一步收到最不可以再收的地方。

接下来一句：

"玄牝之门，是谓天地之根。"

门是什么？是进出之处。

玄牝之门——涌出的入口。

从无到有的那一瞬。大地破土长出谷芽的那一刻，母体涌出新生命的那一刻。

天地之根在哪里？

这一句的字面深度——它把传统宗教意象里"神在天上"、"根在高处"的图景直接翻过来。

老子说：

根不在高处。根就在每一次涌出的那个点。

每一次无中生有的瞬间——那就是天地的根。

这跟第一章那句"有，名万物之母"对得上。母不是高高在上的生产者，母是让发生成为可能的那个位置。

同样——根不是宇宙底部的某个实体，根是每一次涌出之所以能发生的那个结构。

老子不往上看——去找天上的神。
也不往下看——去找宇宙底部的实体。
他往最近的地方看——每一次水从溪谷流出来的那个点，每一次孩子从母体里出来的那个点。

天地之根就在那里。

四、绵绵，不是轰然

下一句：

"绵绵呵其若存。"

绵绵——连绵不断，但很细。像蚕吐丝一样——每一根都很细，但从来不断。

呵其若存——像在又像不在。

这是第四章"湛呵似或存"的同一句话换一个说法。

为什么余项的运作是绵绵的？

因为余项的运作从来不是轰轰烈烈的显现，是细密而持续的在。

溪谷的水流就是这样——你不会看见溪谷在"用力流"，你只会发现水一直在流，从来不停。每一滴水都很细，但绵绵不绝。

母体生育也是这样——从受精到分娩不是一个轰烈的事件，是一个绵绵不断的过程。胚胎一天一天长，每一天都是很小的变化，但永不停下。

涌出的机理向来是绵绵的，不是轰然的。

这跟第四章"湛呵似或存"完全是同一件事——

你专门盯着去找它，找不到。因为它不是一个构，不能被抓。
你不去找它，它就在那里。因为它是一切构的共同条件，永远在运作。

构越紧，余项越逃。
构越松，余项越现。

这一章把这件事换成了水和母体的意象再讲一次。

水你不去截它的时候，它一直在流。
你截住它了——比如修个水坝——水反而停了。

母体你不去干预的时候，它该生就生。
你硬要干预——比如不必要的剖腹产、催产、各种医疗介入——生育的自然节律反而被打断。

"绵绵呵其若存"——细密、持续、不刺眼地在那里。

这是涌出的本然面貌。

五、不竭，不是不费力

最后一句：

"用之不堇。"

堇通"尽"。用之不竭——用不尽。

通行本写的是"用之不勤"——用之不费力。

一字之差。

但字面方向完全不同。

帛书"不堇"——用不竭。字面强调无尽：余项作为涌出机理永远在涌，不会枯竭。
通行本"不勤"——用不费力。字面强调省力：不需要费力气就能用。

两个读法都成立，但字面深度不同。

通行本"不勤"把这一章拉到了"使用技巧"的字脉——好像老子在教你怎么轻松地用某个东西。

帛书"不堇"把这一章固定在"涌出机理"的字脉——老子在讲一个永远不会枯竭的运作。

整章字脉是涌出。

浴神不死——涌出不止。
玄牝之门——涌出的入口。
绵绵呵其若存——涌出的方式：细密、持续。
用之不堇——涌出的机理：不竭。

四句一以贯之。

通行本"不勤"把这条字脉的最后一句换了方向。一换，整章读起来就有点散——前三句讲涌出，最后一句突然讲"用东西不费力"——节奏断了。

帛书"不堇"把这条字脉走完。

为什么涌出会不竭？

因为它不是一个蓄满的容器。

容器越用越少。装一桶水，舀一瓢就少一瓢。

但余项不是容器。余项是过程。只要凿构循环在跑，余项就还在涌。

溪谷明天还会流水。下一个母体还会生育下一个生命。

用不尽不是因为存量大。是因为它根本不是存量，是一个永远在涌出的机理。

六、同一件事再讲一次

第六章是第四章的再演。

第四章用人造物的意象——井——讲余项的不可遏止涌出。
第六章用生命本身的意象——溪谷、母体——讲同一件事。

为什么要再讲一次？

因为意象层次不一样。

井是人造的。井里的水是人挖出来的。读者可能会想——井终究是人造的东西，"用之有弗盈"也许只是一个人造物的特性。

老子把意象收到溪谷和母体——这两个都不是人造的。这是生命本身的运作机理。

读到第六章，读者再没有"也许这只是人造物的特性"的退路。

余项作为不可遏止的涌出——是天地本然，是生命本然。

不在人造之外。也不在人造之中。

就是这世界运作的样子。

第六章其实没说任何新的东西。它把第四章的命题用另一组意象再讲一次。

为什么要这样？

因为人读书的时候，抽象命题读一次容易滑过去。但意象层的转换会让读者重新看到——

哦，这是同一件事。
哦，这是另一件事的同一件事。
哦，原来一切都是这一件事。

道写成井，是这件事。
道写成溪谷，是这件事。
道写成母体，是这件事。

到了第六章读完，读者已经看见不同尺度上的同一件事了。

接下来的章节会继续这样——用越来越多的不同意象把同一件事再讲。每讲一次，读者就再看一次。看见的人会更清楚那是同一件事；看不见的人会读到很多漂亮的比喻。

老子不强调，不解释，不论证。

他只是反复用不同的意象把同一件事铺出来。

绵绵呵其若存。

用之不堇。

I. Two Thousand Years of Metaphysics

What Chapter Six is talking about is actually something very simple — water in a valley stream never stops flowing; a mother never stops giving birth.

But for two thousand years this chapter has been read as a hymn to the metaphysical.

The moment commentators see the two characters "valley spirit," they race toward profundity — "the void at the center of the valley," "the numinous spirit of emptiness," "the method for nourishing the spirit and not dying" — each generation's reading more mystical than the last.

What Laozi was using was something every person of his era saw every day. The mountain valley stream. The village cow.

He was not expounding any mysterious cultivation method.

He was saying something very plain:

This does not stop.

II. "Yu," Not "Gu"

The first character of Chapter Six in the silk manuscript is —

浴 (yù) — "valley with water flowing through it."

The received text has 谷 (gǔ) — "valley spirit does not die."

This is a significant character difference.

The character 浴 combines "water" and "valley" — it means "a mountain valley with water running through it." The character carries water in it literally.

The character 谷 also originally meant "a place where spring water flows out through a valley" — "valley as a channel of water." The two characters overlap in the "mountain valley" meaning.

So where is the difference?

The character 谷 later developed a second meaning: grain. The 谷 in "five grains."

The character 浴 has no such branch.

One character, and the imagery immediately diverges.

Read "valley spirit does not die" — readers can understand 谷 as "valley" or as "grain." The image scatters.

Read "yù-spirit does not die" — the character itself constrains the image. Only one branch: water flowing out of the valley stream.

The chapter's word-thread therefore stays tight throughout —

Valley-spirit does not die: the water flow of the valley stream never stops.
The gate of the dark female: the opening from which new life surges from the mother.
Continuous — as though present: the water flows unbroken.
Used, it is not exhausted: used and used, the water does not run dry.

All water and surging. One continuous thread.

The received text's 谷 loosens this thread by one degree. Commentators reading 谷 often run toward the "grain abundance" branch, and once the imagery scatters, the thread from line one to line four breaks.

The silk manuscript's 浴 holds the thread.

The image is clear — water in a valley stream that never stops flowing.

That is the image Laozi is establishing.

III. The Gate of the Dark Female; the Root of Heaven and Earth

The next line:

"The valley-spirit does not die — this is called the dark female (xuán pìn)."

The water flow of the valley stream does not stop. That is called the dark female.

What is xuán pìn?

Xuán — Chapter One explained this. Xuán is the chisel-construct cycle itself.

Pìn is female. But not the abstract "feminine principle" of later culture — in Laozi's era, pìn most plainly meant —

The maternal body that gives birth.

So xuán pìn together means —

The birthing mechanism as cyclical operation.

Not a "mysterious feminine principle." It is the concrete manifestation of the cycle at the level of birth.

Water surges continuously from the valley.
A child surges out of the maternal body.

Laozi brings these two things together here.

Why?

Because they are the same thing operating at different scales.

At the scale of heaven and earth: the remainder as unstoppable surging appears as water flow.
At the biological scale: the remainder as unstoppable surging appears as birth.

Different scales, same structure.

Chapter Four: the Dao is like a well — an artifact. Chapter Six: the Dao is like a valley stream, like a maternal body — the imagery of life itself.

The imagery gets progressively more fundamental. From the human-made well to the natural valley to the living maternal body — stepping closer and closer to what cannot be stepped closer to.

The next line:

"The gate of the dark female — this is called the root of heaven and earth."

A gate is a place of passage in and out.

The gate of the dark female — the opening from which surging emerges.

The instant of going from nothing to something. The moment earth breaks open and a sprout appears. The moment new life surges out from the maternal body.

Where is the root of heaven and earth?

This line's literal depth — it flips the traditional religious image of "God is above," "the root is in the heights."

Laozi says:

The root is not above. The root is at each point of surging.

Every instant of something from nothing — that is the root of heaven and earth.

This corresponds to Chapter One: "being: the naming of the mother of the ten thousand things." The mother is not a producer on high; the mother is the position that makes production possible.

Similarly — the root is not some entity at the bottom of the cosmos. The root is the structure that makes each surging possible.

Laozi does not look upward — to find God above.
Nor downward — to find some cosmic substrate below.
He looks at what is nearest — each point where water flows out from the valley, each point where a child comes out from the maternal body.

The root of heaven and earth is right there.

IV. Continuous — Not Thunderous

The next line:

"Continuous — as though present."

Continuous — unbroken but very fine. Like silk being spun — each thread very fine, but never severing.

"As though present" — as though there, as though not.

This is Chapter Four's "limpid — as though it were there or not" restated in different words.

Why is the remainder's operation continuous rather than thunderous?

Because the remainder never manifests dramatically — it is a fine, persistent presence.

The valley stream is exactly like this — you never see the valley "working hard to flow." You simply discover that water is always flowing, never stopping. Each drop is fine, but the continuity never breaks.

Birth is the same — from fertilization to delivery is not a dramatic event but a continuously unfolding process. The embryo grows day by day; each day the change is tiny, but it never stops.

The mechanism of surging is always continuous — never thunderous.

This is exactly the same thing as Chapter Four's "limpid — as though it were there or not" —

If you fix your gaze on it, you can't find it. Because it's not a construct — it can't be seized.
If you're not looking for it, it's there. Because it is the common condition of all constructs, always operating.

The tighter the construct, the more the remainder retreats.
The looser the construct, the more the remainder appears.

This chapter restates that with water and mother imagery.

When you're not damming the water, it flows continuously.
Dam it — build a dam — and the water stops.

When you're not interfering with the maternal body, birth happens when it should.
Interfere — unnecessary cesareans, induced labor, all manner of medical intervention — and the natural rhythm of birth gets disrupted.

"Continuous — as though present" — fine, persistent, unpretentious presence.

That is the natural face of surging.

V. Inexhaustible — Not Effortless

The final line:

"Used, it is not exhausted."

The silk manuscript uses a character meaning "exhausted." Used and not exhausted — inexhaustible.

The received text has "used, it is not laborious." One character's difference.

But completely different directions.

Silk manuscript "not exhausted" — the literal emphasizes inexhaustibility: the remainder as a surging mechanism keeps surging forever, never runs dry.
Received text "not laborious" — the literal emphasizes ease: doesn't take effort to use.

Both readings hold, but differ in depth.

The received text's "not laborious" pulls the chapter toward a "technique of use" thread — as if Laozi is teaching you how to use something easily.

The silk manuscript's "not exhausted" anchors the chapter in the "surging mechanism" thread — Laozi is talking about an operation that will never run dry.

The chapter's entire word-thread is surging.

Valley-spirit does not die — surging does not stop.
The gate of the dark female — the opening of surging.
Continuous, as though present — the mode of surging: fine, persistent.
Used, not exhausted — the mechanism of surging: inexhaustible.

Four lines, one continuous thread.

The received text's "not laborious" changes direction in the last line. Change it and the chapter loses rhythm — first three lines about surging, last line suddenly about "using something without effort." The thread breaks.

The silk manuscript's "not exhausted" carries the thread to completion.

Why is surging inexhaustible?

Because it is not a filled vessel.

A vessel diminishes with use. Fill a bucket with water; scoop one ladle and you have one less ladle's worth.

But the remainder is not a vessel. The remainder is a process. As long as the chisel-construct cycle runs, the remainder keeps surging.

Tomorrow the valley will still flow with water. The next maternal body will still birth the next life.

Inexhaustible not because the stock is large — but because it isn't stock at all. It is a mechanism of perpetual surging.

VI. The Same Thing Told Again

Chapter Six is a re-demonstration of Chapter Four.

Chapter Four used an artifact image — a well — to talk about the remainder's unstoppable surging.
Chapter Six uses the imagery of life itself — valley stream, maternal body — to talk about the same thing.

Why tell it again?

Because the level of imagery is different.

A well is human-made. The water in a well was put there by human digging. A reader might think — after all, a well is a human artifact; "used repeatedly, never fills" might just be a property of human-made things.

Laozi moves the imagery to valley streams and maternal bodies — neither of which is human-made. This is the operating mechanism of life itself.

By Chapter Six, the reader has no retreat to "perhaps this is just a property of artifacts."

The remainder as unstoppable surging — is the natural state of heaven and earth, the natural state of life.

Not outside the human-made. Not inside it either.

This is simply how the world operates.

Chapter Six says nothing new. It takes Chapter Four's proposition and tells it again with a different set of images.

Why do this?

Because when reading, abstract propositions read once tend to slide by. But shifting the imagery makes the reader see again —

Oh, this is the same thing.
Oh, that other thing is the same thing too.
Oh, everything is this one thing.

The Dao written as a well: this thing.
The Dao written as a valley: this thing.
The Dao written as a maternal body: this thing.

Finishing Chapter Six, the reader has now seen the same thing at different scales.

The chapters ahead will continue in this way — using more and more different images to tell the same thing again. Each telling is another look. Those who see will see more clearly that it's the same thing; those who don't will encounter many beautiful comparisons.

Laozi does not emphasize, does not explain, does not argue.

He simply lays the same thing out again and again in different images.

Continuous — as though present.

Used, not exhausted.
