# 越管，越乱

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 5 月

一、规则越多，问题越多

有一个几乎所有组织都遇到过的怪圈——

出了一个问题,加一条规则。 又出一个问题,再加一条规则。 规则越来越多,流程越来越复杂,禁令越来越密——

但问题不但没少,反而越来越多。

甚至,很多新问题,恰恰是那些规则自己制造出来的。

老子在《道德经》第五十七章里,把这个怪圈讲到了根上——

你立的禁忌越多,人反而越穷困; 法令越森严,盗贼反而越多。

不是规则不够多,是规则本身,正在制造它想解决的问题。

然后老子给出了那个反直觉的答案——

最好的治理,不是管得更多,是撤掉那些限制,让人自己长。

这一章讲的是——

为什么"越管越乱",以及真正高明的管理是什么样的。

二、得天下，靠的不是正，也不是奇

第五十七章开头——

"以正治邦,以奇用兵,以无事取天下。"

用"正"(规矩、秩序)来治理邦国,用"奇"(出其不意)来用兵打仗,用"无事"(不折腾)来取得天下。

这里有三个层次,一层比一层高——

以正治邦——治理一个邦国,靠"正":立规矩、立秩序、按常规来。

以奇用兵——打仗用兵,靠"奇":出其不意、不按常理、反常规。

以无事取天下——但真正要"取天下"(赢得人心、成就大局),靠的既不是"正",也不是"奇",而是"无事"。

什么是"无事"?

"无事"就是不折腾、不立一堆招牌、不靠强力去抓。

"正"是立规矩的招牌,"奇"是用计谋的招牌——这两个都还在"立招牌、用手段"的层面。

而"取天下"这件最大的事,恰恰不能靠立招牌、用手段——

越是想用力去"取",越取不到(这接前面 ch29 讲过的:想强行取天下的,反而得不到)。

真正得人心、成大局,靠的是"无事"——不折腾,让一切自然运转。

放到 2026 年看——

管一个小团队,也许靠规矩(正)能管好。

打一场硬仗、抢一个市场,也许靠奇招(奇)能赢。

但要真正赢得人心、建立一个能长久的事业、让一群人真心追随你——

靠的不是更多规矩,也不是更多奇招,是"无事"——不折腾、不过度干预、让人在一个宽松的环境里自然地投入。

三、你越禁止，越制造

接着老子自问——

"吾何以知其然哉?"

我怎么知道是这样的呢?

然后他给出四条,条条都是那个怪圈——

"夫天下多忌讳而民弥贫,人多利器而国家滋昏,民多伎能而奇物滋起,法物滋彰而盗贼多有。"

天下的禁忌越多,百姓越穷困;人手里的利器越多,国家越混乱;人的巧技越多,稀奇古怪的东西越层出不穷;法令、器物越森严鲜明,盗贼反而越多。

这四条,说的是同一个结构——

你越立招牌去禁止、去管控,反而越制造出你想禁止的东西。

多忌讳 → 民弥贫:禁忌、限制越多,百姓被捆得越死,越没法自由地生产生活,反而越穷。

多利器 → 国家滋昏:争权夺利的手段、工具越多,国家越混乱。

多伎能 → 奇物滋起:越是追逐巧技、花样,各种乱七八糟的东西越冒出来。

法物滋彰 → 盗贼多有:法令越严苛、越把"什么值钱、什么禁止"标得越清楚,反而越刺激出盗贼。

这里有一个极其重要的点——

老子不是在做道德批判(说"立禁忌是坏的")。

他是在陈述一个结构因果——立招牌这个动作本身,就在制造它想禁止的东西。

为什么?

你立一个"禁止"的招牌,就等于在所有人面前标出了那个东西——

法令越把"偷窃"标得清清楚楚、把"什么值钱"标得明明白白,反而越是在提示人们"这里有值得偷的东西"。

禁忌越多,人越被框住、越动辄得咎,反而越活不好。

你想禁止什么,就先在所有人眼前立了一个那个东西的招牌——这个招牌本身,就在制造那个东西。

放到 2026 年看——

一个公司规则越多,员工越是把心思花在"钻规则空子"上,而不是把事情做好。

一个平台限制越多,用户越是想方设法绕过限制。

一个家庭禁令越多,孩子越是对那些被禁的东西充满好奇和逆反。

你越禁止,越制造。这不是道德问题,是结构规律。

四、撤掉限制，人自己会长好

讲完了"越管越乱"(反面),老子给出正面的答案——

"是以圣人之言曰:我无为也而民自化,我好静而民自正,我无事而民自富,我欲无欲而民自朴。"

所以圣人说:我不折腾(无为),百姓自己就化育了;我保持安静(好静),百姓自己就端正了;我不生事(无事),百姓自己就富足了;我不起贪欲(欲无欲),百姓自己就淳朴了。

这四句,是这一章的核心,也是老子对"最高管理"的终极描述——

我无为 → 民自化:我不去强行塑造、不折腾,百姓反而自己成长、自己化育。

我好静 → 民自正:我保持安静、不瞎指挥,百姓反而自己走上正轨。

我无事 → 民自富:我不生出一堆事、不过度干预,百姓反而自己富足起来。

我欲无欲 → 民自朴:我不起贪欲、不带头去追逐,百姓反而自己回归淳朴。

这里最关键的一点是——

不是圣人去"化"百姓、去"正"百姓、去"富"百姓、去"朴"百姓——

是圣人"撤掉"了那些限制、招牌、干预之后,百姓自己化、自己正、自己富、自己朴。

圣人做的,不是"更用力地管",是"撤掉那些多余的管"——

然后,把空间还给人,人自己就长好了。

其中"我欲无欲"要特别说一下——

"欲无欲"不是"我没有任何欲望"(那是压抑)——

是"我唯一的'欲',就是'不立那些贪欲的招牌'"。

不是灭掉欲望,是不带头去追逐、不把'想要更多'立成风向标。

领导不带头贪求,下面的人自然就淳朴了;领导带头追逐名利,下面的人自然也跟着争抢。

放到 2026 年看——

最好的管理者:不是事事都管、时时都盯,是搭好环境、定好大方向,然后放手——让团队自己发挥、自己成长(我无为而民自化)。

最好的父母:不是处处限制、时时纠正,是托住底、保持稳定,然后给孩子空间——让孩子自己探索、自己长(我好静而民自正)。

最好的平台:不是用一堆规则把用户框死,是提供好的基础,然后让生态自己生长(我无事而民自富)。

最好的带头人:不是带头追逐名利,而是自己不贪、不炫、不折腾——下面的人自然也就踏实淳朴(我欲无欲而民自朴)。

撤掉多余的限制,把空间还给人——人自己会长好。

五、圣人说的那个"我"，不一样

这里有一个很微妙、但很重要的地方——

老子在前面自问的时候,用的是"吾"("吾何以知其然哉")。

到了圣人自陈的时候,却连用了四个"我"("我无为""我好静""我无事""我欲无欲")。

这个"我",和我们平常那个"我",不一样。

我们平常说"我",往往带着一个立招牌的自己——

"我"要证明自己、"我"要抓住什么、"我"要显得厉害、"我"要别人认可。

这个"我",是处处想彰显、想占有、想被看见的"我"。

而圣人说的"我无为""我好静",这个"我"已经不一样了——

它不再是那个要彰显、要抓取、要立招牌的"我"。

圣人说"我无为",不是"我要通过无为来显得高明"——

而是这个"我"已经放下了所有想彰显、想占有的冲动,回到了一个安静的、不折腾的、把空间让给别人的状态。

换句话说——

真正高明的人,不是消灭那个"我"(变得没有自我)——

是那个"我"已经不再需要通过抓取、彰显、立招牌来证明自己了。

他还是"我",还在做事,但这个"我"是安静的、放松的、不与人争的——

所以他能"无为""好静""无事""欲无欲",从而让身边的人自己长好。

放到 2026 年看——

一个还需要靠职位、靠成就、靠别人的认可来证明自己的领导,忍不住要处处彰显、处处掌控(那个立招牌的"我")。

而一个真正成熟的领导,内在是稳的——他不需要靠彰显和掌控来证明自己(那个安静的"我")——

所以他反而敢放手、敢无为、敢把舞台让给别人。

能不能"无为",取决于你那个"我",还需不需要靠抓取来证明自己。

六、给读者的

这一章给每一个管理者、父母、带头人一个反直觉、却极其重要的提醒——

你想解决的很多问题,可能正是你"管得太多"制造出来的。

具体来说——

第一:检查你是不是在"越管越乱"。

你是不是每遇到一个问题,就加一条规则、一道限制、一个流程?

结果规则越来越多,问题却没见少?

试试——减掉一些规则,而不是增加。

很多问题,恰恰是限制本身制造的。撤掉多余的限制,问题反而消失了。

第二:把空间还给人。

你是不是习惯了事事都管、处处都盯,生怕一放手就出乱子?

试试——托住底(定好大方向、给足基础),然后放手。

人在一个宽松、被信任的环境里,会自己长好(民自化、自正、自富)。

你越是把人框死,人越长不出自己的判断和能力。

第三:自己先不折腾、不贪求。

你是不是带头追逐、带头折腾、带头彰显?

记住"我欲无欲而民自朴"——带头人自己安静、不贪、不折腾,下面的人自然踏实淳朴。

你自己是什么状态,比你定多少规矩,影响大得多。

这一章的核心,是一个特别深的管理智慧——

最高的"管",是"不管"(无为);最高的"作为",是"不折腾"(无事)。

不是撒手不管、放任自流——

而是托住底,撤掉多余的限制,把空间和信任还给人——然后,让人自己长好。

你做得越少(撤掉多余的干预),人反而长得越好(自化、自正、自富、自朴)。

最后,回到开头那个怪圈:规则越多,问题越多。

老子两千四百年前就看穿了——

你越立招牌去禁止,越制造出你想禁止的东西。

真正的答案,不是更多的规则、更严的限制、更用力的管控——

是撤掉那些多余的限制,把空间还给人。

我无为,而民自化。 我好静,而民自正。 我无事,而民自富。 我欲无欲,而民自朴。

撤掉限制,人自己会长好。

这,是老子留给每一个"想管好一群人"的人的,最深的一课。

I. More Rules, More Problems

Nearly every organization has encountered this trap —

A problem arises: add a rule. Another problem: add another rule. Rules multiply, procedures grow more complex, prohibitions grow denser —

And yet the problems don't diminish. They multiply.

Many of the new problems are created by the rules themselves.

In Chapter 57 of the Daodejing, Laozi gets at the root of this trap —

The more prohibitions you establish, the poorer the people become. The more elaborate the laws, the more thieves appear.

It is not that the rules aren't numerous enough. It is that the rules themselves are generating the very problems they aim to solve.

Then Laozi offers the counterintuitive answer —

The best governance is not more management, but removing those constraints and letting people grow on their own.

This chapter is about —

Why "the more you control, the more chaos" — and what genuinely skillful management looks like.

II. Winning the World Belongs to Neither "Correct" Nor "Clever"

The chapter opens —

"Govern the state with correctness (正). Wage war with surprise (奇). Win the world with non-interference (無事)."

Three levels here, each surpassing the last —

Govern the state with correctness — running a state requires 正: establishing order, following convention.

Wage war with surprise — military action requires 奇: the unexpected, the unconventional.

Win the world with non-interference — but truly "winning the world" (winning hearts and minds, achieving the great work) requires neither 正 nor 奇, but 無事: not stirring things up.

What is 無事?

Not stirring things up — not hanging out signs and banners, not grasping with force.

"Correctness" is the banner of establishing rules; "surprise" is the banner of using stratagems — both still operate at the level of "hanging banners and using methods."

But the greatest thing — winning the world — cannot be achieved by banners and methods —

The harder you try to "seize" it, the more it slips away (connecting ch. 29: those who try to forcibly take the world under heaven cannot succeed).

Truly winning hearts and achieving lasting work requires 無事 — not stirring things up, letting everything run naturally.

Seen from 2026 —

Managing a small team might work with rules (正). Winning a hard fight or capturing a market might work with clever moves (奇). But truly winning people's hearts, building an enduring enterprise, making a group of people genuinely committed —

That requires 無事 — not stirring things up, not over-interfering, letting people engage naturally in a spacious environment.

III. Whatever You Prohibit, You Create

Laozi then asks himself —

"How do I know this is so?"

He offers four lines, each one the same trap —

"The more taboos there are under heaven, the poorer the people become. The more sharp weapons people have, the more confused the state grows. The more cunning skills people have, the more strange things appear. The more laws and decrees are made prominent, the more thieves and brigands there are."

All four describe the same structure —

The more you hang up banners of prohibition and control, the more you generate what you're trying to prohibit.

More taboos → people grow poorer: the more prohibitions and restrictions, the more people are tied down, the less able they are to produce and live freely — so they grow poorer.

More sharp weapons → the state grows confused: the more instruments and tools for gaining power, the more the state descends into chaos.

More cunning skills → strange things multiply: the more people chase clever techniques and novelties, the more bizarre things erupt.

More laws prominently displayed → more thieves: the more strictly the law spells out "what is forbidden, what is valuable," the more it stimulates theft.

Here is an extremely important point —

Laozi is not making a moral judgment (saying "establishing prohibitions is bad").

He is stating a structural causality — the act of hanging up a banner of prohibition itself generates what it wants to prohibit.

Why?

When you post a "forbidden" sign, you are marking that thing in front of everyone —

The more clearly the law spells out "theft is forbidden" and "here is what is valuable," the more it is pointing out to everyone: "there are things worth stealing here."

Whatever you want to prohibit, you first erect its banner in everyone's sight — and that banner itself generates the thing.

Seen from 2026 —

A company with more rules has employees spending energy finding loopholes, not doing the work. A platform with more restrictions has users constantly finding ways around them. A household with more prohibitions has children more curious about and rebellious toward exactly what's forbidden.

The more you prohibit, the more you create. This isn't a moral problem — it's a structural law.

IV. Remove the Constraints — People Will Grow on Their Own

After "the more you control, the more chaos" (the negative), Laozi gives the positive answer —

"Therefore the sage says: I do not interfere (wu wei), and the people transform themselves; I love stillness, and the people correct themselves; I do not stir things up (wu shi), and the people enrich themselves; I desire non-desire, and the people return to simplicity on their own."

These four lines are this chapter's core — Laozi's ultimate description of the highest governance —

I don't interfere → people transform themselves: I don't forcibly shape things, don't stir them up — the people grow and transform on their own.

I love stillness → people correct themselves: I stay still, don't issue confused directives — the people find their own right course.

I don't stir things up → people enrich themselves: I don't generate a heap of busywork, don't over-interfere — the people enrich themselves.

I desire non-desire → people return to simplicity: I don't stir up cravings, don't lead the way in chasing things — the people return to simplicity on their own.

The most critical point —

The sage does not "transform," "correct," "enrich," "simplify" the people —

After the sage "removes" those constraints, banners, and interferences — the people transform, correct, enrich, and simplify themselves.

What the sage does is not "govern more energetically" — it is "remove the surplus governing" —

Then return the space to the people, and the people grow well on their own.

A note on "I desire non-desire" —

"Desiring non-desire" is not "having no desires at all" (that would be suppression) —

It is "my only 'desire' is not to erect the banner of craving."

Not extinguishing desire — just not leading the charge in pursuit, not making "wanting more" the prevailing standard.

When a leader doesn't lead in craving, those below naturally grow simple; when a leader leads in chasing fame and gain, those below naturally follow in competing.

Seen from 2026 —

The best managers don't micromanage — they set the direction, build the environment, then step back: let the team perform and grow on its own (I don't interfere, the people transform themselves).

The best parents don't restrict everything — they hold the floor, stay stable, then give the child space to explore and grow (I love stillness, the people correct themselves).

The best platforms don't cage users with rules — they provide good foundations and let the ecosystem grow naturally (I don't stir things up, the people enrich themselves).

The best leaders don't chase fame and gain — they themselves don't crave, don't flaunt, don't stir things up — and those around them naturally settle into simplicity (I desire non-desire, the people return to simplicity).

Remove the surplus constraints. Return the space to people. People will grow well on their own.

V. That "I" Is Different

There is something subtle but important here —

When Laozi asks his own question earlier, he uses "吾" ("How do I know this is so?"). But when the sage speaks, he uses four instances of "我" — "I do not interfere," "I love stillness," "I do not stir things up," "I desire non-desire."

This "I" is different from our ordinary "I."

Our ordinary "I" usually comes with a sign-hanging self — "I" must prove myself, "I" must grasp something, "I" must seem capable, "I" must be recognized.

This "I" is the self that constantly wants to broadcast, possess, be seen.

But the sage's "I do not interfere," "I love stillness" — this "I" is already different —

It is no longer the "I" that must broadcast, grasp, and hang signs.

The sage says "I do not interfere" — not meaning "I will achieve greatness through non-interference" — but that this "I" has already released all impulse to broadcast and possess, returned to a quiet, unagitated state that gives space to others.

In other words —

The truly skillful person does not eliminate that "I" (become selfless) —

Rather, that "I" no longer needs to prove itself through grasping, broadcasting, and hanging signs.

The "I" is still there, still doing things — but this "I" is quiet, relaxed, not contending with others —

So it can "not interfere," "love stillness," "not stir things up," "desire non-desire" — and thereby let those around it grow well.

VI. To the Reader

This chapter gives every manager, parent, and leader a counterintuitive yet crucial reminder —

Many of the problems you want to solve may be created by "managing too much."

First: check whether you are in the "more control, more chaos" trap.

Every time a problem arises, do you add a rule, a restriction, a procedure?

And the rules keep growing, but the problems don't shrink?

Try — reduce rules, rather than add them. Many problems are created by the constraints themselves. Remove the surplus constraints and the problems often disappear.

Second: return the space to people.

Are you habituated to managing everything, monitoring everything, afraid that the moment you let go something will go wrong?

Try — hold the floor (set the direction, provide the foundations), then step back.

People in a spacious, trusted environment will grow well on their own. The more you cage people, the less they develop their own judgment and capacity.

Third: you yourself — first stop stirring things up, stop craving.

Are you leading the charge in chasing, in agitation, in broadcasting?

Remember "I desire non-desire, the people return to simplicity" — when the leader is quiet, non-craving, unagitated, those around them naturally settle into simplicity.

What you yourself are is far more influential than how many rules you set.

This chapter's core is a profoundly deep wisdom about management —

The highest "governing" is "not governing" (wu wei); the highest "action" is "not stirring things up" (wu shi).

Not hands-off abandonment — but holding the floor, removing surplus interference, returning space and trust to people — and then letting people grow well.

The less you do (removing surplus interference), the better people grow (transforming, correcting, enriching, simplifying on their own).

Finally, back to the opening trap: more rules, more problems.

Laozi saw through it 2,400 years ago —

The more you hang up banners of prohibition, the more you generate what you want to prohibit.

The real answer is not more rules, stricter restrictions, more forceful control —

It is removing the surplus constraints and returning the space to people.

I do not interfere — and the people transform themselves.
I love stillness — and the people correct themselves.
I do not stir things up — and the people enrich themselves.
I desire non-desire — and the people return to simplicity.

Remove the constraints. People will grow well on their own.

This is the deepest lesson Laozi left for everyone who wants to "govern a group of people well."
