秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

# 婴儿的力量

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 5 月

一、婴儿的握力

你有没有把手指伸给一个刚出生的婴儿?

他会用小手紧紧攥住你的手指,力气大得惊人——你甚至能感觉到,好像可以把他整个提起来。

奇怪的是——

这个浑身软绵绵、骨头还没长硬、连头都抬不起来的小家伙,握力却那么牢。

老子在《道德经》第五十五章里,专门讲了这件事——

德最深厚的状态,就像婴儿一样:最柔软,却最有力量。

我们总以为力量来自强壮、来自紧绷、来自用力——

老子说反过来:最柔软、最放松、最不用力的婴儿,反而有一种我们成年人失去了的、最本真的生命力。

这一章讲的是——

为什么柔软放松反而更有力量。以及,为什么越用力、越想使劲,反而衰败得越快。

二、德最厚的人，像个婴儿

第五十五章开头——

"含德之厚者,比于赤子。"

德最深厚的人,像刚出生的婴儿。

"含德之厚"——把德深深怀在里面,不外露、不张扬。

注意是"含"——含在里面,不是挂在外面。

真正德厚的人,不把"德"挂出来展示(接前面讲的"上德不德")。

他的德,像婴儿一样,是浑然天成、不加修饰的。

"比于赤子"——像刚出生的婴儿。

为什么用婴儿作比?

因为婴儿是最本真、最自然、最没有被后天的"想法、欲望、用力"扭曲的状态。

婴儿不装、不争、不用力表现自己、不立任何招牌——

他就是他本来的样子。

德最厚的人,就是回到了这种本真状态。

放到 2026 年看——

我们成年人,身上背了太多东西——身份、面子、算计、焦虑、"我必须表现得怎样"——

而婴儿什么都没有,他只是自然地存在着。

老子说:德最厚的状态,不是往身上加更多,是卸掉这些,回到婴儿那种本真、自然、不用力的状态。

三、最柔软，却最牢固

接下来老子描述婴儿的状态——

"蜂虿虺蛇弗蜇,攫鸟猛兽弗搏,骨弱筋柔而握固。"

蜂、蝎、毒蛇不蜇他,凶猛的鸟兽不抓他,骨头软、筋肉柔,但握东西却很牢固。

先看"骨弱筋柔而握固"——

婴儿骨头是软的、筋肉是柔的(最柔软的状态)——

但他握东西却出奇地牢固。

这就是老子要点出的关键——

柔软,不等于没力量。恰恰相反,最柔软的状态,有一种最本真、最牢固的力量。

不是靠肌肉紧绷、靠用力、靠使劲——

是一种放松的、自然的、却异常牢固的力量。

再看"蜂虿弗蜇,攫鸟弗搏"——毒虫猛兽都不伤害他。

为什么?

不是婴儿有什么护身法术。

是因为——婴儿不带任何攻击性,不在任何争斗的位置上。

他不挑衅、不威胁、不与任何东西为敌——

所以也没有什么与他为敌。

这接前面讲过的(ch50)——真正善于保全自己的人,不是能对抗危险,是根本不把自己放在危险的对立面上。

婴儿就是这样——他不树敌,所以无敌。

放到 2026 年看——

关于"柔软的力量":

真正有力量的人,不是时刻紧绷、时刻用力、时刻剑拔弩张的人——

是那种放松、柔和,却有一种内在稳定力量的人。

紧绷是消耗,柔软才持久。

关于"不树敌所以无敌":

那些四处树敌、时刻防备、把所有人当对手的人,反而危机四伏——

而那些不带攻击性、不与人为敌的人,反而少有人与他为敌。

柔软、不树敌——这是婴儿教给我们的力量。

四、不用力，反而到极致

老子继续描述婴儿——

"未知牝牡之合而朘作,精之至也;终日号而不嚘,和之至也。"

还不懂男女之事,小生殖器却会自然勃起,这是"精"到了极致;整天号哭却不气逆(嗓子不哑、气不噎),这是"和"到了极致。

这两个例子,说的是同一件事——

婴儿的一切,都是自然发生的,没有一点"刻意用力"。

朘作(生殖器的自然反应)——不是因为他"想",是纯粹自然的生命力。这是"精"最纯粹的状态——没有被任何欲望、念头扭曲的、本真的生命力。

终日号而不嚘(整天哭也不气逆)——婴儿可以哭一整天,嗓子不哑、气不噎。为什么?因为他哭得完全自然、完全放松,没有一点刻意用力、没有一点憋着较劲。这是"和"最极致的状态——完全自然、完全放松的和谐。

这里的关键是——

婴儿达到"精之至""和之至",恰恰是因为他"不用力、不刻意、不较劲"。

成年人越想使劲,越紧绷,越较劲——反而达不到那种自然的极致。

最高的状态,是自然的,不是使劲够来的。

放到 2026 年看——

你有没有发现——

越想睡着,越睡不着(越使劲越糟); 越想表现好,越紧张出错; 越刻意讨好,越显得别扭。

而那些最好的状态,往往是在你放松、不较劲、顺其自然的时候发生的。

这就是婴儿的"精之至、和之至"——最高的状态,来自最自然的放松,不是来自最用力的较劲。

五、和，就是常道

接下来一句——

"和曰常,知常曰明。"

"和"这种状态,就是"常"(恒常的道);知道了"常",就是"明"(真正的明白)。

"和曰常"——婴儿那种完全自然、放松、和谐的状态,本身就是"常"(恒常的道)。

不需要额外做什么、加什么、证明什么——

那个自然和谐的状态本身,就是道。

"知常曰明"——认识到这个"常",就是真正的明白。

真正的明白,不是知道很多、懂很多道理——

是认识到那个自然、和谐、本真的状态,就是根本。

这接前面几章反复讲的"明"(ch16、ch52)——

真正的"明",是看见那个贯穿一切的、恒常的根本。

而这一章告诉我们:那个根本,就是婴儿身上那种自然、和谐、不用力的状态。

六、越想多活，越用力，越糟

讲完婴儿(正面),老子紧接着讲反面——

"益生曰祥,心使气曰强。物壮则老,谓之不道,不道早亡。"

刻意去"增益生命"反而是灾祸的征兆,用心去逼迫气(强行运作)就是"强为"。事物一旦壮到极致就开始衰老,这叫"不合道",不合道的东西早早就完了。

这两句要点清古义,否则会读反——

"益生曰祥"——这里的"祥"不是"吉祥",是"妖祥"(反常的、不好的征兆)。

"益生"就是刻意地、拼命地想让生命更长、更旺、更多。

老子说:这种"太想多活"的刻意,反而是灾祸的征兆。

这接前面讲过的(ch50)——太执着于"活"(生生),反而把自己搞坏了。

"心使气曰强"——这里的"强"不是"强壮",是"强为"(用力过度、违背自然)。

"心使气"就是用自己的意志、念头,去强行逼迫、驱使身体的自然之气。

老子说:这种"用意志强行逼迫"就是"强为"——违背自然的用力。

和婴儿正好相反:婴儿是"不用力、顺自然",而"心使气"是"用意志硬逼"。

然后是那句反复出现的警告——

"物壮则老,谓之不道,不道早亡。"

事物壮到极致,就开始衰老。这叫不合道。不合道的东西,早早就完了。

为什么?

因为——

"壮到极致"就是那种"用力、紧绷、刻意增益"的状态推到了顶点。

而任何东西推到顶点(壮极),接下来就是衰败(则老)——

这接前面反复讲的(ch30、ch40、ch42):推到极致必然反转,又强又硬的走不到最后。

这一章的正反对照,特别清楚——

婴儿(正面):柔软、放松、自然、不用力——精之至、和之至——这是"常"(合道),有最本真的生命力。

壮极(反面):紧绷、用力、刻意增益、强行逼迫——物壮则老——这是"不道",早早衰亡。

柔软放松的婴儿,反而生命力最旺;紧绷用力的壮极,反而衰败最快。

七、给读者的

这一章给读者一个特别反直觉、却特别重要的提醒——

柔软、放松,反而更有力量;紧绷、用力,反而更快衰败。

具体来说——

关于力量——

你是不是以为,要时刻紧绷、时刻用力、时刻使劲,才算有力量?

看看婴儿——最柔软、最放松,却握得最牢、生命力最旺。

试试:在该放松的时候,真正放松下来。

不是懈怠,是像婴儿那样——柔软而不失内在的稳定。

紧绷是消耗,柔软才持久。

关于"较劲"——

你是不是发现,越想使劲达到某个状态(睡着、表现好、被喜欢),反而越达不到?

记住婴儿的"精之至、和之至"——最高的状态,来自自然放松,不是来自用力较劲。

试试:在那些你一直"较劲"的地方,松一松。

顺其自然,往往比使劲够,更接近你想要的。

关于"太想要"——

你是不是太想让某样东西更多、更好、更旺(健康、成就、掌控)?

记住"益生曰祥"——太刻意地增益,反而是灾祸的征兆。

也记住"物壮则老"——推到极致,必然开始衰败。

试试:别把任何东西推到极致。留一点余地,保持柔软,反而更长久。

这一章的核心,是一个婴儿的画面——

那个浑身柔软、完全放松、不用一点力,却握得最牢、哭一天也不累、浑身透着最本真生命力的小家伙。

老子说:那,就是德最深厚的状态。

不是我们成年人以为的强壮、紧绷、用力——

是回到那种柔软、放松、自然、不较劲的本真状态。

最后,回到开头那只攥住你手指的小手。

它那么软,却那么有力。

它不用力,却握得最牢。

它不设防,却没有什么能伤害它。

老子说:这就是最高的德,最本真的力量——

柔软,放松,自然,不较劲。

我们成年人用了一生去变得强硬、紧绷、用力——

老子却说:真正的力量,是回到婴儿那种柔软。

I. The Infant's Grip

Have you ever extended a finger to a newborn?

The infant will clutch it with those tiny hands — with a grip that astonishes you. You almost feel you could lift the child whole.

Strange as it seems —

This little creature, all softness and unset bone, too weak even to lift its own head, grips with extraordinary force.

In Chapter 55 of the Daodejing, Laozi addresses exactly this —

The state of deepest virtue resembles the infant: supremely soft, yet supremely powerful.

We assume that strength comes from hardness, from tension, from effort —

Laozi says the opposite: the infant — softest, most relaxed, exerting no effort at all — possesses a primal vitality that we adults have lost.

This chapter is about —

Why softness and relaxation are the deeper strength. And why the harder you push, the faster you decay.

II. The Person of Deepest Virtue Resembles an Infant

The chapter opens —

"One who holds virtue deep within is compared to a newborn infant."

The most virtuous person resembles a newly born child.

"Holding virtue deep within" — carrying virtue inside, not displaying it, not broadcasting it.

Note the character "含" — held inside, not hung outside.

The truly virtuous person does not exhibit "virtue" as a badge (this connects the preceding discussion of 上德不德 — "the highest virtue does not perform virtue").

Their virtue, like the infant's, is natural — unadorned, not constructed.

"Compared to a newborn infant" — why the infant as the measure?

Because the infant represents the most original, most natural state — untwisted by the later accretions of ideas, desires, and deliberate effort.

The infant does not perform, does not contend, does not work to prove itself, does not hang out any sign —

It simply is what it is.

The person of deepest virtue has returned to precisely this original state.

Seen from 2026 —

We adults carry so much on our backs — identity, face, calculation, anxiety, "how I must appear" —

The infant carries none of it, existing naturally.

Laozi says: the state of deepest virtue is not about adding more to yourself. It is stripping that away, returning to the infant's naturalness — unforced, original, effortless.

III. Softest, Yet Most Secure

Laozi describes the infant's condition —

"Wasps and scorpions, vipers and snakes do not sting it; gripping birds and fierce beasts do not seize it. Its bones are soft, its sinews weak — yet its grip is firm."

First, "soft bones, weak sinews, yet a firm grip" —

The infant's bones are soft, sinews yielding — the most pliant state imaginable —

Yet its grip is startlingly firm.

This is precisely Laozi's point —

Softness is not the same as powerlessness. The softest state possesses the most original, most secure form of strength.

Not through clenched muscle, not through effort, not through straining —

But a relaxed, natural, yet surprisingly unyielding force.

Then, "wasps do not sting, fierce birds do not seize" — why?

Not because the infant possesses some protective magic.

But because — the infant carries no aggression, stands in no position of contest.

It does not provoke, does not threaten, is enemy to nothing —

So nothing is its enemy.

This connects the earlier passage (ch. 50) — those truly skilled at self-preservation do not oppose danger; they never place themselves where danger can reach them.

The infant does exactly this — it makes no enemies, and so has none.

Seen from 2026 —

On the power of softness: the truly powerful are not those in constant tension, constant bristling readiness — but those who are relaxed, gentle, carrying an inward stable force. Tension is expenditure. Softness endures.

On making no enemies: those who make enemies everywhere find themselves surrounded by crises. Those who carry no aggression find few who set themselves against them. Softness, making no enemies — this is the power the infant teaches.

IV. Without Straining, Reaching the Extreme

Laozi continues —

"Not yet knowing the union of male and female, yet the organ spontaneously arises — this is the fullness of essential vitality. Crying all day without the throat going hoarse — this is the fullness of natural harmony."

Both examples speak to the same thing —

Everything in the infant is natural, entirely without deliberate effort.

The organ's spontaneous response — not from intention, but from pure vitality. This is essential energy (精) in its purest state — original life-force untorted by any desire or deliberate thought.

Crying all day without going hoarse — the infant can cry for hours, yet the voice does not give out. Because the crying is completely natural, completely relaxed — not a particle of deliberate force, not a trace of straining. This is harmony at its most extreme — complete naturalness, complete ease.

The key here —

The infant achieves "fullness of vitality" and "fullness of harmony" precisely because it neither strains nor tries.

The harder adults push, the more tense they become — yet they cannot reach that natural extremity.

The highest state is natural — not forced.

Seen from 2026 —

Haven't you noticed — the more desperately you try to fall asleep, the more you lie awake; the more anxious you are to perform well, the more mistakes you make; the more deliberately you try to please, the more awkward it becomes.

The best states tend to arrive when you relax, stop straining, let things unfold.

This is the infant's fullness — the highest states come from natural ease, not from maximum effort.

V. Harmony Is the Enduring Way

The next line —

"Harmony is called the enduring (常). Knowing the enduring is called illumination (明)."

"Harmony is the enduring" — the infant's state of complete naturalness is itself the enduring Dao.

Nothing needs to be added, nothing proven —

That naturally harmonious state is itself the Dao.

"Knowing the enduring is illumination" — knowing this constancy is true understanding.

True understanding is not knowing more, accumulating more doctrine — it is recognizing that the natural, harmonious, original state is the foundation of everything.

This connects the recurring discussion of "illumination" (ch. 16, ch. 52) — true illumination is seeing the enduring foundation that runs through all things. This chapter tells us: that foundation is the infant's naturalness, harmony, effortlessness.

VI. The More You Chase Life, the Faster You Decay

Having given the positive (the infant), Laozi turns to the negative —

"Augmenting life is called an ill omen. The mind driving the vital breath is called forcing. When things reach their prime they age — this is called 'not the Dao.' What is not the Dao ends early."

Two phrases that must be read precisely —

"Augmenting life is an ill omen" — here "omen" (祥) means inauspicious omen, not blessing.

"Augmenting life" means deliberately, frantically trying to make life longer, stronger, more.

Laozi says: this craving to prolong life is itself a harbinger of disaster.

This connects the earlier passage (ch. 50) — excessive attachment to "living" ends up destroying the life it clings to.

"The mind driving the vital breath is forcing" — here "forcing" means excessive effort that violates the natural.

Using one's will to coerce the body's natural vitality — Laozi says: this is "forcing" — unnatural exertion that violates spontaneity.

The precise opposite of the infant: the infant is effortless; "mind driving breath" is the will coercing by force.

Then that recurring warning — "things in their prime age — this is not the Dao — what is not the Dao ends early."

Because "reaching the prime" is the state of forcing and deliberate augmentation pushed to its apex — and whatever is pushed to its apex must turn back. This connects the thread running through ch. 30, ch. 40, ch. 42: push anything to the extreme and it reverses. The hard and strong do not endure.

The chapter's opposition is clear —

The infant (positive): soft, relaxed, natural, effortless — fullness of vitality, fullness of harmony — this is the enduring Dao, with the most original vitality.

The forced (negative): tense, effortful, deliberately augmenting — things in their prime age — this is not the Dao, ending early.

The soft and relaxed infant has the most robust vitality; the tense and forced thing decays the fastest.

VII. To the Reader

This chapter offers one profoundly counterintuitive yet important reminder —

Softness and relaxation are the deeper strength; tension and force accelerate decay.

On strength —

Do you believe you must be constantly tense, constantly effortful, constantly at the ready to be strong?

Look at the infant — softest, most relaxed, yet gripping most firmly, vitality most abundant.

Try: when relaxation is called for, actually relax. Not slackness — but the infant's way: soft yet inwardly stable. Tension is expenditure. Softness endures.

On straining —

Have you noticed that the harder you try to achieve some state, the further it recedes?

Remember the infant's fullness — the highest states come from natural ease, not from maximum straining.

Try: in the places where you've been straining, ease off a little. Letting things unfold naturally often brings you closer to what you want than forcing your way there.

On excessive wanting —

Are you constantly trying to make something more, better, greater — health, achievement, control?

Remember "augmenting life is an ill omen" — the deliberate craving to add more is itself a harbinger of disaster.

And remember "things in their prime age" — push anything to its extreme and decline follows.

Try: don't push anything to its extreme. Keep a margin, stay soft — it lasts longer.

This chapter's core is the image of an infant —

That completely soft, fully relaxed, utterly effortless creature — whose grip is firmest, who can cry all day without fatigue, who radiates the most original vitality.

Laozi says: that is the state of deepest virtue.

Not what we adults imagine as strength — hardness, tension, force —

But returning to that soft, relaxed, natural, unstraining original state.

Finally, back to that small hand gripping your finger.

So soft, yet so strong. No effort — yet gripping most firmly. No defense — yet nothing that could harm it.

Laozi says: this is the highest virtue, the most original power —

Soft. Relaxed. Natural. Unstrained.

We spend a lifetime becoming hard, tense, effortful —

Laozi says: real power is returning to the infant's softness.
