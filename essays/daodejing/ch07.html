第七章
Chapter 7 of 81

长生

Long Life

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、长生不是长生不老

老子第七章讲长生。

但他讲的不是长生不老那个长生。

后世道家从这一章确实发展出一整套养生术，包括各种服丹、辟谷、内丹外丹、各种功法，所有这些技术都来源于把老子的"长生"读成了"生命永远延续"。

我不谈道家本身的解读是否读偏了，但是我认为老子在这一章讲的是一件结构上更精确的事：

一个主体怎么持续运作而不崩溃。

不是关于身体能活多久。是关于一个"自己"怎么不消耗自己。

二、为什么人会累

先想一个具体的问题——

为什么人会累？

不是因为做了多少事。
是因为一直在维持自己。

每天起床要确认"我是谁"。要维持别人对你的看法。要在工作场合维持专业感，在朋友面前维持轻松感，在家人面前维持靠谱感。要时时计算"这对我有没有好处"，"这会不会让我有危险"。

这些维持，每一项都在跑——像后台进程一样占用资源。

维持本身就在消耗。

而且消耗得很厉害。

人累的不是身体活动。是这一整套"我要是我"的心理动作每天24小时不停在跑。

天地不做这件事。

天地不维持自己。天地根本没有把自己当成一个需要维持的东西。

所以天地不消耗。

不消耗，就不会尽。

这就是老子在这一章里说的"不自生"。

三、不自生

第一句：

"天长地久。天地之所以能长且久者，以其不自生也，故能长生。"

"不自生"三个字是这一章的钥匙。

最容易出的错是把它读成"天地不自己生长"。但如果是这个意思，"不自生"就成了"天地不长"——那天地就没有了，读不通。

"不自生"的真义是：

不把"自"立成一个需要维持的构。

每个有主体意识的人，在某一刻会把自己的边界感知出来——"我"、"我的身份"、"我的位置"、"我的形象"。然后开始维持这个边界。

这个维持的动作，就是"自生"。

要先说清楚一件事，免得读者滑到错误的层级上——老子讲的"自"是有主体意识层面的"自"，是人的心理层面的自我维护，不是生物层面的"自"。

细胞当然要有膜，身体当然要吃饭。这些不是老子在讲的事。

老子讲的是更上层的那个"我要维护这个我"的心理动作——当这个动作变成你生活的常驻后台进程，它就开始消耗你。

天地没有这个动作。天地没有把自己立成一个有边界的"自"去维护。

所以天地不需要维持。
所以天地不消耗。
所以"长生"。

"长生"在老子这里也不是字面的"永远活着"。是另一件更精确的事——

位置常存，内容流过。

回到第六章。浴神不死不是某一条溪谷里的水永远是同一滴水——是溪谷里的水一直在流、一直在被新的水替换。今天流过的水到了下游，源头又有新的水涌上来。

水流这个位置永存，位置上的内容一直在流过。

天地长生也是这样。天地不是一个老不死的巨人在挣扎活下去。

天地是一个让一切生灭在其中发生的位置。

这个位置上的内容——山川、河流、生物、星辰——一直在变。但位置本身不需要被维护。

因为它不是一个需要守的"自"。是一个让流转发生的场。

四、退其身，外其身

讲完天地，老子把这件事搬到圣人身上：

"是以圣人退其身而身先，外其身而身存。"

退其身而身先——主动撤步，反而在前面。

帛书写"退"，通行本写"后"。这是一个有趣的字差。

"后"是位置描述——"我在后面"。
"退"是有意识的动作——"我主动从前面那个位上撤回来"。

通行本的"后"读起来像处世技巧——谦虚就能得到好处。这是字面读法。但这样读把老子读成了一个心机家。

帛书的"退"读起来不一样——它是主动选择不占那个位。

为什么主动撤？

不是因为谦虚是美德。是因为结构上必须这样。

把自己放在"要争第一"的位置上，就是把"自"立成了一个需要维护的构。要维护这个第一的位，就消耗能量去抢去守。结果不是累死就是被挤下来。

主动从那个位上撤下来——那个位就没有被"我"占据的张力。一件事需要有人做，你能做，你做了，你就是做那件事的那个位。

不是你争来的。是结构上你在那里。

"身先"不是奖励。是不消耗于自我维持时的自然结果。

这跟第二章那句"夫唯弗居，是以弗去"完全是同一个机理。不占位，反而不丢位。这里换了个角度再说一遍。

外其身而身存——把自己置于外，反而存下来。

同理。

不把"我要活下去"立成一个执着的构——不每天算计"这对我有没有好处"、"这会不会让我有危险"——反而活得久。

为什么？

因为消耗最多的不是生理活动，是"我要活下去"这个构本身的维持。

维持这个构需要时时警觉、时时计算、时时怕失去。

这套心理运作比任何体力活都耗。

不立这个构，身反而存。

老子在这里讲的不是"佛系活着比较健康"那种现代焦虑文学。他在讲一个更结构的事——

自我维护是最大的能量消耗。

放下这个维护，能量全部用于运作。

运作本身产出位置。

五、两个私

这一章最后一句：

"不以其无私与？故能成其私。"

不是因为他无私吗？所以才能成就他的私。

读起来像心机家言论——假装无私是手段，得到真正的好处是目的。

这是这一章最常见的误读。

但其实老子在做一个非常精确的区分——

"无私"的"私"，和"成其私"的"私"，不是同一个"私"。

一个是假私。
一个是真私。

什么是假私？

社会尺子下的私。

人人都在争的那些东西——财富、名声、地位、面子、"我是个成功的人"的自我形象——所有这些。

它们看起来是你的，但其实并不真正是你的。它们是你跟别人用同一把尺子量出来的同一组东西。

每一个被叫做"我的"的东西，都是社会先立了一把尺子之后，你按这把尺子去争来的位置。

这是被社会公共尺子筛过的"私"。

不是真正的私。是假私。是被社会殖民出来的私。

什么是真私？

真正属于你自己的独特余项。

它不在你的工资数字上，不在你的职位上，不在你的粉丝数上。

它在你处理某件具体事情的那个独特方式上——别人会用别的方式，你只能这么做。
它在你看一个问题的那个独特角度上——别人看不到这个角度，你能看到。
它在你跟某个人之间的那种独特关系里——这个关系只有你们两个人有，没人能复制。

那一刻你是不可替代的。

那就是你的真私。

每个具体的人，作为一个具体的主体，都有这种东西——但它不在社会尺子量出来的那些位置上。它在每个人自己独有的地方。

社会尺子量不到。

老子说的"不以其无私与？故能成其私。"——是这件事：

不立假私，故能成真私。

为什么？

因为如果你一直在守社会尺子下的那些"私"——争财富、争名声、怕失去——你的能量全用在守这些构上。你自己真正的独特涌出就没有空间出来。

放下那套社会之私的构——你自己真正的独特余项才能长出来。

不是"假装无私来达到真私"那种计谋——

是两个私不是同一个。

一个守住了，另一个就出不来。
一个放下了，另一个才能显现。

这才是"成其私"的结构机理。

六、满街都是圣人

读到这里，读者可能会想——这听起来太难了。

要不立"自"。要不守边界。要放下所有维持。

哪个普通人能做到？

如果这就是圣人——那不就是一个普通人终生达不到的神话？

但这里要清楚一件事——

圣人不是一个"必须彻底达到才算"的终点状态。

老子从来不是这个意思。

后世把"圣人"建成了一个不可及的形象——清高、超脱、得道、永远在上——所以普通人读到"圣人如何如何"，第一反应是"那是说他们，跟我没关系"。

老子那里不是这样。

圣人是一个方向上的用心，不是一个彻底达到的实体。

你在这一刻、这件事上——放下了"自"——让别人先，让别人存，让"成其私"的念头自己掉下来——

你在这一刻就在圣人的位置上了。

你下一刻又立了"自"——你又不在了。
再下一刻你又放下——你又在了。

位置是瞬时的，不是终身的。

只要你有心朝那个方向走，你就随时可以在。

王阳明龙场悟道之后说"满街都是圣人"——不是降低标准，是看清了圣人的结构。

不是街上每个人都"完全做到"了无私长生。是每个人都随时可能在圣人的位置上——只要他那一刻心在那方向。

这个位置的门永远开着。

但有一个条件——

你一宣称自己进去了，你就立刻退出来。

因为你一宣称"我是圣人"，你立刻又立了"圣人的自"——又开始维护"我是个圣人"的形象。这就违反了整章想说的事。

这是一个结构性的不可逆——先放下自，再被别人回头那么称呼。

不能反过来。

老子自己就是这么做的。

他写《道德经》的时候没有想"我要成为一个伟大的哲人"。他只是把他想讲的东西讲了五千字就停了。他骑青牛出函谷关的时候，他就是一个要离开这个国家的老人——不是一个正在"成其私"的圣人。

"成其私"这件事永远只能从外面看见。

是两千四百年后的我们回头，指着这位老人说："啊，他没有守自己，结果他的话还在运作。"

他自己从来不这么看自己。

如果他这么看自己，这一切就立刻塌陷。

第七章对读者的真实信息是：

门永远开着。

不需要终生达到。不需要"完全做到"。不需要被认证。

有心朝那方向，你在这一刻就在这位置上。

下一刻又立了自——再放下就好。

放了又立，立了又放——位置一直在那儿，进进出出而已。

I. Long Life Is Not Immortality

Chapter Seven speaks of long life.

But not the long life of immortality.

Later Daoism did develop, from this chapter, an entire tradition of life-cultivation techniques — ingesting elixirs, avoiding grains, inner and outer alchemy, various practices — all deriving from reading Laozi's "long life" as "life continuing forever."

I won't argue whether Daoist tradition has misread its founder. What I think Laozi is saying in this chapter is something structurally more precise:

How a subject continues to operate without breaking down.

Not about how long the body can live. About how a "self" can avoid consuming itself.

II. Why People Get Tired

Start with a concrete question —

Why do people get tired?

Not because of how much they do.
Because they're continuously maintaining themselves.

Wake up each morning and confirm "who am I." Maintain other people's perception of you. Maintain a professional image at work, a relaxed image with friends, a reliable image with family. Constantly calculate "is this good for me?" "will this put me in danger?"

These maintenance tasks are all running — like background processes consuming resources.

Maintenance itself consumes.

And it consumes heavily.

People are tired not from physical activity. They're tired from this whole apparatus of "I must be me" running around the clock, twenty-four hours a day.

Heaven and earth don't do this.

Heaven and earth do not maintain themselves. Heaven and earth simply don't treat themselves as something that needs maintaining.

So heaven and earth don't consume.

Not consuming, they never run out.

This is what Laozi means in this chapter by "not self-living."

III. Not Self-Living

The first line:

"Heaven is enduring, earth is lasting. The reason heaven and earth can be enduring and lasting is that they do not live for themselves — and therefore can have long life."

The three characters "not self-living" are this chapter's key.

The easiest mistake is reading it as "heaven and earth do not grow on their own." But if that were the meaning, "not self-living" would mean "heaven and earth do not grow" — they would cease to exist, which makes no sense.

The real meaning of "not self-living":

Not erecting "the self" as a construct that needs maintaining.

Every conscious subject, at some moment, perceives their own boundary — "I," "my identity," "my position," "my image." Then they start maintaining that boundary.

That maintenance action is "self-living."

One clarification to prevent slipping to the wrong level — the "self" Laozi talks about is the self of conscious subjectivity: the psychological act of self-maintenance, not the biological self.

Of course cells need membranes; of course bodies need to eat. That's not what Laozi is talking about.

Laozi is talking about the higher-level psychological action of "I must maintain this I" — when this action becomes the permanent background process of your life, it begins consuming you.

Heaven and earth don't have this action. Heaven and earth simply haven't erected themselves as a boundaried "self" to maintain.

So heaven and earth need no maintaining.
So heaven and earth don't consume.
So "long life."

"Long life" for Laozi is also not the literal "live forever." It is something more precise —

The position endures; the content flows through.

Back to Chapter Six. The valley-spirit not dying doesn't mean the water in a specific valley is always the same water — it means the water in the valley keeps flowing, keeps being replaced by new water. Today's water reaches downstream; new water surges up at the source.

The position of water-flow endures; the content of the position keeps flowing through.

Heaven and earth's long life is the same. Heaven and earth are not an ageless giant struggling to survive.

Heaven and earth are a position in which all arising and ceasing takes place.

The content of this position — mountains, rivers, living things, stars — changes constantly. But the position itself needs no maintaining.

Because it's not a "self" that needs guarding. It's a field in which flow happens.

IV. Withdraw the Body; Place the Body Outside

Having spoken of heaven and earth, Laozi moves this to the sage:

"Therefore the sage withdraws his body and the body is in front; places his body outside and the body endures."

Withdraw the body and the body is in front — step back actively, and end up in front.

The silk manuscript has "withdraw" (退); the received text has "behind" (後). An interesting difference.

"Behind" is positional description — "I am behind."
"Withdraw" is a conscious action — "I actively step back from the front position."

The received text's "behind" reads like a worldly strategy — humility earns rewards. That's the literal reading, but it turns Laozi into a schemer.

The silk manuscript's "withdraw" is different — it is actively choosing not to occupy that position.

Why actively withdraw?

Not because humility is a virtue. Because structurally this is necessary.

Place yourself in the "fighting to be first" position and you've erected "the self" as a construct that needs maintaining. Maintaining that first position consumes energy in grabbing and guarding. The result is either collapse from exhaustion or being pushed out.

Actively withdraw from that position — and the position carries no tension of being "occupied by me." Something needs doing, you can do it, you do it — you simply are the position of doing that thing.

Not something you competed for. Structurally you are there.

"The body is in front" is not a reward. It is the natural outcome of not consuming energy on self-maintenance.

This is exactly the same mechanism as Chapter Two's "precisely because he does not take up residence, it therefore does not leave." Don't occupy the position — it doesn't get lost. Restated here from a different angle.

Place the body outside and the body endures — put yourself outside, and survive.

Same logic.

Don't erect "I must survive" as a gripping construct — don't constantly calculate "is this good for me?" "will this endanger me?" — and you'll actually live longer.

Why?

Because what consumes the most is not physical activity but the maintenance of the construct "I must survive" itself.

Maintaining that construct requires constant vigilance, constant calculation, constant fear of loss.

That psychological operation is more draining than any physical labor.

Don't erect that construct — and the body endures.

This is not the modern anxiety-literature idea that "Buddhist-mode living is healthier." Laozi is making a structural point —

Self-maintenance is the greatest energy consumption.

Release that maintenance and all energy goes to operation.

Operation itself produces position.

V. Two Kinds of Private

The chapter's last line:

"Is it not because he has no private interest? Therefore he can accomplish his private interest."

Sounds like the logic of a schemer — pretend to be selfless as a means; getting real benefit is the goal.

That's the most common misreading of this chapter.

But Laozi is making a very precise distinction —

The "private" of "having no private interest" and the "private" of "accomplish his private interest" are not the same "private."

One is false private.
One is true private.

What is false private?

Private interest as measured by society's rulers.

The things everyone competes for — wealth, fame, status, face, the self-image of "I am a successful person" — all of this.

It looks like yours, but it's not really yours. It's things you and others measured out using the same ruler.

Everything called "mine" is a position you competed for by following a ruler that society erected first.

This is "private" filtered through society's public ruler.

Not really private. It is false private. Private that was colonized into you by society.

What is true private?

The genuinely unique remainder that belongs only to you.

Not in your salary number, not in your job title, not in your follower count.

It's in the unique way you handle a specific situation — others would do it differently; you can only do it this way.
It's in the unique angle from which you see a problem — others can't see this angle; you can.
It's in the unique relationship between you and a specific person — only the two of you have this relationship; no one can copy it.

In that moment you are irreplaceable.

That is your true private.

Every concrete person, as a concrete subject, has this — but it doesn't live at the positions measured by society's rulers. It lives in each person's own unique place.

Society's ruler cannot measure it.

What Laozi says — "is it not because he has no private interest? Therefore he can accomplish his private interest" — means this:

Don't erect false private, and true private can be accomplished.

Why?

Because if you're always maintaining society's version of "private interest" — competing for wealth, competing for fame, afraid of losing — all your energy goes to maintaining these constructs. Your genuinely unique surging has no space to emerge.

Let go of that set of social-private constructs — and your genuinely unique remainder can grow out.

Not the scheme of "feign selflessness to achieve true selfishness" —

It is that the two privates are not the same.

Hold one, and the other can't emerge.
Let one go, and the other can appear.

That is the structural mechanism of "accomplish his private interest."

VI. Sages Are Everywhere

Reading this far, a reader might think — this sounds too hard.

Not erecting "the self." Not guarding boundaries. Releasing all maintenance.

What ordinary person can do this?

If this is what a sage is — isn't that a myth no ordinary person can reach in a lifetime?

But here's what needs to be clear —

The sage is not an endpoint-state that must be completely reached.

Laozi has never meant that.

Later ages built "the sage" into an unreachable image — lofty, detached, enlightened, permanently elevated — so ordinary readers, encountering "the sage is such-and-such," react immediately with "that's about them, not me."

Laozi's sages are not like that.

The sage is a direction one's heart tends toward — not an entity one completely becomes.

In this moment, in this situation — you've set "the self" down — let others go first, let others persist, let the thought of "accomplishing private interest" drop on its own —

In this moment you are in the sage's position.

The next moment you erect "the self" again — you're out of it.
The moment after that you set it down again — you're back in.

The position is momentary, not lifelong.

Have the heart to move in that direction and you can be there at any moment.

After Wang Yangming's enlightenment at Longchang, he said "the streets are full of sages" — not lowering the standard, but seeing the structure of sagehood clearly.

Not that every person on the street has "completely achieved" selfless long life. But that every person can be in the sage's position at any moment — as long as their heart is in that direction in that moment.

The door of this position is always open.

But there is one condition —

The moment you declare that you've entered, you immediately exit.

Because the moment you declare "I am a sage," you've immediately erected "the sage's self" — and started maintaining the image of "I am a sage." This violates everything the chapter is saying.

This is a structural irreversibility — first set the self down; let others later look back and call you that.

Not the other way around.

Laozi himself did it this way.

He didn't think "I want to become a great philosopher" when writing the Daodejing. He simply said what he wanted to say in five thousand words and stopped. Riding his ox out through Hangu Pass, he was just an old man leaving the country — not a sage in the process of "accomplishing his private interest."

"Accomplishing his private interest" can always only be seen from outside.

It is those of us looking back twenty-four hundred years later, pointing at this old man and saying: "Ah — he didn't guard his self, and as a result his words are still at work."

He never looked at himself that way.

If he had, the whole thing would immediately collapse.

The real message of Chapter Seven for readers:

The door is always open.

No need to reach it in a lifetime. No need to "fully do it." No certification required.

Have the heart to move in that direction and you are in this position right now.

The next moment you erect the self again — just set it down again.

Set down, erect, erect, set down — the position is always there; you simply enter and exit it.
