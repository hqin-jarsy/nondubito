# 很多事，都是快成的时候败的

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 7 月

一、越接近成功，越容易栽

有一种失败,特别让人心痛——

不是一开始就搞砸的,而是眼看就要成了,却在最后一步栽了。

项目做到九成,人一松口气,结果最后一公里出了岔子;一件事筹备了大半年,前面都顺,临到收尾却功亏一篑;一个人努力了很久,快要拿到手了,却在最接近的地方掉了链子。

老子在《道德经》第六十四章里,把这种失败看得很透——

"民之从事,恒于几成而败之。"

人做事,常常是在快要成的时候,反而把它败了。

为什么?这一章给了一整套回答。而且这套回答,比"快到终点要小心"深得多——

它讲的是:一件事的成败,不是在最后一步定的,而是在最初、最小、还没成形的地方就定了;从头到尾,都在那个"还没成形"的地方用力,不硬来,郑重到最后——才不会栽。

这一章,是新一组九章（第六十四到七十二章）的第一篇。

二、在它还没发生的时候，就把它安顿好

这一章开头,老子连用了四个对子——

"其安也,易持也;其未兆也,易谋也;其脆也,易判也;其微也,易散也。"

还安稳的时候,容易持守;还没显出征兆的时候,容易谋划;还脆、还没硬起来的时候,容易剖开;还细微的时候,容易散开。

然后是这一章最有名的两句——

"为之于其未有也,治之于其未乱也。"

在它还没发生的时候,就动手;在它还没乱的时候,就疏理。

注意,这和上一章（ch63）讲的"图难于易"不完全一样——

ch63 说的是:趁事情还小、还容易的时候解决它（问题已经出现了,但还小）。

ch64 更往前一步:在事情还没出现、还没成形的时候,就把条件铺好。

一个项目的架构,是在最初几周定下的;一家公司的文化,是在头十个人身上定下的;一个团队会不会内耗,常常在还没开始磨合的时候,就已经埋下了。

这些东西,都不是"出了问题再改"能救的——它们在"还没成形"的时候最容易安顿,一旦成形、成势,就很难动了。

但这里有一个特别容易走偏的地方——

"为之于未有"不是让你"抢先把一切都定死"。

不是一上来就写一份两百页的计划、把每个细节都框死、把所有可能都提前堵上——那不叫"在最初处用力",那叫"硬框"。老子在别处专门讲过这种毛病（ch38"前识"）:抢先给还没长出来的东西贴满标签、把路径定死,反而会把它憋死。

"为之于未有"真正的意思是——轻轻地铺条件、留出余地、把方向理顺,让它能自然长。

像在水还没分岔的地方,轻轻拨一下,让它顺着走;而不是筑一道墙,硬把它拦死。

这一点,下面"为者败"会讲得更清楚。

三、大树，是从一颗芽长出来的

接下来三句,是全书最有名的句子之一——

"合抱之木,作于毫末;九尺之台,起于蔂土;百仞之高,始于足下。"

两手合抱才围得住的大树,是从一点点嫩芽长起来的;九尺高的台子,是从一筐土堆起来的;百仞的高度,是从脚下第一步登上去的。

等一下——你熟悉的可能不是"百仞之高",而是"千里之行,始于足下"。

没错。通行本（后世流传最广的版本）写的是"千里之行,始于足下"。但马王堆帛书——也就是今天能见到的最早的《道德经》抄本——写的是"百仞之高,始于足下"。

这一个字的差别,其实很关键。

老子这三句,是排比,三个例子讲的是同一件事——高大的东西,都是从最小、最初的地方累积起来的:

大树的"粗",从毫末（一点嫩芽）来;高台的"高",从一筐土来;百仞的"高",从脚下来。

三个例子,说的都是"规模"——从最小处,累积成最大。

可通行本把第三句换成了"千里之行"——"千里"讲的是"距离"（走多远）,不是"规模"（长多高、堆多高）。一换,三个例子就不齐了:前两个讲"从小长到大",第三个变成"从近走到远"。

帛书的"百仞之高",让三个例子严丝合缝——讲的都是同一件事:大,从最小处来。

回到这一章的主线——

既然大树是从一颗芽长出来的,那么:你想要一棵好树,得在它还是芽的时候就照顾好——而不是等它长歪了,再回过头去掰一棵合抱粗的大树。

这就接上了前面的"为之于未有":在最小、最初、还没成形的地方用力,是最省力、也最有效的。

四、越想抓住，越容易失去

接下来两句,是老子讲了一整本书的一个核心——

"为之者败之,执之者失之。是以圣人无为也,故无败也;无执也,故无失也。"

越是想使劲去"做成"它的,反而把它做败了;越是想紧紧"抓住"它的,反而把它弄丢了。所以真正的高手,不硬"做",所以没有"败";不硬"抓",所以没有"失"。

这句话,最容易被误解成"什么都别做"。

不是的。老子讲的"无为",从来不是"躺平不动"——

而是:不硬来、不折腾、不把手攥死。

为什么"越想抓住,越容易失去"?

因为一件活的东西——一个团队、一个产品、一段关系、一个正在长起来的势头——它有它自己的节奏。你越是想一把攥死、事事都要按你的意思来,越是把它憋坏了。

我在硅谷做了快二十年,见过太多这样的事——

一个创始人,舍不得放手,什么决定都要过他一遍,结果团队动弹不得,能干的人一个个走掉;一个管理者,盯一个指标盯得太死,结果所有人都开始"为了那个数字"做事,数字好看了,东西却坏了。

这最后一种,有个说法:当一个指标变成了目标,它就不再是个好指标——你越是死死攥住那个数字,那个数字就越会骗你。

这就是"执之者失之":你越想抓住那个"成果",越会把真正让它成立的东西弄丢。

那么,前面说"在最初处用力",这里又说"别硬做、别硬抓"——不矛盾吗?

不矛盾。这恰恰是老子最精微的地方——

在最初处用力,是"轻轻地理顺、铺好条件、留出余地";不是"一把攥死、事事硬推"。

好的园丁,会在种子还没发芽时松好土、浇好水——但他绝不会把手伸进土里,硬把芽拽出来。

"揠苗助长"就是最典型的"为之者败之":你太想让它快点长,反而把它拽死了。

在对的时候（最初）,用对的力（轻轻疏理）——这才是这一章的分寸。

五、越到最后，越要像刚开始

现在回到开头那个让人心痛的失败——

"民之从事也,恒于几成而败之。慎终若始,则无败事。"

人做事,常常在快要成的时候,反而把它败了。如果对待"快结束"的时候,还像对待"刚开始"的时候一样郑重,就不会有败事。

为什么人总在"快成"的时候栽?

因为"快成了"这三个字,本身就是一个陷阱——

一旦你觉得"稳了、快到手了、剩下的都是小事",你就松了:不再那么用心,不再想在前面,不再把细处当回事。

恰恰是这一松,把前面九成的功夫,全折了进去。

"几成"（快要成）,是一件事最微妙的关口——它还没真正成,却让你以为它已经成了。你在这个关口上松一口气、立一个"我快赢了"的招牌,它反而就翻了。

"慎终若始"——对待最后一步,像对待第一步一样郑重。

这不是让你一直紧绷、一直焦虑——

而是:别在快到终点的时候,把那份最初的用心丢掉。

放到今天看——

多少产品,死在上线前那点收尾;多少事,毁在"就差最后一哆嗦"的松懈上;多少人,熬过了最难的九成,却栽在最容易的最后一成。

真正靠谱的人,不是一开始冲得最猛的,而是到最后还和第一天一样认真的。

越到最后,越像刚开始。

六、真正的高手，往回走

这一章最后三句,有点绕,但讲的东西很实在——

"是以圣人欲不欲,而不贵难得之货;学不学,而复众人之所过;能辅万物之自然,而弗敢为。"

先说"欲不欲"和"学不学"——这两个词,字面有点拗口。

"欲不欲",不是"没有欲望",而是:他要的,恰恰是"不要"这件事本身。

"学不学",不是"不学习",而是:他学的,恰恰是"不学"这件事本身。

什么意思?

大多数人,都在追同一批东西——最热的赛道、最亮的头衔、人人都在抢的那个"难得之货"。所有人一窝蜂往那儿冲,把最根本的东西,反而都越过去了。

而真正的高手,"要"的,是别人都不要的那份克制;"学"的,是回到那个被所有人越过的地方。

"复众人之所过"——回到众人越过的那一步。别人都在往前冲、往上追,他反而往回走,走回那个最基本、最容易被跳过的地方,把它补上。

在一个人人都在追风口的地方,回到基本功,本身就是一种稀缺。

最后一句,是整章的落点——

"能辅万物之自然,而弗敢为。"

能辅助万物自己的节奏,而不敢去"替它做主"。

"辅"是帮忙,不是代替。万物有它自己生长的方向,圣人做的是"顺着它、帮它一把",而不是"我来替它长"。

这又回到了前面的"别硬来"——

你能做的,是把条件铺好、把障碍清掉、让它顺着自己的势往前走;你不能做的,是伸手替它长、硬把它掰成你想要的样子。

帮它长,不替它长。这是这一章,也是老子一整套"无为"的落点。

七、给读者的

这一章,给我们几件当下就能用的事——

第一:在还没成形的时候,就安顿它。

一件事最容易调整的时候,是它还没发生、还没成形的时候（为之于未有,治之于未乱）。架构、文化、方向,趁早理顺——别等它成了势,再回头硬掰。

第二:在最小处用力,但轻轻用力。

大的东西,都从最小处长出来（合抱之木,作于毫末）。所以在最初、最细的地方下功夫——但记住是"铺条件、留余地",不是"一把攥死"。

第三:别硬做,别硬抓。

越想使劲做成、越想紧紧抓住,反而越容易做败、弄丢（为者败,执者失）。给活的东西,留出它自己的节奏。别当那个把芽拽出土的人。

第四:越到最后,越像刚开始。

大多数失败,不在开头,在"快成"的松懈里（几成而败）。对待最后一步,像对待第一步一样郑重（慎终若始）——那份最初的用心,别在终点前丢掉。

第五:往回走,补上被所有人越过的那一步。

当所有人都在追同一个风口,回到基本功,本身就是稀缺（复众人之所过）。要别人不要的克制,学别人不学的根本。

第六:帮它长,不替它长。

你能铺条件、清障碍、顺势推一把;你不能伸手替它长（能辅万物之自然,而弗敢为）。

这一章的核心,可以收成一句话——

在对的时候（最初、最小、还没成形）,用对的力（轻轻理顺、不硬来）,一路郑重到最后——事情反而做成了。

八、这一组的开始

第六十四章,是新一组九章（第六十四到七十二章）的第一篇。

《道德经》八十一章,分成九组。这是倒数第二组。走到这里,老子已经把"道"讲得差不多了,接下来这两组,更多是把前面所有的东西,收到"怎么做人、怎么做事、怎么活"上。

而这一组的开篇——第六十四章——立的,正是一个最朴素、也最难做到的功夫:

从最初到最后,都在还没成形的地方用力,不硬来,郑重到底。

看起来"慢"、看起来"轻"、看起来"没使劲"——

可恰恰是这份"在对的时候用对的力",让大树从一颗芽长成合抱之粗,让难事在还没变难的时候就化解,让人在快成的时候不栽。

最后,回到开头那句话:很多事,都是快成的时候败的。

老子两千四百年前就说了——

"慎终若始,则无败事。"

对待最后,像对待开始一样。

那份最初的郑重,别在终点前丢掉。

越到最后——

越像刚开始。

I. The Closer to Success, the Easier It Is to Fall

There is a kind of failure that is especially heartbreaking —

Not the kind that goes wrong from the start, but the kind where you can almost see the finish line, and then stumble in the very last step.

A project ninety percent complete — someone exhales in relief — and something goes wrong in the final stretch. An endeavor prepared for months, everything running smoothly, only to collapse at the close. Someone who struggled for a long time, almost within reach, then dropped the ball at the closest point.

Laozi saw this kind of failure clearly in Chapter 64 of the Daodejing:

"In human affairs, things constantly fail at the point when they are almost complete."

Why? This chapter offers a full account. And the account goes much deeper than "be careful near the finish line."

What it teaches is: the success or failure of a thing is not decided at the final step, but at the very beginning — at the smallest, not-yet-formed stage. Apply effort from start to finish in that "not-yet-formed" place, without forcing, with gravity all the way through — and you will not fall.

This chapter is the first of a new group of nine (Chapters 64–72).

II. Settle It Before It Has Happened

The chapter opens with four paired observations:

"What is stable is easy to hold. What shows no sign yet is easy to plan for. What is still brittle is easy to split. What is still minute is easy to disperse."

Then come the two most celebrated lines of this chapter:

"Act on it before it has come into being. Bring order to it before disorder has arisen."

Note that this is not quite the same as ch. 63's "handle the difficult when it is still easy."

Chapter 63 said: take care of things while they are still small and easy (the problem has already appeared, but is still small).

Chapter 64 goes one step further: before the thing has even appeared — before it has taken form — lay the conditions for it.

A project's architecture is set in its first few weeks. A company's culture is set by its first ten people. Whether a team will tear itself apart is often already embedded before they've had a chance to work together.

These things cannot be rescued by "fix it after a problem appears" — they are easiest to arrange when they haven't yet formed; once they have taken shape and built momentum, they are hard to move.

But here is where it is easy to go wrong —

"Act before it exists" does not mean "rush to lock everything down first."

It is not: write a two-hundred-page plan up front, fix every detail in place, preemptively seal off every possibility. That is not "applying effort at the origin" — that is rigidity. Laozi addresses this failure elsewhere (ch. 38's "foreknowledge"): rushing to label something before it has grown, locking down the path — you suffocate what was trying to grow.

What "act before it exists" really means: gently lay the conditions, leave room, let the direction clarify — so it can grow naturally.

Like giving water a gentle nudge at the point before it branches, letting it find its way — not building a wall and forcing it to stop.

III. A Great Tree Grows from a Single Sprout

The next three lines are among the most celebrated in the whole book:

"The tree you need two arms to embrace grew from a hair-thin sprout. A platform nine stories high rose from a basketful of earth. A height of a hundred ren begins from the ground beneath your feet."

Hold on — you may know a different version: "A journey of a thousand li begins with a single step."

That is the received text — the version most widely transmitted in later centuries. But the Mawangdui silk manuscript — the oldest known copy of the Daodejing — reads: "A height of a hundred ren begins from the ground beneath your feet."

The difference matters.

These three lines form a parallel structure, three examples making one point: things of great scale all accumulate from the smallest, earliest beginning.

The tree's girth — from a hair-thin sprout. The platform's height — from a basketful of earth. The hundred-ren height — from the ground underfoot.

All three say the same thing: scale — from the smallest, to the greatest.

When the received text replaces the third line with "a journey of a thousand li," it introduces distance (how far one travels), not scale (how tall something grows). The parallel breaks.

The silk text's "a hundred ren" keeps all three examples in lockstep: greatness comes from the smallest beginning.

Back to the chapter's main thread —

If a great tree grows from a single sprout, then: if you want a good tree, you must care for it when it is still a sprout — not wait until it has grown crooked and then try to straighten a trunk too thick to budge.

This connects to "act before it exists": applying effort at the smallest, earliest, not-yet-formed stage is the least costly and the most effective.

IV. The More You Try to Grasp, the More Easily It Slips Away

The next lines carry one of the deepest themes in the whole book:

"Those who force it ruin it. Those who grasp at it lose it. Therefore the sage does not force, and so does not ruin; does not grasp, and so does not lose."

This is most easily misread as "don't do anything."

That is not what Laozi means. His "wu wei" has never meant lying still —

It means: don't force it; don't stir things up needlessly; don't clench your fist around it.

Why does grasping lead to losing?

Because a living thing — a team, a product, a relationship, a momentum that is building — has its own rhythm. The more you try to lock it down and bend it entirely to your will, the more you suffocate it.

Nearly two decades in Silicon Valley — I have seen this too many times.

A founder who cannot let go, who must approve every decision, until the team cannot move and the capable people leave one by one. A manager who watches one metric too closely, until everyone starts performing for the number — the number looks good, and the real thing goes bad.

There is a name for that last one: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The harder you grip the number, the more the number deceives you.

This is "those who grasp at it lose it": the harder you grip the "result," the more you lose what made it real.

Does this contradict "apply effort at the origin"?

Not at all. This is exactly Laozi's finest precision:

"Applying effort at the origin" means gently clarifying the direction, laying conditions, leaving room — not locking everything down and forcing every step.

A good gardener loosens the soil and waters before the seed sprouts — but never thrusts a hand into the earth to yank the sprout out.

Trying to hurry a plant by pulling it upward is the classic case of "forcing it ruins it": you were so eager for it to grow faster, you pulled it to death.

At the right moment (the origin), with the right kind of effort (gentle clarification) — that is the measure this chapter is pointing to.

V. Toward the End, Be Like the Beginning

Now back to the heartbreaking failure at the opening:

"In human affairs, things constantly fail at the point when they are almost complete. If you treat the end with the same gravity as the beginning, nothing will fail."

Why do people so often fall at the "almost done" moment?

Because "almost done" is itself a trap —

The moment you feel "it's settled, nearly in hand, the rest is small stuff," you relax: no longer as attentive, no longer thinking ahead, no longer treating the details as mattering.

That one moment of relaxation can squander the entire ninety percent that came before.

"Almost complete" is the subtlest threshold — it hasn't truly succeeded yet, but it makes you feel as though it already has. Exhale at this threshold — plant a "I'm about to win" sign — and the whole thing flips over.

"Treat the end with the same gravity as the beginning" —

This does not mean staying perpetually tense and anxious.

It means: don't drop that original attentiveness just before the finish line.

Seen from today —

How many products die in the final cleanup before launch. How many things collapse from "just one more push" fatigue. How many people survive the hardest ninety percent, only to stumble in the easiest final ten.

The truly dependable person is not the one who charges hardest at the start — but the one who is just as careful on the last day as on the first.

The closer to the end — the more like the beginning.

VI. The Real Master Goes Back

The chapter's final three lines are a little intricate, but what they teach is very concrete:

"Therefore the sage desires what others do not desire, and sets no store by goods that are hard to obtain. He learns what others do not learn, and returns to what the multitude has passed over. He can assist the self-so nature of ten thousand things — and dares not act in their stead."

First, on "desires what others do not desire" and "learns what others do not learn" — these phrases sound a little paradoxical.

"Desires what others do not desire" does not mean "has no desires" — it means: what he wants is precisely the restraint of not-wanting.

"Learns what others do not learn" does not mean "doesn't learn" — it means: what he studies is precisely the discipline of returning to what others have skipped past.

Why?

Most people are chasing the same handful of things — the hottest track, the brightest credential, the "rare goods" everyone is scrambling for. Everyone rushing in that direction has already passed over what is most fundamental.

The real master "wants" the restraint that everyone else discards; "learns" by going back to the step everyone else has leapt over.

"Returns to what the multitude has passed over" — goes back to that bypassed step. While others are charging forward and climbing upward, he walks back — back to the most basic, most easily skipped place — and fills it in.

In a world where everyone is chasing the next wave, returning to fundamentals is itself a kind of scarcity.

The final line is the chapter's landing point —

"He can assist the self-so nature of ten thousand things — and dares not act in their stead."

He can help things follow their own rhythm — but dares not do the growing for them.

"Assist" is to help, not to substitute. Ten thousand things have their own direction of growth; the sage goes along with them and gives them a hand — he does not step in and grow in their place.

This circles back to "don't force it" —

What you can do: lay the conditions, clear the obstacles, give a nudge with the momentum. What you cannot do: reach in and grow for it, force it into the shape you want.

Help it grow — don't grow in its place. That is this chapter's final point, and the destination of Laozi's entire teaching of wu wei.

VII. To the Reader

This chapter offers several things you can put to use right now:

First: settle it before it has taken form.

The easiest moment to adjust something is before it has happened, before it has solidified (act before it exists; bring order before disorder arises). Architecture, culture, direction — clarify them early. Don't wait for the momentum to set and then try to force things.

Second: apply effort at the smallest place — but gently.

Great things grow from the smallest beginning (the tree from a hair-thin sprout). Work at the earliest and finest level — but remember: "lay the conditions and leave room," not "lock it down."

Third: don't force it; don't grip it.

The harder you try to make it succeed, the harder you grip it, the more likely you are to ruin it or lose it (forcing ruins; grasping loses). Give living things room for their own rhythm. Don't be the person yanking the sprout out of the earth.

Fourth: the closer to the end, the more like the beginning.

Most failures happen not at the start but in the relaxation of "almost done" (fails at almost-complete). Treat the final step with the same gravity as the first (treat the end like the beginning) — don't drop that original attentiveness before the finish line.

Fifth: go back, and fill in the step everyone else has passed over.

When everyone is chasing the same wave, returning to fundamentals is itself a form of scarcity (returns to what the multitude has passed over). Want the restraint others don't want; learn the fundamentals others won't study.

Sixth: help it grow — don't grow in its place.

You can lay conditions, clear obstacles, nudge with the momentum; you cannot reach in and grow for it (can assist the self-so nature of ten thousand things, and dares not act in their stead).

This chapter's core can be summed in one line —

At the right moment (the earliest, the smallest, the not-yet-formed), with the right kind of effort (gentle clarification, without forcing), all the way to the very end with the same gravity — things get done.

VIII. Opening This New Group

Chapter 64 is the first of a new group of nine chapters (Chapters 64–72).

The eighty-one chapters of the Daodejing divide into nine groups. This is the second-to-last. By this point, Laozi has said most of what he needs to say about the Dao; these final two groups gather everything into "how to be, how to act, how to live."

And this group's opening chapter — Chapter 64 — establishes the most elemental, most difficult discipline:

From the very first moment to the very last, apply effort in the not-yet-formed place; without forcing; with gravity, all the way through.

Looks "slow." Looks "light." Looks like "not trying hard." —

But this — "the right effort at the right moment" — is precisely what lets the great tree grow from a single sprout, dissolves difficulty before it has turned difficult, and keeps a person from stumbling when they are almost done.

Finally, back to the opening: many things fail just before they succeed.

Laozi said it 2,400 years ago —

"Treat the end with the same gravity as the beginning, and nothing will fail."

Treat the last moment as you treated the first.

Don't drop that original gravity before the finish line.

The closer to the end —

the more like the beginning.
