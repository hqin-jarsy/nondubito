第十五章
Chapter 15 of 81

七张日常照片

Seven Everyday Photographs

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、读完前面十四章之后

读完前面十四章，读者可能会问——

这些"涵育"、"无身"、"执今"、"尊重余项"——都是什么样的人在做？

如果我在生活里遇到一个真正在做这些事的人，我能认出来吗？

我自己离这种人有多远？

第十五章正好回答这些问题。

它给出一张画像——

但不是肖像。

是七张日常照片。

七张里没有一张是高悬的仙人画——全是当时人每天看见的画面：

冬天涉水的人。
小心翼翼的客人。
将化的冰湖。
未雕的木头。
浊水。
山谷。

老子在指着这些画面说——

"看，那种走通这条路的人，长这个样。"

二、善为道者，不是士

第十五章开篇就有一处字差需要先说清楚。

帛书：古之善为道者。
通行本：古之善为士者。

道 vs 士。一字之差，整章的主题换了。

按通行本读——这一章在画"合格的士人"。士是社会身份，是士农工商的士，是有学问有担当的社会阶层。整章变成士阶层的修养守则。

按帛书读——这一章在画"善于践行道的人"。道是这本书的核心主题，善为道者就是能走通余项运作的人——和社会身份无关。

这件事在 2026 年读特别重要。

按帛书读，这个人不一定是士——他可以是程序员、是设计师、是父母、是医生、是教师、是出租车司机、是创业者、是退休的人、是任何一个具体在做事的人。

只要他尊重余项、善用余项——他就是这一章里画的那个人。

不在身份，在做法。

三、深不可识，对谁？

第二句——

"微妙玄达，深不可识。"

读到"深不可识"，传统读法会把它读成圣人的本质属性——圣人是高深莫测的人。

但这个读法有一个问题——

深不可识，对谁？

老子没说圣人本身高深莫测。他说从外面看上去，这个人看不透。

那从里面呢？

——已经在那个位置上的人互相看，并不深不可识。

走通这条路的人互相认得出。对方的七个画像他们自己也有，彼此之间不觉得对方神秘——像认出同类一样。

"深不可识"是对还没到那个位置的人的描述。

读到这里要把一个误读挡住——

老子不是在神秘化圣人。

他是在给有志读者一个提示：不要试图用你现在手里的构去识别他。

为什么？

因为这个人不立显眼的构去抓你的注意——

一个立了许多显眼构的人，观点鲜明、姿态强烈、立场突出——一眼能识别："这个人是 X 派"、"那个人是 Y 家"。

善为道者不立这些显眼构。

所以你手里那套识别人的工具找不到他的位——你识别一个人是靠对方立的构。他不立构，你抓不到。

但这不代表他不在那里。

他在那里。只是用你的工具识别不到。

要识他，不是认知问题，是位置问题——

只有你自己也走到那个位置，才能认出走在那个位置的人。

想看清这种人长什么样，不是学更多识别圣人的概念，是自己也开始做尊重余项、善用余项的事。

那时候再看他，就不是"深不可识"了——就是"啊，他和我一样"。

四、七张日常照片

老子接下来一句——

"夫唯不可识，故强为之容。"

既然深不可识，那就勉强给画一个画像。

注意"强"字——勉强。

老子知道自己画出来的画像不是真的圣人，只是一个近似。真的圣人无法被任何画像完全定义——那就又成了一个构去抓他。

而且这个画像是画给还没到那个位置的读者——已经到那个位置的人不需要画像。

下面七个画像——

画像一：与呵，其若冬涉水

（呵字读"啊"——是先秦汉语里这个语气词的本字，通行本改为"兮"，两千年来读成"希"——这是字形替换造成的。马王堆帛书出土后证实了清代孔广森的猜想：这个字本来就是"呵"，读"啊"。这件事很重要——它让老子的口吻回到指着东西感叹的口语感，不是经书宣读。本章七处"呵"全部读"啊"。）

冬天涉水什么样？

水凉。脚踩不清底。每一步都要探。

不能大步走，不能想快就快——每一步都要慢下来看着走。

不是因为胆小。是因为水底看不清，脚底要感受每一块石头，每次踩下去都要等一下看稳不稳。

善为道者在行动上就是这样——每一步踩稳再走。

不是优柔寡断。是尊重每一步的不确定性——余项总在具体行动中涌出。他不用一个现成的构去决定行动。他让每一步自己长出来。

这需要慢。

画像二：犹呵，其若畏四邻

怕四邻什么样？

一个刚搬到新邻里的人，不知道四周都是谁、习惯是什么、规矩是什么——时时在听、在看、在留心。

不是害怕。是不假设自己熟悉。

善为道者跟环境的关系就是这样——

不假设自己已经了解了四周。

他知道环境随时在变、余项随时在涌出、昨天熟悉的今天可能已经不同。所以时时在感知、时时在校准——不预设、不自满、不以为自己全懂。

画像三：严呵，其若客

做客什么样？

到别人家里——不抢上座，不先动筷，不乱翻东西，不以为这是自己家。

端庄不是拘束。是知道这里不是自己的位。

善为道者对任何位置都是做客的态度——

不抢主位，不以为任何位是自己的。

这跟第七章"不自生"、第十三章"以身为天下"一脉——身份感很轻，位置感很轻。他在哪一位上都是来访者，不是主人。

画像四：涣呵，其若凌泽

冰湖将化是什么？

一个冬天结实的冰面，春天要化了——还没化开，但已经能听到冰下水流的声音，已经能看到冰面微微松动——将要化开的那一刻。

善为道者的姿态就是这样——

松开着，不紧，不坚持，随时可以改变。

他不把自己冻在一个姿态里。

像将化的冰，保持着"将要松开"的状态。

不是散漫——冰还在。

是不冻结——随时能化。

画像五：沌呵，其若朴

朴木什么样？

一块未雕的原木。看上去没有什么特别的形状，没有明确的用途，不像家具不像工艺品——朴素、未成器。

但正因为未成器，它还可以成为任何器。

善为道者内在就是这样——

浑然一体，没有被各种构切成一块块。

他不把自己切成"理性 / 感性"、"公 / 私"、"工作 / 生活"、"信念 A / 信念 B"这些分块。

他保持浑然。

画像六：湷呵，其若浊

浊水什么样？

刚搅动的水、雨后的河水、加了泥沙的水——看不透，不透明，暂时不清。

善为道者在外人眼里常常是"浊"的——

看不透，不一眼分明。

为什么？

因为他不把自己变成一个透明清晰的构供人一眼看穿。

不是他在藏，是他不演。

一眼看穿的人一定是在演那个"一眼看穿的形象"——演一个清晰的人、演一个鲜明的角色、演一个明确的立场。

善为道者不演。所以外人眼里他是浊的。

画像七：旷呵，其若谷

山谷什么样？

空的、广的、能容纳的——风可以吹进吹出，水可以流进流出，声音可以回响，万物可以生长。

山谷不是空无——是容纳空间。

善为道者容纳的能力就是这样——

不把自己塞满，留出空间。

只有留着位的人，余项才能在他身上涌出——他自己的余项、他涵育的他人的余项、场景的余项，都能在他留出的空间里运作。

五、七把自测尺

七个画像合起来画的是同一个人在七个位置上的运作样貌——

行动上慢。
对环境警觉。
位置感轻。
姿态松。
内在浑。
外表不演。
容量空。

但这七条不是七种性格。

是七把自测尺——读者可以在此刻用来问自己现在在哪里。

如果老子今天来写，他可能会让我们这样自测——

我今天有没有慢下来？还是事事都在赶 deadline，每个决定都在 5 分钟里做完？

我有没有假设自己了解一切？进入新团队、新行业、新关系，我是先听还是先讲？

我有没有抢主位？会议里，我是急着发言定调，还是先看看别人在做什么？

我有没有把自己冻在一个角色里？"我是 senior engineer，所以我应该 X" 是不是已经成了我的默认？

我有没有把自己切成几块？工作时的我和家里的我是不是两个完全不同的人？我在不同场合切来切去，自己都对不上？

我有没有在演？在 LinkedIn 上是一个版本，在 Twitter 上是另一个版本，在朋友面前是第三个版本——哪一个是真的我？

我的日程留位了吗？还是从早八点到晚十点全部排满，连吃饭都在开 zoom？

每一把尺都可以在此刻用来问自己——不是为了考核（考核又成了一个构），是为了感受自己现在在哪里。

看见自己在哪里，就是一次校准。

老子画这七张照片不是要立一个让你够不到的高位。

他是在指着这七张照片说——

"这是可以走的方向。"

你这一刻在"慢下来"那个画像上，下一刻可能就不在。下一刻又在，再下一刻又不在。

位置是瞬时的，门一直开着——跟第七章"满街都是圣人"是同一件事。

你随时可以朝某一个画像走一步。走完，你就在那一刻的那个画像位置上。

六、徐清徐生，不欲盈

画完七张照片，老子讲这个人的工夫——

"浊而静之徐清，安以动之徐生。"

水浊的时候，不搅它，静下来，让它自己慢慢清。

关键在"徐"字——不急、不催、让它自己。

换一个人遇到浊水可能就用滤网、用明矾、用各种手段强行变清——

那是殖民。

善为道者不这样——他静下来，等余项自己沉淀、自己清下来。

安定的时候，动起来，让它慢慢生出新东西。

同理。一个安定的状态要开始变化了——不强迫变化，不急着变化，让它自己慢慢生出来。

换一个人可能逼着自己出成果、催着自己出变化——

那也是殖民。是殖民自己的节奏。

善为道者让它自己来。

这就是"徐"的工夫——慢下来，让过程按自己的节奏运作，不殖民自己也不殖民他人的节奏。

"保此道者不欲盈。"

守着这个道的人不想盈满。

满是什么？

构立满了。位占满了。身份维护满了。成果累积满了。

满了，就停了。

不欲盈不是害怕满。是不以满为目标。

换一个人可能一辈子在追求盈满——把事业做满、把影响做满、把财富做满、把知识做满、把名声做满。

善为道者根本不把盈当目标——他做事，他活着，但不追着那个盈走。

不欲盈和七个画像完全一致——都是不把位塞满的具体表现。

七、蔽而不成

最后一句——

"夫唯不欲盈，故能蔽而不成。"

这一句帛书和通行本有一字差——帛书"蔽而不成"，通行本"蔽而新成"。

先说"蔽"字。

历代注家常把这里的"蔽"读作"敝"（破旧），整句读成"能经得起破旧而新成"。但帛书原字就是"蔽"——本义是遮盖、不显。

按本字读——"蔽"正是整章七个画像的关键词。

善为道者不显山露水（浊水）、不抢主位（做客）、不演（不立透明清晰的构）、外人深不可识——

蔽就是这个不显的姿态。

老子在收束处用"蔽"字把整章七个画像的共通底色点出来。

回到字差。

帛书"蔽而不成"——遮盖着自己且不以成为目标。

通行本"蔽而新成"——遮盖着自己然后新成。

帛书读法：蔽是不显，不成是不以完成为目标——两者方向完全一致，都是彻底走"非"的姿态。不追满。不显山。不求成。

跟前面"不欲盈"一贯——既然不欲盈，当然也不欲成。

生命本身就是运作，不是为了达成什么。

通行本"新成"读法把这一姿态降级——

蔽变成了手段（暂时遮盖自己），新成变成了目的。

按这个读法，善为道者暂时不显是为了最后新成——不显、不欲盈、退让，都成了达成"新成"的战术。

老子从一个不以成为目标的人，被读成了一个以退为进的策略家。

按帛书读——

善为道者真的不以成为目标。

他活着，他做事，他涵育他人——但不追着成。

他不是在演"退让"——他真的不想要那个"成"。

不是策略，是姿态。

这一姿态比"以退为进"难得多——

以退为进的人每一步都在算计退完之后能不能进。
不以成为目标的人根本不算这个——他做事，因为这件事该做；他帮人，因为这个人需要帮；他写书，因为有东西要写——

每件事本身就是目的。

不在外面有一个"完成态"等着他。

生命本身就是过程。

回到这一章开篇——

读完前面十四章，读者可能会问"如果我遇到这种人，我能认出来吗？"

读完十五章再问这个问题——

可能你已经在某些时刻自己就是这个人了。

某一次你不抢主位地参加一次会议——你那一刻就在画像三上。
某一次你慢下来想清楚一件事再做——你那一刻就在画像一上。
某一次你不演，让自己浊一点——你那一刻就在画像六上。
某一次你日程留出半天什么都不安排——你那一刻就在画像七上。

你已经在画像上了——只是没有连成片。

七个画像不是终点。

是七个你可以更经常停留的位置。

每停留一次，门开一次。

一、After Reading the First Fourteen Chapters

After reading the first fourteen chapters, a reader might ask —

All this "nurturing," "no-identity," "grasp today," "respecting the remainder" — what kind of person actually does these things?

If I encountered such a person in life, would I recognize them?

How far am I from being that person?

Chapter 15 answers exactly these questions.

It gives a portrait —

Not a formal likeness. Seven everyday photographs.

None of the seven is an image of a sage hovering in the clouds — all are scenes that people of Laozi's time saw every day:

A person crossing water in winter.
A cautious guest.
Ice about to melt.
Uncarved wood.
Muddy water.
A valley.

Laozi points at these images and says —

"Look — the person who has walked this path looks like this."

二、Adept at the Dao — Not a Scholar-Official

Chapter 15 opens with a textual variant that needs to be stated first.

Silk manuscript: 古之善为道者 — "The ancients who were adept at the dao."
Standard text: 古之善为士者 — "The ancients who were adept at being shi (scholar-officials)."

Dao vs. shi. One character different, the chapter's whole subject changes.

Standard-text reading: this chapter draws a portrait of the "qualified scholar-official." Shi is a social status — the shi in the four estates of shi, farmer, artisan, merchant — a class of the learned and conscientious. The chapter becomes a code of cultivation for the scholar-official class.

Silk-manuscript reading: this chapter draws a portrait of "one who is adept at practicing the dao." The dao is the book's central subject; one adept at the dao is one who can walk the operation of the remainder — unrelated to social status.

This distinction matters enormously in 2026.

Reading the silk manuscript, this person is not necessarily a scholar-official — they can be a programmer, a designer, a parent, a doctor, a teacher, a taxi driver, an entrepreneur, a retired person, any concrete person doing anything.

As long as they respect the remainder, skillfully work with the remainder — they are the person drawn in this chapter.

Not about status. About practice.

三、Unfathomable — To Whom?

Second line —

"微妙玄达，深不可识。"

"Subtle, profound, xuan, far-reaching — deep and unrecognizable."

Reading "deep and unrecognizable," the traditional reading turns it into an intrinsic attribute of the sage — the sage is an unfathomable person.

But this reading has a problem —

Unfathomable — to whom?

Laozi is not saying the sage is unfathomable in themselves. He is saying as seen from the outside, this person is hard to see through.

From the inside, then?

— Those already at that position can recognize each other immediately. They see the other person's seven photographs and recognize them. They do not find each other mysterious — they recognize each other like recognizing one of their own.

"Deep and unrecognizable" describes people not yet at that position.

Reading to this point, block one more misreading —

Laozi is not mystifying the sage.

He is giving aspiring readers a hint: do not try to recognize them using the constructs you currently hold.

Why?

Because this person does not establish conspicuous constructs to seize your attention —

A person who has established many conspicuous constructs — sharp opinions, strong postures, prominent stances — is recognizable at a glance: "this person is of the X school," "that person is of the Y house."

One adept at the dao establishes none of these conspicuous constructs.

So the tools you use to recognize people find no purchase on them — your tool for recognizing people works by the constructs the other party establishes. They establish no constructs; you cannot find the hook.

But this does not mean they are not there.

They are there. Only invisible to your tools.

To recognize them is not a cognitive problem; it is a position problem —

Only when you yourself have moved to that position can you recognize those at that position.

To see clearly what such a person looks like — not by learning more concepts for recognizing sages; it is by yourself beginning to do the things that respect and work with the remainder.

At that point, looking at them again, "deep and unrecognizable" becomes — "ah, they are like me."

四、Seven Everyday Photographs

Laozi's next line —

"夫唯不可识，故强为之容。"

"Precisely because they are unrecognizable, we strain to draw a likeness."

Note "strain" (强) — reluctantly.

Laozi knows that the portrait he is about to draw is not the real sage — only an approximation. A real sage cannot be fully defined by any portrait — that would again be a construct to grasp them.

And this portrait is drawn for readers not yet at that position — those already there do not need a portrait.

The seven photographs follow —

Photograph One: 与呵，其若冬涉水
"Careful, ah — like crossing water in winter."

(A note on 呵: this character reads "ah," not "xi." It is the original pre-Qin modal particle, confirmed by the Mawangdui silk manuscripts excavated in 1973, which show that everywhere the standard text has "X 兮," the silk manuscript originally wrote "X 呵." Qing dynasty phonologist Kong Guangsen had already proposed this. The discovery restored Laozi's colloquial register. All seven 呵 in this chapter read "ah.")

What is crossing water in winter?

The water is cold. The bottom is unclear. Every step must be felt out.

You cannot stride forward. You cannot rush because you feel like it — every step must slow down and look.

Not timidity. It is each step taken with care for the uncertainty of the bottom — the remainder always yields in concrete action. They do not use a ready-made construct to decide action. They let each step grow on its own.

This requires slowness.

Photograph Two: 犹呵，其若畏四邻
"Alert, ah — like one wary of all four neighbors."

What is being wary of neighbors?

Someone who has just moved to a new neighborhood, not yet knowing who surrounds them, what habits they have, what conventions they follow — always listening, watching, attending.

Not fear. Not assuming familiarity.

One adept at the dao relates to the environment this way —

Never assumes they already understand what surrounds them.

They know that the environment always changes, the remainder always yields, what was familiar yesterday may already be different today. So they are always perceiving, always calibrating — no preconceptions, no complacency, no assumption of knowing everything.

Photograph Three: 严呵，其若客
"Formal, ah — like a guest."

What is being a guest?

At someone else's home — not taking the seat of honor, not moving first with chopsticks, not rummaging through things, not treating it as one's own home.

Dignity is not constraint. It is knowing this is not your position.

One adept at the dao is a guest at every position —

Not seizing the main position; not treating any position as their own.

This runs through ch. 7's "not self-producing" and ch. 13's "taking the self as the world" — their sense of identity is very light, their sense of position very light. Wherever they are they are visitors, not hosts.

Photograph Four: 涣呵，其若凌泽
"Dissolving, ah — like ice at the edge of a pond."

What is ice about to melt?

A winter's solid ice surface, come spring — not yet melted, but already audible: water moving underneath, the ice surface subtly loosening — on the verge of opening.

One adept at the dao holds this posture —

Loose, not taut, not insistent, always ready to change.

They do not freeze themselves in a single posture.

Like ice on the verge of melting, holding the state of "about to loosen."

Not diffuse — the ice is still there.

Not frozen — ready to melt at any moment.

Photograph Five: 沌呵，其若朴
"Undivided, ah — like the unhewn."

What is uncarved wood (the unhewn)?

A block of raw timber. No particular shape, no definite purpose, not yet a piece of furniture or a work of craft — plain, not-yet-formed.

Precisely because not yet formed, it can still become any thing.

One adept at the dao is like this internally —

Whole, undivided, not cut into pieces by various constructs.

They do not cut themselves into "rational / emotional," "public / private," "work / life," "belief A / belief B." They remain whole.

Photograph Six: 湷呵，其若浊
"Turbid, ah — like muddy water."

What is muddy water?

Just-stirred water, post-rain river water, water with silt — opaque, not clear, temporarily not transparent.

One adept at the dao often appears "muddy" to outsiders —

Cannot be seen through; not immediately legible.

Why?

Because they do not turn themselves into a transparent legible construct for others to read at a glance.

Not that they are hiding; they simply do not perform.

A person legible at a glance is necessarily performing that legible image — performing a clear person, performing a vivid role, performing a definite stance.

One adept at the dao does not perform. So outsiders find them muddy.

Photograph Seven: 旷呵，其若谷
"Vast, ah — like a valley."

What is a valley?

Empty, wide, capable of receiving — wind blows in and out, water flows in and out, sound resonates, all things grow.

A valley is not emptiness — it is a space that can receive.

One adept at the dao has this receiving capacity —

Not filling themselves full; leaving space.

Only a person who holds space can have their own remainder yield through them — their own remainder, the remainder of those they nurture, the remainder of the situation, all can operate in the space they have left.

五、Seven Self-Measuring Rulers

The seven photographs together draw one person in seven positions of daily operation —

Slow in action. Alert to the environment. Light in sense of position. Loose in posture. Undivided inside. Not performing. Vast in capacity.

But these seven are not seven personality types.

They are seven self-measuring rulers — which readers can use right now to ask where they are.

If Laozi were writing today, he might have us self-measure like this —

Have I slowed down today? Or was every decision made in five minutes, every deadline chased?

Have I assumed I know everything? Entering a new team, new industry, new relationship — did I listen first or speak first?

Have I seized the main position? In a meeting — did I rush to speak and set the tone, or did I first watch what others were doing?

Have I frozen myself in a role? "I am a senior engineer, so I should do X" — has this become my default?

Have I cut myself into pieces? Is the me at work completely different from the me at home — switching back and forth, even I can't reconcile them?

Have I been performing? One version on LinkedIn, another on Twitter, a third in front of friends — which is the real me?

Has my schedule left space? Or is it packed from eight in the morning to ten at night, even meals on Zoom?

Each ruler can be used to ask yourself right now — not to grade yourself (grading is again a construct), but to sense where you are right now.

Seeing where you are is one calibration.

Laozi draws these seven photographs not to establish a high position you cannot reach.

He is pointing at the seven photographs and saying —

"This is a walkable direction."

In this moment you are on one photograph; the next moment you may not be. The next moment back on it, then off again.

The position is momentary; the door is always open — the same as ch. 7's "the streets are full of sages."

You can take one step toward any photograph at any moment. Take the step — and in that moment you are at that photograph's position.

六、Slow to Clear, Slow to Grow — Not Wanting Fullness

After drawing the seven photographs, Laozi describes this person's practice —

"浊而静之徐清，安以动之徐生。"

"When turbid, still it — slowly it clears. When settled, move it — slowly it grows."

When the water is muddy, do not stir it — still it, let it slowly clear on its own.

The key is the character 徐 — unhurried; not forcing; letting it be.

A different person encountering muddy water might use a filter, use alum, use any means to force it clear —

That is colonizing.

One adept at the dao does not do this — they go still, and wait for the remainder to settle and clear on its own.

When things are settled and about to change — do not force the change, do not rush — let it slowly grow on its own.

Same principle. A different person might force themselves to produce results, force change —

That too is colonizing. Colonizing one's own rhythm.

One adept at the dao lets it come by itself.

This is the practice of 徐 — slow down; let the process operate at its own rhythm; colonize neither your own nor another's rhythm.

"保此道者不欲盈。"

"One who holds this dao does not desire fullness."

What is fullness?

Constructs fully established. Positions fully occupied. Identity fully maintained. Results fully accumulated. Fullness fully achieved.

Full — stopped.

Not desiring fullness is not fearing fullness. It is not taking fullness as the goal.

A different person might spend a lifetime chasing fullness — fully build the career, fully grow the influence, fully accumulate the wealth, fully know, fully be known.

One adept at the dao simply does not pursue that fullness — they act, they live, they nurture others, but they do not chase after fullness.

Not desiring fullness and the seven photographs are completely consistent — all expressions of not filling the position full.

七、Covered and Not Completing

Final line —

"夫唯不欲盈，故能蔽而不成。"

"Precisely because they do not desire fullness, they can be covered and not complete."

There is a one-character textual difference here — silk manuscript: "蔽而不成," standard text: "蔽而新成."

First, the character 蔽 (bì).

Commentators have often read 蔽 here as 敝 (worn, tattered), reading the whole line as "can endure wearing out and newly complete." But the silk manuscript's original character is 蔽 — meaning to cover, to not display.

Reading the original character — 蔽 is precisely the key word of all seven photographs in this chapter.

One adept at the dao does not display themselves (muddy water), does not seize the main position (being a guest), outsiders find them unrecognizable — all of this is 蔽: the posture of not displaying.

Laozi at the closing uses 蔽 to name the common underlying color of all seven photographs.

Back to the textual variant.

Silk manuscript: "蔽而不成" — covering themselves and not taking completion as the goal.

Standard text: "蔽而新成" — covering themselves and then newly completing.

Silk-manuscript reading: covering-and-not-displaying is not-displaying; not completing is not taking completion as the goal — both move in the same direction, both are the thoroughgoing posture of "no." Not pursuing fullness. Not displaying. Not seeking completion.

Consistent with the preceding "not desiring fullness" — not desiring fullness, of course not desiring completion either.

Life itself is the operation; it is not for the sake of achieving anything.

Standard text's "newly complete" reading degrades this posture —

蔽 becomes a means (temporarily covering oneself), newly complete becomes the goal.

On this reading, one adept at the dao temporarily does not display as a way of eventually newly completing — not displaying, not desiring fullness, yielding, all become tactics for achieving "newly complete."

Laozi shifts from a person who does not take completion as the goal to a strategist who advances by retreating.

Reading the silk manuscript —

One adept at the dao genuinely does not take completion as the goal.

They live, they act, they nurture others — but they do not chase that "completion."

Not strategy. Posture.

This posture is far rarer than "advancing by retreating" —

The person advancing by retreating is calculating at every step whether the retreat can eventually advance.
The person not taking completion as the goal simply does not run that calculation — they act because this thing should be done; they help because this person needs help; they write because there is something to write —

Each thing is itself the goal.

There is no "completed state" waiting for them outside.

Life itself is the process.

Coming back to this chapter's opening —

After reading the first fourteen chapters, readers might ask: "If I encountered such a person, would I recognize them?"

Reading through Chapter 15, ask this question again —

Perhaps you have already been that person in certain moments.

One time you participated in a meeting without seizing the main position — in that moment you were on photograph three.
One time you slowed down to think through something before doing it — in that moment you were on photograph one.
One time you did not perform; you let yourself be a bit muddy — in that moment you were on photograph six.
One time you left half a day completely unscheduled — in that moment you were on photograph seven.

You have already been on the photographs — just not consecutively.

The seven photographs are not a destination.

They are seven positions you can stop at more often.

Each time you stop — the door opens once.
