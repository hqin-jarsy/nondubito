Chapter 75 of 81

当活着本身，已经很费劲

When Staying Alive Takes Too Much

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

民之饥也，以其上食税之多也，是以饥。百姓之不治也，以其上之有以为也，是以不治。民之轻死也，以其求生之厚也，是以轻死。夫唯无以生为者，是贤贵生。

一、别急着问他为什么不在乎了

有个人从前做事很起劲。开会会提问题，事情交出去以后还会追问结果。后来，他渐渐不问了。安排什么就做什么，夸奖时笑一下，批评时也不太解释。

旁人很容易给这段变化起名字：没热情了，躺平了，心态出了问题。这些话说出来，事情似乎就被放回了他自己身上。接下来，无非是谈一次话，讲一讲目标，提醒他应该振作。

可是，如果他每天只是维持生活，就已经用掉大部分力气呢？如果每一次额外付出，都换来下一次更理所当然的加码呢？你要求他重新在乎之前，是否看过那些力气到底去了哪里？

当然，沉默和退缩可能有许多原因，不能隔着一段表现就替人诊断。第七十五章提供的，是一个常被省略的观察方向：先别只检查那个看起来出了问题的人，也看看他被放在怎样的条件里。

二、把目光转向作出安排的人

民之饥也，以其上食税之多也，是以饥。

这句话的归因非常明确。人们挨饿，不是因为一句笼统的“不够努力”，而与在上者取走太多有关。问责的目光，不能只落在已经缺少的人身上。

第二句说：“百姓之不治也，以其上之有以为也，是以不治。”治理没有达到应有的结果，要检查上面那些不断施加的作为。制造问题的，可能恰恰是自称前来解决问题的安排。

第三句转向“民之轻死”与“求生之厚”。这里的“其”怎样理解，不同传本和解释各有侧重；不能像前两句那样，不经说明就把所有主语排成同一张表。不过，它仍让生存与权力、资源和负担之间的关系进入视野。

归因的方向会改变人的动作。只看到员工不够积极，容易不断增加考核；看到哪些任务互相打架、哪些负担被反复推到同一群人身上，才可能动到真正需要改变的地方。

这不是说任何个人错误都应被抹掉，而是说，追问个人之前，不能把制度和安排当成无须检查的背景。背景也由具体的人作出，它同样应当回答问题。

三、一个字的不同，不等于责任翻了方向

本站采用“百姓之不治”，通行本常作“民之难治”。前者可以让人注意治理的结果，后者更直接地说难以治理，这一差别值得读。

但通行本的这句话，同样把原因指向在上者：“以其上之有为，是以难治。”不能只截取“难治”二字，就说它把原本属于治理者的责任转给了百姓。王弼的注文甚至明确说“皆由上不由其下”。

这样核对，不会削弱本篇对权力的追问。相反，它让我们少依赖一个并不存在的版本对立，把注意力放回两个文本都留下的难题：为什么施行得越多，反而越难治理？

第六十五章也讨论过知、智与统治的关系。那里可以有新的哲学读法，却不能单凭一两个字，就断定后人存心把一切改成责怪百姓。

读古书时，有时最值得放下的，也是那个过于整齐的解释：早的一定都对，晚的一定改坏。文字的历史未必如此省事，而今天该承担的责任，也不需要靠这个故事才能成立。

四、好理由，不能替后果作答

“有以为”比“有为”多一个“以”。本文由此想到，人常常借着某个理由行动。但“以”本身并不必然意味着虚假的借口，也不能证明凡是有目的的行动都在压迫别人。

真正值得警惕的，是一个正当目标怎样变成免于核对的通行证。为了增长，于是任何临时加码都不用再解释；为了规范，于是每个人都要重复填写没人阅读的材料；为了孩子好，于是孩子自己的理由总是排在最后。

你要是提出异议，谈话就突然换了题目。本来是在问这个办法是否合适，却被问成：难道你反对增长，反对规范，反对别人好起来吗？目标替手段挡住了检查。

善意当然有意义。真心帮助和存心控制，并不是毫无差别。可善意不能取消被帮助者实际承受的重量，也不能替代一句很具体的询问：这样做，真的帮到了你吗？

有些介入是必要的。补充资源、建立安全规则、纠正不公平，都可能扩大人的余地。我们要看的不是那只手有没有动，而是它动过以后，别人的生活是否更能由自己掌握，还是只能更努力地适应那只手。

五、求生，为什么会变得那么重

民之轻死也，以其求生之厚也，是以轻死。

“求生之厚”不只有一种理解。有的传本明确写到在上者厚求其生，也有从过度追求保全、丰厚生活来解释的读路。本文沿着前文的饥与扰，侧重另一层现实：维持生活所需付出的代价，可能已经把人逼得太紧。

这不是把“厚”的字典意义简单定成昂贵，而是把古文放进一种我们能够看见的处境。房租、照护、通勤、不能中断的工作，一项一项未必惊人，合起来却可能不给人留下喘息的地方。

在这种时候，外面再送来一句“要有长远眼光”，可能显得很远。人不是不知道将来重要，而是今天已经需要他把明天的一部分力气借过来。

负担沉重的人，也并不必然失去希望、爱惜或恐惧。有人恰恰用很大的努力守住这些。把他们一概描画成什么都不在乎，仍然是在用一个解释遮住具体的人。

因此，这句话不宜用来猜透别人，倒适合提醒有能力改变条件的人：在要求热情之前，先看看有没有休息；在要求投入之前，先看看额外付出会不会得到公平回应；在要求承担风险之前，先看看失败的代价最终落在谁身上。

上一章问，人在不畏死的处境里，威吓还能做什么。这一章把目光带向更前面：不只研究怎样使人服从，也研究怎样不把人的生活逼到只能服从或绝望之间。

六、把力气用在条件上

“夫唯无以生为者，是贤贵生。”关于这句，既可以有个人不以厚养生命为执念的理解，也可以结合全章来谈在上者怎样对待人的生活。本文取后一条应用方向，不宣称它已排除了所有别的句法。

有一种管理者一直很忙。新会议、新表格、新计划，每一样都说是为了团队更好。另一种也忙，却是在替人协调资源、撤掉重复的要求、澄清相互矛盾的优先级。后者的工作不一定更轻，只是不总压到每个人眼前。

这并不是“一个做事，一个不做事”。一个不断要求人证明自己适合条件，另一个愿意检查条件是否适合人。两者都可能需要决定和行动，只是决定不再自动以更多控制为答案。

所谓把干扰挡在外面，也不是让别人永远不知道现实有多难。必要的信息需要共享，真正的限制应当说明。以保护之名垄断所有消息，同样可能使人失去判断的机会。

不打着“为你好”的旗号占住生活，并不妨碍提供帮助。它要求的，是帮助能够被讨论、被调整，在不合适的时候也能被拒绝。照顾不是把人安放在我的好意里面，而是让他更有可能活出自己的日子。

七、先改变一件真正耗人的事

如果你有安排工作的权力，可以先找一项所有人都在做、却说不清为何还要做的事。取消它未必需要一场改革，却可能让许多人第二天少赶一次没有意义的截止时间。

如果一个人突然不再主动，不妨少问一次“你怎么了”，多问一次“最近有什么一直压着你”。问的时候要给对方不回答的余地，也要准备面对那个可能涉及自己的答案。

当别人说“都是为了你好”，可以把谈话拉回具体安排：哪一部分真的有帮助，哪一部分增加了负担，有没有别的做法？不必先否认对方的好意，才有资格讨论它的后果。

而如果你就是那个已经很累的人，这篇文章不是来提醒你还应更懂事、更谦逊。能看见负担从哪里来，并不意味着你立刻有能力改变它。先确认自己不是因为“不够积极”才失去被尊重的资格，也是一件重要的事。

这章的希望，不在一个人终于学会忍受更多，而在那些能够少耗掉一点人的安排，开始被认真作出。

八、三章之间，问的是同一份生活

第七十二章提醒人不要倚赖威压，第七十四章追问最重的威吓能否解决问题，这里则转身检查，人们正在怎样生活。

三章并不是一条所有人都会经历的心理曲线。它们放在一起，可以让治理者不再只从不满出现的那一刻开始思考，而愿意回头看自己参与造成了哪些条件。

本篇与第五十七章的无为也在这里相遇。少一点扰动，不是希望需要帮助的人自己消失在视线之外，而是使有用的支持不再附带不必要的占据。

下一章会以柔与刚、生与死的形象，谈仍然能够改变的生命。我们可以先从这里带过去一个问题：一个人不再愿意动，究竟是他只剩下僵硬，还是周围已经没有给他转身的地方？

I. The person who stopped volunteering

They used to bring ideas to the meeting. If something went wrong, they tried to fix it; if a colleague needed help, they found time. Now they do what is required and leave. Their manager says they have lost motivation. That may be true, but it explains less than it seems to.

What happened between those two scenes? There may be personal circumstances no one knows about. Interests can change. There may also be a history of excessive workload, ignored suggestions, and new demands added whenever the person proved capable of surviving the previous ones.

We cannot diagnose withdrawal from a distance. We can resist immediately turning it into an individual defect. A change in someone’s conduct may also reveal something about the conditions under which they are expected to live and work.

Chapter 75 directs attention upward. Faced with hunger, disorder, and dangerous indifference, it asks what those with power have been doing. This does not abolish personal responsibility. It prevents personal responsibility from becoming a screen behind which the decisions of the powerful disappear.

II. Three lines of inquiry

The first causal statement is direct: “民之饥也，以其上食税之多也，是以饥.” People go hungry because those above consume too much through taxation. The inquiry does not begin by asking why hungry people fail to work harder. It identifies excessive extraction.

The second associates the failure of governance with action from above: “百姓之不治也，以其上之有以为也，是以不治.” The problem is not presented simply as defective human material awaiting stronger control. The arrangements imposed upon people may themselves be producing the difficulty.

The third connects taking death lightly with “求生之厚.” Here interpretation requires greater care. The phrase has been connected with an excessive pursuit of life or preservation, with rulers’ lavish concern for their own living in some readings, and, in this series’ social application, with the burdens of maintaining existence.

What joins the three lines is a refusal to examine people as though they lived outside political and economic arrangements. If an order extracts too much, adds obstacles, and makes each day difficult, the responses it produces cannot be understood solely by inspecting the character of those who respond.

We do not need a single cause for every social problem. We do need to include the causes that power can most conveniently leave out of the discussion: its own decisions, incentives, and demands.

III. Poor governance is not proof of ungovernable people

The adopted text uses “不治”; the received version commonly has “难治,” difficult to govern. It is tempting to turn this difference into a complete reversal of blame: one version criticizes government, the other blames the people. But the received text also explicitly attributes the difficulty to the rulers’ activity.

The political criticism does not require a story in which a later variant erased it. It remains present in both formulations. Wang Bi’s commentary likewise directs the explanation toward those above rather than those below.

The comparison can still sharpen our hearing. “This team is impossible” closes an inquiry that “Why can’t this team work effectively?” has just opened. The second question makes room for conflicting priorities, missing resources, distorted incentives, or rules that prevent people from solving the very problems for which they are held answerable.

Chapter 65 questioned, in the reading followed here, the clever management of people as objects. It did not require withholding education from them. Chapter 75 adds material weight to that concern: people need conditions in which to act, not merely encouragement to adopt a better attitude.

Changing the question will not repair an organization by itself. It can stop the explanation ending before it reaches the place where change is possible. Sometimes that place is the desk from which the complaint about everyone else has just been issued.

IV. Everything has a good reason

“有以为” does not inherently mean inventing a dishonest pretext. An intervention can have a genuine purpose. That is exactly why we need to examine what its purpose makes easy to overlook.

A report is added for transparency, a meeting for coordination, an approval stage for safety, a metric for efficiency. Each measure has a defensible explanation. Together, they may create a setting in which people spend more time demonstrating that they work than doing the work.

The same pattern appears in family life. One activity is chosen for a child’s future, another for development, another to ensure they do not fall behind. Eventually the schedule is full of opportunities and empty of time that belongs to the child. Nobody intended to overwhelm them. The burden does not vanish because each decision had an affectionate motive.

This criticism is not permission to remove essential safeguards or describe every shared obligation as oppression. A safety rule may protect exactly those who have the least power to refuse. We need to ask what problem a measure solves, what burden it adds, who bears that burden, and whether protection can be achieved in a less costly way.

“We are doing this for your good” should begin a conversation with the person whose good is being invoked. If they can never question the measure, their welfare risks becoming a respectable name for somebody else’s preference.

V. The cost of keeping afloat

We cannot settle “求生之厚” by declaring that “厚” means only expensive or burdensome. Other readings concern the intensity or excess with which life is pursued and preserved. The social reading developed here attends to the weight of subsistence because it illuminates the chapter’s political concern, not because it is the only possible translation.

Life becomes heavy when every basic need consumes the margin that would help meet the next one. Rent, food, care, transport; then the hours lost to administration and the energy spent continually proving entitlement to assistance. The issue is not only money. It is how much remains after the essentials have been sustained.

From a comfortable position, certain decisions can look reckless or indifferent. From inside a life with almost no room to maneuver, they may reflect severely restricted options. Understanding that does not mean endorsing every action, or imagining that poverty necessarily destroys fear, hope, or care. People under great pressure often protect what sustains them with remarkable attention.

The connection with chapter 74 is clear. Increasing punishment does not, by itself, create a life in which people can place confidence. To understand why someone takes a risk, we must also ask what realistic alternatives are available.

Those alternatives can improve. Wages, housing, services, rights, and practical assistance belong inside this reflection. They are ways of restoring room to a life. A criticism of pressure need not end in resignation about the conditions that produce it.

VI. Supporting life without taking it over

The closing sentence, “夫唯无以生为者，是贤贵生,” permits different interpretations of action and the preservation of life. In the political application followed here, it invites us to imagine supporting people’s existence without turning the whole of it into an object of management. This does not require claiming that every grammatical subject in the chapter must be the ruler.

Consider two managers. One demonstrates commitment through an uninterrupted stream of instructions. Every difficulty receives a procedure; every procedure receives a follow-up meeting. The other establishes intelligible priorities, obtains resources, removes obstacles, and protects the team from incompatible demands.

The second manager is also working. Sometimes they work hard so that other people’s work can proceed with less friction. Their activity has not disappeared. It has stopped occupying the center of everyone else’s experience.

Shielding a team from interference must not mean hiding every difficulty. Real constraints and necessary information should be shared. A manager who monopolizes knowledge of the situation to spare people worry may also deprive them of the chance to exercise judgment.

A useful road does not require travelers to remember its builder at every step. Likewise, some conditions support living precisely because they allow people to attend to more than the system that provides them.

Noninterference is not abandonment. When food, protection, or necessary care is missing, stepping aside may deepen dependence. Providing resources and enforceable rights can be the appropriate intervention. The question is whether assistance enlarges a person’s capacity to live or demands ownership of that life in return.

VII. Review the burden, not only the attitude

When someone stops participating, we can ask what has changed before recommending enthusiasm. Listening will not deliver a complete explanation immediately, but it prevents our preferred explanation from arriving as an established fact.

If we make decisions, we can examine the accumulated burden rather than justify each piece in isolation. How many priorities have we simultaneously declared urgent? What did we stop requiring when we added something new? Who absorbs the invisible work our measures do not record?

We can also examine something we call help. Can the recipient refuse it, alter it, or say it does not work for them? Can they receive support without surrendering all discretion? Those answers distinguish care from a well-intentioned occupation of someone else’s life.

A person carrying the burden may need more than inward clarity: collective support, negotiation, outside resources, or protection. They should not be asked to solve a structural problem through serenity. Equally, removing unnecessary demands should not mean dismantling obligations that protect safety, fairness, or people who depend on a service.

One question gathers these inquiries together: after our decisions, do people have more room to live and work, or are they simply easier for us to monitor? The answer may require changing something we had proudly described as an improvement.

VIII. Making room for a response

Chapter 72 distinguished fear from trust. Chapter 74 examined the limits of threats and the seizure of another person’s function. Chapter 75 returns to the conditions surrounding both: what is extracted, what is demanded, and how much room remains for living.

This is not an inevitable sequence toward despair. It is an invitation to interrupt causes we can recognize and change. Chapter 57 already questioned the multiplication of measures that can end up generating the difficulties they promise to solve.

Chapter 76 will begin by associating life with flexibility and death with rigidity. We will need to read that as an image, not a biological classification. The transition is nevertheless suggestive: after asking how much weight we put on life, we ask whether we leave it a capacity to respond and develop.

Perhaps the person who stopped volunteering does not need another talk about commitment. Perhaps a burden needs to be removed, a promise kept, or an explanation heard by someone willing to change an arrangement. Enthusiasm cannot simply be ordered. Some of the conditions in which it might return can be built.
