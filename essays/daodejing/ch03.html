第三章
Chapter 3 of 81

不上贤

Don't Elevate the Worthy

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、愚民派的骂名

老子被骂愚民派两千年。

主要源头就是第三章。

"不上贤，使民不争。不贵难得之货，使民不为盗。不见可欲，使民不乱。"

传统常见字面的误读是：不让老百姓崇尚有才能的人，他们就不争；不让稀有的东西显得贵，他们就不偷；不展示值得欲求的东西，他们就不乱。

后面还有更狠的：

"虚其心，实其腹，弱其志，强其骨。"

传统常见误读直接理解，或者说曲解成：把民的心搞空，把民的志搞弱，让他们腹饱骨壮，这样他们就成为了一个理想的、可控的、没有思想的群体。

历代不喜欢老子的人，主要就抓这一章。儒家骂他反智，新文化运动骂他反进步，现代读者觉得他主张奴化。

这个骂名其实更多是通行本和注释需要背的锅，因为它确实容易读成愚民。

但读帛书本，尤其是读到这一章字面深度顶位那一句，整个意思翻了过来。

二、上，是动词

先看前三句怎么读才对。

"不上贤"——"上"是动词。使动用法。"使……为上"。

老子说的不是"不崇尚贤者这种情感态度"，是"不要做'把贤立为标签'这个动作"。

通行本写"尚"，把动作软化成了情感态度。帛书的"上"更具体——它是一刀。

立"贤"为构，是给社会切一刀。

这一刀切下去，世界上原本各有所长的人，被这把尺子分成了两堆——一堆叫"贤"，一堆叫"不贤"。后面这堆不是原来就在那里的，是这把尺子立完那一刹被甩出来的。

接下来发生什么？

人开始为了落在"贤"那一堆里而互相挤。

争从哪里来？不是人天生就争。是有了这把尺子之后，人开始争。

这件事在每个科技公司里每天都在发生。

立"卓越员工"这个标签，所有不在这个标签里的人就被自动归为"不卓越"。然后大家开始在年度考核里争——不是争具体做什么事，是争落在标签的哪一边。立"独角兽"这个标签，所有不到十亿估值的公司就成了相对失败。然后大家开始为了估值数字而做不该做的事。

立一个标签等于切一刀，等于制造一类受压者，等于召唤出一种争。

"不贵难得之货"——同样的逻辑。立"稀有"为价值标签。所有没拥有稀有物的人被相对贬损。盗从这把尺子来。盗不是人性恶，是稀有这个构召唤出来的。

"不见可欲"——立"值得欲求的"为标签。欲从这把尺子来。欲不是心里本有的，是被立的构召唤出来的。

三句话的真正意思是：

不要做立构这个动作。

立一个，甩一类。立一个，制造一类受压者。

第二章讲过：天下皆知美之为美，恶已。立美必甩丑，这是凿构循环的代价。第三章就是把这个代价落到治理上——

治理的第一步不是怎么做，是先撤掉那些在立构的动作。

三句都"不"字打头——这不是消极避世，是治理层的减法。是先把会产生殖民压力的动作撤掉，再说怎么做。

三、四向不是养生口诀

讲完前三句的"撤"，老子接着讲圣人怎么"做"：

"是以圣人之治也，虚其心，实其腹，弱其志，强其骨，恒使民无知无欲也。"

这五句读起来像养生口诀。其实是精确的结构性操作。

关键在于——这些操作的对象是民，但动作的真义不在"让民变成什么样"，而在让民从圣人的构里脱出来。

为什么？

因为民的心、志、欲，很多时候不是民自己长出来的，是圣人立完构之后，民被殖民来的。

上贤一立，民就以"贤"为志。
贵货一立，民就以"得货"为欲。
可欲一见，民的心就被填满。

民看起来在追求自己想要的东西，其实在替圣人立的构跑腿。

这就是SAE框架里"内在殖民"的标准形态——你以为你在追求自己的目标，其实你在跑别人立的构的服务。

最常见的版本是这样的：一个孩子从小被告知"学霸"是一个值得成为的标签。他长大之后真的拼了命去做"学霸"，把所有时间都投到那个方向。他觉得这是他自己的志。但其实——是上面那把尺子下来之后，他被切到了"贤"那一侧，然后他余下的一生都在替这把尺子工作。

他自己的志在哪里？没机会长出来。位置被那个殖民来的假志占满了。

所以四向操作真正要做的事是——让出位置：

虚其心：把那些被圣人概念填进来的预测框架虚掉。心一空，民才能放自己的东西进去。
实其腹：让生命的底子有余量。这是四向里唯一真正"做"的动作。
弱其志：弱化那个被殖民来的假志。圣人若占着民那一位，民自己的志就没地方长。
强其骨：让民有立得住自己的志所需的底子。

四向合起来：让出民自立所需的那个位置。

最后那一句"恒使民无知无欲"——很多人读到这里就跳起来：你看，老子要让民没有知识、没有欲望，这不是愚民是什么？

不是。

老子说的是：让民不被知者的构殖民——让民没有"知者那种知"，没有"知者那种欲"。

民自己的知、自己的欲，要自己长出来。不能让知者的志灌进民的位。

这一句一读透，老子的整个面貌就翻了一遍：他不是愚民派，是反殖民派，尤其反在位者用自己的知和欲去殖民普通的民本来自己可以有的知和欲。

四、字面深度顶位

第三章最关键的一句在最后：

"使夫知不敢弗为而已，则无不治矣。"

通行本写的是：

"使夫智者不敢为也。"

两个本子，方向完全相反。

帛书：使夫知不敢弗为——双重否定。让有知的人不敢"不为"，等于必须为。

通行本：使夫智者不敢为——单重否定。让有智的人不敢做事。

通行本一读，整章的核心姿态就变成了：圣人最高的治理是把所有有能力的人都禁声。

这跟全书反殖民、不反知、不反为的整体立场完全不合。

帛书读法是：

让那些有知的人不敢撤场。

老子从来不反"知"，也不反"为"。他反的是立构殖民。圣人要做的不是让知者退场，是——

让所有知者都出场，但都不立构。

"而已"两个字是分寸。圣人对知者的要求只到"不许撤场"为止——不立标准、不立指引、不立规则。底线之上，知者各自做事，圣人不干涉。

这一段把整章前半翻了一面。

前半讲对民——让位面：让出位置让民自立。
后半讲对知者——防范面：不让撤场让知者必须出场。

两边一起做，才完整。

为什么知者必须不许躲？

想象一下：如果圣人真的让所有有能力的人都退场，会发生什么？

那些不肯退场的人会瞬间涌入填补这个空位——而不肯退场的人，往往就是殖民意愿最强的那一种。

圣人一撤一退，殖民者就涌进。

所以圣人涵育的真义不是"让所有知者退场"，是——

让所有知者出场，但都不立构。

这就是双向涵育的字面：

对民：让位。让民自立其志。
对知者：防范。不让撤场。
两边都不撒手，两边都不立构殖民。

防范本身就是涵育的一种——不是涵育之外的另一种动作，是涵育在不同对象上的不同显现。对民显现为让位，对知者显现为防范。

通行本把"弗"字删了。一删，双向涵育里的"防范面"就丢了，整章读成了愚民派宣言。

帛书把"弗"字留着。这是为什么用帛书做底比较稳。

五、为什么没什么治不了

老子的最后一句：

"则无不治矣。"

没什么治理不了的。

这不是"一切会自己慢慢好起来"的软话。"无不治"是最强的治理声明——所有问题都被治住了。

为什么这件事能这么大？

因为问题的源头被釜底抽薪了：

民不被殖民——所以民自己长出的诉求是真诉求。真诉求跟环境本来就是适配的。不需要"治"。
知者不撤场——所以做事的人在场。做事的人在场，事情就不会无人做。
知者也不立构——所以做事的过程里不会再生出新的需要治理的伪问题。

殖民问题不再产生。撤场问题不再产生。

需要治理的那些问题根本不存在了。

这是老子的悖论：

治理能力的最大化，来自不立构殖民加不许撤场。

越"有为"——立更多构、压更多层——治理越难。因为每一次立构都制造新的殖民压力，新的殖民压力制造新的不治问题，然后得再加新的构去处理新问题。

殖民创造需要更多殖民去处理的问题。

这是一个自我放大的死循环。

现代治理的整个思路——立法、监管、增加部门、升级系统——基本都在"有为"的逻辑里。每一个新问题呼唤一个新机构、一条新法规、一套新流程。问题越治越多，因为治理本身在制造问题。

老子两千四百年前就看到了这条死路。

他给的解法不是放任——而是双管齐下：不立构殖民加不许撤场。

两件事一起做。

撤掉殖民动作，那些由殖民产生的不治问题立刻就不存在了。但只撤是不够的——还得防范知者撤场，否则空位被更糟的殖民者填补。

两件事一起做，才是"无不治"的全义。

六、不是愚民派

回到那个骂名。

老子被骂愚民派两千年。

读完这一章——按帛书读——这个骂名站不住。

老子不反知，不反为，不反贤。他反的是把这些立成构、用构去殖民别人的层。

老子的"虚其心"不是让民没思想，是让民的思想从圣人的构里脱出来。
老子的"使民无知"不是让民愚蠢，是让民不被知者的知殖民。
老子的"使夫知不敢弗为"不是让有能力的人闭嘴，是让有能力的人必须出场。

老子讲的从来不是消极避世。他讲的是另一种主动——

主动撤掉立构动作。
主动让出位置。
主动不让撤场。

这三种"主动"加起来，比立一万条法规都强。

老子不要愚民。他要自立其志的民。
他不要顺从的知者。他要不撤场的知者。
他不要更多治理。他要让需要治理的问题消失。

第二章给出涵育的操作定义。第三章给出涵育在治理层的具体处方——包括它的让位面，也包括它的防范面。

防范这一面，被通行本删了。两千年来读者读到的是一个反智的老子。

帛书把它还原了。

I. The Charge of Obscurantism

Laozi has been accused of being an obscurantist for two thousand years.

The main source is Chapter Three.

"Don't elevate the worthy as a label, and the people won't compete. Don't prize scarce goods, and the people won't steal. Don't display things worth desiring, and the people won't be thrown into disorder."

The common misreading: don't let people admire the talented, so they won't compete; don't make rare things look valuable, so they won't steal; don't show things worth wanting, so they won't be unruly.

Then comes something harsher:

"Empty their hearts, fill their bellies, weaken their ambitions, strengthen their bones."

The common misreading takes this directly — or twists it — as: hollow out the people's minds, weaken their will, keep their bellies full and their bodies strong, so they become an ideal, manageable, thoughtless mass.

This charge has been leveled at Laozi throughout history. Confucians call him anti-intellectual. The New Culture Movement called him anti-progress. Modern readers find him advocating subjugation.

In fairness, this reputation belongs mostly to the received text and its commentaries — the chapter really can be read that way in that version.

But read the silk manuscript — especially the pivotal line at the chapter's deepest point — and the whole meaning reverses.

II. "Up" Is a Verb

First: how should the three opening lines be read?

"Don't up the worthy" — "up" is a verb. A causative: "cause to be elevated."

Laozi is not saying "don't have the attitude of admiring the worthy." He is saying "don't perform the action of erecting 'the worthy' as a label."

The received text uses shàng (esteem), which softens the action into an attitude. The silk manuscript's character means to physically elevate — it is a cut.

Erecting "the worthy" as a construct is cutting society with a knife.

Once cut, people who each had their own strengths get divided into two piles — one called "worthy," one called "not worthy." The second pile didn't exist before. It was thrown off the instant the ruler was erected.

What happens next?

People start pushing against each other to land in the "worthy" pile.

Where does the competition come from? Not from human nature. From having this ruler. The competition didn't exist before the ruler; it appeared with the ruler.

This happens in every tech company every day.

Erect the "exceptional employee" label, and everyone not in that label gets automatically classified as "not exceptional." Then everyone starts competing in the annual review — not competing to do the right things, but competing to land on the right side of the label. Erect the "unicorn" label, and every company below one billion valuation becomes a relative failure. Then everyone starts doing things they shouldn't in pursuit of that valuation number.

Erecting a label equals making a cut, equals producing a class of people under pressure, equals summoning a form of competition.

"Don't prize scarce goods" — same logic. Erect "rarity" as a value label. Everyone without the scarce thing gets relatively diminished. Theft comes from this ruler. Theft is not a human failing — it's what the scarcity construct summons.

"Don't display things worth desiring" — erect "worthy of desire" as a label. Desire comes from this ruler. Desire is not innately in the heart; it's summoned by the erection of the construct.

The real meaning of all three lines:

Don't perform the action of erecting constructs.

Erect one, throw off one class. Erect one, produce one class of people under pressure.

Chapter Two showed this: when all recognize the beautiful as beautiful, ugliness is already there. Erecting the beautiful necessarily throws off the ugly — that's the cost of the chisel-construct cycle. Chapter Three applies this cost to governance:

The first step of governance is not "what to do." It is first removing the actions that are already erecting constructs.

All three lines begin with "don't" — not passive withdrawal, but subtraction at the governance level. First remove the actions generating colonizing pressure, then talk about what to do.

III. The Four Directions Are Not a Wellness Formula

After the three subtractive lines, Laozi turns to what the sage does:

"Therefore in the sage's governance: empty their hearts, fill their bellies, weaken their ambitions, strengthen their bones. Enduringly cause the people to be without the knowing and without the desiring of the knowing-class."

These five lines read like a wellness formula. They are in fact precise structural operations.

The key: these operations target the people, but their real purpose is not "making the people into a certain type." It is freeing the people from the sage's constructs.

Why?

Because the people's hearts, ambitions, and desires are often not self-grown. They are colonized into the people once the sage's constructs are erected.

Erect "the worthy" — the people adopt "becoming worthy" as their ambition.
Prize scarce goods — the people adopt "acquiring those goods" as their desire.
Display what's worth wanting — the people's hearts get filled up.

The people look like they're pursuing what they want. They're actually running service for the sage's constructs.

This is the textbook form of "internal colonization" in the SAE framework — you think you're pursuing your own goals; you're actually running someone else's erection.

The most common version: a child is told from early on that "top student" is a label worth becoming. They grow up fighting to be a "top student," pouring all their time in that direction. They feel this is their own ambition. But actually — the ruler came down from above, cut them to the "worthy" side, and they spent the rest of their life working for that ruler.

Where is their actual ambition? It never had room to grow. The position was occupied by the colonized fake ambition.

So what the four operations are actually doing is — vacating the position:

Empty their hearts: clear out the predictive frameworks the sage's concepts have filled in. Once the heart is empty, the people can put their own things in.
Fill their bellies: give life's foundation a surplus. The only genuinely "doing" action in the four.
Weaken their ambitions: weaken the colonized fake ambition. If the sage occupies the people's position, their own ambition has nowhere to grow.
Strengthen their bones: give the people the foundation they need to stand in their own ambition.

The four together: vacate the position the people need in order to stand on their own.

The final line — "enduringly cause the people to be without knowing and without desiring" — makes many readers jump up: "You see! Laozi wants the people to have no knowledge, no desires. What is this if not obscurantism?"

No.

Laozi is saying: free the people from colonization by the knowing-class's constructs — free them from "the knowing-class's kind of knowing," from "the knowing-class's kind of desiring."

The people's own knowing, their own desiring, must grow on their own. The knowing-class's ambition must not be poured into the people's position.

Read through this line and Laozi's whole image flips: he is not an obscurantist, he is an anti-colonialist — specifically opposed to those in power using their own knowing and desiring to colonize the knowing and desiring that ordinary people could naturally develop on their own.

IV. The Pivotal Line

The most critical line in Chapter Three is the last:

"Cause those with knowing not to dare refrain from acting — that is all. Then there is nothing that cannot be governed."

The received text has:

"Cause the wise not to dare act."

Two versions, opposite directions.

Silk manuscript: cause those with knowing not to dare not-act — a double negative. Cause the knowledgeable not to dare abstain, which means they must act.

Received text: cause the wise not to dare act — a single negative. Cause the capable to dare do nothing.

Read the received text and the chapter's central posture becomes: the sage's supreme governance is silencing all capable people.

This is entirely incompatible with the book's overall stance — which is anti-colonization, not anti-knowing, not anti-acting.

The silk manuscript reading:

Cause those with knowing not to dare leave the field.

Laozi never opposes "knowing," never opposes "acting." What he opposes is erecting constructs and colonizing. What the sage must do is not send the knowers off stage — it is:

Have all knowers on stage, but none of them erecting constructs.

"That is all" — the two-character limit. The sage's requirement of knowers goes only as far as "you may not leave the field" — no erecting of standards, guidance, or rules. Above that baseline, knowers each do their work, and the sage does not interfere.

This reverses the first half of the chapter.

First half — toward the people — the vacating face: vacate the position, let the people stand on their own.
Second half — toward the knowers — the preventive face: keep them from leaving the field; the knowers must stay present.

Both sides done together, the picture is complete.

Why must the knowers not be allowed to hide?

Imagine: if the sage actually sent all capable people off stage, what would happen?

Those unwilling to leave would immediately rush in to fill the void — and those unwilling to leave are typically the ones with the strongest colonizing impulse.

The sage retreats; colonizers flood in.

So the real meaning of the sage's cultivation is not "send all knowers off stage." It is:

Have all knowers on stage, but none of them erecting constructs.

This is the text of two-directional cultivation:

Toward the people: vacate. Let the people stand up their own ambitions.
Toward the knowers: prevent. Don't let them leave the field.
Don't release either side; don't colonize either side.

Prevention is itself a form of cultivation — not something outside cultivation, but cultivation appearing differently for different targets. For the people it appears as vacating; for the knowers it appears as prevention.

The received text deleted the character meaning "not." Delete it and the preventive face of two-directional cultivation disappears, and the whole chapter reads as an obscurantist manifesto.

The silk manuscript keeps that character. This is why using the silk manuscript as base text is more reliable.

V. Why Nothing Cannot Be Governed

The final line:

"Then there is nothing that cannot be governed."

This is not the soft assurance that "everything will slowly work out on its own." "Nothing that cannot be governed" is the strongest possible governance statement — all problems are handled.

Why can this one approach accomplish so much?

Because the root of the problems has been cut away:

The people are not colonized — so the demands the people develop on their own are genuine demands. Genuine demands are naturally adapted to the environment. They don't need "governing."
The knowers stay present — so the people who do things are in the room. People are in the room; things get done.
The knowers don't erect constructs — so the process of doing things doesn't generate new pseudo-problems that need governing.

Colonization problems stop being produced. Absence problems stop being produced.

The things that would have needed governing simply don't exist.

This is Laozi's paradox:

Maximum governance capacity comes from non-colonizing non-erection plus non-absence prevention.

The more "having-action" — erecting more constructs, pressing more layers — the harder governance becomes. Because every construct erected produces new colonizing pressure, new colonizing pressure produces new ungovernable problems, and then new constructs must be added to address those new problems.

Colonization produces problems that require more colonization to handle.

A self-amplifying dead loop.

The entire logic of modern governance — legislation, regulation, adding departments, upgrading systems — is basically inside the "having-action" logic. Every new problem calls for a new institution, a new regulation, a new process. The more you govern the more problems appear, because the governing itself is producing problems.

Laozi saw this dead end twenty-four hundred years ago.

His solution is not laissez-faire — it is a two-pronged approach: non-colonizing non-erection plus non-absence prevention.

Both at once.

Remove the colonizing actions and the ungovernable problems that colonization produced simply cease to exist. But removal alone is not enough — you also need to prevent the knowers from leaving the field, or the vacancy gets filled by even worse colonizers.

Both at once — that is the full meaning of "nothing that cannot be governed."

VI. Not Obscurantism

Back to the charge.

Laozi has been called an obscurantist for two thousand years.

Read through this chapter — read it from the silk manuscript — and that charge cannot stand.

Laozi does not oppose knowing. Does not oppose acting. Does not oppose the worthy. What he opposes is erecting these things as constructs and using the constructs to colonize other people's layers.

Laozi's "empty their hearts" does not mean make the people mindless — it means free the people's thinking from the sage's constructs.
Laozi's "cause the people to be without knowing" does not mean make the people stupid — it means keep the people from being colonized by the knowing-class's knowing.
Laozi's "cause those with knowing not to dare refrain from acting" does not mean silence the capable — it means the capable must stay present and act.

Laozi has never preached passive withdrawal. He preaches a different kind of active:

Actively remove the construct-erecting actions.
Actively vacate the positions.
Actively keep people from leaving the field.

These three kinds of "active" together outperform ten thousand regulations.

Laozi does not want an obscured populace. He wants a people who stand up their own ambitions.
He does not want compliant knowers. He wants knowers who stay on stage.
He does not want more governance. He wants the problems that would need governing to disappear.

Chapter Two gave the operational definition of cultivation. Chapter Three gives cultivation's specific prescription at the governance level — including its vacating face, and including its preventive face.

The preventive face was deleted by the received text. For two thousand years, readers have encountered an anti-intellectual Laozi.

The silk manuscript restores it.
