# 把事情看难一点，反而不难

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 5 月

一、越觉得容易，越会栽跟头

有一种人,做事之前总觉得"这有什么难的"——

轻易就答应下来,轻易就觉得能搞定,轻易就把事情看得很简单。

结果呢?往往栽跟头栽得最惨。

反过来,有一种人,对每件事都保持一份郑重——

哪怕是看起来简单的事,也认真对待、想在前面、做在细处。

结果呢?反而很少出大问题。

老子在《道德经》第六十三章里,把这个道理讲透了——

"多易必多难。"

你把事情看得越容易,遇到的困难反而越多。

"是以圣人犹难之,故终于无难。"

所以真正的高手,反而把每件事都看得郑重、看得"难"一点——正因为这样,他最后反而没有难。

这一章是这一组文章(第五十五到六十三章)的最后一篇。

它讲的是——

怎么做成难事和大事。以及,为什么"把事情看难一点"反而让你不难。

二、做事，不留下多余的招牌

第六十三章开头三句——

"为无为,事无事,味无味。"

以"无为"的方式去做,以"无事"的方式去做事,以"无味"的方式去体会味道。

这三句,是老子讲了一整本书的"无为"的一次总结。

"为无为"——做事,但不留下多余的痕迹和招牌。

不是"什么都不做"(这是对"无为"最大的误解)——

而是"做了,但不折腾、不张扬、不立一堆招牌"。

"事无事"——做事,但不生出多余的事端。

不无中生有地折腾,不把简单的事搞复杂。

"味无味"——去体会那个"平淡"里的滋味。

最深的味道,往往是最平淡的(接前面 ch35"道之出言,淡兮其无味")——不是浓烈刺激,是平淡中的隽永。

这三句合起来,是一种做事和生活的方式——

做事不折腾、不张扬、不搞复杂;体会平淡中的深味。

这接前面一路讲的(ch48 做减法、ch57 不折腾、ch60 若烹小鲜)——

真正高明的做事,是"少折腾"的:不立多余的招牌,不生多余的事端,守住平淡里的真味。

三、报怨以德：不是当烂好人，是不结怨

接下来一句,是历来争议最大、也最容易被误解的一句——

"大小多少,报怨以德。"

把小的看作大、把少的看作多;用"德"来对待怨。

先说"报怨以德"——这句话被误解了两千多年。

很多人以为,"报怨以德"就是——

别人伤害了你,你不但不还手,反而对他更好。

这就是所谓的"烂好人"——被人欺负了还赔笑脸。

孔子当年就反对这种做法,他说"以直报怨"(《论语》)——别人伤害你,你应该用正直、公正的方式对待,而不是一味忍让讨好。

但老子的"报怨以德",根本不是"烂好人"那个意思。

"报怨以德"真正的意思是——

"报"不是"报答、回报",是"对待、处理"。

"以德"是"凭着自己持有的德"。

整句是:因为你自己持守着"德"(内在的稳定、通透、留有余地),所以当"怨"来的时候,你根本不会卷进去。

关键在于——

这不是"被伤害之后怎么反应",而是"根本不让伤害发生"。

一个内在有"德"、有分寸、留有余地的人——

他不会去结怨,不会把自己放在与人对立、互相伤害的位置上——

所以"怨"根本打不到他身上。

这接前面讲过的一整条线——

ch50:善于保全自己的人,根本不把自己置于危险(无死地)。 ch55:婴儿不带攻击性,所以毒虫猛兽都不伤他。 ch56:什么都不推到极致,所以谁也拿捏不住他。 ch60:圣人不把自己摆在被伤的位置,所以伤害找不到他。 ch63:自己持守着德,所以怨根本打不到他(报怨以德)。

一路下来,都是同一个智慧:不是"被伤害后怎么反击",而是"根本不让自己处在能被伤害的位置"。

所以"报怨以德"和孔子的"以直报怨",其实不在同一个层面——

孔子讲的是:怨已经来了,你该怎么对付(用正直,不用讨好)。

老子讲的是:你根本不让怨打到你(自己持德,不结怨,不卷入)。

一个在"怨来了怎么办"的层面,一个在"怨能不能打到你"的层面——不是矛盾,是两回事。

老子的"报怨以德",绝不是让你当烂好人。

四、大事，都是从小处做起

接下来是这一章最实用的部分——

"图难乎其易也,为大乎其细也。天下之难作于易,天下之大作于细。"

图谋难事,要从它还容易的时候入手;成就大事,要从它还细小的时候入手。天下的难事,都是从容易时发展起来的;天下的大事,都是从细小处积累起来的。

"图难乎其易"——处理难事,要在它还容易的时候。

一件事,等它变成大麻烦了才处理,就晚了——

要在它还小、还容易解决的时候,就着手。

"为大乎其细"——成就大事,要从细小处入手。

不是一上来就想着"我要做一件惊天动地的大事"——

而是把眼前的每一个细节做好,大事自然从细处长出来。

"天下之难作于易,天下之大作于细"——

天下所有的难事,都是从"还容易的时候"发展起来的; 天下所有的大事,都是从"还细小的时候"积累起来的。

没有一个大问题是突然出现的——它一定是从一个小问题,没被及时处理,慢慢长大的。

没有一件大事是凭空成就的——它一定是从无数个细节,一点点积累起来的。

放到 2026 年看——

处理问题:一个小 bug 不修,最后变成系统崩溃;一个小矛盾不解决,最后变成团队分裂。在问题还小的时候解决它,是最省力的。

成就事业:没有一夜成功,所有的"大"都是从把一个个细节做好开始的。把眼前的细处做好,大事自然生长。

这就是"图难于易,为大于细"——在小处、易处、细处下功夫,才能真正处理难事、成就大事。

五、不把自己当成大人物，反而成就了大

接下来一句——

"是以圣人终不为大,故能成其大。"

所以圣人始终不把自己当成"大人物",反而能成就真正的大。

"终不为大"——始终不把自己立成"大"。

不摆架子、不自我膨胀、不急于显得自己了不起。

"故能成其大"——反而能成就真正的大。

为什么"不把自己当大人物",反而能成就大?

因为——

一个总想显得自己"大"的人,把精力都花在了维护"我很大"这个形象上,反而做不成真正的大事。

而一个踏踏实实、从细处做起、不摆架子的人,反而一点点成就了真正的大。

这接前面讲过的(ch34:不立自己为大,反而成大;ch62:不要天下最贵的位子,反而最贵)——

越不把自己当回事,越能成就大事;越急着显得自己大,越成不了大。

放到 2026 年看——

那些真正成就了大事业的人,往往最谦逊、最务实、最不摆架子——

他们不急于显得自己"大",只是踏实地把一件件小事做好——结果成就了真正的大。

而那些整天想显得自己了不起、急于证明自己"大"的人,反而困在了那个"我要显得很大"的执念里,成不了真正的大事。

六、把事情看难一点，反而不难

整章的收束——

"夫轻诺必寡信,多易必多难。是以圣人犹难之,故终于无难。"

轻易许下的承诺,必然缺乏信用;把事情看得太容易,必然遭遇很多困难。所以圣人反而把每件事都看得郑重、看得"难",正因为这样,他最终反而没有困难。

"轻诺必寡信"——轻易承诺,必然失信。

一个动不动就拍胸脯保证的人,反而最没有信用——

因为他答应得太轻率,根本没考虑能不能做到。

"多易必多难"——把事情看得太容易,必然遭遇很多困难。

为什么?

因为——

你把事情看得太容易,就不会认真准备、不会想在前面、不会做在细处——结果处处是没预料到的困难。

"是以圣人犹难之,故终于无难"——所以圣人反而把每件事看得"难",最终反而没有难。

这是这一章最深的一句——

真正的高手,不是把事情看得很简单、很有把握——

而是对每件事都保持一份郑重,把它看得"难"一点。

正因为他看得"难",所以他会——

认真准备、想在前面、做在细处、不掉以轻心——

结果,那些本来会绊倒别人的困难,都被他提前化解了。

所以他"终于无难"——最后反而没有难。

放到 2026 年看——

那种觉得"这有什么难的"、轻易答应、掉以轻心的人——

往往在没预料到的地方栽大跟头(多易必多难)。

而那种对每件事都保持郑重、认真对待、想在前面的人——

反而很少出大问题(犹难之,故终于无难)。

把事情看难一点,不是让你焦虑、让你畏难——而是让你郑重、让你用心、让你把困难化解在它出现之前。

七、给读者的

这一章给读者几个特别实用的做事智慧——

第一:做事少折腾。

做了,但不张扬、不搞复杂、不立多余的招牌(为无为、事无事)。

真正高明的做事,是"少折腾"的。

第二:不结怨,而不是当烂好人。

"报怨以德"不是让你被欺负了还赔笑脸——

而是:自己内在有德、有分寸、留有余地,根本不把自己放在与人结怨、互相伤害的位置上。

不是被伤害后怎么反击,而是根本不让自己处在能被伤害的位置。

第三:在小处、易处、细处下功夫。

处理问题,在它还小、还容易的时候就解决(图难于易); 成就大事,从把每个细节做好开始(为大于细)。

没有突然出现的大麻烦,也没有凭空成就的大事——一切都在小处、细处。

第四:别急着显得自己"大"。

越不把自己当大人物、越踏实从细处做起,反而越能成就真正的大(终不为大,故能成其大)。

第五:把事情看难一点。

别轻易承诺、别掉以轻心、别觉得"这有什么难的"——

对每件事都保持一份郑重(犹难之)——

正因为你看得"难",那些本来会绊倒你的困难,才会被你提前化解——最终反而没有难。

这一章的核心,是一种郑重而务实的做事态度——

不折腾、不结怨、从细处做起、不摆架子、把事情看得郑重——

看起来"慢"、看起来"笨"、看起来"难"——

但恰恰是这种态度,让你做成难事、成就大事、最终没有难。

八、这一组的结尾

第六十三章,是这一组九章(第五十五到六十三章)的最后一篇。

回头看这一组,老子从"最柔软的婴儿"讲起,一路讲到"最务实的做事"——

从婴儿的力量(ch55,最柔软反而最有生命力),到谁也拿捏不住的人(ch56,什么都不推到极致),到越管越乱(ch57,撤掉限制让人自己长),到顺的时候藏着险(ch58,福祸相倚、留有余地),到省着用才走得远(ch59,蓄养不透支),到煎小鱼别老翻(ch60,别折腾、别树敌),到越强大越要往低处走(ch61,强者谦下),到道从不因你的过去拒绝你(ch62,道不弃任何人),到今天的把事情看难一点反而不难(ch63,郑重而务实)。

这一组的核心,可以收成一句话——

柔软、留余、不折腾、不结怨、从细处做起、不摆架子、看得郑重——

这些看起来"柔"、看起来"慢"、看起来"低"的东西,恰恰是最深的力量、最高的智慧、最长久的道路。

最后,回到开头那句话:越觉得容易,越会栽跟头。

老子两千四百年前就说了——

"多易必多难。是以圣人犹难之,故终于无难。"

不要把事情看得太容易。

对每一件事,都保持一份郑重、一份用心、一份"看得难一点"的谨慎——

正因为这样,那些本来会难倒你的事,反而都不难了。

把事情看难一点。

反而不难。

I. The More You Think It's Easy, the Harder It Gets

There is a type of person who thinks before doing anything, "What's hard about this?"

They make commitments easily, feel confident easily, see things as simple easily.

The result? They are the ones who stumble hardest.

Then there is another type — who brings a certain gravity to each thing.

Even something that looks simple, they take seriously, think ahead, and do in the details.

The result? They rarely run into serious problems.

In Chapter 63 of the Daodejing, Laozi makes this transparent —

"Treat things as easy, and you will encounter many difficulties."

"The sage treats every matter as difficult — and in the end encounters no difficulty at all."

This is the last chapter of this group (Chapters 55–63). It is about —

How to accomplish difficult and great things. And why "treating things as difficult" is what keeps you free of difficulty.

II. Do — Without Leaving Surplus Traces

The chapter opens with three lines —

"Do without doing (wu wei). Act without stirring things up (wu shi). Taste without taste (wu wei)."

These three lines are a summation of the "wu wei" that runs through the entire Daodejing.

"Do without doing" — do things, but leave no surplus traces or banners.

Not "do nothing" (that is the greatest misreading of wu wei) — but "do things, yet without stirring things up, broadcasting, or hanging up a heap of signs."

"Act without stirring things up" — act, but generate no surplus complications.

Don't create things from nothing; don't make simple things complex.

"Taste without taste" — find the taste in "plainness."

The deepest flavor is often the plainest (connecting ch. 35's "the Dao in words is bland — it has no flavor") — not intense stimulation, but the quiet depth of the plain.

Together these three describe a way of doing things and living —

Act without stirring things up, without broadcasting, without complexity; find the deep flavor in plainness.

This connects the thread running through earlier chapters (ch. 48's subtracting, ch. 57's not stirring up, ch. 60's like frying a small fish) — truly skilled action is "less stirring up": leaving no surplus signs, generating no surplus complications, holding the true flavor of plainness.

III. Repaying Resentment with Virtue — Not Being a Pushover. Not Getting into Resentment in the First Place

The next line — the one most disputed and most misread over two thousand years —

"Take the small as large and the few as many; repay resentment with virtue (de)."

First, on "repaying resentment with virtue" — this has been misread for two thousand years.

Many people take "repaying resentment with virtue" to mean — when someone hurts you, not only do you not strike back, you treat them even better. That is what we call being a pushover — meeting abuse with a smile.

Confucius himself opposed this interpretation, saying "repay resentment with uprightness" (Analects) — when someone harms you, respond with uprightness and fairness, not with obsequious yielding.

But Laozi's "repaying resentment with virtue" is not the pushover interpretation at all.

What "repaying resentment with virtue" really means —

"Repay" here is not "repay, return in kind" — it is "deal with, handle."

"With virtue" means "by means of one's own held virtue (de)."

The whole line: because you yourself hold and guard "virtue" (inner stability, clarity, keeping margin), when "resentment" comes you simply don't get drawn into it.

The key is —

This is not about how to react after being hurt — it's about never letting the hurt reach you in the first place.

A person who inwardly holds "virtue," has measure, keeps margin — they don't go setting themselves up against people, don't place themselves in a position of mutual hurt and resentment —

So "resentment" simply cannot land on them.

This connects the thread running through the group —

Ch. 50: no place of death — those skilled at self-preservation never place themselves where danger can reach. Ch. 55: the infant carries no aggression, so venomous creatures leave it alone. Ch. 56: pushing nothing to the extreme, so no one can grasp them. Ch. 60: the sage doesn't place himself in a position of being harmed, so harm cannot reach him. Ch. 63: holding virtue within, so resentment cannot land on them.

One line running through: not "how to fight back after being hurt" — but "not placing yourself in a position where you can be hurt."

So "repaying resentment with virtue" and Confucius's "repaying resentment with uprightness" are not at the same level — they are two different things:

Confucius is addressing: resentment has already arrived — how should you handle it? (With uprightness and fairness, not obsequious yielding.)

Laozi is addressing: can resentment even reach you? (Hold virtue within, don't set up resentment, don't get drawn in — and resentment simply can't land.)

One is at the level of "what to do when resentment comes," the other is at the level of "can resentment even reach you" — not contradictory, just two different things.

Laozi's "repaying resentment with virtue" absolutely does not mean being a pushover.

IV. Great Things Begin at the Small

Now the most practically useful part of the chapter —

"Plan the difficult when it is still easy. Work on the great when it is still small. All the difficult things under heaven begin from the easy; all the great things under heaven begin from the small."

"Plan the difficult when it is still easy" — handle difficult things while they are still easy.

A problem, left until it has become a serious trouble to address — too late. Handle it when it is still small, still easy to solve.

"Work on the great when it is still small" — work toward great things starting from the still-small.

Don't start by imagining "I'm going to do something earth-shaking" — instead, do each small thing well, and great things naturally grow from the small.

"All difficult things begin from the easy; all great things begin from the small" —

All difficult things under heaven grow from "still easy" moments; all great things under heaven are accumulated from "still small" ones.

No serious problem appears suddenly — it must have been a small problem that wasn't addressed in time, slowly growing large.

No great achievement appears from thin air — it must have been countless details, accumulated one by one.

Seen from 2026 —

Handling problems: a small bug left unrepaired becomes a system crash; a small conflict left unresolved becomes a team fracture. Solve problems when they are still small. That is the least costly approach.

Building something great: there is no overnight success — all "greatness" starts with doing the small things well. Do the small thing in front of you well, and greatness grows naturally.

This is "plan the difficult at the easy, work toward the great at the small" — concentrate effort on the small, the easy, the detailed, and you can truly handle difficult things and achieve great ones.

V. Not Making Yourself into a "Big Deal" — and Actually Achieving the Great

The next line —

"Therefore the sage never positions himself as doing something great — and thus can achieve the truly great."

"Never positions himself as great" — never erects himself as a "big deal."

No putting on airs, no self-inflation, no rush to seem impressive.

"And thus can achieve the truly great" — and thus can achieve the genuinely great.

Why does "not positioning yourself as great" lead to achieving the truly great?

Because — a person always trying to seem like a "big deal" spends their energy maintaining the image of "I am great," and doesn't actually accomplish anything great.

But someone who works steadily, starts from the small details, puts on no airs — gradually achieves the truly great, one step at a time.

This connects the earlier thread (ch. 34: not positioning yourself as great, and thus achieving great; ch. 62: not seeking the world's most honored position, and thus possessing the truly precious) — the less you treat yourself as a big deal, the more you can achieve great things. The more urgently you try to seem great, the less you achieve anything truly great.

Seen from 2026 —

Those who truly accomplished great things are usually the most humble, the most realistic, the least likely to put on airs — they never rushed to seem great; they just solidly did each small thing well — and in the end achieved what was truly great.

Those who are always trying to seem impressive, always proving how great they are, are trapped in the obsession of "I need to seem very great" — and cannot do any truly great thing.

VI. Treat Things as Difficult — and in the End, No Difficulty

The chapter's close —

"Easy promises are rarely kept. Treating things as easy leads to many difficulties. Therefore the sage treats everything as difficult — and in the end encounters no difficulty at all."

"Easy promises are rarely kept" — promises made lightly are rarely fulfilled.

The person who readily beats their chest and makes guarantees is the one with the least credibility — because they committed too casually, never considering whether they could actually deliver.

"Treating things as easy leads to many difficulties" — treating things as too easy leads to encountering many difficulties.

Why?

Because — if you see things as too easy, you won't prepare seriously, won't think ahead, won't do things in the details — and you encounter unforeseen difficulties everywhere.

"Therefore the sage treats everything as difficult — and in the end encounters no difficulty at all."

This is the deepest line in the chapter —

The real master does not see things as simple and handled — but brings gravity and seriousness to every matter, treating it as "a little difficult."

Precisely because they see things as "difficult," they will — prepare carefully, think ahead, work in the details, not take it lightly — and the difficulties that would have tripped up others are dissolved before they appear.

So they "in the end encounter no difficulty" — in the end there is nothing difficult.

Seen from 2026 —

The type who thinks "what's hard about this?" and commits lightly and takes things casually — stumbles hardest in the places they never anticipated (treat as easy, encounter many difficulties).

The type who brings gravity to each thing, prepares seriously, thinks ahead — rarely runs into serious problems (treating as difficult, in the end no difficulty).

Treating things as a little more difficult is not making yourself anxious or fearful — it is making yourself serious, attentive, resolving difficulties before they arise.

VII. To the Reader

This chapter offers several practically useful wisdoms —

First: do things without stirring them up.

Do, but don't broadcast, don't make things complex, don't hang up surplus signs (do without doing, act without stirring up things). Truly skilled action is "less stirring up."

Second: don't set up resentment — not being a pushover.

"Repaying resentment with virtue" does not mean meeting abuse with a smile — but: hold virtue within, have measure, keep margin, and don't place yourself in a position of mutual hurt and resentment.

Not how to fight back after being hurt — but not placing yourself in a position where you can be hurt in the first place.

Third: concentrate effort on the small, the easy, the detailed.

Handle problems when they are still small, still easy (plan the difficult at the easy); work toward great things starting from doing each small thing well (work toward the great at the small).

No sudden serious problems; no great achievements from thin air — everything is in the small and detailed.

Fourth: don't rush to seem like a "big deal."

The more you position yourself as "doing something great," the less you actually achieve. The more you work steadily and realistically from the small details, the more you achieve what is truly great (never positioning as great, thus achieving the great).

Fifth: treat things as a little more difficult.

Don't make easy promises; don't take things casually; don't think "what's hard about this?" — bring gravity to each matter (treating as difficult) — precisely because of that gravity, difficulties are dissolved before they appear — and in the end there is nothing difficult.

This chapter's core is a serious and realistic way of doing things —

Not stirring things up, not setting up resentment, starting from the small, not putting on airs, treating things with gravity —

Looks "slow," looks "ordinary," looks "difficult" —

But precisely this attitude is what accomplishes difficult things, achieves great things, and in the end finds nothing difficult.

VIII. Closing This Group

Chapter 63 is the last of this group of nine chapters (Chapters 55–63).

Looking back across the group, Laozi moved from "the softest infant" all the way to "the most realistic doing" —

From the power of the infant (ch. 55, the softest has the most vitality), to the person nobody can grasp (ch. 56, pushing nothing to the extreme), to the more you control the more chaos (ch. 57, remove constraints and let people grow), to in easy times danger hides (ch. 58, fortune and calamity are intertwined, keep margin), to save something and go far (ch. 59, nurture and don't burn through), to fry small fish don't keep flipping them (ch. 60, don't interfere needlessly, don't make enemies), to the more powerful the lower you must go (ch. 61, the powerful must practice humility), to the Dao never refuses you for your past (ch. 62, the Dao abandons no one), to today's treat things as difficult and in the end nothing is difficult (ch. 63, serious and realistic).

The core of this group can be summed in one line —

Soft, keeping margin, not stirring things up, not setting up resentment, starting from the small, putting on no airs, bringing gravity —

These things that look "soft," look "slow," look "low" are precisely the deepest power, the highest wisdom, the longest-lasting path.

Finally, back to the opening: the more you think it's easy, the harder it gets.

Laozi said it 2,400 years ago —

"Treat things as easy, and you will encounter many difficulties. The sage treats everything as difficult — and in the end encounters no difficulty at all."

Don't take things too lightly.

Bring gravity to each matter — think ahead, work in the details, don't be careless —

Precisely because of that, the difficulties that would have stopped you are dissolved before they appear — and in the end there is nothing difficult.

Treat things as difficult.

And in the end — nothing is difficult.
