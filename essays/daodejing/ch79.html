# 再公道的调解，也一定会剩下点什么

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 7 月

一、那件事"解决"了，可两个人再也回不去了

你多半经历过这样的场面——

两个人闹翻了。中间人出面,摆事实、讲道理,最后给了一个大家都说不出什么的处理:各让一步,这事就算过去了。

表面上,平了。

可你心里清楚:不一样了。 那两个人从此说话客气了,再也不开玩笑了;开会时避开彼此的眼神;有事宁可绕一圈,也不直接找对方。

事情是解决了。可有些东西,再也没回来。

老子在《道德经》第七十九章开头,就把这件事说破了。而他说破之后给出的方向,可能出乎你意料——他没有教你怎么调解得更漂亮。

二、就算判得再公，也一定会剩下点什么

老子说——

"和大怨,必有余怨,焉可以为善?"

调和一场大的怨恨,必然还会剩下没消掉的怨;这怎么能算是"善"呢?

"必有余怨"这三个字,老子说得非常绝。

不是"可能有",是一定有。

为什么一定?

因为大怨一旦结下,它就已经在那儿了。你后来做的一切,都是在处理它,而不是让它没发生过。

而处理的结果,通常是这三种:

要么这一方觉得自己吃亏了;

要么那一方觉得自己委屈了;

要么两边都觉得自己让得更多。

判得再公道,也躲不开这一点。 因为怨这个东西,不是靠"谁对谁错"算得清的:一个人心里那份"我被这样对待过"的记忆,不会因为一纸结论就消失。

所以老子问了一句:"焉可以为善?"——这怎么能算善呢?

注意,他不是说调解没用、不该做。 事情来了当然要处理。

他是说:如果你把"善"理解成"出事之后摆平它",那你永远只能做到"剩下一点怨"。

真正的善,得往前挪——挪到大怨还没结下的地方去。

三、崇尚在中间搭桥的人，而不是追责的人

那往前挪,挪到哪儿?老子说——

"是以圣人右介而不以责于人。"

所以圣人崇尚居中介入,而不用追责去对人。

这句话里,有两个字历来被读偏。

"右"在这里是动词,是"崇尚、看重"的意思（古时"右"通"佑",《史记》里说皇帝"右丞相李斯",就是看重李斯;"右文"就是崇尚文治）。

"介"是介入、居中协调（古时外交使团里有"上介""众介",就是居中传话协调的那些人;今天"介绍""中介"里的"介",还是这个意思）。

所以"右介"就是:看重那个居中搭桥的位置。

（通行本这里写作"执左契"——手里拿着债券的左半边,整句就被读进了"债主怎么对待欠债人"的框架里。帛书的"右介"跟下一句"司介"接得直接,这一章按帛书读。）

"不以责于人"——不拿追责去对着人。

这不是说不追究、和稀泥。 是说:你的重心,不放在"这事该怪谁"上。

因为"怪谁"这件事,越查越清楚,也越查越把人推远。

放到今天看——

出了事故,一种做法是开会复盘,一层层追下去,直到找出那个按错按钮的人;另一种做法是问:这个流程为什么允许一个人按错按钮就出事?

前一种,你得到了一个可以怪的人;后一种,你得到了一个不会再出事的流程。

而且前一种做多了,下次出问题,没有人再敢第一时间说出来。 追责追得越狠,消息藏得越深——等它藏不住的时候,已经是大怨了。

那"事前介入",具体是什么样?

多半是些小到不值一提的动作:

看见两个人最近说话有点冲,私下各问一句"最近怎么了";

发现一件事两个人的理解不一样,当场停下来对一次,别让它各走各的;

听见一句半玩笑的抱怨,认真接住它,而不是笑一笑翻过去。

这些动作都太小了,小到你做完了,什么也没发生——所以也没人会谢你。

而这正是它难的地方:防住的那场大怨,永远不会出现,所以没有人知道你防住了什么。

四、有德的去调停，还没有德的去开垦

接着,老子说了一句被误读了两千年的话——

"故有德司介,无德司彻。"

所以有德的人掌管居中调停,还没有德的人去做开垦的事。

这里有两处要说清楚。

第一,"彻"是开垦、垦治,不是收税。

《诗经·大雅》里有"彻田为粮"——开垦田地来种粮食。 这就是"彻"的本义。

（后世很多注家把"司彻"读成"管收税",那是因为顺着"执左契"那个债务框架读下来的。按帛书的"介"读,前后都是治理上的具体活儿:一个是居中调停,一个是开垦耕种。）

第二,"无德"是"还没有德",不是"缺德"。

这个差别特别大。

读成"缺德"，这句话就成了:没德的人只配去干粗活。 那是一句挖苦。

读成"还没有德"，这句话就成了:每个人都有他现在能做、也该做的那份事。 那是一个安排。

而后一种读法,才跟老子一贯的意思对得上。

为什么这么分?

因为居中调停,是一件需要德的活。

你得站在中间不偏;你得听得进两边都不爱听的话;最要紧的是,那份"必有余怨"里剩下的怨,得由你来担着。 没有这份德,你去调停,只会把事情搅得更乱。

这一点值得多说一句。

前面说过:再公道的处理,也一定会剩下怨。那剩下的那份,总得有个去处。

扛得住的调停者,自己把它接下来了——两边都稍稍消了气,而那点没消的不满,落在他身上,他不辩解、也不往外推。

扛不住的,会把它再推回给当事人:"我已经很公平了,是你们自己想不开。"这句话一出口,那份余怨立刻翻倍,而且从此对准了他。

所以"有德司介"不是一句客气话。那个位置,是真的要吃下东西的。

这里还有一层:为什么"做判断"这件事,不是谁都做得了?

多半不是因为看不清。 很多事情谁在理、谁过分,旁观的人心里都有数。

难的是:你一旦判了,就得站在那儿,把两边的不痛快都接住。

判断是一瞬间的事,承担是往后很长的事。 而大多数人卡住的地方,不在前面那一瞬,在后面那一段——所以宁可不表态、和稀泥、把话说得两边都挑不出毛病。

看得清的人不少,肯站在那儿挨着的人不多。这就是"有德"和"还没有德"的分界。

而这,正是上一章那句话的另一面。

（ch78）说"受邦之垢,是谓社稷之主"——肯把一个地方的脏和难接过来的人,才是那里的主。

这一章接着说:那份接,不是谁都接得住。

一个说:位置是由你吃下多少来定的;一个说:不是谁都吃得下。两章合起来,才是完整的一句话。

而开垦,是不需要这份德的活。 它需要的是力气、是耐心、是把一块地一点点侍弄好——这件事本身，就在长人。

这不是把人分成三六九等,恰恰相反:

老子的意思是——你现在还没有的那份德,不是靠说教长出来的,是在你能做的那件事里,一天一天做出来的。

先给他一件他现在做得了的事。德,是在做事里长的。

五、天道不偏心，可它谁都不落下

全章最后八个字——

"夫天道无亲,恒与善人。"

天道没有偏亲,却一直与善人相应。

这句话流传极广,也最容易被读成:"老天爷偏爱好人。"

可老子上一句刚说完"无亲"——没有偏亲。 说完"不偏心",紧接着说"偏爱好人",那不是自相矛盾吗?

所以这两句不是矛盾,是一件事的两面:

天道确实不偏心（这正是（ch5）那句"天地不仁":天地对谁都一样,没有偏爱,也没有情绪）。

而"恒与善人"说的是:那些做法跟天道走在同一个方向上的人,自然就顺。

不是天奖赏他,是他走的路本来就是通的。

就像顺流划船的人比逆流的省力——不是河偏爱他,是他顺着水走。

但也别把这句读成一句成功学:顺着走,不等于事事顺遂。

好人一样会遇到倒霉事,一样会被辜负——天道"无亲"这三个字,老子说得很实在,它真的不会为谁开一道口子。

"恒与善人"说的是方向,不是保票: 你走的那条路,是通的;至于路上有没有石头,那是另一回事。

而这一句,也把这一整章收住了:

天道谁都不偏爱,可它也谁都不放弃。

有德的,给他调停的事;还没有德的,给他开垦的事。谁都有一份可做的事,谁都在这份事里,一点点长成善人。

这才是老子说的"善"——不是等大怨结下之后再来评理,是让每个人都在他做得了的位置上,慢慢长。

六、给读者的

这一章,给我们几件当下就能用的事——

第一:别指望把事情"摆平"就等于解决了。

再公道的处理,也一定会剩下点什么（和大怨,必有余怨）。看见那份剩下的,是成熟的开始。

第二:真正的功夫在事情变大之前。

大怨一旦结下,你能做的就只剩打折的处理了。平时那些"算了,不至于"的小疙瘩,才是该动手的地方。

第三:少追一点"该怪谁",多问一句"为什么会这样"。

追责得到一个人,追因得到一个不再出事的做法（不以责于人）。而且追责越狠,坏消息藏得越深。

第四:看得清,不等于担得住。

判断谁对谁错,常常不难;难的是判完之后,站在那儿把两边的不痛快都接着（有德司介）。要把那个位置交给担得住的人,也别在自己还担不住的时候,硬去站。

第五:先给他一件他现在做得了的事。

德不是说教出来的,是在做事里长出来的（无德司彻）。"还没有",不等于"不行"。

这一章的核心,可以收成一句话——

再公道的调解也会剩下怨,所以真正的功夫在更前面:不追着怪谁,让每个人都有一件他现在做得了的事——德,是在做事里一天天长出来的。

七、承上启下

第七十九章,先接住上一章那句最重的话——

（ch78）说:肯把一个地方的脏和难接过来的人,才是那里的主。这一章说:那份接,不是谁都接得住——所以"有德司介"。 一个讲位置由什么定,一个讲谁站得住那个位置。

而这一章的"天道无亲",又接着前面几章的"天之道"往下走——（ch73）说天网恢恢、疏而不失;（ch77）说天之道损有余而益不足;这一章说天道无亲、恒与善人。 三句话是同一个意思:没有谁在上面裁决,可方向就在那儿;你顺着走,自然就顺。

而"有德司介,无德司彻"这一句,又接上了老子讲过很多次的那件事:（ch27）说"圣人常善救人,故无弃人"——圣人总是善于成全人,所以没有一个人是该被丢下的。 到这一章,他把它落得更实了:不丢下,不是嘴上说说,是给他一件他现在做得了的事。

下一章（ch80）,是全书最有画面感的一章:"小邦寡民……甘其食,美其服,乐其俗,安其居"——让人觉得自己吃的饭是香的,穿的衣是好的,过的日子是安稳的。 我们下一篇见。

秦汉（字大知）· @hqinjarsy · 大知道德经解系列

完整笺注、帛书 vs 通行本字差的字面考据、与《余项经》层级的对照表，见 Zenodo 存档。本章属 Paper 9（第 73-81 章）：10.5281/zenodo.20145192。前八篇：Paper 1、Paper 2、Paper 3、Paper 4、Paper 5、Paper 6、Paper 7、Paper 8。

I. The Matter Was "Resolved" — but the Two of Them Can Never Go Back

You have probably witnessed something like this —

Two people fell out. A mediator stepped in: laid out the facts, made the arguments, and finally delivered a resolution that neither side could directly object to. Each gave a little. The matter was put behind them.

On the surface, it was settled.

But in your heart you know: something is different now. Those two people are polite with each other from then on, no longer joking; they avoid each other's eyes in meetings; when they need something, they prefer to go around rather than go directly to the other person.

The matter was resolved. But something never came back.

In the opening of Chapter 79, Laozi names this directly. And the direction he points after naming it may surprise you — he does not teach you how to mediate more skillfully.

II. No Matter How Fair the Judgment, Something Always Remains

Laozi says —

"In resolving great resentment, there will certainly be a remainder of resentment. How can this be called good?"

When you resolve a great resentment, there will inevitably be some resentment left that has not dissolved. How can that be called doing good?

The three characters "certainly there will be remainder" — Laozi states this absolutely.

Not "there may be" — there will certainly be.

Why certainly?

Because once great resentment has formed, it already exists. Everything you do afterward is managing it — not making it as though it never happened.

And the outcome of management is usually one of three forms:

Either one side feels it got the worse deal;

or the other side feels it was wronged;

or both sides feel they conceded more than the other did.

No matter how fair the judgment, this cannot be avoided. Because resentment is not the kind of thing that can be cleared away by "figuring out who was right": the memory in a person's heart of "I was treated this way" does not disappear because a conclusion was reached.

So Laozi asks: "How can this be called good?"

Note: he is not saying mediation is useless or should not be done. When something happens, of course it must be dealt with.

He is saying: if you understand "good" as "settling things after they go wrong," you will never do better than "a remainder of resentment."

True good has to move earlier — to where great resentment has not yet formed.

III. Valuing the One Who Bridges — Not the One Who Assigns Blame

So where does "earlier" mean? Laozi says —

"Therefore the sage esteems the mediating position and does not use blame against people."

So the sage values the role of the one who intervenes from the middle — and does not deploy blame against people.

Two characters in this line have historically been misread.

"Value" (yòu) here is a verb meaning "to esteem, to prize" (in ancient usage yòu also meant "to favor"; the Shiji says the emperor "valued Prime Minister Li Si" — meaning held him in esteem; "valuing literature" means esteeming civil governance).

"Jiè" means to intervene, to mediate from the middle (diplomatic missions of antiquity included "senior intermediaries" and "company of intermediaries" — people who carried messages and mediated from the center; the "jiè" in "introduce" and "intermediary" today still carries this meaning).

So "valuing jiè" means: prizing the role of the person who bridges from the center.

(The received text has "holds the left half of the tally" — a contract metaphor — which pulls the whole line into a framework about creditors and debtors. The Silk Manuscript's "jiè" connects directly to the following "governs jiè." This chapter follows the Silk Manuscript.)

"Does not use blame against people" — does not deploy blame as the instrument for addressing people.

This does not mean not holding people accountable, or muddling things together. It means: your center of gravity is not placed on "who is to blame for this."

Because the more you pursue "who is to blame," the clearer things become — and the further apart people are pushed.

Seen from today —

After an accident, one approach is to hold a review meeting and trace down layer by layer until you find the person who pressed the wrong button. Another approach is to ask: why does this process allow a single wrong button press to cause an accident?

The first gives you someone to blame; the second gives you a process that will not break down the same way again.

And the first approach, applied repeatedly, means that next time something goes wrong, no one will dare speak up immediately. The harder you pursue blame, the deeper the bad news gets hidden — and by the time it can no longer be hidden, it has become a great resentment.

What does "earlier intervention" actually look like?

Usually, gestures too small to mention:

Noticing that two people have been a little sharp with each other lately — asking each of them privately, "how are things going?"

Finding that two people have different understandings of the same thing — stopping right there to compare notes, instead of letting them walk off in different directions.

Hearing a half-joking complaint — taking it seriously instead of laughing it off and moving on.

These moves are all too small — so small that when you do them, nothing happens — which is why no one will thank you for them.

And this is precisely what makes it hard: the great resentment that was prevented will never appear, so no one will ever know what was prevented.

IV. Those with Virtue Govern Mediation; Those Not Yet There Govern Cultivation

Next, Laozi says a line that has been misread for two thousand years —

"Therefore those who have virtue govern mediating; those who have not yet arrived at virtue govern cultivating."

Those who hold virtue take on the work of mediation from the middle; those who have not yet arrived at virtue take on the work of cultivating the ground.

Two things need to be clarified here.

First: "chè" means cultivating land — not collecting taxes.

The Book of Songs has "cultivate the fields to grow grain" — this is chè's original meaning.

(Later commentators read "governs chè" as "manages tax collection" because they were reading along the creditor/debtor framework introduced by "holds the left tally." With the Silk Manuscript's "jiè," everything around it becomes concrete governing work: one is mediating, the other is cultivating.)

Second: "not yet at virtue" (wú dé) means "has not yet developed virtue" — not "is devoid of virtue."

This difference is enormous.

Read as "devoid of virtue," the line becomes: people without virtue deserve only the crude work. That is a sneer.

Read as "not yet there," the line becomes: everyone has the work they can do now and that suits them now. That is an arrangement.

Only the second reading fits Laozi's consistent meaning.

Why this arrangement?

Because mediating from the center is work that requires virtue.

You must stand in the middle without tilting; you must be able to hear things from both sides that neither side wants to hear; and most importantly, that remainder of resentment left behind by "inevitably some resentment remains" must be absorbed by you. Without this virtue, your mediation will only make things worse.

This point deserves a little more.

We said earlier: even the fairest handling will leave some resentment. That remainder has to go somewhere.

A mediator who can hold it absorbs it themselves — both sides have released a little, and what remains falls on the mediator, who neither argues nor pushes it back outward.

One who cannot hold it pushes it back to the parties: "I was completely fair — you two are the ones who can't let it go." The moment those words are said, the remaining resentment doubles — and from then on, it is aimed at the mediator.

So "those with virtue govern mediating" is not a courtesy formula. That position genuinely requires you to take things in.

There is another layer here: why is making a judgment not something anyone can do?

Usually it is not that people cannot see clearly. On many things, who is in the right and who overstepped — people watching from the side can often tell.

The hard part is: once you have made a judgment, you have to stand there and absorb the displeasure from both sides.

Judging is a momentary thing; bearing the consequences is a long ongoing thing. And most people get stuck not in the moment of judgment, but in the stretch that follows — which is why people prefer to stay neutral, muddle through, and say things that neither side can object to.

Many people can see clearly; few are willing to stand there and take it. This is the boundary between "has virtue" and "has not yet arrived at virtue."

And this is exactly the other side of the preceding chapter's teaching.

(ch. 78) says: whoever is willing to take on the filth and difficulty of a place is its true ruler.

This chapter adds: not everyone can take that on.

One says: what defines your position is how much you can absorb; the other says: not everyone can absorb it. Together these two chapters are one complete statement.

And cultivating is work that does not require this virtue. What it requires is effort, patience, and tending a piece of ground bit by bit — and this work itself is what grows the person.

This is not dividing people into grades. It is precisely the opposite:

Laozi's meaning is: the virtue you do not yet have is not grown through lectures — it is grown day by day in the work you can do right now.

First give someone the work they can do today. Virtue grows in doing.

V. The Way of Heaven Has No Favorites — and Abandons No One

The chapter's final eight characters —

"The way of Heaven has no favorites; it is always with the good person."

The way of Heaven has no partiality — and yet it is always aligned with those who are good.

This line is among the most widely transmitted — and most easily misread as "Heaven favors good people."

But Laozi just said in the same breath "has no favorites." Having said "no partiality," immediately following with "favors the good" would be self-contradictory.

So these two are not contradictory — they are two sides of one thing:

The way of Heaven genuinely has no partiality (exactly (ch. 5)'s "Heaven and earth are not humane": they treat all things the same, with no preferences and no emotions).

And "always with the good person" means: those whose conduct aligns with the direction the way of Heaven moves in — naturally find things flowing.

It is not that Heaven rewards them — it is that the path they walk is simply open.

Like someone rowing with the current versus against it — the river does not favor the one going with it; they are simply going the way the water flows.

But don't read this as a success philosophy: going with the current does not mean everything goes smoothly.

Good people still encounter misfortune; still get let down — "has no favorites" — Laozi says this plainly; it will truly not make an exception for anyone.

"Always with the good person" describes a direction, not a guarantee: the path you are on is open — whether there are stones on it is another matter.

And this line closes out the whole chapter:

The way of Heaven favors no one — and abandons no one.

Those with virtue get the work of mediating; those not yet there get the work of cultivating. Everyone has something they can do; everyone, in doing it, is gradually growing into a good person.

This is what Laozi means by "good" — not waiting for great resentment to form and then adjudicating it, but putting each person in the position where they can grow, and letting them grow slowly.

VI. To the Reader

This chapter gives us several things useful right now —

First: don't think that "settling" something is the same as resolving it.

Even the fairest handling will leave something behind. Seeing that remainder is the beginning of maturity.

Second: the real work happens before things escalate.

Once great resentment has formed, the best you can do is a discounted resolution. Those small tensions that seem too trivial to bother with — those are where the real work should happen.

Third: ask less "who's to blame" and more "why did this happen."

Pursuing blame gives you a person to blame; pursuing cause gives you a practice that won't break down the same way again. And the harder you pursue blame, the deeper bad news gets buried.

Fourth: seeing clearly is not the same as being able to hold it.

Judging who was right and who overstepped is often not the hard part; the hard part is standing there afterward and absorbing the displeasure from both sides. Give that position to someone who can hold it — and don't force yourself to stand there before you can hold it either.

Fifth: first give someone the work they can do right now.

Virtue is not grown through lectures — it is grown in doing. "Not yet there" is not the same as "unable."

The core of this chapter can be gathered into one line —

Even the fairest resolution leaves some resentment — so the real work happens earlier: stop chasing blame, give each person something they can do right now — virtue grows in doing, day by day.

VII. Connecting Forward and Back

Chapter 79 picks up from the heaviest line of the preceding chapter — (ch. 78) says: whoever is willing to take on the filth and difficulty of a place is its true ruler; this chapter says: that kind of taking-on is not something everyone can do — hence "those with virtue govern mediating." One says what defines a position; the other says who can stand in it.

And this chapter's "the way of Heaven has no favorites" continues the "way of Heaven" thread from recent chapters — (ch. 73) said "the net of Heaven is vast — sparse yet nothing slips through"; (ch. 77) said "the way of Heaven reduces excess and supplements deficiency"; this chapter says "the way of Heaven has no favorites, yet is always with the good person." Three statements, one meaning: there is no one adjudicating above, yet the direction is there; go with it, and things flow naturally.

And "those with virtue govern mediating; those not yet there govern cultivating" connects to something Laozi has said many times: (ch. 27) said "the sage is always good at rescuing people — therefore no one is abandoned." In this chapter, he makes it more concrete: not abandoning someone is not just words — it is giving them something they can do today.

The next chapter (ch. 80) is the most vivid in the whole book: "small state, few people… feel the food in their bowl is fragrant, feel their clothing is fine, feel their customs are meaningful, feel their home is settled." We will see it next.
