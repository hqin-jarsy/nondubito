# 替别人抡斧头的，多半会伤到自己的手

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 7 月

一、那个"这事我来定"的人

你多半见过这样一幕:

一件事,本来有专门负责的人在做,他懂行、有经验、握着分寸。可另一个人——通常是位置更高的那个——伸手接了过来:"这个我来定。"

我们通常觉得这样不好,理由是"他不专业,容易做砸"。

但老子给的理由,比这个狠得多。

他说:这样的人,很少有不伤到自己手的。

不是"事情会办砸",是"你自己会受伤"。 这一章讲的,就是这件事。

二、这一章，老子谈的是最重的那件事

先说清楚这一章的处境。

《道德经》第七十四章,老子谈的是他那个年代最重的一件事:刑罚,和杀。

他用了三段假设,一段一段推下来,最后落在一句凌厉的警告上:

如果人已经被逼到不怕死了,会怎样;如果秩序还正常、人还知道畏惧,又该怎样;而在那样的秩序里,那个专门掌管的位置,是免不了的。

然后他说:可你要是越过那个位置、自己伸手去做——你会伤到自己。

而第一段,正接着上一组末尾那句话。

三、当最重的手段，也吓不住的时候

老子开头就问了一句——

"若民恒且不畏死,奈何以杀惧之也?"

如果人们已经长期不怕死了,你还想用"杀"来吓住他们吗?

这里有两个字要留意:"恒且"。 恒是"一直",且是"已经"。

老子说的不是"人天生不怕死",而是"人已经被弄到长期不怕死的地步了"。

这正接着上一章组末尾那句（ch72）:"民之不畏威,则大威将至矣"——当人们已经不怕你的威压,更大的反噬就要来了。 两句话,是同一件事的两段:先是不怕你的威,再是连死都不怕了。

（这里有一处字差:帛书写的是"以杀惧之",一个具体的动作;通行本改成了"以死惧之",变成了一种抽象的状态。帛书的"杀"跟后文的"司杀者""代司杀者杀"接得更紧,这一章按帛书读。）

这句话里的道理，其实非常普遍。

任何靠"怕"维持的关系,都有一个尽头。

你手里那张最重的牌,只要打出去了,对方还是不动——你就没牌了。 而这时候人最容易犯的错,是加码:再狠一点、再重一点。可你已经没有更重的了。

那个把话说到最狠的家长,那个把话撂到最绝的管理者,常常发现:对方反而更不理他了。 不是他不够狠,是狠已经用完了。

为什么会用完?

因为"怕"这件事,靠的是一个落差:你手里有他在乎、也怕失去的东西。 一旦对方心里那句"我已经没什么可失去的了"冒出来——落差没了,那份怕,也就跟着没了。

所以真正让人不怕的,往往不是他变强了,是他被逼到已经不剩什么了。

这就是老子那句话的分量:到了那一步,你不是缺一个更重的手段,你是根本不该走到那一步。

四、但老子并不天真

接着两段,老子说的话可能出乎你的意料——

"若民恒且畏死,而为畸者吾将得而杀之,夫孰敢矣?若民恒且必畏死,则恒有司杀者。"

如果人们一直是知道畏惧的(也就是还没被逼到那一步),那么有人行为不端,抓住处置了,就没人再敢。而如果人们一直是知道畏惧的,那就一直有"司杀者"——那个专门掌管这件事的位置。

注意:老子没有说"取消一切刑罚"。

他承认:在正常的秩序里,那个专门的位置,是必要的存在。 但他也没有把它抬高——它就是人间的一个位置、一个机构,不是天的代理人。

老子反对的,从来不是"有那个位置",而是"越位"。

而这里最值得留意的是:这番话,是对谁说的?

是对握权的那个人说的。 上面那句"吾将得而杀之"里的"吾",就是在位者自己。老子当面对他说:那件事有专门掌管的位置,你不要伸手去代替它。

换句话说,老子在两千多年前就划了一条线:该谁动刀,归那个专门的位置;握着权的人,不亲自去动。 这条线的方向,跟今天讲的司法独立是相通的——只是理由完全不同:后世讲的是权利与制衡,而老子讲的是一句贴身得多、也冷得多的话——你会伤到自己的手。

这里还有一个很值得看的字。

老子用的字是"畸"——偏离正道、行为不端。这个字非常朴素,只描述"他做了什么",不评判"他是什么人"。

而通行本把它改成了"奇"——奇异、邪门。一字之改,味道就变了:从"这个人做了偏离正道的事",变成了"这个人是个异类"。

这个差别,今天依然重要。 说一个人"做了这件不对的事",和说一个人"是个不对的人"——前者对着的是行为,后者对着的是这个人本身。 老子用的是前者。

为什么这很要紧?

因为一旦一个人被贴上"他就是那种人"的标签,接下来他做什么,都会被这个标签解释:他做得好,是"装的";他做得不好,是"果然如此"。标签一贴,这个人就再也没有被重新看见的机会了。

而老子那个朴素的"畸"字,给人留了一条路——偏离的是那件事,人还在那儿,还可以走回来。 这正是他一路讲过的:（ch27）圣人不抛弃任何人;（ch62）道从不因为一个人的过去就拒绝他。

五、代大匠斫：你一接过斧子，就接过了后果

现在,到了这一章的落点。老子说——

"夫代司杀者杀,是代大匠斫也。夫代大匠斫者,稀有不伤其手矣。"

代替那个专门掌管的人去动手,就等于代替大木匠去砍木头;而代替大木匠砍木头的人,很少有不伤到自己手的。

这个比喻,是这一章最有用的一句话。

为什么"几乎一定"会伤手?

因为斧子这种东西,大木匠握着它,靠的是几十年练出来的手感:木头的纹路走向、该用多大力、从哪个角度下去、什么时候该停——这些东西都不在道理里,在手上。

你可以把所有的原理都听懂,可"听懂"和"握得住"之间,隔着几十年。 很多真正的本事就是这样:它不在能说出口的那一部分里,而在说不出口的那一部分里。 道理你学得会,那双手,你学不来。

你没有那双手,却抡起了那把斧子。而斧子,不认你是谁。

放到今天看——

一个不懂行的人硬要拍板专业的方案,方案出了问题,第一个被追着问责的,是他自己——因为那是他定的;

一个家长替孩子把所有决定都做完,等孩子长大后过得不顺,那份怨和那份苦,最后都落回他身上;

一个管理者绕过负责的人直接插手,事情办砸了,他既怪不到别人头上,又已经把那个真正能办的人推远了。

机制其实很简单:那件事原本的风险,是长在别人位置上的。你一伸手接过来,它就成了你的。

你接过的从来不只是那把斧子,还有它落下去之后的全部后果。

那为什么人还是忍不住要伸手?

因为越位的诱惑,恰恰来自"我确实能做得不错"。

一个完全不懂的人,反而不敢碰那把斧子。最容易越位的,是那些"懂一点"的人——他真的懂一些,也真的着急,于是那句"我来吧,这样更快"就冒出来了。

而"更快",几乎总是真的。 你伸手接过来,那一刻确实更快了。代价从来不在那一刻结账——它在后面等着。

而这个比喻里,老子还藏了更深的一层。

"代大匠斫者，稀有不伤其手"——这句话反过来听:那就连大匠自己,抡斧头也是会伤手的。

斫木本来就是一件带着危险的活。 专业的人做,尚且要万分小心、时时警醒;何况一个根本不在那个位置上的人,轻轻松松就把手伸了过去。

所以这句话里有两重意思——

一重是:你不在那个位置上,你没有那双手。

另一重更重:有些事本身就重,重到连最内行的人都不敢大意。你凭什么觉得,自己可以轻描淡写地接过来?

那不越位,是不是就等于撒手不管?

不是。 老子从来没说"别管事"——他说的是"别越位"。

你在你自己的位置上,有你自己该做的事: 把方向定清楚、把资源给足、把该问的问题问到、出了事把责任扛下来。这些事没有一件是轻的,而且没有一件,是别人能替你做的。

真正的分寸是:担你该担的那份,别抢他手里那把斧子。

六、给读者的

这一章,给我们几件当下就能用的事——

第一:别把一段关系,押在"怕"上。

怕是会用完的（民恒且不畏死）。等到你最重的话也不起作用了,再加码是没有用的——你已经没有更重的了。

第二:承认有些位置是专门的。

不是谁都能站在那儿（恒有司杀者）。但也别把它神化,它只是一个位置。

第三:不是你的活,就别抢着抡那把斧头。

你一接过斧子,就同时接过了它落下之后的全部后果（代大匠斫）。原本长在别人位置上的风险,你一伸手,它就成了你的。

第四:越重的事,越别轻易伸手。

连最内行的人做起来都要万分小心（大匠斫木亦伤手）。你觉得"这有什么难的"的那一刻,往往正是最危险的时候。

第五:说他做了什么,别急着说他是什么人。

老子用的是那个最朴素的字（畸）:对着行为,不对着人。一旦你把一个人整个贴上标签,你就再也看不见他具体做了什么了。

这一章的核心,可以收成一句话——

每个位置都有它自己的分寸和风险。你越过位置去替别人动手,看起来是"我来担",其实是把那份本不属于你的后果,亲手接到了自己身上。

七、承上启下

第七十四章,是上一组末尾那句话的下半段——（ch72）说"人们已经不怕你的威了,更大的反噬就要来了";这一章说"人们连死都不怕了,你还想用什么吓住他们呢"。 两句话,一个道理,只是后一句更沉。

而"代大匠斫",是老子讲了一整本书的一件事,落到了最具体的一次:（ch29）和（ch64）都说过"为者败之,执者失之"——硬要去做的,反而做败;硬要去抓的,反而弄丢。 到这一章,他把它变成了一个你一看就懂的画面:一个不会用斧子的人,抡起了斧子。

下一章（ch75）,老子会接着"人为什么会把命看轻"往下问,并给出一句很深的话:"夫唯无以生为者,是贤贵生"——唯独那些不把"活着"当成一件要拼命经营的事的人,才真正懂得什么叫珍惜生命。 我们下一篇见。

秦汉（字大知）· @hqinjarsy · 大知道德经解系列

I. The Person Who Says "I'll Handle This"

You have probably seen this scene before.

There is a task with someone specifically in charge — someone who knows the field, has experience, and holds the measure of it. Then another person, usually the one with a higher position, reaches over and takes it: "I'll decide this one."

We usually sense that this is wrong, and the reason we give is "they're not expert enough; they'll probably botch it."

But the reason Laozi gives is far sharper than that.

He says: people who do this rarely avoid injuring their own hand.

Not "the task will be ruined" — but "you yourself will be hurt." That is what this chapter is about.

II. The Heaviest Subject in This Chapter

Let us be clear about the setting of this chapter.

In Chapter 74 of the Daodejing, Laozi is addressing the heaviest matter of his era: punishment, and killing.

He builds through three conditional arguments, one after another, arriving finally at a cold warning:

What happens when people have been driven to the point where they no longer fear death; what holds when order still functions and people still know fear; and that within that order, the designated role is unavoidable.

Then he says: but if you step over that role and reach in to do it yourself — you will hurt yourself.

And the first argument picks up directly from the final line of the preceding group.

III. When Even the Heaviest Threat No Longer Works

Laozi opens with a question —

"If people have long and continuously not feared death, how can you frighten them with killing?"

If people have already been brought, persistently, to the point where they do not fear death — what is the use of threatening them with killing?

Two words here deserve attention: "long and continuously" (héng qiě). Héng: always, continuously. Qiě: already.

Laozi is not saying "people are naturally unafraid of death." He is saying "people have already been driven, over time, to the point of not fearing death."

This picks up directly from the final line of Chapter 72: "When people no longer fear your authority, a far greater reckoning is coming." — When people no longer fear your pressure, what comes next is even larger. The two lines are two stages of the same thing: first, they stop fearing your authority; then, they stop fearing death.

(There is a textual variant here: the Silk Manuscript writes "frighten them with killing" — a concrete action; the received text changes this to "frighten them with death" — an abstract state. The Silk Manuscript's "killing" connects more tightly with "the one who governs killing" and "to kill in place of the one who governs killing" that follow. This chapter follows the Silk Manuscript.)

The principle in this line is far more universal than its historical context.

Any relationship maintained through fear has an end point.

When you have played your heaviest card and the other party still does not move — you are out of cards. And the most common mistake at this point is to escalate: a little harder, a little heavier. But you have nothing heavier left.

The parent who has said the harshest possible thing, the manager who has made the most absolute threat, often finds: the other party becomes even less responsive. It is not that they were insufficiently harsh — it is that harshness has been used up.

Why does it run out?

Because fear depends on a gap: you hold something the other person cares about and is afraid to lose. The moment "I have nothing left to lose" rises in the other person's mind — the gap closes, and the fear closes with it.

So what truly makes people unafraid is usually not that they have grown stronger — it is that they have been driven to the point where nothing remains.

That is the weight of what Laozi is saying: at that point, the problem is not that you lack a heavier instrument. The problem is that you should never have gotten there.

IV. But Laozi Is Not Naive

The next two arguments may surprise you —

"If people continuously fear death, then when someone behaves improperly, we seize them and kill them — who would dare? If people continuously and certainly fear death, then there will always be a designated one who governs killing."

If people still know fear — that is, if they have not yet been driven to that point — then when someone acts improperly and is dealt with accordingly, no one else dares. And if people continuously know fear, then there will always be a designated role for governing such matters.

Note: Laozi does not say "abolish all punishment."

He acknowledges: within normal order, that designated role is a necessary presence. But he does not elevate it — it is a position, an institution in the human world; it is not an agent of Heaven.

What Laozi opposes has never been "having that role" — it is overstepping that role.

And here the most important question: to whom is this addressed?

It is addressed to the one who holds power. The "I" in "I shall seize them and kill them" is the ruler himself. Laozi speaks to him directly: there is a role specifically designated for that matter — do not reach in to replace it.

In other words, Laozi drew a line over two thousand years ago: whoever wields the knife belongs to the designated role; the one holding power does not personally reach in. The direction of this line resonates with what we today call judicial independence — but the reasoning is entirely different: later ages speak of rights and checks and balances, while Laozi offers something far more personal, and far colder — you will hurt your own hand.

There is also a character here very worth noticing.

The word Laozi uses is "jī" — deviating from the proper course, behaving improperly. This character is very plain; it describes "what this person did" without judging "what kind of person they are."

The received text changes it to "qí" — strange, aberrant. One character changed, and the tone entirely shifts: from "this person did something that deviated from the proper course" to "this person is an aberration."

This distinction remains important today. Saying that a person "did this particular wrong thing" is different from saying "this person is a wrong person" — the first addresses the action; the second addresses the person themselves. Laozi uses the first.

Why does this matter?

Because once a person is labeled "that is the kind of person they are," everything they do afterward gets interpreted through that label: when they do well, it is "an act"; when they do poorly, it is "as expected." Once the label is affixed, that person never gets the chance to be seen fresh again.

Laozi's plain character jī leaves a path open — what deviated was the action; the person is still there and can still find their way back. This is exactly what he has said throughout: (ch. 27) the sage abandons no one; (ch. 62) the Dao never refuses someone on account of their past.

V. Swinging the Master Carpenter's Axe — You Take the Consequences with It

Now we arrive at the chapter's landing point. Laozi says —

"To kill in place of the one who governs killing is to chop in place of the master carpenter. Those who chop in place of the master carpenter — very rarely do they not injure their hand."

To step in and act in place of the one designated for that role is like taking the axe from the master carpenter; and those who take the master carpenter's axe almost invariably cut themselves.

This analogy is the most useful line in the chapter.

Why does this "almost certainly" result in injury?

Because the axe, in the master carpenter's hands, runs on decades of feel: the grain of the wood, how much force to apply, the angle of approach, when to stop — none of this lives in explanations; it lives in the hands.

You can understand every principle, but between "understanding" and "being able to hold it" lies decades. Much of what constitutes real skill is exactly like this: it does not live in the part that can be put into words — it lives in the part that cannot. You can learn the theory; the hands, you cannot learn.

You do not have those hands. But you have raised the axe. And the axe does not know who you are.

Seen from today —

The person who insists on overriding expert decisions, and when the decision goes wrong, they are the first to be held accountable — because it was their call.

The parent who makes every decision for their child, and when the child struggles as an adult, all the resentment and grief eventually circles back.

The manager who bypasses the responsible person and intervenes directly, and when things go wrong, they can blame no one else, and they have already pushed away the very person who could have handled it.

The mechanism is simple: the risk that originally resided in someone else's position becomes yours the moment you reach in and take over.

What you take over is never just the axe — it is the full weight of everything that follows when it comes down.

So why do people still reach in?

Because the temptation to overstep comes precisely from "I actually could do this reasonably well."

A person who knows nothing about it would not dare touch the axe. The ones most prone to overstepping are those who "know a little" — they genuinely know something, they genuinely feel urgency, and so "let me — this way is faster" comes out.

And "faster" is almost always true. The moment you reach in, it genuinely does go faster. The cost is never settled in that moment — it waits for you further on.

And within this analogy, Laozi has hidden a deeper layer.

"Those who chop in place of the master carpenter rarely avoid injuring their hand" — heard the other way: even the master carpenter himself can injure his hand chopping wood.

Chopping wood is inherently dangerous work. Even those who have made it their life's work must be careful every time; how much more so someone who has no business being there, lifting the axe as though it were nothing?

So this line carries two meanings —

One: you are not in that role; you do not have those hands.

The other, heavier: some things are inherently weighty — so weighty that even the most expert practitioner cannot afford to be careless. On what grounds do you think you can pick it up lightly?

Does "not overstepping" mean washing your hands of everything?

No. Laozi never said "don't manage anything" — he said "don't overstep."

In your own position, there is your own work to do: set the direction clearly, make sure resources are sufficient, ask the questions that need to be asked, and when something goes wrong, take the responsibility. None of these things is light — and none of them can be done by someone else in your place.

The real measure is: carry what is yours to carry, and don't grab the axe out of someone else's hands.

VI. To the Reader

This chapter gives us several things useful right now —

First: don't stake a relationship on fear.

Fear has a bottom — it runs out. Once your heaviest word no longer works, escalating further is useless: you have nothing heavier left.

Second: acknowledge that some roles are specialized.

Not everyone can stand in that place. But don't mythologize it either — it is a position, nothing more.

Third: if it is not your work, don't grab that axe.

The moment you take the axe, you also take all the consequences that follow when it comes down. The risk that originally lived in someone else's position becomes yours the moment you reach in.

Fourth: the heavier the matter, the more carefully before reaching in.

Even the most expert practitioner must be careful at every stroke. The moment you think "what's so hard about this?" is often exactly when you are most at risk.

Fifth: say what someone did — don't rush to say what kind of person they are.

Laozi uses the plainest character: it addresses the action, not the person. Once you label a person entirely, you can no longer see what they actually do.

The core of this chapter can be gathered into one line —

Every position has its own measure and its own risk. When you overstep and do someone else's work for them, it looks like "I'm shouldering this" — but what you are actually doing is taking into your own hands consequences that were never meant to be yours.

VII. Connecting Forward and Back

Chapter 74 is the second half of the statement that closed the preceding group — (ch. 72) said "when people no longer fear your authority, a far greater reckoning is coming"; this chapter says "when people have long not feared death, what do you think you can frighten them with?" Two statements, one principle — the second one heavier.

And "chopping in place of the master carpenter" is the most concrete rendering of something Laozi has said throughout the book: (ch. 29) and (ch. 64) both say "those who force things ruin them; those who grip things lose them." In this chapter he turns it into an image you grasp immediately: someone who cannot use an axe, raising an axe.

The next chapter (ch. 75) will follow the thread of "why people come to regard their lives as cheap," and offer a line of great depth: "Only the one who does not make a project of living truly knows how to value life." We will see it next.
