# 道，从不因为你的过去拒绝你

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 5 月

一、没有人是该被放弃的

有些人会觉得——

我过去做错了太多事,来不及了。 我现在这个样子,已经没救了。 我不够好,不配拥有更好的东西。

老子在《道德经》第六十二章里,对所有这样想的人,说了一句特别温暖的话——

"人之不善,何弃之有?"

一个人,就算还没变好、还没走上正道、还带着过去的错——

哪有什么理由,就该把他抛弃呢?

这一章讲的是——道,对所有人都不弃。

无论你是已经走在正道上的人,还是还在泥泞里挣扎的人; 无论你过去做过什么、现在是什么状态——

道都不会因为你的过去而拒绝你。它随时都在那里,等着你走向它。

这一章讲的是——

为什么道对所有人一视同仁、从不放弃任何人。以及,什么才是真正珍贵的东西。

二、道，是每个人的宝

第六十二章开头——

"道者万物之主也,善人之宝也,不善人之所葆也。"

道,是万物归向的地方;是"向善之人"的宝贝;也是"还没向善之人"的守护。

"道者万物之主"——道是万物归向的地方。

这个"主"不是"主宰、统治"——

是"万物都归向它、汇聚到它"的那个中心(接上一章 ch61"大邦下流"、以及 ch4"万物之宗")。

就像百川归海——道,是万物自然归向的地方。

接下来是关键的一句:道对两种人,都是好的——

"善人之宝"——对于"向善的人"(正在走上正道、正在向上走的人),道是他的宝贝。

他把道当作最珍贵的东西,用道来指引自己、涵养自己、帮助别人。

"不善人之所葆"——对于"还没向善的人"(还在挣扎、还带着过去的错的人),道也守护着他。

注意——道不是只对"好人"开放。

对那些还没走上正道的人,道也在守护着他们——

道不因为他们"还不够好"就抛弃他们,而是依然守护着他们,等着他们走向它。

这是这一章最动人的地方——

道不挑人。

不是"你要先变好,道才接纳你";

而是"无论你现在是什么样,道都在守护你、等着你"。

就像阳光,不会只照好人、不照坏人;

就像大地,不会只承载优秀的人、抛弃平凡的人——

道,对每一个人,都不弃。

三、华丽的言辞、体面的排场，都比不上真东西

接下来一句——

"美言可以市,尊行可以贺人。"

华丽的言辞可以用来交易,体面的行为可以用来送人情。

"美言可以市"——漂亮话可以拿来做交易。

"尊行可以贺人"——体面的举动可以拿来送人情、抬高别人。

老子这里不是在赞美"美言""尊行"——

恰恰相反,他是在说:这些华丽的言辞、体面的排场,都还停留在"表面功夫、外在交易"的层面。

漂亮话可以换来好处(市),体面的姿态可以拿来做人情(贺人)——

这些都是外在的、可以交易的、做给人看的东西。

它们和接下来要讲的"坐进此道"(真正向道),形成鲜明对比——

一个是外在的表面功夫,一个是内在的真东西。

放到 2026 年看——

我们身边充满了"美言"和"尊行"——

漂亮的话术、体面的包装、精心设计的人设、拿来交换利益的姿态——

这些都是"美言可以市,尊行可以贺人"。

老子提醒我们:别被这些表面的东西迷惑。真正珍贵的,是内在的真东西,不是外在的漂亮包装。

四、就算还没变好，也不该被抛弃

接下来就是那句温暖的话——

"人之不善,何弃之有?"

一个人就算还没变好,又有什么理由抛弃他呢?

"人之不善"——一个人还没变好、还没走上正道、还带着过去的问题。

"何弃之有"——哪有什么理由就该抛弃他?

这句话的分量很重——

老子说的不是"好人不该被抛弃"(那是常识)——

他说的是:就连还没变好的人,也不该被抛弃。

那些还在挣扎的、还带着过去错误的、还没走上正道的人——

恰恰是这些人,更不该被放弃。

为什么?

因为——

每个人都有向道的可能。

一个人现在"不善",不代表他永远"不善";

只要道还在守护他、还给他留着门——

他随时都可能走向道、走上正道。

如果你现在就把他抛弃了,就断了他所有的可能。

这接前面讲过的道理——

ch27:圣人总是善于救人,从不抛弃任何人(无弃人)。 ch49:对好的人好,对还没变好的人也好(不善者亦善之)。 ch62:就算还没变好,也不该被抛弃(何弃之有)。

一路下来:道,不弃任何人。

放到 2026 年看——

一个还没做出成绩的下属、一个还在犯错的孩子、一个还没走上正轨的朋友、一个过去有过失败的自己——

都不该被放弃。

因为——只要门还在,人就还有走向更好的可能。

而这个"门",永远都在。

五、真正珍贵的，不是排场，是安坐入道

接下来老子做了一个对比——

"故立天子置三卿,虽有拱之璧以先驷马,不若坐进此道。"

所以,就算立了天子、设了三公高官,就算献上珍贵的玉璧、驾着四马大车的隆重礼节——都不如安安静静地坐入此道。

"立天子置三卿"——建立天子之位,设置三公高官。

这是人间最尊贵的位置。

"拱之璧以先驷马"——双手合抱的玉璧、四匹马拉的大车。

这是人间最隆重的礼节、最贵重的排场。

但老子说——

"不若坐进此道"——这一切,都不如安坐入道。

再尊贵的位子、再隆重的排场、再贵重的礼物——

都比不上安安静静地走进道里。

"坐进此道"是什么?

就是安安静静地、不需要任何外在仪式地,走进道、向着道、活在道里。

不需要天子的位子,不需要高官的头衔,不需要贵重的玉璧,不需要隆重的排场——

你只需要,安静地坐进道里。

这是每个人都能做到的——不论你有没有地位、有没有钱、有没有排场。

这里有一个特别深的地方——

真正得道的人,反而不去追求那些外在的尊贵位置。

历史上——

庄子拒绝了楚王请他做宰相的邀请; 颜回甘于住陋巷、吃粗食,不改其乐; 老子辞官,骑青牛出关而去。

他们本来最有能力去坐那些尊贵的位子,却恰恰避开了。

为什么?

因为他们知道——

那些外在的尊贵,远不如内在地安坐入道。

六、不要那些尊贵的，反而最尊贵

整章的收束——

"古之所以贵此道者何也?不谓求以得,有罪以免与?故为天下贵。"

古时候人们为什么把道看得这么珍贵?不就是因为:向它寻求就能得到,有过错也能得到救助吗?所以,道是天下最珍贵的。

"求以得"——向道寻求,就能得到。

道不遥远、不难得——

你向它,它就在;你走向它,就能得到它。

不需要复杂的仪式、艰难的条件——求道就在道中。

"有罪以免"——有过错,也能得到救助。

这里要特别说清楚——

"有罪以免"不是说"信了道,做坏事就没有后果了"——

而是说:道不因为你过去的错误而抛弃你。

即使你曾经犯过错、走过弯路、跌过跟头——

只要你愿意走向道,道依然守护你、接纳你,不因你的过去而拒绝你。

这就是"不善人之所葆"——道,连还没变好的人,也守护着。

"故为天下贵"——所以,道是天下最珍贵的。

为什么道最珍贵?

因为——

你向它,就能得到(求以得); 你有错,它也不弃你(有罪以免); 它对每一个人都开放,从不放弃任何人(何弃之有)。

这样的道,才是天下最珍贵的。

这里藏着这一章最深的一个道理——

那些世俗眼中最"贵"的东西——天子的位子、高官的头衔、贵重的玉璧、隆重的排场——

真正得道的人反而不要。

而正因为不要那些外在的尊贵,他反而拥有了最珍贵的东西——道。

不要天下最贵的位子,反而成了天下最贵的人。

这是《道德经》一以贯之的道理(接 ch7、ch22、ch34)——

不争,反而没有人能争过你; 不要外在的尊贵,反而拥有真正的尊贵。

七、给读者的

这一章给读者两份特别温暖、也特别重要的礼物——

第一份:无论你现在是什么样,你都没有被放弃。

如果你觉得——

我过去错得太多、我现在不够好、我已经来不及了——

请记住老子这句话:"人之不善,何弃之有?"

没有人是该被放弃的,包括你自己。

道从不因为你的过去而拒绝你——

只要你愿意走向它,它随时都在(求以得); 就算你曾经跌倒过,它也不弃你(有罪以免)。

门,永远都在。你随时可以走进去。

同样地——对待别人,也别轻易放弃。

那个还没做出成绩的人、那个还在犯错的孩子、那个还没走上正轨的朋友——

只要门还在,他们就有变好的可能。别断了他们的门。

第二份:真正珍贵的,不是外在的排场,是内在的安坐入道。

你是不是常常在追逐那些外在的东西——更高的位子、更好的头衔、更体面的排场、更漂亮的人设?

记住"不若坐进此道"——

这一切,都不如安安静静地,活在道里。

真正的珍贵,不需要任何外在的仪式和排场——

它是每个人都能拥有的:安静地,走进道。

这一章的核心,是一种最深的温柔和最高的智慧——

最深的温柔:道不弃任何人。无论你是谁、过去做过什么、现在是什么样——门永远都在。

最高的智慧:真正珍贵的,不是外在的尊贵,是内在的安坐入道。不要那些世俗的贵,反而拥有真正的贵。

最后,回到开头那句话:没有人是该被放弃的。

老子两千四百年前就说了——

"人之不善,何弃之有?"

无论你现在多么"不善"、多么"不够好"、多么觉得"来不及了"——

道都不会因为你的过去,而拒绝你。

它就在那里,安安静静地,等着你走向它。

门永远都在。

你随时,都可以坐进去。

I. No One Should Be Abandoned

Some people feel —

I've done too many things wrong in the past. It's too late. I'm not good enough. I don't deserve anything better.

In Chapter 62 of the Daodejing, Laozi says something especially warm to everyone who feels this way —

"As for those who are not yet good — why should there be any cause to abandon them?"

A person, even if they haven't yet become good, haven't yet found the right path, still carry the weight of past mistakes —

What possible reason is there to cast them aside?

This chapter is about — the Dao does not abandon anyone.

Whether you are already walking the right path, or still struggling in the mud; whether you have done great things in the past or stumbled badly — the Dao never refuses you because of your past. It is always there, waiting for you to walk toward it.

This chapter is about —

Why the Dao treats all people without exception, abandoning none. And what is truly precious.

II. The Dao Is Everyone's Treasure

The chapter opens —

"The Dao is the master of all things; it is the treasure of those who are good; it is the protection of those who are not yet good."

"The Dao is the master of all things" — the Dao is where all things converge.

This "master" is not "ruler, controller" — it is "the center toward which all things naturally converge and gather" (connecting ch. 61's lower course, and ch. 4's ancestor of all things).

Like all rivers returning to the sea — the Dao is where all things naturally flow.

Now comes the crucial line: the Dao is good for two kinds of people —

"The treasure of those who are good" — for those who are already walking toward the good (those on the path, moving upward), the Dao is their treasure.

They hold the Dao as the most precious thing, using it to guide themselves, nurture themselves, help others.

"The protection of those who are not yet good" — for those who haven't yet walked toward the good (still struggling, still carrying past mistakes), the Dao also protects them.

Note — the Dao is not only open to "good people."

For those who haven't yet found the right path, the Dao also protects them — the Dao does not abandon them because they "aren't good enough yet." It still protects them, waiting for them to walk toward it.

This is the most moving part of this chapter —

The Dao does not pick its people.

Not "you must become good first, then the Dao accepts you" — but "whatever you are right now, the Dao is protecting you and waiting for you."

Like sunlight, which doesn't shine only on good people and not on bad ones; like the earth, which doesn't carry only the excellent and discard the ordinary — the Dao abandons no one.

III. Beautiful Words and Grand Gestures Cannot Compare to the Real Thing

The next line —

"Beautiful words can be traded; honorable conduct can be offered to others as a gift."

"Beautiful words can be traded" — fine words can be used for exchange.

"Honorable conduct can be offered as a gift" — dignified behavior can be given as a favor to others.

Laozi is not praising "beautiful words" and "honorable conduct" here —

He is pointing out that these polished words and dignified appearances remain at the level of surface performance, external exchange.

Fine words can get you something (traded), dignified posture can be used to do favors (offered as gifts) — all of these are external, tradable, performed for others to see.

They stand in sharp contrast with what comes next — "sitting quietly into this Dao" (truly entering the Dao) —

One is surface performance, the other is the real inner thing.

Seen from 2026 —

We are surrounded by "beautiful words" and "honorable conduct" — fine rhetoric, polished packaging, carefully crafted personas, postures exchanged for benefits — all "beautiful words can be traded, honorable conduct can be given as gifts."

Laozi reminds us: don't be deceived by surface things. What is truly precious is the inner real thing, not the outer beautiful packaging.

IV. Even the Not-Yet-Good Should Not Be Abandoned

Now the warm line —

"As for those who are not yet good — why should there be any cause to abandon them?"

"Those who are not yet good" — people who haven't yet become good, haven't yet found the right path, still carry the weight of past mistakes.

"Why should there be any cause to abandon them" — what possible reason is there to cast them aside?

The weight of this line is considerable —

Laozi is not saying "good people shouldn't be abandoned" (that's obvious) — he is saying: even those who haven't yet become good should not be abandoned.

Those still struggling, those still carrying past mistakes, those who haven't yet found the right path — precisely these people are the ones who especially must not be given up on.

Why?

Because — every person has the potential to walk toward the Dao.

A person being "not yet good" now doesn't mean they will be "not yet good" forever; as long as the Dao is still protecting them, still keeping the door open — they may walk toward the Dao at any time, find the right path.

If you abandon them now, you cut off all their possibilities.

This connects the thread running through earlier chapters —

Ch. 27: the sage is always skilled at saving people, never abandoning anyone (no abandoned people). Ch. 49: treat the good well, and treat those not yet good well also (be good even to the not-good). Ch. 62: even those not yet good should not be abandoned (why cause to abandon them?).

One line running through: the Dao abandons no one.

Seen from 2026 —

A subordinate who hasn't yet produced results, a child still making mistakes, a friend not yet on track, a past self with failures — none of these should be abandoned.

Because — as long as the door is still there, there is still the possibility of becoming better.

And that "door" is always there.

V. What Is Truly Precious — Not Grand Ceremony but Quietly Sitting into the Dao

Laozi then makes a comparison —

"Therefore: even if you establish the Son of Heaven, install the three high ministers, even if you present jade discs with both hands preceded by teams of four horses — this is not as good as quietly sitting into this Dao."

"Establishing the Son of Heaven, installing the three ministers" — the most honored position in the human world.

"Jade discs presented with both hands, teams of four horses" — the most elaborate ceremony, the most precious and grand display.

But Laozi says — "this is not as good as quietly sitting into this Dao."

The most honored position, the most elaborate ceremony, the most precious gifts, the grandest display — none of it compares to quietly sitting into the Dao.

"Quietly sitting into this Dao" — what is it?

It is, quietly, without needing any external ceremony, walking into the Dao — toward the Dao — living in the Dao.

No need for the position of Son of Heaven, no need for the title of high minister, no need for precious jade discs, no need for an elaborate display — you only need to quietly sit into the Dao.

This is something everyone can do — regardless of whether you have status, money, or ceremony.

Here lies something profoundly deep —

Those who truly attain the Dao don't go after those external honored positions.

Throughout history — Zhuangzi declined the King of Chu's invitation to become Chancellor; Yan Hui was content in a narrow lane eating coarse food, his joy unaltered; Laozi resigned his office, rode a green ox out through the pass and was gone.

They were the most capable of occupying those honored positions, and precisely they avoided them.

Why? Because they knew — that external honor is far less than inwardly sitting into the Dao.

VI. Not Wanting Those Honors — and Thus Having the Most Precious Thing

The chapter's close —

"Why did the ancients prize this Dao so highly? Was it not because: seek it and you receive, have wrongs and you are sheltered? Therefore it is the most precious thing under heaven."

"Seek it and you receive" — walk toward the Dao and you receive it.

The Dao is not distant, not hard to obtain — turn toward it and it is there; walk toward it and you receive it.

No complex ritual, no difficult conditions — seek the Dao and you are in the Dao.

"Have wrongs and you are sheltered" — having past wrongs, you still find shelter.

This must be read carefully —

"Having wrongs and being sheltered" does not mean "believe in the Dao and bad actions have no consequences" — it means: the Dao does not abandon you because of your past wrongs.

Even if you have committed mistakes, walked crooked paths, stumbled and fallen — as long as you are willing to walk toward the Dao, the Dao still protects you, still accepts you, does not refuse you because of your past.

This is "the protection of those not yet good" — the Dao protects even those who haven't yet become good.

"Therefore it is the most precious thing under heaven."

Why is the Dao most precious?

Because — walk toward it and you receive it (seek it and you receive); have wrongs and it doesn't abandon you (have wrongs and you are sheltered); it is open to every person, abandoning no one (why cause to abandon them?).

A Dao like this is the most precious thing under heaven.

Hidden here is the deepest insight of this chapter —

Those things that are "most honored" in worldly eyes — the Son of Heaven's position, high ministers' titles, precious jade, grand ceremonies — those who truly attain the Dao don't go after them.

And precisely because they don't seek those external honors, they come to possess the most precious thing — the Dao.

Not seeking what is most honored under heaven — and thus becoming the most precious under heaven.

This is the consistent thread running through the Daodejing (connecting ch. 7, ch. 22, ch. 34) — not contending, and thus no one can contend against you; not seeking external honor, and thus possessing true honor.

VII. To the Reader

This chapter offers two especially warm and important gifts —

First gift: no matter what you are right now, you have not been abandoned.

If you feel — I've done too many things wrong in the past; I'm not good enough now; it's already too late —

Please remember Laozi's words: "As for those who are not yet good — why should there be any cause to abandon them?"

No one should be abandoned. Including you.

The Dao never refuses you because of your past — as long as you are willing to walk toward it, it is always there (seek it and you receive); even if you have stumbled before, it does not abandon you (have wrongs and you are sheltered).

The door is always there. You can walk through it at any time.

And equally — don't be quick to abandon others.

That person who hasn't yet produced results, that child still making mistakes, that friend not yet on track — as long as the door is still there, they still have the possibility of becoming better. Don't close their door.

Second gift: what is truly precious is not external ceremony — it is inwardly sitting into the Dao.

Are you often chasing external things — a higher position, a better title, a more dignified display, a more polished persona?

Remember "not as good as quietly sitting into this Dao" — none of this compares to quietly living in the Dao.

The truly precious requires no external ceremony or display — it is something everyone can possess: quietly, walking into the Dao.

This chapter's core is the deepest gentleness and the highest wisdom —

The deepest gentleness: the Dao abandons no one. Whatever you are, whatever your past — the door is always there.

The highest wisdom: what is truly precious is not external honor — it is inwardly sitting into the Dao. Not seeking the world's most honored position, and thus coming to possess the truly precious.

Finally, back to the opening: no one should be abandoned.

Laozi said it 2,400 years ago —

"As for those who are not yet good — why should there be any cause to abandon them?"

No matter how "not good" you are right now, how "not good enough" you feel, how much you feel "it's already too late" —

The Dao will never refuse you because of your past.

It is quietly there, waiting for you to walk toward it.

The door is always there.

At any time, you can sit quietly into it.
