Chapter 48 of 81

做减法，比做加法难

Subtraction Is Harder Than Addition

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、我们都在做加法

我们这个时代,擅长做加法。

学更多技能、加更多项目、考更多证书、建更多人脉、立更多身份标签。

简历要越来越长,技能要越来越多,头衔要越来越响。

"成长"几乎等于"往身上加东西"。

老子在《道德经》第四十八章里,讲了一件相反的事——

"为学者日益,闻道者日损。"

求学的人,每天往身上加东西;

听从道的人,每天往身上去东西。

真正的功夫,不在加,在减。

而且——

减,比加难得多。

这一章讲的是——

怎么把自己身上那些堆积的东西,一样一样拿掉,直到回到最本真的状态。

二、加法的人，和减法的人

第四十八章开头是两句对照——

"为学者日益,闻道者日损。"

先看"为学者日益"——

"为学"不是"学习"那个普通意思(学习是好的、自然的)。

"为学"是:把"学问"立成一个招牌,把知识变成"我懂的东西",往身上贴。

"日益"是每天增加——

每天多学一点、多懂一点、多一个标签、多一项技能——

往身上加的东西越来越多。

这听起来是好事啊——懂得越多不是越好吗?

老子说:不一定。

因为——你往身上加的每一样"我懂的东西",都可能变成一道遮挡。

加得越多,你越被这些"我已经懂了"的东西框住——

你越来越难看到新的、不一样的、超出你已有框架的东西。

这接上一章(ch47)"越往外走,知道得越少"——

加得越多,反而把真正重要的东西遮住了。

再看"闻道者日损"——

"闻道"不是"为道"(主动去追求道、去得到道)。

"闻"是听从——一种安静的、接纳的姿态。不是主动去抓道,是站在自己的位上,让道自然显出来。

"日损"是每天减少——

每天去掉一个执念、放下一个偏见、卸下一个身份标签、撕掉一个"我必须是这样"的招牌——

往身上去的东西越来越少。

减到最后,那些遮挡都没了,道反而自然显出来了。

两种人,两个方向——

加法的人(为学):往身上贴,越贴越多,越贴越被框住。

减法的人(闻道):往身上减,越减越少,越减越通透。

三、减，是手术刀般的主动

这里有一个最容易被误解的地方,必须说清楚——

"闻道者日损"听起来很被动、很消极——

好像是"什么都不做、躺平、放弃"。

完全不是。

"闻道"的姿态确实是"被动"的——

但这个"被动",是指不向外强求——

不立"我必须得到什么"的目标,不向外面去抓、去争、去求。

但"日损"这个动作本身,是极其主动、极其用力的。

为什么?

因为——

你要拿掉的,是自己身上长出来的东西。

你的偏见、你的立场、你的身份认同、你多年养成的习惯、你引以为傲的标签——

这些都是长在你身上的,跟你长在一起了。

要把它们一个一个拿掉——

像做手术一样,把长在自己身上的东西,一块一块切下来。

这需要极大的勇气、极强的自我觉察、极狠的对自己下手。

这不是躺平,这是手术刀般的主动。

放到 2026 年看——

加法很容易——报个课、买本书、加个技能,往身上贴就行,不疼。

减法很难——

要承认"我引以为傲的某个身份,其实困住了我";

要放下"我坚持多年的某个立场,其实是偏见";

要卸下"我用来定义自己的某个标签,其实不是真正的我"——

这些都是往自己身上动刀,疼得很。

这接 ch33"战胜自己才是真强、持续走的人才有志"——

减法,就是战胜自己。是把自己身上立着的招牌,一个一个拿掉的主动功夫。

加法谁都会,减法才见功夫。

四、减到最后，是"无为"

老子接着说——

"损之又损,以至于无为。"

减了又减,一直减到"无为"。

"损之又损"——

不是减一次就完了。

是减了又减,一个接一个地拿掉——

拿掉一个招牌,发现底下还有一个;再拿掉,还有一个——

一层一层往下减。

"以至于无为"——

一直减到"无为"。

"无为"不是"什么都不做"(这是对老子最大的误解)。

"无为"是:减到最后,身上再没有多余的招牌了,回到了最本真、最自然的状态。

到了这个状态,你做事不再是"为了维护某个身份"、"为了证明某个标签"、"为了撑住某个招牌"——

你做事,只是自然地做,顺着事情本来的样子做。

这就是"无为"——不是不做,是不带着那些多余的招牌去做。

放到 2026 年看——

一个把所有身份标签都看淡的人(无为),反而活得最自由——

他不需要为了"维护成功人士形象"而焦虑,不需要为了"撑住专家人设"而紧绷,不需要为了"证明自己"而用力——

他只是自然地活、自然地做。

这就是减到最后的状态。

五、不带招牌地做，反而什么都成

老子接着说了一句很有名的话——

"无为而无不为。"

不带招牌地做,反而没有什么做不成的。

这句话常常被误读成"什么都不做,却什么都做成了"——好像在讲一种玄虚的魔法。

不是。

真意是:不带着那些多余的招牌去做,反而什么都能成。

为什么?

因为——

当你带着招牌做事的时候,你的注意力有一半在"维护招牌"上。

做一件事,你一半的心思在"这样做能不能显得我很厉害"、"会不会损害我的形象"、"符不符合我的人设"——

你被招牌分了心,反而做不好。

当你卸掉招牌做事的时候,你的全部注意力都在事情本身上。

你不再关心"这样做显不显得我厉害",你只关心"这件事怎么做才对"——

心无旁骛,反而什么都能做成。

放到 2026 年看——

一个不在乎"显不显得专业"的人,反而能做出最专业的东西(因为他全部心思在事情上,不在表演专业上)。

一个不在乎"赢不赢"的人,反而常常赢(因为他专注做事,不被输赢的焦虑干扰)。

卸掉招牌,反而什么都成。这就是"无为而无不为"。

这接 ch37"守住不乱管,万物自己演化"——

不立招牌、不强求,事情反而按它自己的方式成了。

六、想得到，反而得不到

整章最后,老子把这个道理用在"取天下"上——

"将欲取天下也恒无事,及其有事也又不足以取天下矣。"

想要取天下,要持续地不折腾;一旦开始折腾,反而取不到天下了。

"恒无事"——持续地不立"我要做什么大事"的招牌,不折腾。

"有事"——开始折腾,立"我要取天下"的招牌,到处动作。

老子的意思是——

你越是立"我要得到天下"的招牌、越是到处折腾去抓,越得不到。

你越是不立这个招牌、不折腾,天下反而自然归你。

这接 ch29"想强行取天下的,我看他得不到"——

想抓的,抓不住;不抓的,反而拥有。

放到 2026 年看——

这个道理远不止"取天下"——

越想抓住一段关系(到处折腾、患得患失),越容易失去;不那么用力,反而长久。

越想抓住用户(各种套路、拼命挽留),越留不住;真正做好产品、不折腾,用户反而留下。

越想证明自己、抓住名声(到处表演、刻意经营),越显得用力;踏实做事、不折腾,名声反而自然来。

想得到,反而得不到。不强求,反而拥有。

七、给读者的

这一章给读者一个反主流的提醒——

在一个拼命做加法的时代,试着做做减法。

具体来说——

关于"学"——

你是不是一直在往身上加(加技能、加证书、加标签),却很少停下来减?

试试——减掉一些。

放下一个你其实不需要的执念,卸下一个困住你的身份标签,撕掉一个"我必须是这样"的招牌。

减比加难,但减了之后,你会更通透。

关于"做事"——

你做事的时候,是不是有一半心思在"维护形象、撑住人设、显得厉害"上?

试试——卸掉那些招牌,只关心事情本身。

不在乎显不显得厉害,只在乎做对没做对——你反而做得更好。

关于"得到"——

你是不是越想得到什么,就越用力去抓?

试试——松开手。

越想抓的越抓不住,不那么用力、不那么折腾,反而自然拥有。

这不是叫你停止学习、停止做事、停止追求。

是提醒你——

真正的功夫,往往不在"加",在"减"。

加法谁都会,减法才见真功夫。

把自己身上那些多余的招牌一个个拿掉,回到最本真的状态——

那时候你会发现,你不需要那么多东西来定义自己、撑住自己、证明自己。

你只是自然地活、自然地做。

而恰恰是这个时候,什么都成了。

最后回到那两句对照——

"为学者日益,闻道者日损。"

加法的人,越加越多,越加越重。

减法的人,越减越少,越减越轻——

直到轻得可以自由地活、自由地做。

在这个拼命往身上加东西的时代——

老子说:试着减一减。

减到最后,你会遇见那个不需要任何招牌的、最本真的自己。

一、We Are Good at Addition

Our age is skilled at addition.

More skills, more projects, more certificates, more connections, more identity labels. The résumé must keep growing, skills must keep multiplying, titles must keep rising.

"Growth" has come to mean "adding things to yourself."

Laozi in Chapter 48 of the Daodejing describes the opposite—

"For those who pursue learning, something is added each day; for those who listen to the Dao, something is removed each day."

The real skill is not in adding. It is in subtracting.

And — subtraction is far harder than addition.

This chapter asks: how do you strip away what has accumulated on you — one thing at a time — until you return to the most authentic state?

二、The Adder and the Subtractor

Chapter 48 opens with two parallel lines—

"For those who pursue learning, something is added each day; for those who listen to the Dao, something is removed each day."

First, "for those who pursue learning, something is added each day"—

"为学" does not mean ordinary learning (learning is good and natural). "为学" means: making "learning" itself into a banner, turning knowledge into "things I know," sticking them onto yourself.

"Added each day" — every day, a bit more learned, a bit more understood, one more label, one more skill—

the things stuck on you keep multiplying.

This sounds like progress — isn't knowing more always better? Laozi says: not necessarily.

Because — every thing you stick on yourself as "something I know" can become a wall. The more you accumulate, the more you are boxed in by all your "I already understand this" — the harder it becomes to see anything new, different, or beyond your existing framework.

This connects to ch. 47 "The further you go, the less you know"— the more you add, the more the truly important gets buried.

Next, "for those who listen to the Dao, something is removed each day"—

"闻道" is not "为道" (actively pursuing and obtaining the Dao). "闻" means listening — a quiet, receptive posture. Not going out to grab the Dao; standing in one's own position and letting the Dao reveal itself naturally.

"Removed each day" — every day, one fixation less, one bias set aside, one identity label unpeeled, one "I must be this way" banner torn down—

the things on you keep decreasing.

Once enough is removed, the obstructions are gone, and the Dao reveals itself naturally.

Two types. Two directions—

The adder (pursuing learning): keeps sticking things on, growing heavier and more confined.

The subtractor (listening to the Dao): keeps removing things, growing lighter and clearer.

三、Subtraction Is a Scalpel — Actively Applied

Here is the most easily misread point that must be clarified—

"Listening to the Dao, removing daily" sounds passive and negative — like "doing nothing, lying flat, giving up."

Not at all.

The posture of "listening to the Dao" is indeed "receptive" — meaning: not forcing outward — not raising "I must get something" as a goal, not grabbing and competing and demanding outward.

But the action of "removing daily" is itself intensely active, intensely effortful.

Why?

Because— what you are removing has grown on you.

Your biases, your positions, your identity, the habits formed over years, the labels you wear with pride — these have grown onto you, grown together with you.

To remove them one by one—

like surgery: cutting away what has grown on your own body, piece by piece.

This demands enormous courage, powerful self-awareness, and a willingness to cut hard against yourself.

This is not lying flat. This is a scalpel — actively applied.

In 2026 terms—

Addition is easy — sign up for a course, buy a book, add a skill, stick it on yourself. Painless.

Subtraction is hard—

Admitting "this identity I take such pride in is actually trapping me"; letting go of "this position I have held for years is actually a bias"; unpeeling "this label I use to define myself is not really me"—

all of this is cutting into yourself. It hurts.

This connects to ch. 33 "Overcoming yourself is true strength; enduring is having purpose"—

Subtraction is overcoming yourself. It is the active work of removing the banners you have raised one by one.

Anyone can add. Subtraction is where real skill shows.

四、Subtract Until Wu Wei

Laozi continues—

"Subtract and subtract, until you reach wu wei."

"Subtract and subtract"—

Not removing once and being done. Subtract, and then subtract again — one after another—

Remove one banner; discover another underneath; remove that one; find another—

layer by layer, going deeper.

"Until you reach wu wei"—

Subtract all the way to wu wei.

Wu wei does not mean "doing nothing" (this is the greatest misreading of Laozi).

Wu wei means: having subtracted until there are no more extra banners, having returned to the most authentic and natural state.

In that state, you no longer act "in order to maintain some identity," "in order to prove some label," "in order to hold up some banner" — you act naturally, following the natural shape of things.

That is wu wei — not not-acting, but acting without carrying those extra banners.

In 2026 terms—

A person who has let go of all identity labels (wu wei) actually lives most freely — no need to worry about "maintaining the image of a successful person," no need to tense up "holding the persona of an expert," no need to exert "proving myself"—

They simply live naturally, act naturally.

That is the state after all subtraction.

五、Act Without Banners, and Nothing Goes Undone

Laozi then offers one of the most famous lines in the Daodejing—

"Through wu wei, nothing is left undone."

This is often misread as "do nothing, yet everything gets done" — a kind of mystical magic.

Not so.

The real meaning: act without those extra banners, and everything can be accomplished.

Why?

Because— when you act while carrying banners, half your attention is on maintaining the banners.

Half your mind is on "will doing this make me look impressive," "will it damage my image," "does this fit my persona" — your banners are dividing your attention, and you do the work poorly.

When you act without banners, all your attention is on the thing itself.

You no longer care whether "doing this looks impressive" — you only care whether "this is the right way to do it" — mind undivided, and things get done.

In 2026 terms—

The person who doesn't care about "looking professional" actually produces the most professional work (their whole mind is on the work, not on performing professionalism).

The person who doesn't care about "winning" often actually wins (focused on doing, not distracted by the anxiety of win-or-lose).

Drop the banners, and nothing is left undone. That is "through wu wei, nothing is left undone."

This connects to ch. 37 "Hold the position of not-meddling, and the ten thousand things transform themselves"— without banners, without forcing, things resolve by their own nature.

六、Want to Gain, and You Lose

At the close, Laozi applies this principle to governing—

"One who wishes to take the world must always be without affairs; the moment they start making affairs, they are no longer enough to take the world."

"Always without affairs" — persistently not raising the banner "I must do something great," not meddling.

"Making affairs" — beginning to meddle, raising the banner "I will take the world," acting everywhere.

Laozi's meaning—

The more you raise the banner "I must take the world" and meddle everywhere to grab it, the less you can take it.

The more you do not raise that banner and do not meddle, the more the world naturally comes to you.

This connects to ch. 29 "One who tries to forcibly take the world — I see they cannot succeed"—

What you grasp slips away; what you do not grasp you end up having.

In 2026 terms—

This goes far beyond governing—

The more you try to grip a relationship (constant anxiety, constant maneuvering), the more easily it slips away; hold it less tightly, and it endures.

The more you try to grip users (every trick, desperate retention), the less you retain them; truly build the product, stop meddling, and users stay.

The more you try to grip reputation (performing everywhere, cultivating every image), the more effortful you appear; work solidly, stop meddling, and reputation comes naturally.

Want to gain, and you lose. Don't force it, and you have it.

七、For the Reader

This chapter offers a counter-current reminder—

In an age that relentlessly adds, try subtracting.

On learning—

Are you always adding to yourself (skills, certificates, labels) and rarely stopping to remove?

Try — remove something.

Let go of a fixation you don't actually need, unpeel an identity that is trapping you, tear down a banner that says "I must be this way."

Subtraction is harder than addition, but after subtracting you will be clearer.

On doing—

When you act, is half your mind on "maintaining image, holding persona, looking impressive"?

Try — drop those banners. Put all attention on the thing itself.

Don't care about looking impressive; care only about whether it is done right — and you will do it better.

On getting—

When you want something, do you grab harder and harder?

Try — open the hand.

The harder you grip, the less you hold; the less you force, the less you meddle — the more you naturally have.

This is not a call to stop learning, stop acting, stop pursuing.

It is a reminder—

Real skill is often not in adding, but in subtracting.

Remove those extra banners one by one and return to the most authentic state — you will find you no longer need so many things to define yourself, prop yourself up, prove yourself.

You simply live naturally, act naturally.

And precisely then — everything gets done.

Return to those two parallel lines—

"For those who pursue learning, something is added each day; for those who listen to the Dao, something is removed each day."

The adder: keeps adding, growing heavier.

The subtractor: keeps removing, growing lighter — until light enough to live freely, act freely.

In this age of relentless addition — Laozi says: try subtracting.

Subtract all the way down, and you will meet that most authentic self — the one that needs no banner at all.
