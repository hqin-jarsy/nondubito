Chapter 31 of 81

打赢了，要哭

When You Win, Weep

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

本章经文本章經文Chinese text for this chapter

夫兵者，不祥之器也，物或恶之，故有欲者弗居。君子居则贵左，用兵则贵右，故兵者非君子之器也。兵者不祥之器也，不得已而用之。铦袭为上，勿美也，若美之，是乐杀人也。夫乐杀人，不可以得志于天下矣。是以吉事上左，丧事上右，是以偏将军居左，上将军居右，言以丧礼居之也。杀人众，以悲哀泣之，战胜，以丧礼处之。

一、先放下孙子

读这一章，不妨先把“怎样打赢”放在一边。

《孙子》说：“上兵伐谋，其次伐交，其次伐兵，其下攻城。”它区分用兵的层次，提醒人不要轻易走到代价最高的一步。第三十一章让我们换一个站位：战报已经送到，胜负已经分出，现在去看看没有回家的人。

这不是在给老子和孙子排年代，也不是说谋略、外交就一定不死人。更不能断言，早期战争只有蛮力，后来的注家都被兵法蒙住了眼睛。这里的对照是两种阅读重心：一边问如何取得胜利，一边问胜利有什么权力遮住死亡。

“损失”“代价”“必要的牺牲”，这些词都可以说得很平稳。换成一张空椅子，一个再也等不到儿子的人，就没那么容易了。农田荒了，还可以再种；死去的人，不会因为地图换了颜色就回来。

所以这一章抓住三件事：兵是不祥的，不要把它当美事；即使打赢，也要让哀悼留在场内。

二、兵是不祥之器

夫兵者，不祥之器也，物或恶之，故有欲者弗居。

“不祥之器”首先改变的是武器在我们眼里的样子。它可以精良，可以使持有者强大，可以带来一场胜利；这些都没有取消它伤人的能力。不能因为“这一次是我们赢了”，便把凶器看成吉物。

本系列把“有欲者”沿着有志于道的方向来读；这是所采用的解释，不把它冒充没有争议的唯一字义。“物或恶之”也不必扩大成一份万物无例外投票反对战争的证明。经文把对兵的厌恶摆出来，让读者先看到承受破坏的一面。

草木被践踏，耕作被中断，人被赶离住所。这些不是胜利之后才补填的附注，而是用兵时已经发生的事。

前几章的“器”可以连起来读：第二十八章讲朴散为器，第二十九章讲天下神器，第三十章限制以兵强天下，这一章则直称兵为不祥之器。这里是主题上的连线，不是四章各有一次“器”字；第三十章经文并没有这个字。

这条线追问的是：有了工具，是否就有权使用？能做到一件事，是否就足以说明应该去做？

三、用兵不是吉事

君子居则贵左，用兵则贵右，故兵者非君子之器也。

这一章借左右位次，让日常的尊位发生转换。依照本章呈现的礼序，吉事尚左，丧事尚右；一到用兵，场景就移到了丧事的一边。

这不是一张适用于所有时代、所有礼仪的左右对照表。也不必把“左”永远等同生命、“右”永远等同死亡。重要的是章内那个转身：同一位有地位的人，拿起兵器时不能继续享用吉庆的光。

“非君子之器”是一道严厉的限制。本页采用的经文紧接着也有“不得已而用之”，因而不能把整章轻易概括成已经解决所有现实困境的绝对禁令。它留下的困难反而更重：即使你认为不得已，也不能因此把用兵变成好事。

“我们没有别的办法”，不是一张免于说明的通行证。谁判断的不得已？是否还有别的办法？谁在承担后果？这些问题不会随着开战而失效。

四、不要把杀戮拖成表演

铦袭为上，勿美也。

本系列所采用的章文写作“铦袭”，传世文本常见“恬淡”。底本怎样释字、怎样处理借字，会影响这里的理解；不能看见两个不同字形，便断定一种必是战术，另一种必是后人把战术改成了心理修养。

前一版把“铦袭”直接解释为快速锋利地结束战争，并引《说文》“臿属”来证明。“臿属”说的是一类铲具，不能单凭它推出“速战速决”的军事原则。这里保留沿着“不要延长杀戮”来读的关切，但不把它立成已经定论的训诂。

更不能从“快”推出“死伤最少”。仓促突击也可能造成更大的伤害。真正需要追问的，是哪些行动正在减少伤害，哪些行动只是在延长某个人的胜利姿态。停火、撤离、救治，都不能为了一个漂亮的结局被搁置。

不论怎样处理这一处异文，紧接着的“勿美也”都在：不要拿杀人的事装点自己。若已走入用兵的困境，更没有资格把它拖成长戏，让别人继续付账。

五、美化战争遮住了谁

勿美也，若美之，是乐杀人也。

这句话的锋利处，在于不让“美”与被美化的事情分家。你赞叹的是阵列、火光、胜利的气势；可这幅图画里的另一些人，正在受伤、失去家人、失去住处。画面越壮丽，越要问：谁从画面里被拿掉了？

但为脱离侵害而松一口气、欢迎亲人平安归来、纪念救人的勇气，不等于享受杀戮。不能用这一章替所有人的庆幸诊断内心。需要警惕的是，把死亡本身当成荣耀，把死者变成好看叙事的材料。

夫乐杀人，不可以得志于天下矣。

我把这句读成对统治欲的一次截断：军事胜利不等于有资格安顿天下。靠恐惧得到的服从，也不等于人愿意与你共同生活。

这不是说残酷的人在现实中一定立刻失败。历史没有如此公正的自动装置。正因为有人能够靠暴力长期得势，这句限制才不能只等输赢来替它作证。

六、主帅站在丧位上

是以吉事上左，丧事上右，是以偏将军居左，上将军居右，言以丧礼居之也。

经文把偏将军放在左边，上将军放在右边，随即解释：用丧礼对待这件事。主帅占据的不是最值得炫耀的位子，而是与死亡相连的位置。

不必把这段直接当成所有古代军队实际遵行同一仪式的记录。它首先是本章用来说明战争应当如何被对待的礼仪图景。具体的礼制历史，还需要别的材料来证明。

这个图景足够沉重：命令从你这里发出，哀悼就不能只留给别人的家属。你不能站在荣耀中央，把死亡交给远处的人独自处理。

如果把战报上的数字重新换成人，主帅的站位就不只是左右问题。他是否还愿意面对自己命令所造成的后果？

七、打赢了，要哭

杀人众，以悲哀泣之，战胜，以丧礼处之。

“泣”把哀伤写到了身体上。不是在胜利演讲的结尾留一句“我们也付出了代价”，然后迅速回到掌声里。经文不让死人只作为一个必要成本被结算。

不过，不能说传世本一概把“泣”改成“莅”：传世王弼注本也有“泣之”。有作“莅”的文本，应具体讨论版本，不能讲成一部整齐的“后人把眼泪抹掉”的历史。传世文本可对读这里。

哭的力量，也不在于检查每个人是否流下了足够多的眼泪。有人失声，有人沉默，有人过了很久才哭得出来。哀悼不是情绪表演的考核，更不能替代救治、安葬、说明和追责。

“以丧礼处之”把胜利放在死者面前。它给出对待战争的尺度，却没有单凭这一句便写好全部丧服样式和仪式流程。穿上丧服的画面可以帮助我们想象，不能冒充经文逐项规定的事实。

无论死者属于哪一方，死亡都没有被“赢了”撤销。这才是那句“打赢了，要哭”不肯让开的地方。

八、识别美化的语言

这一章先讲真实的战争。若急着把它改成“商业竞争获胜后要谦虚”，真实的死人就又被比喻带走了。商业活动也可能伤人，但它与战争不能不加区别地互换。

读者可以留下一个很具体的问题：眼前这段语言，是否允许承受代价的人出现？新闻标题里有他们吗？纪念仪式里有他们吗？电影把胜利拍得酣畅淋漓时，有没有留下一个不愿欢呼的人？

这不是要求你对每一次抵抗都保持冷漠。恰好相反：可以关心谁需要保护，可以庆幸屠杀停止，同时不让这些理由变成继续杀人的兴奋。

最后，不妨想象一场凯旋：旗帜、钟鼓、欢呼，所有人都被要求朝同一个方向看。画面边缘还有一扇没有打开的门。这个场景是我们的想象，不是老子亲眼见过某次凯旋的史实。

第三十一章把目光拉到那扇门前。

战胜，以丧礼处之。

胜利可以改变很多事。唯独不能使那个人重新回来。

I. First, Set Aside Sun Tzu

Before reading this chapter, put the question of how to win to one side.

The Art of War ranks attacking an enemy's plans above attacking alliances, armies, and finally fortified cities. It distinguishes approaches to conflict and warns against costly ones. Chapter 31 asks us to stand somewhere else: the dispatch has arrived, the outcome is known, and someone has not come home.

This contrast is not a claim about which book came first. Nor does strategy or diplomacy guarantee that nobody dies. We need not invent an age of purely brute-force warfare, followed by centuries of commentators unable to see past Sun Tzu. We are changing the question. What does victory have the right to conceal?

“Losses,” “costs,” “necessary sacrifices”: such words can be spoken very calmly. An empty chair is harder to speak past. Abandoned fields can be cultivated again. A son does not return because the map has changed color.

The chapter holds on to three things: weapons are ominous instruments; do not turn their use into something glorious; even in victory, leave room for mourning.

II. An Ominous Instrument

Weapons are ominous instruments. Beings may abhor them; one intent on the Way does not make a home in their use.

That last phrase follows this series' interpretation of the adopted Chinese text, not an uncontested definition of every character. Nor must “beings may abhor them” become a claim that every living thing has cast an identical vote. The passage makes us look first from the side that bears destruction.

A weapon can be superbly made. It can strengthen its owner and help secure a victory. None of that cancels its capacity to injure. “Our side won” does not turn an instrument of death into a lucky charm.

Crops are trampled, cultivation interrupted, people driven from their homes. These are not footnotes to be added after the account of success. They are part of what is happening while the campaign succeeds.

The preceding chapters form a useful thematic sequence: the uncarved becomes particular instruments in Chapter 28; the world is a sacred vessel in 29; military domination is constrained in 30; weapons are named ominous instruments in 31. This is a connection of themes, not four occurrences of the same word: Chapter 30 does not contain the character 器.

Having an instrument does not settle whether we should use it. Being able to do something is not yet a reason to do it.

III. War Does Not Become Auspicious

In ordinary life the noble person honors the left; in military affairs, the right. Weapons are not the noble person's instruments.

Within this chapter's ritual arrangement, auspicious affairs honor the left and mourning honors the right. Taking up arms moves the scene to the side of mourning. A person accustomed to honor cannot carry the atmosphere of celebration unchanged onto the battlefield.

This is not a universal chart of left and right across every period and ceremony. What matters is the movement within the passage: war cannot simply inherit the honors of ordinary life.

The adopted text on this page itself speaks of using weapons only when unavoidable. We should not pretend the chapter has therefore settled every possible dilemma by a simple absolute ban. Its harder demand remains: even what you judge unavoidable does not thereby become a good thing.

“We had no choice” cannot close the conversation. Who made that judgment? What alternatives were considered? Who will bear the consequences? Those questions remain answerable after the fighting begins.

IV. Do Not Turn Killing into a Performance

铦袭为上，勿美也。

The text adopted here has xian xi; received versions commonly have tian dan, associated with calmness. Reading manuscript characters and possible phonetic loans is not as simple as assigning a modern dictionary meaning to each graph. Different written forms do not prove that a tactical instruction was deliberately replaced by advice about inner composure.

An earlier version of this essay read the first expression as a sharp, swift conclusion to war. Its appeal to the Shuowen definition of 铦 as a kind of spade did not establish that military meaning. The concern behind the reading—do not prolong killing—can remain, without being presented as settled philology.

Speed does not itself minimize casualties. A hurried assault may cause greater harm. The practical questions concern which actions actually reduce injury and which merely prolong someone's triumphant posture. Ceasefires, evacuation, and medical care must not wait for an impressive ending.

Whatever reading one adopts for the disputed expression, the next warning remains: do not glorify it. People caught in a war must not keep paying so that someone else can extend the performance.

V. Whom Does the Beautiful Picture Leave Out?

Do not glorify it. To glorify it is to delight in killing.

The force of this sentence is its refusal to separate a beautiful picture from the activity being pictured. You admire the formations, the fire, the sweep of victory. Someone inside that picture is losing a relative, a home, a limb. The more splendid the image, the more urgently we should ask who has been removed from it.

Relief at escaping invasion, joy at a loved one's safe return, and gratitude for courage that saved lives are not identical to enjoyment of killing. This chapter cannot diagnose every survivor's heart. Its warning is against making death itself glorious, turning the dead into material for an attractive story.

One who delights in killing cannot realize their purpose under heaven.

I read this as a limit placed on domination. Military success does not confer the right to order everyone's life. Obedience obtained through fear is not the same as people wishing to live with you.

It is not a prediction that cruel rulers always lose promptly. History has no such reliable mechanism of justice. Precisely because violence can secure lasting power, this limit must not depend on the outcome of a battle to validate it.

VI. The Commander Stands on the Side of Mourning

The passage places the lesser general on the left and the commanding general on the right, then gives its explanation: military affairs are to be treated with funeral rites.

The commander occupies a position associated with death, not simply the most impressive place in a victory procession. Orders issued from that position have consequences for people who will never appear beside the commander.

This is first of all the chapter's ritual picture of how war should be regarded. It does not establish, by itself, that every ancient army actually followed the same ceremonial protocol. That historical question requires other evidence.

The picture is heavy enough without that claim. Mourning cannot be delegated entirely to other people's families. You cannot stand in the middle of glory and leave distant households to deal alone with the deaths your orders helped produce.

Replace the figures in the dispatch with people. Where does the commander stand then?

VII. When You Win, Weep

When many have been killed, mourn them with grief and tears. Treat victory in battle with funeral rites.

The word for weeping brings grief into the body. It does not let a victory speech dispose of the dead with a brief acknowledgment of “the price we paid” before returning to applause.

But it is inaccurate to say that received texts uniformly replace 泣, “weep,” with 莅, “attend to.” A transmitted Wang Bi text also reads 泣. Variants need to be identified individually; they do not support a neat history in which later editors erased all the tears. Compare the transmitted text here.

Nor is weeping an examination in emotional performance. Some people sob, some fall silent, some cannot cry until much later. The point is not to inspect the quantity of tears. Mourning also cannot substitute for care, burial, explanation, or accountability.

Funeral rites place victory before the dead. The sentence gives a measure for our response; it does not supply every item of mourning dress and ceremonial procedure. We may imagine those clothes, but must not pass the image off as a list the text explicitly prescribes.

Whichever side a dead person belonged to, winning has not undone their death. That is what the title refuses to let us move past.

VIII. Recognizing the Language of Glorification

This chapter speaks first about actual war. If we hurry to make it a lesson in humility after a business victory, the real dead disappear into metaphor again. Business can also cause harm; it is still not interchangeable with armed conflict.

Keep one practical question: does the language in front of you allow those bearing the costs to appear? Are they in the headline? In the ceremony? When a film makes victory exhilarating, does it leave a place for someone who cannot cheer?

That question does not require indifference to resistance or rescue. We can care who needs protection, rejoice that a massacre has stopped, and still refuse to turn those reasons into an appetite for further killing.

Imagine a triumphant return: banners, drums, cheering, everyone expected to look in one direction. At the edge of the scene is a door that has not opened. This is our imagined scene, not a documented event Laozi witnessed.

The chapter turns our attention toward that door.

Treat victory with funeral rites.

Victory can change many things. It cannot bring that person back.
