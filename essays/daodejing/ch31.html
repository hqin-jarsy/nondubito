第三十一章，共八十一章
Chapter 31 of 81

打赢了，要哭

When You Win, Weep

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、先放下孙子

读这一章之前，要先放下一个东西——孙子。

孙子说"上兵伐谋，其次伐交，其次伐兵，其下攻城"。这句话把战争层次化了。最高明的战争是谋略战，其次是外交战，动刀兵是次一等，攻城是最下等。"伐谋"不死人，"伐交"也不死人。战争在孙子手里变成了一门可以精巧操作的技艺。

老子比孙子早。老子那个时代的兵，就是硬来。诸侯互相征伐，打就是打。兵直接等于杀人。杀人等于死人。死人等于农田变荒地、家里没了儿子、村里没了男人。一切都是最直接、最物理的破坏。

孙子之后两千年的注家读《道德经》第三十一章时，脑子里的"兵"已经被孙子的层次化软化过了。所以他们把这一章读成"反战的道德训诫"——觉得老子在表达一种反战的态度、一种和平主义的立场。不是。

老子在讲最具体的杀人和死人。这一章老子用的全是大白话。不是因为内容简单，是因为他怕读者听不懂——怕读者把战争软化成谋略和智术。老子要把读者拽回去。拽回"兵就是杀人，杀人就是死人，死人就要哭"这个最朴素的事实。

所以这一章不讲谋略、不讲层次、不讲胜败。只讲三件事：兵是不祥的。不要拿它当美事。打赢了，要哭。

二、兵是不祥之器

第三十一章开头：

"夫兵者，不祥之器也，物或恶之，故有欲者弗居。"

兵这件事，是不祥的器。万物都厌恶它。所以有志于道的人绝不居于这一位。

老子一开口就拎死——兵就是不祥的。不是"用得不好就不祥"，不是"过度就不祥"，不是"非正义的战争才不祥"。兵本身就是不祥的。没有任何条件，没有任何例外。

"物或恶之"——万物都厌恶它。草木被踩，土地被烧，农田变荒，人被杀。所有物都厌恶兵。

"器"这个字在这里是 Paper 4 起头四章的第四次出现——

ch28：圣人用之则为官长——器可以被善用（人的专业度）。
ch29：天下神器——有的器不可强为（天下作为整体）。
ch30：不以兵强于天下——兵作为立强位的工具。
ch31：夫兵者，不祥之器也——兵这种器，本身就是不祥的。

四章一气贯下——器可以被善用，但不是所有器都可以被用。天下作为神器不可强为，兵作为器不可立强位，而且兵这种器本身就是不祥的。

三、用兵自始至终是凶位

接下来老子用古代礼制立据：

"君子居则贵左，用兵则贵右，故兵者非君子之器也。"

君子平时贵左，用兵时贵右。所以兵不是君子用的器。

古代礼制里左右有分工——左是阳位、吉位、生位。右是阴位、凶位、死位。平时以吉事生事为重，所以贵左。用兵则切换到凶事死事的位次，所以贵右。

这个礼制安排拎死了一件事——用兵自始至终是凶位。不是"打输了才凶"，不是"杀错人了才凶"，不是"非正义才凶"。只要用兵，就是凶位。从拿起武器的那一刻起，整件事就在死位上运作。

"故兵者非君子之器也"——所以兵不是君子用的器。

对照本注书名"君子不器"——ch28 立的是"君子不让自己变成单一的器"。ch31 立的是"兵不是君子用的器"。君子有所用，有所不用。用人的专业度，可以（ch28）。用兵这种不祥之器，不可以。

四、打要快，不要拖

接下来一句藏着这一章最关键的字差：

"铦袭为上，勿美也。"

铦袭为上——以快速锋利地结束为上。铦是锋利（《说文》"臿属"——锋利的器具）。袭是突然进攻。铦袭 = 锋利快速地结束。用兵的核心原则——快速结束，不要拖。

通行本这里写的是"恬淡为上"。恬淡 = 淡然处之。一字组之差，两个完全不同的层次——

帛书"铦袭"是兵事的具体战术——打要快。
通行本"恬淡"是心理修养——心要静。

通行本一改，把老子"打要快、不要拖、不要把杀人的事拖成长戏"这一最具体的战术原则，改成了"心要安静"的心理修养。这正是孙子之后的注家把老子层次化的字面证据。

为什么打要快？正因为打赢了也是丧事，所以打要快，不要拖成长戏，不要把杀人的事拉得很长很热闹。每多拖一天，多死的人是真实的人。铦袭为上——这不是嗜杀，恰恰是把死人减到最少的原则。

五、美化战争就是以杀人为乐

"勿美也，若美之，是乐杀人也。"

不要把它当美事。如果把它当美事，就是以杀人为乐。

"美"在这里不是"美丽"——是当作美事来对待、来宣扬、来庆祝。

"若美之，是乐杀人也"——这一句老子没有任何缓冲。任何对战争的美化、庆祝、赞颂，本质上都是以杀人为乐。

不留"我只是赞美胜利，不是赞美杀人"的退路。不留"我歌颂的是英雄主义，不是暴力"的退路。不留"我庆祝的是保家卫国，不是杀戮"的退路。美化战争，就是乐杀人。等号直接画死。

"夫乐杀人，不可以得志于天下矣。"

以杀人为乐的人，不可能在天下立志。

这是整章字面最重的一句。"得志于天下"——在天下立得住自己的志、能在天下成事。老子的断言——以杀人为乐的人，就算打赢了具体的仗，也不可能在天下立志、不可能在天下成事。

这一句直接对接 ch29——ch29 说：想"取天下"必败。ch31 说：以杀人为乐的人也不可能在天下立志。打赢了具体的仗，不等于在天下立住了志。

六、主帅站在丧位上

接下来老子用古代兵礼的具体操作做字面级证明：

"是以吉事上左，丧事上右，是以偏将军居左，上将军居右，言以丧礼居之也。"

吉事尚左，丧事尚右。所以偏将军居左位，上将军居右位。这就是说，用丧礼对待用兵。

这一段藏着一个非常具体的细节——按通常的职位高低排，主帅（上将军）本应居贵位（左位）。但古代兵礼相反——主帅居右位。右位是凶位、丧位。

为什么主帅站在丧位上？因为用兵就是丧事。主帅是丧事的主官，所以居于丧事的主位。

这不是某个学派的解读。是古代兵礼本身就是这样安排的。老子只是把这个安排的含义指出来——兵礼不是吉礼，是丧礼。军队的礼制结构本身，就承认了"用兵是丧事"这个事实。

七、打赢了，要哭

整章的最后两句，是老子最沉痛的两句：

"杀人众，以悲哀泣之，战胜，以丧礼处之。"

杀了很多人，要用悲哀的心情哭泣。打赢了，要按丧礼对待。

"泣"这个字要按字面读。泣，字面就是哭。老子没有用"哀"（心理状态），没有用"悼"（仪式动作），没有用"念"（心念）。用的是"泣"——眼泪流出来的哭。这是字面级的具体要求。

杀人多了，要哭。不是"心里难过"，不是"表情严肃"，不是"沉痛悼念"。是哭。眼泪要流出来。

通行本这里改成了"以悲哀莅之"——用悲哀的心情对待。莅是临、对待——字面级的"哭"被改成了抽象的"对待"。老子要求的是身体动作（眼泪流出来），通行本改成了心理姿态（用悲哀的心情对待）。本注按帛书读。老子要求的是哭。

"战胜，以丧礼处之。"

打赢了，要按丧礼对待。

不是"打赢了要谦虚"，不是"打赢了不要骄傲"，不是"打赢了要反思代价"。老子的字面是——按丧礼对待。用丧礼意味着什么？要穿丧服，不是吉服。要哭，不是庆祝。要按丧仪流程走，不是凯旋仪式。要把它当死人的事对待，不是当胜利的事对待。

老子的字面具体到了礼制操作层——打赢了的具体动作是哭，不是笑。穿丧服，不是穿凯旋服。

为什么？因为人死了就是死了。不管是敌人的人还是自己的人。人死了，就要哭。这就是这一章给打赢的人的最终画面——打赢了，穿上丧服，按丧礼，哭。

八、识别美化的语言

这一章对 2026 年的读者意味着什么？

首先要说清楚一件事——这一章不能被软化成商业隐喻。商业竞争里没有死人。把"打赢了要哭"读成"商业胜利要谦虚"，正是老子在这一章里反对的软化——把杀人的事抽象化、层次化、隐喻化。这一章讲的是真实的兵、真实的杀人、真实的死人。战争在 2026 年仍然真实存在。死人仍然是真实的死人。

这一章给读者的工具，是一把识别的尺——识别美化暴力的语言。

按老子的标准——任何把战争讲成美事的语言，是乐杀人。任何把军事胜利当成庆典的仪式，是乐杀人。任何把杀戮包装成荣耀、伟业、必然代价的叙事，是乐杀人。

老子不留退路——"若美之，是乐杀人也"。读者每天都会遇到这些语言——新闻里、社交媒体上、历史叙事里、电影里、游戏里。读过这一章的人，至少多了一把尺——听到把战争讲成美事的语言时，知道老子在两千四百年前已经把这件事说死了。

美化战争，就是以杀人为乐。以杀人为乐的人，不可以得志于天下。

最后回到这一章的画面。老子那个时代，打赢的君主凯旋回城，旗帜招展，钟鼓齐鸣，万民欢呼。老子看着这一切，写下——

战胜，以丧礼处之。

打赢了，要哭。因为人死了就是死了。人死了，就要哭。

两千四百年过去了。这句话还在等着被听懂。

I. First, Set Aside Sun Tzu

Before reading this chapter, set one thing aside — Sun Tzu.

Sun Tzu said: "The supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting; next is to sever their alliances; next is to attack with armies; and the worst is to besiege walled cities." This statement stratified warfare. The highest form is strategic war, then diplomatic war, then arms, and besieging cities is lowest. Strategy doesn't kill people; diplomacy doesn't kill people either. In Sun Tzu's hands, war becomes a refined art that can be operated with elegance.

Laozi predates Sun Tzu. The warfare of Laozi's time was simply brute force. The lords fought one another; fighting meant fighting. Arms directly equaled killing people. Killing people equaled dead people. Dead people equaled farmland becoming wasteland, sons gone from households, men gone from villages. All of it was the most direct, most physical destruction.

Two thousand years after Sun Tzu, the commentators who read Daodejing Chapter 31 had already had their understanding of "arms" softened by Sun Tzu's stratification. So they read this chapter as "an anti-war moral sermon" — as if Laozi is expressing an anti-war attitude, a pacifist stance. That is not it.

Laozi is talking about the most concrete killing and dying. In this chapter Laozi uses plain language throughout. Not because the content is simple — because he feared readers would miss it. Feared readers would soften warfare into strategy and cleverness. Laozi wants to drag readers back. Back to the plainest fact: arms means killing people, killing people means dead people, dead people means weeping.

So this chapter says nothing about strategy, nothing about levels, nothing about winning and losing. Only three things: arms are inauspicious. Don't treat it as a fine thing. When you win, weep.

II. Arms Are an Inauspicious Vessel

Chapter 31 opens:

"Arms are an inauspicious vessel. All things abhor them. Therefore one who has intent toward the Dao does not dwell in this position."

Laozi's first words nail it shut — arms are inauspicious. Not "inauspicious if used badly." Not "inauspicious if excessive." Not "inauspicious if the cause is unjust." Arms themselves are inauspicious. No conditions. No exceptions.

"All things abhor them" — grasses and trees are trampled, land is burned, farmland turns to waste, people are killed. All things abhor arms.

"Vessel" (器) here is the fourth appearance of this character in the opening four chapters of Paper 4 —

Ch. 28: the sage uses them (vessels) and becomes lord — vessels can be used well (people's professional skills).
Ch. 29: the world as numinous vessel — some vessels cannot be forcibly acted on (the world as a whole).
Ch. 30: not imposing strength through arms — arms as a tool for staking a position of strength.
Ch. 31: arms are an inauspicious vessel — arms as a vessel are inauspicious in themselves.

Four chapters in one breath — vessels can be used well, but not all vessels can be used. The world as numinous vessel cannot be forcibly acted on; arms as a vessel cannot be used to stake a position of strength; and arms as a vessel are themselves inauspicious.

III. Using Arms Is a Position of Ill-Omen from Start to Finish

Laozi next draws on ancient ritual protocol:

"The noble person values left in ordinary life; in military affairs values right. Therefore arms are not a vessel the noble person uses."

Ancient ritual had a division between left and right — left is the yang position, the auspicious position, the position of life. Right is the yin position, the inauspicious position, the position of death. In ordinary life, auspicious and life-affirming affairs take precedence, so left is valued. Military affairs switch to the position of ill-omen and death, so right is valued.

This ritual arrangement nails one thing shut — using arms is a position of ill-omen from start to finish. Not "ill-omen if you lose." Not "ill-omen if you kill the wrong people." Not "ill-omen if the cause is unjust." The moment arms are used, the whole affair operates in the position of death.

"Therefore arms are not a vessel the noble person uses." Cross-referenced with this commentary's title, "The Noble Person Is Not an Instrument" — ch. 28 established "the noble person does not let himself become a single instrument." Ch. 31 establishes "arms are not a vessel the noble person uses." The noble person uses some things; others, not. Using a person's professional skill — yes (ch. 28). Using this inauspicious vessel of arms — no.

IV. Strike Fast; Don't Drag It Out

The next line contains this chapter's most critical textual difference:

"Sharp and swift is best. Do not make it a fine thing."

Sharp and swift is best — "xian xi" (铦袭) = finishing sharply and quickly. "Xian" (铦) is sharp-edged (Shuowen: a cutting implement — a sharp tool). "Xi" (袭) is a sudden strike. Xian xi = finishing sharply and quickly. The core principle of military affairs: finish quickly; don't drag it out.

The received text here reads "tranquil equanimity (恬淡) is best" — meaning to maintain calm composure. One-character difference, completely different levels —

The silk manuscript's "sharp and swift" is a concrete military tactic — strike fast.
The received text's "tranquil equanimity" is psychological cultivation — keep the mind calm.

With one change the received text converts Laozi's most concrete tactical principle — "finish it fast, don't drag out the killing" — into psychological advice about maintaining equanimity. This is the textual evidence of post-Sun Tzu commentators stratifying Laozi.

Why finish fast? Precisely because even winning is a funeral affair — so finish fast, don't drag it into a long spectacle, don't turn killing into an extended performance. Every day it drags on, more real people die. Sharp and swift is best — not out of bloodlust, but the opposite: it is the principle that minimizes the dead.

V. Beautifying War Is Taking Pleasure in Killing

"Do not make it a fine thing. If it is made a fine thing, that is taking pleasure in killing."

"Fine" (美) here is not "beautiful" — it is treating it as a fine thing, glorifying it, celebrating it.

"If it is made a fine thing, that is taking pleasure in killing" — Laozi leaves no buffer here. Any glorification, celebration, or praise of war is, in essence, taking pleasure in killing.

No room for "I'm praising the victory, not the killing." No room for "I celebrate the heroism, not the violence." No room for "I honor the defenders, not the slaughter." Beautify war, and you are taking pleasure in killing. The equation is drawn without qualification.

"One who takes pleasure in killing cannot establish their intent in the world."

This is the heaviest line in the chapter. "Establish intent in the world" — standing in the world with one's will intact, being able to accomplish things in the world. Laozi's assertion: one who takes pleasure in killing, even if they win specific battles, cannot establish their intent in the world, cannot accomplish anything in it.

This connects directly to ch. 29 — ch. 29 says: those who want to "take the world" must fail. Ch. 31 says: those who take pleasure in killing cannot establish their intent in the world either. Winning specific battles does not mean standing in the world with one's will intact.

VI. The Commander-in-Chief Stands in the Funeral Position

Laozi next uses the concrete operations of ancient military ritual as literal-level proof:

"Therefore in auspicious affairs left is honored; in funeral affairs right is honored. The subordinate general is on the left; the supreme general is on the right. This says: military affairs are conducted with funeral rites."

This passage contains a very concrete detail — by ordinary rank, the supreme general should be in the honored position (left). But ancient military ritual was the reverse — the supreme general is on the right. Right is the ill-omen position, the funeral position.

Why does the supreme commander stand in the funeral position? Because military affairs are a funeral affair. The supreme commander is the presiding officer of the funeral, so he occupies the presiding position of the funeral.

This is not one school's interpretation. It is how ancient military ritual was actually arranged. Laozi simply points out what this arrangement means — military ritual is not auspicious ritual; it is funeral ritual. The structure of military ceremony itself acknowledges that "military affairs are a funeral affair."

VII. When You Win, Weep

The last two lines of the chapter are Laozi's most grievous:

"When many are killed, mourn them with grief and tears. When victorious in battle, conduct it with funeral rites."

"Qi" (泣) must be read at face value. Qi literally means to weep. Laozi did not use "ai" (哀, a mental state), "dao" (悼, a ceremonial gesture), or "nian" (念, a mental noting). He used "qi" — weeping with actual tears. This is a concrete face-value requirement.

When many are killed, weep. Not "feel sad inside." Not "wear a solemn expression." Not "solemnly commemorate." Weep. Tears must come.

The received text changes this to "approach it with grief" (以悲哀莅之) — "li" (莅) meaning to attend to, to approach. The literal weeping is changed to an abstract attitude of grief. Laozi required a bodily action (tears flowing); the received text changed it to a mental posture (approaching with grief). This commentary follows the silk manuscript. Laozi required weeping.

"When victorious in battle, conduct it with funeral rites."

Not "be humble after winning." Not "don't be arrogant after winning." Not "reflect on the costs after winning." Laozi's face value is — conduct it with funeral rites. What does funeral rites mean? Wear mourning dress, not festive dress. Weep, don't celebrate. Follow the funeral ceremony, not the victory procession. Treat it as the affair of dead people, not as a victory affair.

Laozi gets concrete down to the level of ritual operations — the concrete action after winning is weeping, not laughing. Mourning dress, not victory dress.

Why? Because dead people are dead. Whether they are the enemy's people or your own people. When people die, weep. This is the final image this chapter gives to whoever wins — when you win, put on mourning dress, follow the funeral rites, and weep.

VIII. Recognizing the Language of Glorification

What does this chapter mean for readers in 2026?

One thing must be said clearly first — this chapter cannot be softened into commercial metaphor. Commercial competition has no dead people. Reading "when you win, weep" as "be humble after a business victory" is precisely the softening Laozi opposes in this chapter — abstracting, stratifying, metaphorizing the affair of killing people. This chapter is about real arms, real killing, real dying. War in 2026 is still real. Dead people are still real dead people.

What this chapter gives the reader is a tool for recognition — recognize the language that glorifies violence.

By Laozi's standard — any language that describes war as a fine thing is taking pleasure in killing. Any ceremony that treats military victory as a celebration is taking pleasure in killing. Any narrative that packages slaughter as glory, as great achievement, as necessary cost, is taking pleasure in killing.

Laozi leaves no room: "if it is made a fine thing, that is taking pleasure in killing." Readers encounter this language every day — in news, on social media, in historical narratives, in film, in games. Having read this chapter, at least you have one measuring tool — when you hear language that describes war as a fine thing, you know that Laozi said it plainly 2,400 years ago.

Beautify war and you take pleasure in killing. Those who take pleasure in killing cannot establish their intent in the world.

Return to this chapter's image. In Laozi's time, a victorious ruler returned to the city in triumph — banners flying, drums and bells ringing, ten thousand people cheering. Laozi, watching all of this, wrote —

When victorious in battle, conduct it with funeral rites.

When you win, weep. Because people are dead. When people die, weep.

2,400 years have passed. These words are still waiting to be heard.
