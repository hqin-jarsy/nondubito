# 你未必有多余的钱，但你一定有多余的"我"

秦汉（字大知）· 2026 年 7 月

一、好东西，总是往已经多的那边流

有一件事,你在哪儿都看得见——

资源,会给到已经做得最好的那个团队;

机会,会落到已经有机会的那个人身上;

谁的盘子已经满了,下一勺还是先盛给谁。

而缺的那一边,不但补不上,常常还要再被抽走一点。

说到这儿,人是很容易动气的。

可老子说这件事的时候,语气是平的。

他没有骂谁。他先讲了一个拉弓的人,然后指出一个机制,最后给了一个方向。

而那个方向,可能出乎你意料。

他没有让你去掰动那个大局,他让你先减掉自己身上那份多余的东西——而且是最不值钱、也最舍不得的那一份。

《道德经》第七十七章,讲的就是这件事。

二、先看一个拉弓的人

老子说——

"天下之道,犹张弓者也。高者抑之,下者举之,有余者损之,不足者补之。"

天下的道,就像一个拉弓的人:高了就压低,低了就抬高;多了就减一些,少了就补一些。

这不是一个抽象的比喻,是一个非常具体的动作。

任何一个真正拉过弓的人都知道:你不是一味用力,你是在不停地调。 这边高了压一压,那边低了抬一抬,力大了收一点,力小了再加一点——手一直在动,始终往"正好"那个点上凑。

"天下之道",在老子这里,就是这么一个不停找平的动作。

而拉弓最难的地方,恰恰不是用力,是找到那个"正好"。

力小了,箭飞不出去;力过了,弓自己先坏。而且这个"正好"不是一次调准就完事的:换一张弓、换一支箭、换一天的风,你都得重新找一遍。

所以"损有余、补不足"不是一条定死的规矩,是一个一直在做的动作。

（这里有一处字差:帛书写的是"天下之道",通行本作"天之道"。帛书先立一个大的"天下之道",下面再分成"天之道"和"人之道"两支;通行本一上来就说"天之道",跟紧接着的那句就重了。帛书的层次更清楚,这一章按帛书读。）

三、天之道：多的减一点，少的添一点

"故:天之道,损有余而益不足。"

所以说:天之道,是把多余的减掉,把不够的添上。

这里要防一个误会:老子不是说"老天在主持公道"。

他在第五章说得很清楚:"天地不仁"——天地没有偏心,也没有情绪。

他说的是一个自然的趋向: 水往低处流;热的东西凉下去,凉的东西暖上来;高山被风雨一点点削平,洼地被泥沙一点点填起。

没有谁在裁决,它自己就往平里去。

但这里要老实说一句:天之道往平里走,既不快,也不温柔。

山不是一天削平的,洼地也不是一夜填起来的。这个"平",是几百年、几千年尺度上的事。 所以老子说这句话,不是在安慰你"你等着,总会平的"——指望它自己来平你这一生的账,是要落空的。

他说这句,是要你看清那个方向,然后自己去学。

（这里还有一处字差:帛书是"益不足"——增益;通行本作"补不足"——填补。"益"跟"损"正好是一对动作:一边减,一边加,都是活的;而"补"更像是把一个缺口堵上。帛书的对照更有来有往。）

四、人之道：少的再减，多的再添

接着,是这一章最有名的一句——

"人之道则不然,损不足而奉有余。"

人之道却不是这样:是把不够的那边再减掉,拿去奉养已经多的那边。

这句话,几乎所有人读起来,都像一句愤怒的控诉。

但老子的语气是平的。他不是在骂人,他是在观察。

他说的是:人这一边,天然就是反着来的。

"天然"这两个字，特别要紧。

为什么人的系统会自己往"多"的那边堆?

因为已经有余的那一方,本来就更容易再拿到:他谈判的时候更有底气,出错了也扛得住,所以更敢下注、更敢等。

而不足的那一方,光是维持住眼下就已经用尽了力气——这正是上一章（ch75）说的"求生之厚"。他不是不想争,是没有余力去争。

所以这件事,不需要有谁存心使坏,它自己就会发生。

放到今天看:一个已经做出成绩的团队,下个季度更容易拿到人和预算;而那个正吃力的团队,反而更可能先被砍。

注意,这里的每一步,单独看都很合理——把资源放到回报最高的地方,本来就是常识;谁也没有存坏心。

可无数个各自合理的决定叠起来,方向就是一边倒的。

这正是老子那句话的意思:它不是谁的恶意,它是这个系统的默认方向。

而老子把它称作"自然",绝不是在替它开脱。恰恰相反——

正因为它这么自然、这么不需要谁使坏就会发生,所以:你要是不专门做点什么,它就一定会发生。

这一层,比骂一顿有用得多。

骂,暗示着有一个坏人:只要找出他、换掉他,事情就能好。 而老子说的是:没有坏人,它照样会这样。

看清了这一点,你才会去想那个真正的问题:那怎么办?

五、谁能反过来做

老子紧接着问了一句——

"孰能有余而有以取奉于天者乎?唯又道者乎?"

谁能在自己有余的位置上,拿出一些来,奉给天呢?大概,只有有道的人吧。

这里有一处重要的字差。

帛书是"奉于天"——奉给天。

通行本改成了"奉天下"——奉给天下。

"奉天下"听起来更宏大:把多余的拿出来,济助天下人。可这一改,上下文那条线就断了。

因为上一句刚说完"天之道,损有余而益不足",紧接着这句问的是:谁能照着天之道去做?

奉于天 —— 是效法:天怎么做,我也怎么做。

奉天下 —— 是施舍:我把多的分给别人。

一个是把自己放进那个运行方式里;一个是站在高处往下发。 老子说的,是前一个。

这个分别,在做的时候特别实在——

施舍的人,自己那个位置是不动的。 他给出去一些,可高的还是高,低的还是低;那个落差,一分没少,有时候反而更清楚了。

而效法的人,是把自己也算进去的。 他减掉的那一份,不是"多出来的善意",是他自己那份有余。 减完之后,他和别人之间的那个差,是真的小了一点。

还要注意老子的语气。

他用的是问句,句末还带着一个"吧"。

他没有说"你们都必须这样"。他是在问:谁能? 而给出的答案也很轻:"大概只有有道的人吧。"

这不是命令,是一个方向。

六、圣人的三次"减"

最后,老子给了最贴身的做法——

"是以圣人为而不恃,功成而不居也。若此,其不欲见贤也。"

所以圣人做了事,不倚仗"我做了";事情成了,不占着那份功;像这样,是不想显出自己的贤能。

"见"在这里通"现",是显现的意思。 "不欲见贤"不是"不想看见贤人",是"不想显摆自己贤"。

这三句话,其实是三次"损有余"——

为而不恃 —— 把"这是我做的"那份多余,减掉;

功成而不居 —— 把"这份功是我的"那份多余,减掉;

不欲见贤 —— 把"我很行"那份多余,减掉。

这就是效法天之道最贴身的一种做法。

你未必有多余的钱、多余的权可以拿出来;可你随时都有多余的"我",可以减掉。

而这三样,恰恰是最难减的——因为它们一分钱都不花,却最舍不得。

难还难在另一处:钱和权,你至少知道自己正在给出什么;而那个多余的"我",你常常根本没意识到自己正攥着它。

它藏在一些很小的动作里:汇报时那句"这个主要是我推的"、别人讲的时候忍不住补一句"其实当初是我提的"、功劳分下去了心里那点不痛快。 没有一件是大事,可它们加起来,就是你身上那份没被减掉的"有余"。

多分一点功劳出去,少说一句"这事亏了我",把那个想被看见的念头按下去——这些都不需要你有余,只需要你肯损。

七、给读者的

这一章,给我们几件当下就能用的事——

第一:先别急着动气,先看清那个机制。

多的更多、少的更少,不需要有坏人就会发生（人之道则不然）。看清这一点,比找一个人来怪,有用得多。

第二:正因为它自然,不做点什么,它就一定发生。

老子说"自然",不是替它开脱,是在提醒你:这件事不会自己变好。

第三:学那个拉弓的人。

高了压一压,低了抬一抬（高者抑之,下者举之）。不是一劳永逸地定一个规矩,是手一直在调。

第四:效法,不是施舍。

不是站在高处把多的分下去,是把自己放进那个"多的减、少的添"的运行方式里（奉于天）。

第五:你随时都有多余的"我"可以减掉。

不倚仗、不居功、不显摆（为而不恃,功成而不居,不欲见贤）。这三样不花钱,却最难舍。

这一章的核心,可以收成一句话——

天往平里走,人往堆里走;这不需要谁使坏,它自己就会这样。所以真要反过来,得有人专门去做——而最先能减的,是你自己那份多余的"我"。

八、承上启下

第七十七章,接着前面几章的"天之道"往下走——（ch73）说天之道"不战而善胜、不言而善应",讲的是它怎么成事;这一章说天之道"损有余而益不足",讲的是它往哪个方向走。

而结尾那三句"为而不恃、功成而不居、不欲见贤",是老子讲了一整本书的老话题:（ch2）就说过"为而弗恃,功成而弗居";（ch10）说过"为而弗恃,长而弗宰";（ch3）说过"不尚贤"。 到这一章,他把这三样,接到了"损有余"上——原来所谓不居功、不显摆,就是在减自己那份多余。

下一章（ch78）,老子会讲他最偏爱的那个意象:"天下莫柔弱于水,而攻坚强者莫之能胜"——天下没有比水更柔弱的,可要说攻坚,没有什么胜得过它。 然后他会说出全书最重的一句承担:"受邦之垢,是谓社稷之主。" 我们下一篇见。

秦汉（字大知）· @hqinjarsy · 大知道德经解系列

完整笺注、帛书 vs 通行本字差的字面考据、与《余项经》层级的对照表，见 Zenodo 存档。本章属 Paper 9（第 73-81 章）：10.5281/zenodo.20145192。前八篇：Paper 1、Paper 2、Paper 3、Paper 4、Paper 5、Paper 6、Paper 7、Paper 8。

I. Good Things Always Flow toward the Side That Already Has More

There is something you can see everywhere —

Resources go to the team that is already performing best.

Opportunities land on the person who already has opportunities.

Whoever's plate is already full gets the next ladle first.

And the side that is lacking — not only does it not get replenished, it often gets drawn on a little more.

It is easy to get angry at this.

But when Laozi speaks of it, his tone is level.

He blames no one. He begins with the image of a person drawing a bow, then identifies a mechanism, and finally points in a direction.

And that direction may surprise you.

He does not ask you to shift the grand scheme of things. He asks you to first reduce the surplus on yourself — the part that costs nothing, and yet is hardest to let go of.

Chapter 77 of the Daodejing is about exactly this.

II. First, Look at Someone Drawing a Bow

Laozi says —

"The Dao of all under Heaven is like a person drawing a bow: what is high, press it down; what is low, lift it up; where there is excess, reduce it; where there is deficiency, supplement it."

The Dao of all under Heaven is like a person drawing a bow: too high, press down; too low, lift up; too much, reduce; too little, add.

This is not an abstract metaphor — it is a very specific action.

Anyone who has actually drawn a bow knows: you are not simply applying force; you are constantly adjusting. This side too high — press it a little; that side too low — lift it; too much force — ease off; too little — add. The hand never stops moving, always converging toward "just right."

The "Dao of all under Heaven," in Laozi's telling, is this kind of continuously-seeking-balance motion.

And the hardest part of drawing a bow is not applying force — it is finding "just right."

Too little, and the arrow won't fly; too much, and the bow itself breaks first. And "just right" is not something you calibrate once and are done: change the bow, change the arrow, change the wind of a different day — and you have to find it again.

So "reduce excess, supplement deficiency" is not a fixed rule — it is an action that is always being performed.

(There is a textual variant here: the Silk Manuscript reads "the Dao of all under Heaven"; the received text has "the Dao of Heaven." The Silk Manuscript first establishes the larger "Dao of all under Heaven," then distinguishes within it "the Dao of Heaven" and "the Dao of humanity"; the received text goes straight to "the Dao of Heaven," which then runs together with the line that follows. The Silk Manuscript's structure is clearer; this chapter follows the Silk Manuscript.)

III. The Dao of Heaven — Reduce the Excess, Add to the Deficient

"Therefore: the Dao of Heaven reduces excess and supplements deficiency."

So: the Dao of Heaven takes from what is too much and gives to what is not enough.

Guard against a misreading here: Laozi is not saying "Heaven administers justice."

He made it plain in Chapter 5: "Heaven and earth are not humane" — Heaven and earth have no preferences and no emotions.

What he is describing is a natural tendency: water flows downhill; what is hot cools, what is cool warms; mountains are worn down by rain and wind bit by bit; valleys are filled with silt bit by bit.

No one is adjudicating — it finds its own level of itself.

But there is an honest thing to say here: the way of Heaven moves toward balance slowly, and not gently.

Mountains are not leveled in a day; valleys are not filled in a night. This "balance" is a matter of hundreds or thousands of years. So when Laozi says this, he is not comforting you with "just wait, it will even out eventually" — counting on it to balance the accounts of your single lifetime will leave you disappointed.

He says this so you can see the direction clearly — and then learn from it yourself.

(Another variant here: the Silk Manuscript writes yì — augment, increase; the received text writes bǔ — fill in, patch. "Yì" and "sǔn" form a perfect pair of actions: reduce on one side, increase on the other — both dynamic. "Bǔ" is more like plugging a hole. The Silk Manuscript's pairing is more alive.)

IV. The Dao of Humanity — Reduce the Deficient, Add to the Excessive

What follows is the most famous line in this chapter —

"The Dao of humanity is otherwise: it reduces from what is already deficient and offers the excess to what already has too much."

The Dao of humanity is the opposite: it takes from the side that already lacks and gives it to the side that already has too much.

Almost everyone who reads this hears it as an angry denunciation.

But Laozi's tone is level. He is not condemning anyone — he is observing.

What he is saying is: the human side naturally runs in reverse.

The word "naturally" matters enormously here.

Why does the human system pile up on the side that already has more?

Because the party that already has surplus can more easily get more: they negotiate from a stronger position, can absorb mistakes, and so dare to bet more and wait longer.

And the party that lacks — just maintaining where they are has already used up everything — this is exactly (ch. 75)'s "the cost of staying alive is too great." It is not that they don't want to compete — they have no capacity left to compete.

So this does not require anyone to intend harm — it happens of itself.

Seen from today: the team that is already producing results will find it easier to get people and budget next quarter; the team that is struggling is more likely to get cut first.

Note: each of these steps, taken alone, looks completely reasonable — putting resources where returns are highest is common sense; no one has bad intentions.

But countless individually reasonable decisions pile up in one direction.

This is exactly what Laozi means: it is not anyone's malice — it is the default direction of this system.

And Laozi calling it "natural" is absolutely not excusing it. On the contrary —

Precisely because it is so natural, so able to happen without anyone intending harm: if you don't specifically do something about it, it will certainly happen.

This is far more useful than condemnation.

Condemnation implies there is a bad person: find them, replace them, and things will be better. But Laozi says: there is no bad person — and it will happen anyway.

Seeing this clearly is what finally prompts you to ask the real question: so what do you do?

V. Who Can Do the Opposite

Laozi immediately asks —

"Who can take from their own surplus and offer it to Heaven? Perhaps only the one who has the Dao."

Who, from a position of surplus, can take something from themselves and offer it to Heaven? Perhaps only the person who holds the Dao.

There is an important textual variant here.

The Silk Manuscript reads "offer it to Heaven" (fèng yú tiān).

The received text changes this to "offer it to all under Heaven" (fèng tiānxià).

"Offer it to all under Heaven" sounds more grand: taking from your surplus to help people everywhere. But this change breaks the thread of the passage.

Because the preceding line just spoke of "the Dao of Heaven, reducing excess and supplementing deficiency" — and this line immediately asks: who can act in accordance with the Dao of Heaven?

Offer to Heaven — is emulation: Heaven does it this way, and I do it this way too.

Offer to all under Heaven — is charity: I take what I have extra and give it to others.

One puts yourself inside that way of operating; the other has you standing above and dispensing downward. Laozi means the first.

This distinction is very real in practice —

The charitable person's position does not move. They give some away, but the high stays high and the low stays low; the gap between them is unchanged — sometimes even clearer than before.

The person who emulates puts themselves into the equation. What they reduce is not "surplus goodwill" — it is their own actual surplus. After the reduction, the gap between them and others has genuinely narrowed a little.

Notice Laozi's tone here.

He poses a question, and the sentence ends with "perhaps."

He does not say "everyone must do this." He asks: who can? And the answer is offered lightly: "perhaps only the person who holds the Dao."

This is not a command — it is a direction.

VI. The Sage's Three Reductions

Finally, Laozi offers the most personal practice —

"Therefore the sage acts without relying on what they have done; accomplishes things without taking up residence in the achievement; they are thus not seeking to display their excellence."

The sage acts — but does not lean on "I did this"; achieves — but does not occupy the achievement; in this way, they are not seeking to show their excellence.

The character jiàn here carries the meaning of "display, show forth." "Not seeking to display their excellence" does not mean "not wanting to see excellent people" — it means "not wanting to show off one's own excellence."

These three lines are actually three instances of "reducing excess" —

Act without relying on what you did — reduce the surplus of "this is what I did";

Accomplish without taking up residence in the achievement — reduce the surplus of "this achievement is mine";

Not seeking to display excellence — reduce the surplus of "I am capable."

This is the most personal way of emulating the Dao of Heaven.

You may not have surplus money or surplus power to give out; but you always have surplus "self" that you can reduce.

And these three things are precisely the hardest to reduce — because they cost nothing, and yet are the hardest to let go of.

There is another difficulty: with money and power, you at least know what you are giving away; but that surplus "self," you often don't even notice you are clutching it.

It hides in small actions: that line in the report — "this was mainly pushed by me"; when someone else is speaking, the irresistible urge to add "actually, I originally proposed that"; the slight discomfort when the credit is distributed to others. None of these is a large thing — but together they are the undiminished surplus on you.

Share a little more credit; say one less "this owes to me"; press down the urge to be seen — none of this requires you to have surplus; it only requires your willingness to reduce.

VII. To the Reader

This chapter gives us several things useful right now —

First: don't rush to get angry — first see the mechanism clearly.

The more getting more, the less getting less — no one needs to intend it for it to happen. Seeing this clearly is far more useful than finding someone to blame.

Second: precisely because it is natural, it will certainly happen if you don't specifically do something about it.

Laozi calling it "natural" is not excusing it — it is reminding you: this will not fix itself.

Third: learn from the person drawing the bow.

Too high, press down a bit; too low, lift a bit. Not a rule set once and done — a hand that is always adjusting.

Fourth: emulate, don't just donate.

Not standing above and distributing downward — putting yourself inside the operating principle of "reduce the excessive, supplement the deficient."

Fifth: you always have surplus "self" that you can reduce.

Not relying on what you did, not occupying the achievement, not displaying your excellence. These three cost nothing — and yet are hardest to release.

The core of this chapter can be gathered into one line —

Heaven moves toward balance; humanity moves toward accumulation. No one needs to intend it — it will happen of itself. So to reverse it, someone has to specifically act — and the first thing that can be reduced is your own surplus "self."

VIII. Connecting Forward and Back

Chapter 77 continues the thread of "the Dao of Heaven" from the preceding chapters — (ch. 73) said the Dao of Heaven "does not fight yet excels at winning, does not speak yet excels at responding" — speaking of how it accomplishes things; this chapter says the Dao of Heaven "reduces excess and supplements deficiency" — speaking of the direction it moves.

And the final three lines — "act without relying on what you did; accomplish without occupying the achievement; not seeking to display excellence" — revisit Laozi's lifelong theme: (ch. 2) already said "act without relying on it; accomplish without taking up residence"; (ch. 10) said "act without relying, lead without dominating"; (ch. 3) said "do not prize the excellent." In this chapter, he connects all three to "reducing excess" — it turns out that not occupying credit, not showing off, is simply reducing your own surplus.

The next chapter (ch. 78) will speak of Laozi's most beloved image: "Nothing under Heaven is softer than water, yet nothing surpasses it in attacking the hard and strong." Then he will say the heaviest line of commitment in the whole book: "To bear the filth of a place — this is to be its true ruler." We will see it next.
