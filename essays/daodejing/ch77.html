Chapter 77 of 81

多出来的那一份，能不能松一松

Moving Something from Surplus to Need

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

本章经文本章經文Chinese text for this chapter

帛书整理文本 · 本注采用帛書整理文本 · 本注採用Silk-manuscript text · the author’s edited Chinese reading

天下之道，犹张弓者也。高者抑之，下者举之，有余者损之，不足者补之。故：天之道，损有余而益不足。人之道则不然，损不足而奉有余。孰能有余而有以取奉于天者乎？唯又道者乎？是以圣人为而不恃，功成而不居也。若此，其不欲见贤也。

一、为什么下一勺，还是先盛给他

一个团队做出了成绩，下一次分配预算时，很自然地被优先考虑。另一个团队正在吃力，成绩暂时不够好，于是更难说明自己为什么需要人。少了人，下一次成绩又更难做好。

把每一个决定单独拿出来，都能找到理由。把资源交给已经证明自己的人，似乎最稳妥。可许多稳妥的决定叠在一起，也可能让已经缺少的一方，越来越难获得证明自己的机会。

这不是说成功不值得支持，也不是说所有差距都来自恶意。只是，有些不平衡并不需要每一步都有人存心使坏，就能够继续扩大。看见这一点，才不会以为换掉一个坏人，一切便自然恢复均衡。

第七十七章从一张弓说起，随后把“有余”和“不足”放到了人间。读到最后，我们可以问自己愿意松开什么；但在问之前，别急着把资源分配的问题缩成一个人心胸够不够宽的问题。

二、弓不是越用力，越好

天下之道，犹张弓者也。高者抑之，下者举之，有余者损之，不足者补之。

这是一幅调整的图像。高了，向下调一点；低了，向上举一点。注意力不只放在增加力量，也放在力量怎样相互配合。

不需要先成为射箭的人，才能看见这种分别。一味绷紧，和使一件东西恰当地发挥作用，不是同一个动作。后者要观察哪里过了、哪里还不够，而不是把“更多”当成所有问题的答案。

本站采用开头的“天下之道”，通行本常作“天之道”。本系列可以沿着采用文字，先看这个总的图像，再读下文天道与人道的对照；但不同开头各有读法，重复也不必自动算成后人损坏了层次。

放到现实安排里，调整更不是把每一处都削成一样。不同的人、阶段和任务，所需要的支持未必相同。所谓不足，要回到实际需要；所谓有余，也不能只看账面多出一个数字。

比喻在这里帮我们的，是改变提问方式：先看看哪一处正在承受不相称的拉力，而不是只问整张弓还能不能绷得更紧。

三、从自然取一个意象，不是等自然替人主持公道

“天之道，损有余而益不足。”多的一边减一些，少的一边添一些。这个方向可以借自然界的某些过程来想象：水向低处流，温差促成热的交换，流水把泥沙带往别处。

但这些都各有条件。自然并非只会平均分配，它也会聚集、冲毁和加大差异。一处洼地得到泥沙，并不能证明人间的贫困最后也会由某种规律自动补足。

所以，这句话值得作为一种规范性的方向来思考，而不该当成宇宙已经替我们保证的结果。若一个人正在挨饿，让他等待“天道迟早找平”，没有给他添一口粮食。

“益”和通行本常见的“补”，都可以指向增加不足；不能说一个字天然有活力，另一个字就只剩僵硬的堵塞。更重要的是，谁的不足被看见了，采取的办法有没有真正改变他的处境。

我们愿意照顾缺少的一方，不必因为世界先替这个愿望签了一份保证书。可以只是因为，人应当能够好好生活，而有些人眼下还没有那个条件。

四、那些看起来各自合理的决定

人之道则不然，损不足而奉有余。

这一对照尖锐，不必替它断定唯一的语气。它可以含有批评，也可以帮助我们观察一种机制：已经拥有资源的人，往往更容易继续获得资源。

他有时间等待，能够承担一次失败，开口时也更容易被认真听见。缺少的人却可能连等待的费用都付不起。于是，表面上相同的机会，在两边并不具有相同的可用性。

前面那个团队的例子便是如此。每次都按过去的表现分配，可能奖励了真实的努力，也可能让曾经缺少的条件永远没有机会被补上。不能只用一句“谁有本事谁拿”，把这两种情况合在一起。

不过，人间也不是天生只能如此。合作、公共支持、重新分配和对规则的修订，都可能改变积累的方向。称它为一种倾向，不是把它封成不可更改的人性。

恶意也仍然可能存在。看见制度的作用，并不免除具体的掠夺、欺骗或滥用权力。只是，除了问谁做了坏事，还要问：在没有人公开作恶的时候，这套安排是否仍让最缺少的人不断付出更多？

因此，愤怒不必先被压下去，行动才算清醒。它可以提醒我们哪里不公。接下来，需要把那份不满带向能够改变条件的事情，而不只停在个人性格的判断上。

五、给出去，不一定是从高处往下给

“孰能有余而有以取奉于天者乎？唯又道者乎？”本系列沿着“奉于天”，把它理解为效法前面的方向：自己也参与损有余、益不足，而不是只赞美它。

通行本的“奉天下”，则很容易使人想到把有余用于天下。两种文字不必被硬分成一种高尚的效法、一种居高临下的施舍。真实的馈赠、救助和公共投入，都可能使别人的处境确实好起来。

区别不只在说法，而在做法。给出一笔资源，却同时要求对方永远感恩、不能反对自己，帮助便附带了另一种占据。给出资源，也尊重对方对用途和生活的判断，关系就可能不同。

这仍然不只是个人品格的问题。一份稳定、公平、不用反复求人才能得到的支持，往往比偶尔出现的慷慨更可靠。规则能够做的事，不应全交给某个人当天愿不愿意做好人。

而当自己确实有余时，也可以具体地问一问：这一份留在我这里，和去到眼下更需要它的地方，各会发生什么？答案不必总一样，但这个问题值得真的进入决定，而不只进入赞美别人的文章。

六、做过的事，不必都变成凭据

是以圣人为而不恃，功成而不居也。若此，其不欲见贤也。

结尾把“有余”带回人的动作：做了，不倚恃；成了，不占住；也不急着展示自己的贤能。“见”在这里可以按显现来理解。

本篇愿意把它读作三种松手：不拿过去的帮助换取今日服从，不把共同的成果永远锁在自己的名下，也不要求每一项善意都当场证明自己是怎样的好人。

可这绝不意味着，你只要希望工作被看见，就有一份需要削掉的“我”。有的人恰恰长期没有得到公平署名、报酬或解释机会。让他们再谦让一点，不能叫作补不足。

值得放下的，是超过应得范围的独占，不是对真实贡献的说明。一次汇报可以准确说出自己做了什么，也准确说出别人做了什么；接受合理的感谢，不必顺手建立一张以后随时可以追索的人情账。

有时，最难松开的确实不是钱，而是那句“要不是我”。它让一次曾经的支持，变成一段关系长期的上位理由。松开它，不是否认自己做过，而是不再要求别人一直活在这件事的利息里。

一个人可以保有自己的名字、价值和判断，同时不把所有成果据为己有。这里要减少的，不是人的存在，而是存在旁边那一圈额外的占地。

七、从你实际能够改变的地方开始

如果你分配资源，不妨在“谁过去表现最好”之外，再问一个问题：谁的表现受到了本来可以改变的条件限制？这不要求放弃评估，而是让评估看见自己尚未计入的东西。

如果你在共同工作里总能获得发言的位置，可以给那个总被打断的人留一次完整的说明。不是代他说话，而是让别人有机会直接听见他。

如果你已经很缺少，不必先搜查自己还有哪些尊严可以交出去。争取应得的支持，和拒绝把一切据为己有，并不冲突。损有余，不该成为向不足者继续索取的漂亮说辞。

做过帮助别人的事，也允许自己知道它有意义。但在下一次意见不合时，试着别立刻把那件事搬出来。一段关系若还想继续生长，不能每次都回到“你欠过我”才站得住。

更大的不公需要共同的行动，较小的独占也有今天可以停止的一次。两者不互相代替，可以一起被认真对待。

八、把有余与不足，放回同一幅图里

第七十三章借天道谈行动的分寸，这里借天道谈一个方向。它们都不应该变成等待世界自动奖善罚恶的理由。

结尾与第二章、第十章的不恃、不居相接，也使第三章对贤名与争夺的警惕更贴近生活：我们可以做好一件事，而不不断扩大它为自己带来的支配权。

下一章会再回到水，以及“受邦之垢”的承担。资源较多、位置较高的人，如果愿意松开一点好处，也需要在难处到来时，看看自己是否同样愿意留下。

有余和不足，不能只在抽象处各自存在。把它们放回同一张桌边，我们才看得见：多出来的那一份，是谁给的，又能让谁的生活宽一点。

I. The team that always gets more

At the budget meeting, the most successful team receives new resources. It has clear results, someone who presents them well, and projects that look comparatively safe. A smaller, overburdened team asks for help with the difficulties that prevent it from producing similar results. Its request can wait.

Considered separately, each allocation may look reasonable. Repeated over years, the decisions create a difference that then justifies repeating them. Those who already have capacity receive the means to increase it. Those struggling to sustain their work must demonstrate, without support, that they deserve support.

Chapter 77 recognizes an uncomfortable direction: taking from insufficiency to serve abundance. It does not merely tell us to stop being envious. It asks about actual distributions and the practices that keep them in place.

Later it brings the question home to credit and self-display. But we should not begin at the end. Someone lacking food, time, or rights is not helped by being told to reduce their ego. Material shortages need material responses. A generous attitude cannot substitute for them.

II. Adjusting the bow

“天下之道，犹张弓者也”: the way is compared to drawing or adjusting a bow. What is high is lowered, what is low raised; surplus is reduced and deficiency supplied.

We do not need to turn this into a precision archery manual. The force of the image lies in the relationship between parts. The aim is not to raise everything indefinitely or add tension at every point. It is to attend to an arrangement in which distribution matters.

Imagine hands that do not ask which part of the bow deserves more honor. They attend to excess and shortage. Adjustment does not punish the high simply for being high, or declare the low morally superior simply for being low. It seeks a relation that allows the whole to work.

The adopted opening “天下之道” and the later “天之道” need not generate a story in which subsequent editors deliberately destroyed an originally perfect coherence. Texts differ. We can think with those differences without attributing intentions we cannot establish.

The image leaves a practical question: when we have room to move something, do we automatically direct it toward whoever is already best placed, or also ask where it could relieve a lack? That question becomes more demanding when the existing allocation benefits us.

III. Nature as an image, not a promise

“天之道，损有余而益不足”: Heaven’s way reduces surplus and increases what is insufficient. Familiar images accompany that direction. Water can flow from higher ground to lower; heat can pass from a warmer body to a cooler one; erosion alters a landscape.

These are bounded comparisons, dependent on conditions. Nature also concentrates resources, produces disparities, and destroys. None of the examples guarantees that time will distribute everything justly among people. A valley does not receive justice when it receives water; it may receive a flood.

Heaven’s way functions here as a philosophical and normative orientation. It allows us to imagine a direction contrary to continual accumulation in the same place. It does not need to become a physical law of universal redistribution.

Nor need we oppose “益,” increasing or benefiting, to “补,” supplying a deficiency, as though one necessarily contained life and the other only mechanical repair. Relieving an urgent lack and enlarging a capacity can both matter in practice.

Waiting for the universe to balance everything would be a strange response to a chapter asking what we can offer. A horizon of thousands of years does not restore today’s missing meal, insecure home, or lost opportunity. The image becomes valuable when it orients action, not when it postpones responsibility indefinitely.

IV. Accumulation has mechanisms

“人之道则不然，损不足而奉有余”: the human way described here runs in the opposite direction. There is political and economic criticism in that sentence. We should not soften it into a neutral observation about individual psychology.

Advantages can compound. Money opens access to opportunities; existing prestige attracts further attention; a large department can assign someone to seek resources that a smaller department lacks time to request. Not every participant in such a mechanism needs to wake up intending to injure someone.

But the absence of malicious intention in some cases does not erase deliberate exploitation in others. Neither does the tendency establish an unchangeable human nature. Access rules, representation for those receiving less, and collective decisions can alter the direction in which resources move.

Return to the budget meeting. Evaluating only current results may mistake a capacity created by previous support for merit independent of that support. Another evaluation would ask what need is being met, what barrier prevents progress, and what investment could unlock a contribution currently blocked.

There will not always be an easy answer, or resources for every claim. The criticism remains useful because it requires an explanation of why past advantage should automatically receive priority again. An arrangement does not become inevitable merely because each person inside it can describe a locally reasonable decision.

V. Giving without buying a superior position

“孰能有余而有以取奉于天者乎？唯又道者乎？” Who can offer from a surplus in this direction? The adopted “又” is read here in relation to “有.” The received wording commonly speaks of offering to “天下,” the world or all under Heaven.

Reading “奉于天” as following Heaven’s pattern is commentary: directing what is available toward what is lacking. It does not prove that the alternative “奉天下” necessarily describes condescending charity. Money, time, and resources can genuinely help. Not every gift is an attempt to place its recipient below the giver.

The practical difference appears in the conditions. Does assistance allow another person greater freedom, or demand permanent gratitude, obedience, and publicity for the donor? Does it answer an expressed need, or turn the recipient into a stage on which someone performs generosity?

These questions accompany material transfer; they do not replace it. Refusing to boast about a donation feeds nobody if the donation never arrives. Likewise, personal goodwill does not replace fair rules of distribution. We may need both: resources that actually move, and relationships that do not make submission their price.

Someone with little is not obliged to invent a surplus. The invitation concerns what we can really offer, not a sacrifice whose size is set by someone who will never have to bear it.

VI. Three ways of not keeping everything

The closing lines gather three movements: “为而不恃,” acting without turning what you have done into a claim over others; “功成而不居,” not settling into achievement as its exclusive owner; and “不欲见贤,” not seeking the display of one’s own excellence.

A meeting makes the distinction visible. Someone explains a solution, and we feel the urge to add, “Actually, that was my idea.” Sometimes the correction is necessary. A real contribution may be disappearing from the record, with consequences for recognition and pay. At other times our contribution is already acknowledged, and what we cannot tolerate is attention resting briefly on someone else.

The chapter invites us to distinguish those situations. Sharing credit does not require falsifying authorship, surrendering fair compensation, or allowing someone to appropriate another person’s work. It questions monopoly: converting a contribution into a permanent entitlement to the center.

A teacher can likewise share knowledge without requiring every student to remain evidence of the teacher’s greatness. Teaching may enlarge understanding on both sides, though it consumes real time and energy. Attention is not an infinite reserve, and rest does not make generosity mean-spirited.

Act, let the result serve, and allow others to become visible. Those movements loosen a form of accumulation that can accompany material advantage. Not everything passing through our hands needs to carry our name.

VII. A redistribution we can point to

Begin with something observable. If you influence a budget, calendar, or division of tasks, identify a lack that the current arrangement keeps reproducing. Feeling fair is not enough. Look at who receives time, tools, rest, and opportunity.

Perhaps an invisible task can be removed from the person who always absorbs it. Perhaps work previously treated as a favor should be paid. Perhaps a discussion needs to include those who will implement the decision, not only those practiced at presenting it.

In matters of recognition, describe contributions accurately. Do not erase your own, inflate it, or annex someone else’s. Precision is more useful than theatrical humility that still demands attention for the manner of its withdrawal.

And when you have no surplus, acknowledge that without shame. Joining collective action, requesting a fair rule, or accepting support are legitimate ways to participate in the relation. A person who needs to receive is not worth less than a person presently able to give.

The bow asks for an adjustment, not a virtuous pose. At the end, it should be possible to identify a burden relieved, a capacity supported, or a right that no longer depends on someone’s favor. A change in attitude matters most when it can make contact with those things.

VIII. From distribution to responsibility

Chapter 73 cautioned us against imagining Heaven as an automatic ledger. Chapter 77 does not contradict that caution. It proposes a direction for action, not a promise that the world will do the work for us.

Chapters 2 and 10 already separated creating from possessing; chapter 3 questioned displays that organize life around competition for rank. Here those concerns meet resources. Inner disposition matters, but it should be able to touch an actual distribution.

Chapter 78 adds another movement: accepting the unwelcome part of a responsibility instead of repeatedly sending its cost downward. Not to glorify a leader’s suffering, but to ask where the burden nobody wants ultimately lands.

We may not be able to correct the whole distribution at once. That does not make a concrete decision insignificant, or justify reducing inequality to a shortage of modesty. We can move resources, alter rules, and share recognition. A beginning is to stop calling a distribution balanced merely because a sufficient portion has always reached our own plate.
