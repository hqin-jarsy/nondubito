Chapter 36 of 81

这一章不是教你权谋

This Chapter Is Not Teaching You Statecraft

秦汉（字大知）· Han Qin

一、被读成厚黑学的一章

《道德经》第三十六章，是历来被读得最歪的一章。

"将欲歙之，必固张之。将欲弱之，必固强之。将欲去之，必固兴之。将欲夺之，必固与之。"

想让它收，先让它张。想让它弱，先让它强。想让它消失，先让它兴起。想让它失去，先给它。

这四句被无数人读成权谋术——"想搞垮一个对手？先捧他，让他膨胀，等他飘到极致，再一举收割。"欲擒故纵。捧杀。放长线钓大鱼。

这是厚黑学版本的老子——把这四句读成阴谋家的操作手册。

读反了。

老子在这一章立的，恰恰是这种权谋术行不通。这一章不是教你怎么设局害人。是告诉你一个结构规律——任何东西走到极致，必然往反方向走——然后告诉你看见这个规律之后该怎么做。而该怎么做的答案，正好是权谋术的反面。

二、四对讲的是结构动力

先把四对的字面读对。

歙是收敛、闭合（《说文》"歙，缩鼻也"——收缩气息）。张是展开、扩张（拉开弓弦）。想让它收敛，必先让它张开。

为什么？因为弓拉到底就要松。张到极致，必然要收回来。这不是谁去操作的，是结构本身的动力。

四对讲的是同一件事——张开到极致，必然收回（弓拉到底就松）。强到极致，必然变弱（接 ch30"物壮则老"）。兴起到极致，必然被消除。给到极致（让它过分占有），必然失去（占太多最后什么都拿不住）。

这是结构动力的反向运动——任何东西走到极致，必然往反方向运动。

这接 ch29 立的那四对（物或行或随、或嘘或吹、或强或羸、或培或隳）——ch29 立"物在每一个维度上都两面运动"，ch36 的四对反向操作是同一字脉——强极必弱、张极必歙、兴极必去、与极必夺。这是物的本来运动。

三、为什么权谋读法行不通

现在回到那个权谋读法——

"我先捧杀对手，让他膨胀到极致，等他强极必衰的时候收割他。"

这个读法看起来很聪明——利用"强极必衰"的规律去害人。老子的字脉证明它行不通。证明在 ch29——为者败之，执者失之。

如果一个人主动去"固张之、固强之、固兴之、固与之"，目的是摧毁对手——这个行为本身就是"强行立构（为之）"。按 ch29"为者败之"——只要动手去设局、去为、去操控，自己就已经跌入"为者败之"的位。

更深一层——给别人设"强极必衰"的局时，自己的控制欲、主宰欲已经达到了极致（甚）。按 ch29"圣人去甚"——自己已经在"甚"的位上了。给对方设的局还没到位，自己已经先被结构反噬。

这就是为什么权谋读法在老子的字脉里行不通——你想用"强极必衰"去害别人。但你设局这个动作本身，就是你自己的"控制欲走到极致"。你自己先到极致，你自己先崩塌。对手还没被你搞垮，你已经先垮了。

所以"将欲歙之，必固张之"在老子这里绝不是主动的战术。它是被动的看见——看着对面那个疯狂立构、疯狂膨胀的对手，看见他必然因为自己的膨胀而崩塌。然后自己不动手——守在柔弱位，不到极致，把时间交给结构。结构动力会自己结算对手的崩塌。不需要你动手。

四、是谓微明

老子给这个规律一个名字——

"是谓微明。"

这就叫微明。

微是细微、不显眼。明是从内部照亮、看清楚（接 ch33"自知者明也"——明是从内部看清结构）。微明 = 细微但显明的看见——这个规律细微（不在表面、不显眼），但对看得见的人来说，是显明的。

微明不是智术，不是聪明，不是算计。是对结构动力的看见。看见任何东西走到极致都会反弹。看见疯狂膨胀的必然崩塌。看见这个之后，自己守在不到极致的位上。

五、柔弱胜刚强

接下来是这一章的总结，也是"柔"这个字在《道德经》里第一次系统出现——

"柔弱胜刚强。"

柔弱胜过刚强。

柔（《说文》"柔，木曲直也"——树木可弯曲）——可弯曲、可适应。刚——坚硬、不弯。柔弱 = 可弯曲、可适应；刚强 = 不弯曲、不适应。

为什么柔弱胜刚强？因为结构动力的必然——强到极致就开始衰（接 ch30"物壮则老"）。刚强的东西已经在极致的位上，接下来就是衰退。柔弱的东西不在极致，保持可弯曲、可适应，反而能持久。保持柔弱（不到极致）反而能久（接 ch33"不失其所者久也"）。

这里要把"柔弱胜刚强"和权谋读法彻底分开——不是用诡计诱导别人变刚强，然后等他崩。是自己守在柔弱位（不到极致），让对面自己走到极致然后崩塌。

区别在哪？诱导别人变刚强——你在动手设局，你的控制欲到了极致，你自己先崩。自己守柔弱——你不动手，你看着对面自己膨胀、自己崩塌，你始终在不崩的位上。

主动诱导别人刚强的人，自己先到极致，自己先崩塌。守在柔弱位的人，让结构自己结算，自己始终安全。这才是柔弱胜刚强。

放到 2026 年看——一个市场里有一个疯狂烧钱、疯狂扩张、疯狂 PR 的对手——

权谋做法：想办法刺激他烧得更猛、扩得更快，等他资金链断裂时收割。——但这个做法本身要你投入大量精力去设局、去操控，你的"必须搞垮他"的执念已经到了极致。你在设局的过程中，自己也烧钱、也扩张、也焦虑。你自己先到极致。

柔弱做法：守住自己的节奏（不到极致），做扎实的产品，看着对手因为自己的过度扩张而崩塌。——你不需要做任何针对他的事。结构动力（强极必衰）会自己结算他。你守在柔弱位（可持续、不极致），他在刚强位（不可持续、已极致）。

柔弱胜刚强。不是你赢了他，是他自己崩了，而你还在。

六、鱼不脱于渊

接下来老子用一个最朴素的意象——

"鱼不脱于渊。"

鱼不能离开深渊（深水）。鱼离开水就活不了。有的东西离不开它赖以存活的位。

给治者（以及任何人）的字面——守在自己的位上，不要脱离自己赖以存活的位。接 ch33"不失其所者久也"——守自己的位 = 持续在场。

那个"位"是什么？对治者来说，是道（接 ch32"侯王若能守之，万物将自宾"——守朴 = 守道）。治者守道（守渊），天下自然归来。治者脱离道（脱渊），失去一切。就像鱼离不开水。

放到今天——你赖以存活的"渊"是什么？是你真正的价值、真正的能力、真正的位。不要为了追逐表面的东西（更高的位、更大的名、更显眼的招牌）而脱离自己真正的渊。脱了渊的鱼，活不了。

七、邦利器不可以示人

最后一句——

"邦利器不可以示人。"

国家的利器，不可以拿出来给人看。

这一句也最容易被读成权谋——"藏好你的底牌，不要让人看见你的实力。"又读歪了。

老子的"邦利器不可以示人"不是藏权术，是不立招牌。为什么不能示人？因为一显示，就立了"我有权力"的招牌。立招牌违背 ch34"以其不为大也，故能成大"——不立自己为大，反而成大。

治者一旦把利器示人，就走到了"自为大"的位置上——立招牌 → 停止运作 → 失去利器（接 ch24 自系列机理）。

所以"邦利器不可以示人"——不是藏着掖着搞神秘（那是权谋），是不把它立成招牌去炫耀（这是不立招牌）。治者守着利器（守自己的位），但不显示（不立招牌），所以利器持续在场。

"鱼不脱于渊"和"邦利器不可以示人"是同一件事的两面——鱼不脱于渊 = 守自己的位。邦利器不可以示人 = 不立招牌。守位 + 不立招牌 = 持续在自己的位上运作。

八、给读者的

这一章给读者的，是一个对"聪明"的彻底重新定义——

大多数人以为的"高明"是——会设局、会算计、会欲擒故纵、会捧杀、会藏底牌、会在恰当的时候出手。老子说，这些恰恰是最快让自己崩塌的方式。因为设局的人，自己的控制欲已经到了极致。自己先到极致，自己先崩。

老子的"微明"是另一种东西——看见结构动力（任何东西走到极致都会反弹），然后自己守在不到极致的位上。

不设局。不算计。不欲擒故纵。不藏底牌玩神秘。

只是——看见疯狂膨胀的必然崩塌（微明）。守住自己可持续的节奏（柔弱）。不脱离自己真正的位（鱼不脱于渊）。不把自己的东西立成招牌炫耀（邦利器不可以示人）。然后把时间交给结构。

放到你的具体处境——

面对一个咄咄逼人、疯狂扩张的对手——不要想办法去算计他、设局害他（你会先把自己搭进去）。守住自己的节奏（柔弱），做扎实的事，看着他因为自己的过度而崩塌。

面对一个想跟你争的人——不要进入那个争的极致（接 ch22"夫唯不争，故莫能与之争"）。守在不争的位，让他自己耗尽。

面对想炫耀实力的诱惑——不要把自己的底牌、能力、资源都亮出来立招牌。守着它们，持续运作，不显示。

这一章最反直觉的地方——真正的强不是设局和算计，是守在柔弱位让结构自己运作。设局的人累，而且自己先崩。守柔弱的人不累，而且笑到最后。不是因为守柔弱的人赢了，是因为刚强的人自己崩了，而守柔弱的人还在。

老子两千四百年前写下"柔弱胜刚强"。这五个字被无数人读成励志口号、读成处世圆滑、读成欲擒故纵的权术。它的真意是——

守在可弯曲、可适应、不到极致的位上。让走到极致的东西自己崩塌。你不需要动手。时间和结构会替你结算一切。

I. The Chapter That Has Been Read as Machiavellianism

Chapter 36 of the Daodejing is the chapter that has been most consistently misread throughout history.

"What you want to draw in, first let it fully extend. What you want to weaken, first let it fully strengthen. What you want to eliminate, first let it fully flourish. What you want to take, first fully give it."

Want it to contract — first let it expand. Want it weak — first let it get strong. Want it gone — first let it rise. Want to take from it — first give to it.

These four lines have been read by countless people as political manipulation — "want to take down a rival? First flatter him, let him inflate, wait until he's at the peak of his arrogance, then harvest him." Draw and release. Rope-a-dope. Play the long game.

This is the Thick-Black-Theory version of Laozi — reading these four lines as an operator's manual for scheming.

It's backward.

What Laozi establishes in this chapter is precisely that this kind of manipulation doesn't work. This chapter is not teaching you how to set traps. It is telling you a structural law — anything that goes to its extreme will necessarily move in the opposite direction — and then telling you what to do once you see this law. And what to do is the exact opposite of manipulation.

II. The Four Pairs Describe Structural Dynamics

Read the four pairs at face value precisely.

Xi (歙) is drawing in, closing (Shuowen: "xi: contracting the breath" — contracting). Zhang (张) is extending, expanding (drawing a bowstring taut). Want it to contract — it must first fully extend.

Why? Because a bow pulled to its limit will release. Extended to the extreme, it must return. This is not anyone operating it — it is the structural dynamic itself.

All four pairs say the same thing — expanded to the extreme, must contract (bow pulled fully, releases). Strengthened to the extreme, must weaken (connecting to ch. 30 "things in their prime begin to age"). Flourished to the extreme, must be eliminated. Given to the extreme (allowed to over-possess), must lose (possess too much and nothing can be held).

This is the reverse movement of structural dynamics — anything that goes to its extreme necessarily moves in the opposite direction.

This connects to the four pairs established in ch. 29 (things either lead or follow, breathe warmly or coldly, are strong or frail, rise or fall) — ch. 29 established "things move both ways in every dimension"; ch. 36's four reverse movements are the same word-thread — extreme strength must weaken, extreme extension must contract, extreme flourishing must be eliminated, extreme giving must lead to loss. This is things' natural movement.

III. Why the Manipulation Reading Doesn't Work

Back to the manipulation reading —

"I'll first inflate my rival, let him expand to the extreme, wait until the inevitable collapse, then harvest him."

This reading looks clever — using the "extreme strength must weaken" law to harm others. Laozi's word-thread proves it doesn't work. The proof is in ch. 29 — those who act on it ruin it; those who hold it lose it.

If someone actively goes about "letting it extend, letting it strengthen, letting it flourish, giving it more" in order to destroy a rival — this action itself is "forcibly constructing (acting on it)." By ch. 29's "those who act on it ruin it" — the moment you go about setting traps, acting, manipulating, you have already fallen into the position of "those who act on it ruin it."

Going deeper — when you set a "must weaken at the extreme" trap for someone else, your own desire for control and mastery has itself reached its extreme (the extreme/甚). By ch. 29's "the sage removes the extreme" — you yourself are already in the position of "the extreme." The trap you set for your rival hasn't played out yet, but you yourself have already been struck back by structure.

This is why the manipulation reading doesn't work within Laozi's word-thread — you want to use "extreme strength must weaken" to harm others. But the very action of setting the trap is your own "desire for control reaching its extreme." You yourself reach the extreme first; you yourself collapse first. Your rival hasn't been brought down yet; you have already gone down.

So "what you want to draw in, first let it fully extend" in Laozi is absolutely not an active tactic. It is passive seeing — watching the rival in front of you frantically constructing and expanding, seeing that he will inevitably collapse from his own expansion. Then not raising a hand yourself — keeping to the soft-and-yielding position, not going to extremes, giving time to structure. Structural dynamics will settle the rival's collapse by themselves. You don't need to raise a hand.

IV. This Is Called Subtle Illumination

Laozi gives this law a name —

"This is called subtle illumination."

Wei (微) is subtle, not conspicuous. Ming (明) is illuminating from within, seeing clearly (connecting to ch. 33 "knowing oneself is illumination" — ming is seeing structure clearly from within). Subtle illumination = subtle but clear seeing — this law is subtle (not on the surface, not conspicuous), but for those who can see, it is clear.

Subtle illumination is not cunning, not cleverness, not calculating. It is seeing structural dynamics. Seeing that anything going to its extreme will rebound. Seeing that frantic inflation must collapse. Having seen this, keeping yourself in the position of not going to extremes.

V. The Soft and Yielding Overcomes the Hard and Strong

What follows is the chapter's summary, and the first systematic appearance of "soft" (rou, 柔) in the Daodejing —

"The soft and yielding overcomes the hard and strong."

Rou (柔, soft) — Shuowen: "rou: wood can bend and straighten" — can bend, can adapt. Gang (刚, hard) — rigid, cannot bend. Soft and yielding = can bend, can adapt; hard and strong = cannot bend, cannot adapt.

Why does soft and yielding overcome hard and strong? Because of structural necessity — pushed to its extreme, strength begins to decline (connecting to ch. 30 "things in their prime begin to age"). What is hard and strong is already at the extreme position; what follows is decline. What is soft and yielding is not at the extreme; it keeps its capacity to bend and adapt, and thus endures. Keeping soft and yielding (not going to extremes) is what allows one to endure (connecting to ch. 33 "those who do not lose their position endure").

Here we must completely separate "the soft and yielding overcomes the hard and strong" from the manipulation reading — it is not using tricks to induce others to become hard and strong and then waiting for their collapse. It is keeping yourself in the soft-and-yielding position (not going to extremes), while those on the other side go to their own extreme and then collapse.

Where is the difference? Inducing others to become hard and strong — you are raising a hand to set a trap; your desire for control has reached its extreme; you collapse first. Keeping to soft and yielding — you don't raise a hand; you watch the other side inflate and collapse of their own accord; you remain in the position of not collapsing throughout.

Those who actively induce others to become hard and strong reach the extreme themselves first and collapse first. Those who keep to the soft-and-yielding position let structure settle things, and remain safe throughout. This is soft and yielding overcoming hard and strong.

In 2026 — there is a rival in your market who is burning cash frantically, expanding frantically, doing PR frantically —

The manipulation approach: find ways to stimulate him to burn more, expand faster, wait for his cash flow to break and then harvest. — But this approach itself requires you to pour enormous energy into setting traps and manipulating; your obsession with "must bring him down" has already reached its extreme. In the process of setting the trap, you yourself burn cash, expand, become anxious. You reach the extreme first.

The soft-and-yielding approach: keep your own pace (don't go to extremes), build solid products, watch your rival collapse from his own over-expansion. — You don't need to do anything targeting him. Structural dynamics (extreme strength must weaken) will settle him on their own. You keep the soft-and-yielding position (sustainable, not at the extreme); he is in the hard-and-strong position (unsustainable, already at the extreme).

The soft and yielding overcomes the hard and strong. Not that you won — he collapsed by himself, and you are still here.

VI. The Fish Does Not Leave the Deep Pool

Laozi then uses the plainest possible image —

"The fish does not leave the deep pool."

Fish cannot leave deep water. A fish out of water cannot survive. Some things cannot leave the position on which they depend for survival.

The literal point for those who govern (and for anyone) — keep to your own position; do not leave the position on which you depend for survival. Connecting to ch. 33 "those who do not lose their position endure" — keeping to one's position = continuously present.

What is that "position"? For a ruler, it is the Dao (connecting to ch. 32 "if lords and kings can keep to it, the ten thousand things will of themselves come to attend" — keeping to the unhewn = keeping to the Dao). The ruler who keeps to the Dao (keeps to the pool) — the world naturally flows back. The ruler who leaves the Dao (leaves the pool) — loses everything. Just like a fish that cannot leave water.

Today — what is the "pool" on which you depend for survival? It is your genuine value, your genuine capability, your genuine position. Do not abandon your genuine pool to chase surface things (a higher position, a bigger name, a more conspicuous sign). The fish that leaves the pool cannot survive.

VII. A State's Sharp Instruments Cannot Be Shown to People

The final line —

"A state's sharp instruments cannot be shown to people."

This line is also most easily misread as manipulation — "hide your hand; don't let people see your real strength." That's wrong again.

Laozi's "a state's sharp instruments cannot be shown" is not about hiding power — it is about not planting signs. Why can't they be shown? Because the moment you display them, you plant the sign "I have power." Planting signs violates ch. 34 "because he does not declare himself great, he can achieve greatness" — don't declare yourself great, and truly become great.

The moment a ruler shows his sharp instruments, he has moved into the "declaring himself great" position — plants the sign → stops operating → loses the sharp instruments (connecting to ch. 24's mechanism of self-certifying).

So "a state's sharp instruments cannot be shown to people" — not hiding things to play at mystery (that is manipulation), but not making them into signs to display (not planting signs). The ruler keeps his sharp instruments (keeps to his position), but does not display them (plants no signs), so the sharp instruments remain continuously present.

"The fish does not leave the deep pool" and "a state's sharp instruments cannot be shown" are two sides of the same thing — fish not leaving the pool = keeping to one's position. Sharp instruments not shown = not planting signs. Keeping to one's position + not planting signs = continuously operating in one's own position.

VIII. What This Chapter Gives the Reader

What this chapter gives readers is a complete redefinition of "being clever" —

What most people think "being clever" means — setting traps, calculating, drawing and releasing, rope-a-doping, hiding your hand, striking at the right moment. Laozi says these are precisely the fastest ways to bring about your own collapse. Because those who set traps have already pushed their own desire for control to the extreme. They reach the extreme first; they collapse first.

Laozi's "subtle illumination" is something else — seeing structural dynamics (anything going to its extreme will rebound), then keeping yourself in the position of not going to extremes.

No trap-setting. No calculating. No drawing and releasing. No hiding your hand to play at mystery.

Only — seeing the inevitable collapse of frantic inflation (subtle illumination). Keeping your own sustainable pace (soft and yielding). Not leaving your genuine position (the fish does not leave the deep pool). Not making your own things into signs to display (a state's sharp instruments cannot be shown). Then giving time to structure.

Applied to your specific situation —

Facing an aggressive, frantically expanding rival — don't try to calculate against him or set traps (you'll sacrifice yourself in the process). Keep your own pace (soft and yielding), do solid work, watch him collapse from his own excess.

Facing someone who wants to contest with you — don't enter the extreme of that contest (connecting to ch. 22 "precisely because he does not contend, no one can contend with him"). Keep to the non-contending position; let him exhaust himself.

Facing the temptation to display your strength — don't lay out all your cards, capabilities, and resources to plant signs. Hold them, keep operating, don't display.

The most counterintuitive thing about this chapter — true strength is not trap-setting and calculating; it is keeping to the soft-and-yielding position and letting structure operate by itself. Those who set traps are exhausted — and they collapse first. Those who keep soft and yielding are not exhausted — and they are still there at the end. Not because they won, but because the hard and strong collapsed of their own accord, while those who kept soft and yielding are still here.

Laozi wrote "the soft and yielding overcomes the hard and strong" 2,400 years ago. Those five characters have been read by countless people as an inspiring slogan, as worldly smoothness, as the tactic of drawing and releasing. Their true meaning is —

Keep to the position of bending, adapting, and not going to extremes. Let what has gone to extremes collapse of its own accord. You do not need to raise a hand. Time and structure will settle everything for you.
