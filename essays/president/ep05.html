第五篇：约翰·昆西·亚当斯与杰克逊——容纳与驱逐同时发生

Essay 5: Quincy Adams and Jackson — Inclusion and Exclusion at the Same Time

Han Qin (秦汉)

上一篇结尾埋下的那个判断，这一篇要正面展开。1825到1837年这段历史，最容易被讲成一个顺滑的进步故事，财产门槛退场，越来越多的人获得选举权，美国一步步走向更民主。但这种讲法漏掉了同一时刻正在发生的另一半。就在白人男性的选举权大幅扩展的同时，东南部的多个原住民族被成规模地强制驱赶出他们的家园，而在好几个州，自由黑人的选举权反而被收紧，被取消。这不是一个先民主化，后排除的线性故事，而是民主的扩大和边界的排除，在同一个时间，由同一批人，一起完成。

把这段时间放回这个系列，可以抓住三条同时上升的线。第一条是容纳，更多的白人男性进入了选民共同体，候选人和政党必须直接去争夺他们，政治整合不再主要靠议员之间的小圈子会议来完成。第二条是驱逐，东南部多个原住民族被剥夺了在原居地继续作为政治共同体存在的条件，被用条约，胁迫，欺骗，暴力和行政执行相结合的方式推过密西西比河。第三条是总统权力的扩张，杰克逊一面以人民代表的姿态对抗国会，银行和部分精英，一面把否决权从一件极少使用，主要针对合宪性的工具，变成了一件经常使用，兼带政策判断的武器。

这种同时性，并不是历史的偶然，它有一个结构上的根由。一个政治共同体，是靠它的边界来定义自己的，而边界的另一个名字，就是排除。所以当这个共同体决定把更多的人纳进来时，它同时也在更清楚地划定，谁被留在外面。更深一层看，被纳入者的那种平等感，常常恰恰是靠与被排除者的对照建立起来的。白人男性之间那种新的平等，一个不再以财产多寡分高下的平等，在很大程度上，是以一条更硬的种族界线为背景的。财产的门槛被拆掉了，肤色的门槛却被砌得更高。换句话说，扩大的那种平等，和强化的那种排除，不是两件碰巧同时发生的事，它们在结构上彼此支撑，前者的实现，部分地依赖于后者的存在。这就是为什么这一篇要把容纳和驱逐写成同一个动作的两面，而不是两个并列的话题。

一、一个有争议的总统的诞生

这个时代是从一场有争议的大选开始的。1824年大选，是旧规则失灵而新规则未稳的典型一役。四位主要候选人都出自同一个正在分裂的政治传统，安德鲁·杰克逊，约翰·昆西·亚当斯，威廉·克劳福德和亨利·克莱。正式的选举人票结果是，杰克逊九十九票，亚当斯八十四票，克劳福德四十一票，克莱三十七票，总数二百六十一票，过半需要一百三十一票，因此无人当选。按第十二修正案，决定权交给众议院，在得票前三名中择一。

这场选举之所以在制度史上重要，不只因为它进入了众议院的或然选举，还因为它正发生在选举方式迅速变化的节点。1824年已经有十八个州通过普选来决定本州的总统选举人，只剩六个州仍由州议会指定。在这十八个州里，杰克逊获得了大约十五万三千张普选票，亚当斯约十一万四千张。但这里要谨慎，1824年并不存在一个全国统一，标准化的普选票计量，所以把这一年按后世的意义解释成赢了全国普选却输了总统，需要保留分寸。

1825年2月9日，众议院按州代表团一州一票表决，亚当斯在第一轮取得十三个州代表团的支持，杰克逊七个，克劳福德四个，当选总统。紧接着，亚当斯任命了亨利·克莱出任国务卿。克莱本人被排除在众议院可选的前三名之外，但他作为众议院议长，对那场表决有极大的影响力。这一步立刻引爆了肮脏交易的指控。杰克逊阵营愤怒地把克莱称作西部的犹大，认为大选被一笔交易偷走了。现代史学一般认为，无法证明存在一桩可以法律定性的密室交易，但肮脏交易这个叙事在政治上极其成功，它成了1825到1828年间，杰克逊派持续动员支持者，重组政党的核心资源。

约翰·昆西·亚当斯因此从一开始就背着一笔合法性的赤字。现代研究对他的概括很精炼，他本来资历过硬，却在一个政治刚刚开始真正重要起来的时代，做了一个糟糕的政治家。他的失败主要不在于眼界狭窄，而在于他既被1824年的选举削弱了，又不愿意用别人正在掌握的那套新型党争方式来保护自己。把他看成一个过渡总统，大体符合现有的研究。他提出的是一套高度国家主义的，重视基础设施和知识建国的纲领，但他面对的，是一个正在转向群众政治，候选人个人化和组织化党争的新制度环境。

二、过渡总统与1828年的重组

亚当斯的纲领其实一点也不保守，相反相当宏阔。他强力支持由联邦政府出资去改善社会条件的项目和机构，主张修建道路，运河，港口和桥梁，也支持科学和教育事业。所以他并不是一个无为的总统，而是一个议程大而联盟弱的总统。他的挫败不是因为没有计划，而是因为他缺少足够的政治机器，把计划送过国会，更因为杰克逊阵营从他上任的第一天起，就把他当成一个需要在1828年被推翻的临时占位者。

就这一篇的结构而言，亚当斯的处境很有意思。他代表的是一种旧的政治风格，士绅式的国家主义，靠才学和资历，靠精英之间的协商。而他撞上的，是一个正在成形的新世界，靠政党机器，靠群众动员，靠候选人个人的号召力。他不是输在能力上，而是输在不肯，也不擅长用新规则打仗。这套构的规则正在改写，而亚当斯恰好是被这次改写甩在身后的人。

1828年大选，把1824年的怨愤转化成了制度性的重组。杰克逊赢得了一百七十八张选举人票和大约六十四万张普选票，亚当斯则是八十三张选举人票和大约五十万张普选票。这同时也是美国最著名的早期污点竞选之一。对杰克逊的攻击，包括他的婚姻，他的决斗史，他暴烈的性格，而对他妻子雷切尔的攻击尤其凶狠，围绕她和前夫离婚程序里的时间问题，把她描绘成重婚者和通奸者。杰克逊后来把妻子的死，和1828年竞选中针对她的攻击联系在一起。

杰克逊胜利之后，很快塑造出一个人民总统的公共形象。他是第一位真正意义上白手起家式的总统，也是第一位来自西部的总统。他在就职之后，迅速把总统从一个行政首席管理者，推进成一个人民的代言人，以人民的直接授权自居，可以与国会既合作也抗衡。1829年的就职典礼上，大批支持者涌进了首都，保守的批评者把场面描述成一片失序，而支持者则把它看成政府向人民敞开大门的象征。

三、白人男性民主的扩展与它的种族边界

如果只看选举的参与，杰克逊时代的确是美国民主扩展的一个重要阶段。1812年战争之后，各州通过修宪和立法，逐步取消了传统的财产投票资格，让越来越多的州级职位和总统选举人，改由民众投票产生。不需要拥有财产也应当拥有投票权，成了这一时期一个重要的政治诉求。

但这种民主扩展，必须严格地加上限定语，它的直接受益者主要是白人男性。近几十年的修正性史学，尤其强调这一点。在美国，白人选民的扩张和黑人选民的剥夺，是重构选民共同体这同一个过程的两面。内战前的最后三十年，一方面白人男性的投票权总体上在扩展，另一方面种族化的选举资格，也被越来越明确地写进了法律和州宪法。有研究指出，到1840年，美国那个规模不大的自由黑人群体里，已经有大约百分之九十三被剥夺了选举资格，宾夕法尼亚，北卡罗来纳等州都在1830年代进一步收紧了自由黑人的投票权。这些材料很关键，因为它们能纠正一种过于顺滑的旧叙事，那种把杰克逊式民主直接等同于民主进步的叙事。更准确的说法应当是，杰克逊时代推进了白人男性的大众政治，同时强化了民主边界的种族化。

这种选民的扩张，也改变了组织政治本身。1824年的选举已经暴露出，旧式的国会党团提名机制，在大众政治面前失灵了。到1828年，围绕杰克逊和范布伦建立起来的全国性动员网络，已经比亚当斯一方更适应这个新环境。这一时期的政党，不再只是国会里的松散联盟，而开始成为真正的选民动员机器，拥有更强的纪律，更广泛的地方报刊网络，以及通过任命，宣传和象征去整合选民的能力。

四、驱逐的制度化，印第安人迁移

就在民主的容纳一侧不断扩展的同时，驱逐的一侧也在被制度化。1830年的印第安人迁移法，是这一时期最关键，也最需要严肃对待的法案之一。它的正式标题，是一部为了与居住在各州或领地内的印第安人交换土地，并把他们迁往密西西比河以西而设立的法。法案授权总统在密西西比河以西划出地区，与东部的部族交换土地，法文里反复使用那些选择交换并迁移的部族这样的措辞，表面上保留了选择的语言，同时又授权总统提供迁移，安置和一年的生计支持。但从立法之后的实际运作看，迁移是通过条约，胁迫，贿赂，威胁，以及在许多场合下对既有条约和司法裁决的违背来推进的。法律上的选择语言，并没有消除它实际的强制性。

这部法案在国会通过得极其惊险。参议院以二十八比十九通过，众议院以一百零二比九十七通过，杰克逊于1830年5月28日签署成法。如此接近的票数本身就说明，即便在当时，这也不是一种没有争议的共识政策。传教士的网络，包括杰里迈亚·埃瓦茨在内的反对者，以及弗里林海森，亨利·克莱，戴维·克罗克特等国会议员，都曾公开反对。

迁移的规模，最好按口径分别陈述，而不要强行合并成一个单一的数字。如果限定在杰克逊任内，政府签署了将近七十份迁移条约，使接近五万名东部印第安人被迁往印第安领地。如果讨论1830到1850年整个迁移时代，被用条约，欺骗，恐吓和暴力移往密西西比河以西的，大约有十万人。五部落通常指切罗基，克里克，乔克托，奇克索和塞米诺尔。以切罗基为例，较稳妥的表述是，近一万六千人被迁移，死亡约在两千到四千之间，若依据一些机构和切罗基传统记忆的常见口径，常写约四千，某些切罗基自己的口径更高。

无论按哪一种口径，事实本身已经足够沉重。这是一场用法律的外壳包装起来的国家驱逐工程。一个民族被迫离开世代居住的土地，在迁徙途中成千上万地死去，许多原本签订的条约被违背，而这一切，发生在那个号称把民主扩展到了更多人的同一个时代，由同一批人主导。

司法的那一根线，更把这件事的性质照得清楚。1832年，首席大法官马歇尔代表最高法院在伍斯特诉乔治亚案里裁定，切罗基构成一个独立的政治共同体，乔治亚州的法律不得适用于切罗基的领地，也就是说，州不能单方面把自己的法权覆盖到切罗基这个政治共同体之上。这本是一次明确的司法胜诉。但乔治亚拒绝执行，州长公开抗拒，而杰克逊没有采取任何行动去迫使乔治亚服从。

把这件事和前面几篇放在一起，会照出这套构一个令人不安的限度。第三篇里，马伯里案确立了司法审查，把总统也关进了法律的笼子，那是这套构用自己长出的制衡约束行政的一次成功。可是在切罗基这件事上，同样是最高法院作出了对原住民有利的判决，结果却是另一回事。当总统干脆拒绝执行，而广大的白人选民又站在驱逐这一边时，司法的胜诉就落了空。这说明这套构的制衡，是在各个分支彼此较真，彼此配合时才管用的，一旦行政公然不执行，立法不愿干预，而民意又压在被排除者的对立面，那么纸面上的胜诉，就保护不了那些被排除在政治共同体之外的人。这套构能把斗争关进规则的笼子，却没能保证笼子里的规则，总会站在最弱势的一方那一边。对那些已经被推到共同体边界之外的人来说，制衡的失灵，往往就是致命的。

五、总统作为人民的代表

杰克逊时代的另一个核心，是总统的权力第一次被系统而高强度地重新界定。这正是这个系列那条主线，被设计来受制衡的位置为什么会反复扩张，第一次抵达的一个高峰。

最直观的指标，是否决权的使用频率。杰克逊任内一共行使了十二次否决权，而在他之前的所有总统加起来，也只有十次。约翰·昆西·亚当斯任内一次都没用过，门罗用过一次，麦迪逊七次，华盛顿两次。但真正的变化不只是数量，更是性质。在杰克逊之前，总统的否决基本上都建立在合宪性的异议上，总统说的是，我认为这部法违宪。而1832年杰克逊对合众国银行续期法案的否决，同时诉诸了政治，经济，道德和宪法的理由。总统不再只是说我认为它违宪，而是公开说，我认为它不公正，不明智，损害人民，偏袒特权，所以即便国会通过了，我也要否决。这就是否决权从一件宪法性工具，向一件政策性工具的历史性转变。

1832年7月10日的银行否决咨文，是这场转变的经典文本。杰克逊先承认这家国家银行在许多方面对政府便利，对人民有用，但随即断言它的部分权力和特权，违背宪法，颠覆各州的权利，危及人民的自由。他进一步把银行描述成一种被授予了独占市场和政府偏爱的特许垄断，认为续期会再一次把数百万美元的赠与送给现有的股东，其中相当一部分还是外国持股者，又强调银行代表的是最富裕阶层的利益，而不是普通人民。这套论证，远远超出了纯粹法律技术的层面，它把总统直接放到了人民对抗特权的位置上。

所以杰克逊建立起来的，是一种新的总统修辞和实践。总统不再消极地等待国会送来合乎宪法的法案，而是以直接代表全国人民的身份，对政策的方向本身提出判断。他把总统从一个首席行政管理者，变成了一个人民的代言人，并且逆转了行政部门长期以来对立法至上的那种自我克制。

这个新燃料，比先例和引力都更猛，也更难驾驭。靠先例扩张，扩张的是惯例，靠既成事实扩张，扩张的是现实的重量，这两者都还在制度的内部运作。而靠民意的直接授权，总统获得的是一种可以越过其他分支的正当性。他可以说，国会不过是一个个选区分别选出来的议员，而我是全国人民共同选出来的，所以在我和国会之间，我才更能代表人民的整体意志。这套逻辑一旦成立，总统就有了一个凌驾于立法之上的话语支点。杰克逊时代第一次把这个支点搭了起来，而它一旦被搭起来，就再也收不回去了。

六、银行战争与废止危机

要看清杰克逊主义为什么不是一种意识形态，最好的办法，是把银行战争和废止危机放在一起看。

银行战争是杰克逊权力扩张最著名的领域之一。第二合众国银行本来要到1836年才到期，但亨利·克莱和银行总裁尼古拉斯·比德尔，希望提前在1832年推动续期，把银行问题变成大选的议题，逼杰克逊在签字得罪反银行的选民，和否决得罪商界与温和派之间二选一。杰克逊选择了否决，并且成功地把这一举动包装成打击特权和腐败。连任之后，斗争进入第二阶段，联邦的存款被从国家银行转出，转入若干家州银行，后者在当时的政治语言里被称为宠物银行。到1833年底，大部分存款已经被移走，银行因此迅速萎缩，而比德尔则企图通过收缩信贷，催收贷款来制造经济上的不满，逼杰克逊让步。这一举动还引发了参议院在1834年对杰克逊的正式谴责，理由是反对者认为，他擅自对财政部和国库行使了不属于总统的权力。

关于银行战争的后果，现有研究长期存在两种相当稳定的解释，最好并置。第一种是反特权的民粹解释，杰克逊打击了一个兼具半官方和私人特权性质，又与政商网络深度缠绕的机构，为多数白人选民击退了金融贵族。第二种是制度稳定的金融解释，第二银行虽然有严重的政治问题，但它确实部分承担了现代中央银行的功能，它被摧毁之后，美国进入了一种更依赖州银行，更碎片化的金融制度，后来一系列的金融波动和银行恐慌，与此并非毫无关系。这两种解释都言之成理，不必替读者先行裁断。

如果说银行战争体现的是杰克逊反精英特权的一面，那么废止危机就展现了他强力联邦主义的另一面，而这一面最能提醒读者，不能用单一的意识形态去概括这个时期。南卡罗来纳因为1828年和1832年的关税，主张各州有权把这些关税在本州视为无效。杰克逊在1832年12月10日的废止宣言里，逐条反击了州单方面废止联邦法的理论基础。宣言直言，南卡的条例颠覆了宪法，而它的逻辑如果贯彻下去，美国很快就将不再是一个国家。

在这里，杰克逊并不只是温和地不同意南卡，而是明确否定了州的废止权乃至分离权。危机最终由两项措施并行缓和，一边是1833年的强制法案，授权总统在必要时动用陆海军和民兵来确保关税法的执行，另一边是亨利·克莱推动的妥协关税，逐步下调税率。同一个总统，既在银行问题上被反对者骂作国王安德鲁，又在关税危机里以联邦的武力维护国家的统一。

这就是为什么杰克逊主义无法被装进一条意识形态的坐标线。它真正一以贯之的，不是小政府，也不是州权，而是把总统变成国家方向与执行力的中心。银行问题上他调动反特权的语言，废止危机上他调动强联邦的语言，这两套看似相反的语言，服务的是同一件事，让总统这个位置，成为整个国家的枢轴。

七、第二党制与国王安德鲁

杰克逊政治留下的最深的制度痕迹，是一个新的政党体系。他的支持者最终整合成了现代民主党的前身，而他的反对者，则在1830年代中期逐渐聚合成辉格党。杰克逊在八年里，把一个围绕他个人的模糊联盟，熔铸成了一个纪律化，全国化的政党机器，而与此同时，他那些充满争议的做法，也反过来逼出了一个有组织的反对党。

辉格党的生成并不只是政策上的分歧，更是对总统统治风格的一次结构性反弹。银行战争，撤存款，否决权的常态化，对行政任命的党派化控制，再加上杰克逊个人极强的政治风格，使反对者越来越倾向于把他描绘成一个以人民的名义突破宪法节制的国王安德鲁。对辉格党来说，问题不止是某一项具体的政策，而是总统这个位置，是否正在吞噬那个以国会为中心的宪政秩序。

放回框架看，辉格党的出现，本身就是这套构那个熟悉的节奏又一次启动。前面几篇里，行政权每一次大的扩张，都会触发一股反方向的力量。杰斐逊的扩张撞上了马歇尔的法院，而杰克逊把总统推到前所未有的高度，也立刻逼出了一个以约束总统为宗旨的反对党。辉格党不是凭空冒出来的，它是这套构对杰克逊式扩张的自我纠正。只不过这一次，纠正的力量没有来自某一个分支，而是来自一个新组织起来的政党，它要在选举的战场上，把那个被杰克逊推高了的总统权力，重新拉回一些。

八、结语

把约翰·昆西·亚当斯和杰克逊这十二年放回这个系列，最值得记住的，是那个贯穿全篇的判断，容纳与驱逐同时发生。

这套构在这一时期，一面把更多的白人男性纳入了政治共同体，把容纳党派纷争的机器造得空前精密，另一面又把东南部的原住民族强制驱逐出去，把好几个州的自由黑人推出选民的行列。民主的扩展本身就是两面的，它纳入一些人的同一个动作，也在更彻底地排除另一些人。这正是那个最深的余项在杰克逊时代的形态，它不是先有民主，后有排除，而是民主的边界，本身就是用排除划出来的。

与此同时，总统的权力迎来了建国以来又一次大扩张，而且这次扩张靠的是一种新的燃料，民意的直接授权。杰克逊把否决权从合宪性的工具变成了政策的武器，把总统从行政的管理者变成了人民的代言人。但他这套权力，又无法被装进任何一种意识形态，因为他在银行问题上反特权，在废止危机上强联邦，这两套相反的语言，服务的是同一个目标，让总统成为国家的枢轴。

接下来登场的，是这套刚刚成形的第二党制开始运转的年代。一场严重的经济恐慌会立刻考验杰克逊留下的金融遗产，而那道在密苏里第一次划下，又在每一块新领土上被重新政治化的地理裂缝，会沿着兼并和扩张，被一寸一寸地刻得更深。那是从范布伦到内战前夜的故事。

The judgment at the end of the previous essay must now be developed in full. The years from 1825 to 1837 are most easily narrated as a progressive story: property qualifications for voting receded, more men gained the franchise, America moved steadily toward more democracy. But that narrative omits what was happening simultaneously. While white male suffrage expanded substantially, multiple southeastern indigenous nations were forcibly expelled from their homelands at massive scale, and in several states, free Black men's voting rights were further restricted or abolished. This is not a story in which democratization came first and exclusion followed. The expansion of democratic inclusion and the hardening of exclusionary boundaries happened at the same time, by the same people, as two faces of one act.

Three simultaneously rising lines run through this period. First, inclusion: more white men entered the electorate, candidates and parties had to compete directly for their loyalty, and political integration could no longer be primarily achieved through small-circle elite deliberation. Second, expulsion: multiple southeastern indigenous peoples were stripped of the conditions necessary for continuing to exist as political communities on their ancestral lands, pushed across the Mississippi through a combination of treaties, coercion, fraud, violence, and administrative execution. Third, the expansion of presidential power: Jackson simultaneously positioned himself as the tribune of the people against Congress, the Bank, and various elites, while converting the veto from a rarely-used constitutionality check into a regularly deployed policy weapon.

This simultaneity was not historical coincidence. A political community defines itself by its boundaries, and the other name for a boundary is exclusion. When a community decides to include more people, it simultaneously clarifies who remains outside. More deeply, the sense of equality among those newly included often rests on contrast with those excluded. The new equality among white men — an equality no longer graded by property — was built against the background of a harder racial line. Property thresholds were dismantled; racial thresholds were reinforced. The expansion of one kind of equality and the intensification of one kind of exclusion were not coincidental events — they were structurally connected, the former partially depending on the latter. That is why this essay treats inclusion and expulsion as two faces of the same action, not two parallel topics.

1. A President Born Contested

The era opened with a contested election. The election of 1824 was the paradigmatic case of old rules failing before new rules had stabilized. Four major candidates — Jackson, John Quincy Adams, William Crawford, and Henry Clay — all came from the same fracturing political tradition. Jackson received 99 electoral votes, Adams 84, Crawford 41, Clay 37. With no candidate reaching the majority of 131, the decision went to the House under the Twelfth Amendment, choosing from the top three.

The election's institutional importance was not just that it triggered a contingent House election, but that it occurred precisely as modes of choosing electors were rapidly changing. By 1824, eighteen states used popular voting to select presidential electors; only six still left the choice to state legislatures. In those eighteen states, Jackson received roughly 153,000 popular votes against Adams's roughly 114,000. Caution is required: no nationally standardized popular vote count existed in 1824, and treating the result as a modern popular-vote-won, presidency-lost scenario overstates the analogy.

On February 9, 1825, the House voted by state delegation — one state, one vote. Adams won thirteen state delegations on the first ballot, Jackson seven, Crawford four. Adams then appointed Henry Clay as Secretary of State. Clay, the House Speaker, had wielded enormous influence over the House balloting despite being ineligible for the House to select. The appointment immediately detonated the Corrupt Bargain charge. Jackson's supporters called Clay the Judas of the West and treated the election as stolen. Modern scholarship generally holds that no legally definable backroom deal can be proven, but the Corrupt Bargain narrative was politically enormously effective — it became the central resource for the Jacksonian coalition's mobilization and party reconstruction through 1828.

Adams thus carried a legitimacy deficit from his first day. Modern characterizations tend toward the same summary: he was a man of formidable credentials who was a poor politician exactly when politics was coming to matter in entirely new ways. His failure was less about narrow vision than about being unable and unwilling to fight with the tools that were displacing the old ones. He proposed a boldly nationalist program — federally funded roads, canals, a national university, scientific expeditions, observatories — but faced an environment already shifting toward mass politics and organized partisan competition. He was the person this construct's rule-change left behind.

2. The Election of 1828 and the Party Reorganization

The election of 1828 converted the resentment of 1824 into institutional reorganization. Jackson won 178 electoral votes against Adams's 83, and roughly 640,000 popular votes against Adams's 500,000. It was also one of the most famously vicious early American campaigns. Jackson's marriage, his history of dueling, and his temperament were all attacked; assaults on his wife Rachel — depicting her as a bigamist and adulteress because of the timing of her divorce from a previous marriage — were particularly savage. Jackson later attributed Rachel's death in December 1828, shortly before his inauguration, partly to the campaign attacks she endured. Adams's side ran equally aggressive attacks on Jackson's conduct and character. The election of 1828 was not evidence of the construct calmly managing disagreement; it was evidence that the construct survived after disagreement was inflamed to extraordinary intensity.

Jackson's victory generated a rapid transformation of the presidential image. He was the first president who could credibly be called self-made, the first from the West. He quickly repositioned the presidency from chief administrative manager to direct representative of the people, claiming a mandate from popular sovereignty that put him in a position to both cooperate with and confront Congress. The inauguration crowds — supporters flooding Washington, boisterously occupying the White House reception — were described as disorder by conservative critics and as the government opening to the people by supporters. The image, however exaggerated, captured something real about how Jackson was redefining what the office meant.

3. White Male Democracy and Its Racial Boundaries

Measured only by participation rates and formal franchise, the Jacksonian era was a genuine moment of democratic expansion. After the War of 1812, states progressively abolished property requirements for voting in state elections and for choosing presidential electors. The claim that men without property should nonetheless have the vote became a politically powerful demand in this period.

But this expansion requires sharp qualification. Its direct beneficiaries were primarily white men. Revisionist scholarship over recent decades has emphasized this insistently: the expansion of white voting and the disfranchisement of Black voters were two faces of the same reconstruction of the electorate. In the decades before the Civil War, white male suffrage expanded broadly while racially defined voting qualifications were written increasingly explicitly into state law and state constitutions. Research indicates that by 1840, roughly ninety-three percent of the small free Black population had been stripped of the franchise. Pennsylvania, North Carolina, and other states specifically tightened restrictions on free Black voting in the 1830s. These materials correct a too-smooth old narrative that treats Jacksonian democracy as straightforwardly democratic progress. The more accurate formulation is that the Jacksonian era advanced mass politics for white men while intensifying the racial definition of democratic boundaries.

This expansion of the electorate also transformed party organization. The 1824 election had exposed the failure of the congressional caucus nomination system under mass politics conditions. By 1828, the national mobilization network built around Jackson and Martin Van Buren was already far better adapted to the new environment than Adams's more gentry-style coalition. Parties in this period were becoming genuine voter-mobilization machines — more disciplined, with broader local press networks, and with the capacity to integrate voters through appointments, publicity, and symbols. The machinery for containing the remainder of partisan strife was being built more elaborately than ever before.

4. Institutionalizing Removal

As democratic inclusion expanded on one side, expulsion was being institutionalized on the other. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 was the most consequential and most serious legislation of the period. Its formal title described the exchange of lands with Indians residing in any of the states or territories, and their removal to the west of the river Mississippi. The act authorized the president to designate land west of the Mississippi for exchange, used language throughout about tribes choosing to exchange and remove, nominally preserved the language of choice, and authorized the president to fund removal, resettlement, and a year of subsistence support. But from the actual implementation, removal proceeded through treaty, coercion, bribery, threat, and in many cases violation of existing treaties and judicial rulings. The legal language of choice did not eliminate the practical coercive character.

The bill's congressional passage was extremely close: twenty-eight to nineteen in the Senate, one hundred two to ninety-seven in the House. Jackson signed it on May 28, 1830. Those margins themselves show that even at the time, this was not a policy of uncontested consensus. A network of missionaries and humanitarian advocates, Congressman Jeremiah Evarts, and congressional opponents including Frelinghuysen, Henry Clay, and Davy Crockett publicly opposed it.

The scale of removal is best described by specifying the measurement window rather than forcing a single number. During Jackson's presidency specifically, the government signed roughly seventy removal treaties, leading to approximately fifty thousand eastern Indians being relocated to Indian Territory. Over the full removal era of 1830–1850, roughly one hundred thousand people were pushed west of the Mississippi through treaty, fraud, intimidation, and force. The Five Civilized Tribes were the Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole. For the Cherokee specifically, approximately sixteen thousand were relocated; death estimates range from roughly two thousand to four thousand in the more conservative scholarly range, with four thousand being the figure most commonly cited by institutional sources and Cherokee oral tradition, and some Cherokee sources placing the figure higher.

Whatever the precise numbers, the fact is heavy enough at any count. This was a state-organized expulsion program wrapped in legal form. Nations were forced from lands they had inhabited for generations; thousands died in transit; treaties were violated; and all of this happened in the same era, by the same political actors, who claimed to be expanding democratic governance.

The judicial thread illuminates the nature of the failure most sharply. In Worcester v. Georgia in 1832, Chief Justice Marshall, writing for the Supreme Court, held that the Cherokee constituted a distinct political community occupying their own territory in which Georgia's laws had no force. This was an unambiguous judicial victory for the Cherokee. But Georgia refused to comply; the governor publicly defied the ruling; and Jackson took no action to compel Georgia's obedience. The famous attributed remark — "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it" — is of highly questionable authenticity. What Jackson actually wrote privately was that he regarded the ruling as still-born. But the practical effect is documented: federal action to enforce the ruling was not forthcoming, and removal proceeded.

Placing this alongside the earlier essays reveals a troubling limit of this construct. The third essay traced how Marbury v. Madison placed the president himself inside the law's cage — a successful moment of the construct using its own generated checks to constrain executive power. But in the Cherokee case, the Supreme Court ruled in favor of the indigenous claimants and the ruling had no effect. When the president chose not to enforce, the legislature was unwilling to compel, and white public opinion stood on the side of expulsion, a judicial victory on paper left the most vulnerable parties without protection. This construct can put the struggle inside the rule's cage; it cannot guarantee that the rules inside the cage will always stand on the side of the weakest. For those already pushed to the boundary of the political community, the failure of the check can be fatal.

5. The President as Tribune of the People

The other core of the Jacksonian era was the first systematic high-intensity redefinition of presidential power — the peak, within this series, of why the position designed to be checked keeps expanding.

The most direct indicator was veto frequency. Jackson vetoed twelve bills in his two terms; all previous presidents together had issued ten vetoes. Adams had issued none; Monroe one; Madison seven; Washington two. But the change was not only quantitative — it was qualitative. Before Jackson, presidential vetoes rested primarily on constitutionality grounds: the president was saying that he believed the bill was unconstitutional. Jackson's 1832 veto of the Bank recharter bill was grounded simultaneously in political, economic, moral, and constitutional reasoning. The president was no longer merely saying a bill was unconstitutional; he was publicly saying that a bill was unjust, imprudent, harmful to ordinary people, and favorable to privilege — and therefore he would veto it even though Congress passed it. This was the historical conversion of the veto from a constitutional tool to a policy weapon.

The Bank Veto Message of July 10, 1832, was the canonical text of this transformation. Jackson acknowledged the Bank's convenience to the government and usefulness to the people in many respects, then asserted that parts of its powers and privileges violated the Constitution, subverted state rights, and endangered the liberties of the people. He described the Bank as a government-favored monopoly with exclusive market privileges, argued that recharter would deliver millions more to existing stockholders including substantial foreign holders, and positioned the Bank explicitly as representing the richest class against ordinary people. This argument far exceeded the domain of legal technicality — it placed the president directly in the position of the people against privilege.

What Jackson built was a new presidential rhetoric and practice. The president would no longer passively await constitutionally acceptable legislation from Congress but would assert — as the direct representative of the entire nation — a claim to judge the direction of policy itself. He converted the executive from chief administrative manager to popular tribune, reversing the long-standing executive deference to legislative primacy. The new fuel he found was more powerful than precedent or gravitational pull from institutional weight, and harder to discipline. By claiming to represent the whole people against a Congress of local representatives, the president had acquired a discursive leverage point above the legislature. Once that leverage point was built, it was never dismantled. Every subsequent president who sought to expand executive authority would return to it, claiming to represent the people's general will against institutions serving partial interests.

6. The Bank War and the Nullification Crisis

The clearest way to see why Jacksonianism resists ideological categorization is to place the Bank War and the Nullification Crisis side by side. Clay and Bank President Nicholas Biddle triggered the Bank War by pushing for recharter in 1832 rather than 1836, hoping to force Jackson to either sign and alienate anti-Bank voters or veto and alienate business and moderate interests. Jackson vetoed, packaged the veto as striking at privilege and corruption, and won re-election. In the second phase, federal deposits were withdrawn from the Bank and redistributed to state banks — the so-called pet banks. By late 1833 most deposits had been moved; Biddle contracted credit sharply in response, attempting to force economic pain sufficient to make Jackson relent. The contraction succeeded in generating a brief panic but not in reversing Jackson's policy. The Senate formally censured Jackson in 1834 for claiming executive authority over the Treasury that the censure resolution said belonged to Congress.

Two interpretations of the Bank War's consequences are well established and should be placed alongside each other. The first is the anti-privilege populist reading: Jackson demolished an institution combining semi-official and private privilege in ways deeply entangled with political-business networks, serving the financial aristocracy at the expense of ordinary white voters. The second is the institutional-stability financial reading: whatever the Bank's political problems, it partially performed the functions of a modern central bank, and its destruction left the United States with a more fragmented, state-bank-dependent financial system that contributed to subsequent panics and instability.

The Nullification Crisis showed the other face — Jackson as strong-Union federalist — making it impossible to reduce Jacksonianism to any single ideological line. South Carolina, objecting to the tariffs of 1828 and 1832, claimed the right to declare federal tariff law void within its borders. Jackson's Nullification Proclamation of December 10, 1832, systematically dismantled the constitutional basis of state nullification. He declared the South Carolina ordinance subversive of the Constitution and warned that its logic, if carried out, would quickly leave the United States without national existence. He denied that states possessed the right to nullify or secede. The crisis was defused through two simultaneous measures: the Force Bill, authorizing the president to use military force to ensure collection of the tariff, and Clay's Compromise Tariff, gradually reducing rates. The same president denounced as King Andrew for his assault on the Bank was simultaneously asserting federal military supremacy over state resistance to federal law.

Jacksonianism's consistent thread was not small government and not state rights. It was making the president the pivot of national direction and execution — deploying anti-privilege language in the Bank context and strong-union language in the tariff context, but in both cases positioning the presidency as the nation's ultimate authority. Understanding this prevents being confused by the apparently contradictory positions.

7. The Second Party System and King Andrew

Jackson's deepest institutional legacy was a new party system. His supporters coalesced into what became the modern Democratic Party's predecessor; his opponents, through the mid-1830s, organized as the Whig Party. In eight years, Jackson converted a loosely personal coalition into a disciplined, nationally organized party machine — and his controversial methods simultaneously forced into existence an organized opposition.

The Whigs emerged not only from policy disagreement but from structural recoil against the style of presidential rule. The Bank War, the removal of deposits, the normalization of the veto, partisan control of executive appointments, and Jackson's personal political intensity led opponents to portray him as King Andrew — a president using popular mandate to burst through constitutional constraints. For the Whigs, the problem was not just any single policy but whether the presidency was consuming the congressional-centered constitutional order.

Placed within this series' framework, the Whig Party's emergence was one more activation of the familiar rhythm. Every major expansion of executive power triggered a counterforce. Jefferson's expansion ran into Marshall's court. Jackson's elevation of the presidency to unprecedented heights immediately generated a party dedicated to constraining it. The Whigs were not an accident — they were the construct's self-correction in response to Jacksonian expansion. The corrective force this time did not come from another branch but from a newly organized opposition party seeking, on the electoral battlefield, to pull the elevated presidency back down.

8. Conclusion

Placing the twelve years of John Quincy Adams and Jackson back in this series, the most important thing to carry forward is the judgment that runs through the whole essay: inclusion and exclusion at the same time.

This construct during this period brought more white men into the political community, built the machine for containing partisan strife more elaborately than ever before, and simultaneously expelled southeastern indigenous nations and pushed free Black men further out of the electorate in state after state. Democratic expansion was two-sided: the same gesture that brought some people in was more thoroughly pushing others out. That is the deepest remainder's form in the Jacksonian era — not first democracy and then exclusion, but the boundaries of democracy themselves being drawn through exclusion.

At the same time, presidential power reached its first great peak — powered now by a new fuel, the direct mandate of popular sovereignty. Jackson converted the veto from a constitutional to a policy instrument; converted the president from executive manager to tribune of the people. But this power could not be fitted into any single ideological framework, because he was anti-privilege on the Bank and strongly unionist on nullification, both in service of the same goal: making the presidency the pivot of the nation. Once the leverage point of direct popular mandate was built into presidential practice, it was never dismantled. Every subsequent president seeking to expand executive authority would return to it.

What follows is the era in which this newly formed Second Party System begins to run. A severe economic panic will immediately test the financial legacy Jackson left. And the geographic fault line first cut in Missouri, re-politicized with every new territorial acquisition, will be cut deeper and deeper along the path of annexation and expansion. That is the story from Van Buren to the eve of the Civil War.
