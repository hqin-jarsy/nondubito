第十篇：约翰逊与重建——谁来定义这个共同体

Essay 10: Johnson and Reconstruction — Who Defines the Community

Han Qin (秦汉)

一、余项刚进核心，又立即被重新约束

上一篇结尾说过，林肯刚刚把那句"人人平等"重新认领进国家的核心，那场重写就在他遇刺之后，落进了一个对它充满敌意的继任者手里。从1865年到1868年，这套构经历的，正是那笔刚刚被认领的债，如何在还没来得及偿还时，就立刻遭到反向的拉扯。

要抓住约翰逊这几年的结构意义，得先放下那种把它写成南方如何复原的单线叙事。这几年真正的核心，不是南方怎样恢复，而是废奴之后，谁能成为这个国家政治共同体的成员——这个问题，突然从一个长期被推到边缘的余项，变成了一个必须在宪法层面正面回答的核心问题。黑人法典，自由民局，民权法案，第十四修正案，1867年的重建法案，以及1868年的弹劾案，看上去是六件事，其实是同一个结构冲突的连续展开。

用这个系列的话来说，这正是那个最深的余项刚刚进入核心，又立即被重新约束的过程。上一篇里，这套构用战争和宪法，第一次正面认领了那句被推迟几代人的承诺，把奴隶制这种身份本身废除了。但认领一笔债，和还清一笔债，是两回事。这一篇要追的，就是在还债的第一步刚迈出时，旧的秩序如何立刻反扑，试图把那个刚被提到中心的余项，重新压回边缘。而这场反扑与反反扑，归根到底争的是同一件事：谁有权定义这个共同体究竟是谁。

二、一个被战争联盟推上来的人

要理解这场对撞，得先理解约翰逊这个人站在什么位置上。他出身贫寒，几乎没有受过正规教育，早年以裁缝为业。1864年，林肯为了平衡选票、争取那些支持战争的民主党人，以"国民联盟"而不是单纯共和党的名义竞选连任，选中了约翰逊做他的副总统搭档，当时约翰逊被看作全国最显眼的战争民主党人之一。换句话说，他坐上总统这个位置，并不是因为他和共和党的重建纲领天然一致，而是因为战时联盟政治的安排。

约翰逊政治上的复杂，在于他反对奴隶制，主要并不是出于黑人平等的理念，而是高度夹杂着对种植园贵族的敌意，以及对非奴隶主白人利益的强调。在他的逻辑里，废除奴隶制并不自动推出黑人应该获得政治平等，既有的选举权原本属于白人男子，而黑人一旦立刻进入选举政治，反而会引发种族战争。这就是他那种在阶级上反种植园、却绝不接受种族平权的核心结构。

他的种族观，在公开的文件里也很清楚。否决民权法案时，他抱怨这部法偏袒黑人。1867年的国情咨文里，他又把国会的重建斥为可能使国家一半"被非洲化"的危险。现代主流史学之所以显著下调他的历史评价，关键并不只是他政治技巧的拙劣，而是他在奴隶制终结之后，仍然顽固地拒绝承认黑人的政治平等，是战后秩序应有的组成部分。

放回框架看，约翰逊的位置有一种结构性的反讽。这套构刚刚在巨大的牺牲之后，把那个最深的余项提到了核心，而它的领导权，却因为一颗子弹的偶然，落到了一个恰好体现着相反冲动的人手里。约翰逊代表的，正是那个旧的白人共和国，在奴隶制被废除之后，试图延续自身支配的努力。他不是一个孤立的坏人，而是一股真实而强大的结构性力量的化身，那股力量想做的，就是把那个刚刚被提到中心的余项，重新按回它原来的位置。

三、总统重建——把定义权先攥在手里

林肯遇刺后，第三十八届国会已经休会，而第三十九届国会要到1865年12月才开会。正是在这几个月的制度空档里，约翰逊几乎单方面地推行了所谓总统重建。1865年5月29日，他一方面发布大赦公告，向大多数参与叛乱的人提供赦免并恢复他们的财产权，另一方面任命临时州长，授权各州召集忠诚的选民去制定新宪法，恢复州政府。以他给北卡罗来纳的命令为例，他明确规定，只有那些符合1861年以前本州选民资格、并宣誓效忠的人，才能参加新的政治秩序——这个安排实际上就把新近获得自由的黑人，排除在了重建的政治之外。

这套方案的结构关键，并不在于宽大本身，而在于它把"谁有权定义战后政治共同体"这个问题，先交给了总统和南方的白人精英去处理，而不是交给那个由宪法选举产生、握有接纳议员之权和修宪之权的国会。国会的共和党人之所以愤怒，不仅因为他宽赦了前邦联者，更因为南方的新政府在几乎不承认黑人权利的条件下，就想恢复完整的代表权，甚至把许多前邦联的精英重新送回国家政治的中心。

所以第三十九届国会一开会，就拒绝让南方各州的代表就座，并成立了重建联合委员会，把约翰逊抢先攥住的那个定义权，重新收回来。这说明，这套构的规则里，总会有一些没被预先安排好的缝隙，而在危机的时刻，这些缝隙就可能被用来抢占那个本该由更具代表性的机构来行使的权力。

四、黑人法典——把自由压回旧制度

总统重建最直接也最具爆炸性的后果之一，就是黑人法典。密西西比的法典，一面承认黑人可以起诉、可以持有个人财产，另一面却禁止他们在城镇以外租地，要求每一个自由黑人都必须有合法的住所或工作并持有书面凭证，劳动合同超过一个月就必须书面化，而劳动者若在期限届满前离开，则要没收当年已经挣得的工资，并可以由官员逮捕、送回原来的雇主。更严厉的是学徒和流浪条款：法院可以把无人抚养的黑人未成年人强行变成学徒，并优先交给他们从前的主人，而没有合法工作的成年人则可以被认定为流浪者，处以罚款和监禁。

南卡罗来纳的法典同样露骨。它把签了劳动契约的有色人直接称为"仆役"，把雇主称为"主人"，农场的劳动时间从日出到日落，法官可以因为所谓的不服从、懒惰、不敬，对仆役施加体罚或者罚款，并立即把他送回工作岗位。黑人若想以工匠、技师、店主这样的身份独立谋生，就必须向地区法官申请一年期的执照并缴纳高额的年费。

这件事在框架里的含义极深。上一篇说过，第十三修正案把奴隶制这种身份本身废除了，这是这套构正面认领那个最深余项的第一步。但黑人法典立刻就显示出，废除奴隶制，并没有把那个余项闭合掉。旧秩序没法再把人当作财产来占有，于是它换了一套办法：用劳动合同、用流浪罪、用职业执照，把自由重新压缩成一种几乎没有自由的状态。这正是前面反复说过的那个道理：那个余项的两面性使它无法被真正吸收，你废除了它的一种形态，它立刻就会在另一种形态里重新冒出来。

五、自由民局——第一次介入从奴隶到公民的过渡

面对这种再收缩，联邦手里主要的修正工具是自由民局。1865年3月3日创设这个局时，国会赋予了它分发粮食、衣物、医疗，开办学校，监督劳动合同，以及管理没收或废弃土地的职能。它既提供救济，也组织学校，监督合同，处理土地问题，帮助黑人建立学校，使婚姻合法化，寻找失散的家人，并提供法律和医疗的援助。

但自由民局从一开始，就受到两种限制：一是资源和执行力的有限，二是总统的敌意。约翰逊在1866年2月否决了第一版的延期法案，国会修改之后再次送审，他在7月又一次否决，但这一次，两院成功地推翻了否决，1866年的法案得以生效，把这个机构的工作又延长了两年。

所以自由民局，既是联邦第一次大规模地介入从奴隶到公民这个过渡的装置，也是它能力边界的证明。它确实推动了教育、救济、婚姻和法律援助，但它既没有足够的军力，也没有完成土地的再分配。把它放回框架，自由民局是这套构第一次尝试去真正偿还那个余项——而不只是承认它——去建立那些能让黑人成为完整公民的制度。但它的限度，恰恰说明了偿还这笔债有多难。承认一个余项，只需要一纸宪法，而真正去偿还它，却需要这套构当时还远远没有建立起来的国家能力。

六、民权法案与第十四修正案——把平等写进宪法核心

国会收回定义权的努力，首先体现在立法上。1866年的民权法案宣布，凡是出生在美国、且不受外国权力支配的人，排除不纳税的印第安人，都是美国公民，并规定这些公民无论种族与肤色，都享有和白人相同的订约、起诉、作证、继承、购买、租赁、持有和转让财产的权利，以及同等地受法律保护其人身和财产的权利。约翰逊否决了这部法律，在否决文里抱怨它偏袒黑人。参议院于1866年4月6日以三十三比十五推翻了否决，众议院在4月9日以一百二十二比四十一跟进。这是战后国会与总统对抗的一道分水岭。

但国会很快意识到，仅仅靠普通法并不足以稳固这个成果，于是转向了修宪。第十四修正案必须分款来看。第一款确立了出生公民权，同时禁止各州剥夺联邦公民的特权或豁免，禁止未经正当程序剥夺任何人的生命、自由和财产，并要求各州给予平等的保护。第二款按全体人口重新计算众议院的代表名额，但若某个州拒绝给予成年男性公民选举权，它的代表名额就要按比例减少——这说明它还没有直接确立普遍的黑人男性选举权。第三款禁止那些曾宣誓拥护宪法、后来却参与叛乱的人担任联邦或州的公职。第四款确认联邦的公债有效，同时否认邦联的债务。

第十四修正案的历史意义至少有三层。第一，它直接推翻了德雷德·斯科特案关于黑人不能成为联邦公民的核心判断——第七篇里说过，最高法院曾经试图用那个判决把对黑人的排除写进宪法的解释，而现在，这套构掉过头来，用一条宪法修正案把那个判决彻底推翻了。第二，它把平等的公民权从政治的辩论推进到了宪法的核心。第三，它后来成为现代美国宪法里最富生命力的条文之一，平等保护和正当程序的现代判例史几乎都绕不开它。

但这里有一处必须按分寸点出来。第十四修正案在重新划定选举权的边界时，第二款的文字第一次把"男性"这个词明确写进了宪法。也就是说，就在这套构沿着种族这条轴激烈地争夺谁能投票的同时，性别这条轴上的排除，不但没有被触动，反而被更明确地写进了根本大法。这两条轴，是同一个未兑现承诺的不同侧面。

七、从中期选举到激进重建——国会收回定义权

1866年是重建从总统主导转为国会主导的决定性一年。约翰逊为了阻止第十四修正案和国会的方案，在北方展开了所谓的环行拉票，到多个州去演讲，辩护他对前邦联州的宽松政策，攻击国会的对手，并批评第十四修正案。但这次巡回极不成功，他在克利夫兰的讲话中当众争吵，进一步削弱了他的政治信誉。

1866年的选举结果几乎终结了总统重建。共和党在参众两院都赢得了压倒性的多数，足以轻易地推翻约翰逊的否决。这不是一般意义上的中期震荡，而是一次全民对"谁负责定义重建"这个问题的再授权。这套构纠正总统的过度，靠的不是某个分支单方面的强力，而是它一直保留着的那个开放的机制——选举。

在这个政治背景下，国会于1867年通过了重建法案系列。第一部法案宣布，除田纳西之外，前邦联各州没有合法的州政府，把它们划为五个军政区，受美国的军事权力管辖。各州若要恢复在国会里的代表权，就必须由二十一岁以上的男性公民、不论种族、肤色或先前的身份，选出制宪会议，州宪法必须保障这些人的选举权，而且州议会必须批准第十四修正案。1867年的重建立法，把黑人从一个社会劳动力的问题，明确地提升为重建合法性的构成因素。

八、弹劾——当两个分支对共同体是谁根本分歧

约翰逊的弹劾案，是总统权力与国会制衡极端对撞的一个样本。1867年3月，国会越过总统的否决，通过了任职法，限制总统未经参议院同意就撤换高级行政官员的能力。约翰逊在1867年8月国会休会时，要求战争部长斯坦顿辞职，遭拒之后将他停职，1868年1月参议院拒绝认可这一安排，斯坦顿复职，2月21日约翰逊再次下令撤换斯坦顿。这一步成了直接的触发点，2月24日众议院以一百二十六比四十七表决弹劾约翰逊，随后形成了十一项弹劾条款。参议院的审判于1868年3月5日开始，在其中三项关键条款上，表决都停在了三十五比十九，仅差一票，没有达到定罪所需的三分之二。

这次未定罪的宪法意义，史学界长期存在两种并置的解释。第一种解释认为，这场审判阻止了国会把弹劾变成一件政策不合即可罢免的工具，一批参议员的关键反水，是为了维护总统这个职位和权力分立的制度平衡。第二种解释则认为，把它仅仅看作保护总统职位，会遮蔽重建的现实——因为坐在被告席上的，不是一个抽象的总统，而是一个持续阻挠黑人公民权、破坏国会重建、为南方白人的反动秩序争取时间的总统。这两种解释，最好并置，而不必替读者择一。

但这次弹劾在框架上真正照亮的，是一件更深的事。它确立了什么和没确立什么：它没有确立总统不可因为阻挠战后重建而被追究，也没有确立任职法本身的宪法正当性是毫无疑问的，它真正确立的是，哪怕政治冲突激烈到了极点，参议院仍然可能把重罪与轻罪的标准，同纯粹的政策分歧区分开来。而与此同时，它也证明了另一件事：一旦总统和国会对"国家共同体究竟是谁"这个最根本的问题发生根本的分歧，宪法的文本本身并不会自动给出一个和平的答案，而是会把冲突推到接近制度极限的位置。

九、结语

把约翰逊与重建这几年放回这个系列，最值得记住的，是那个贯穿全篇的判断：那个最深的余项刚刚进入核心，就立即遭到了反向的约束。

上一篇里，这套构用战争和宪法，第一次正面认领了那句被推迟几代人的承诺。而这一篇显示的是，认领之后的第一步，立刻就陷入了一场关于谁来定义这个共同体的激烈争夺。一边是约翰逊和他所代表的旧白人秩序，想用总统重建、黑人法典，把那个刚被提到中心的余项重新压回边缘。另一边是国会，用拒绝接纳南方议员、推翻否决、民权法案、第十四修正案，以及1867年的重建法案，一步步把黑人的平等公民权写进了重建的门槛。黑人法典证明，废除奴隶制并没有闭合那个余项，它只是换了一副面孔重新涌现。而国会的反击则显示，这套构纠正总统过度的方式，仍然是它一贯的那一套——靠选举的公开裁决，靠制衡的重新发力，把被一方抢走的定义权重新夺回来。

用这个系列的语言来精确地说，这几年发生的，是认领和约束的同时进行。这套构一面把那个余项往核心里写得更深，一面又被旧秩序拼命地往回拽。这两个方向同时在用力，谁也没能彻底压倒谁。这恰恰是不可闭合的又一种样子：那个余项既没有被旧秩序成功地重新压回边缘，也没有被新秩序彻底地兑现成平等。它停在了一个两边都没法把它收尾的僵局里，而这个僵局，就以一场弹劾、一次次否决与反否决的形式，持续地公开发展着。

接下来登场的，是这场争夺如何从华盛顿的庙堂，蔓延回南方的土地。一个战争英雄将入主白宫，试图用联邦的武力去保护刚刚获得选举权的黑人，去对抗那些用恐怖手段卷土重来的暴力组织。但一场经济恐慌，一场有争议的大选，以及北方逐渐耗尽的意志，将合在一起，让这场重建在一纸交易里悄然终结。那是格兰特到海斯的故事，是重写如何开始被退却的故事。

I. The Residue Pushed to Center, Then Immediately Pulled Back

Lincoln had just recommitted the nation to the promise of equality and written abolition into the Constitution. Five days after Lee's surrender, a bullet transferred the presidency to a man who embodied the opposing impulse. The years from 1865 to 1868 are the story of a newly acknowledged debt meeting immediate resistance: who has the right to define the membership of the political community?

The standard narrative frames these years as "Southern restoration" versus "Radical Reconstruction." The more illuminating frame is this: the abolition of slavery created a question the constitutional order had never had to answer directly — who belongs to the political community as a full member? This question, long managed by being deferred to the margins, now had to be answered at the constitutional level. The Black Codes, the Freedmen's Bureau, the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the Fourteenth Amendment, the Reconstruction Acts, and the impeachment trial are not six separate events; they are one extended structural conflict about a single issue: who defines the community, and who gets to be in it?

In the series' language: the deepest residue had just entered the core, and the old order's immediate response was to push it back out. Acknowledging a debt and paying a debt are different things. This essay traces what happened in the first years after the acknowledgment.

II. A Man Placed by War Coalition Politics

Andrew Johnson's political position requires understanding before assessing his choices. Born poor, nearly without formal education, a tailor by trade, he had risen through Tennessee politics as a Democrat who opposed secession — not out of antislavery conviction but because he identified with non-slaveholding white men against the planter aristocracy. In 1864, Lincoln ran on a National Union ticket rather than a purely Republican one, selecting Johnson as a running mate precisely because Johnson was the most prominent War Democrat in the country. Johnson became president not because he shared the Republican reconstruction program but because wartime coalition politics placed him on the ticket.

Johnson's opposition to slavery was real but consistently entangled with racial hierarchy. He understood emancipation as a class matter — the overthrow of the planter elite — rather than as a rights matter. In his logic, abolishing slavery did not entail Black political equality. Existing voting rights belonged to white men; immediate Black political participation would trigger race war. His racial views appear directly in the record: in vetoing the Civil Rights Act he complained it discriminated against white people; his 1867 annual message denounced congressional Reconstruction as risking the "Africanization" of half the country. Contemporary historians who rank him at or near the bottom of the presidential rankings do so primarily not because he was politically inept — though he was — but because he refused after emancipation to acknowledge Black political equality as a proper component of the postwar order.

The structural irony is sharp. The constitutional order had just, at enormous cost, moved its deepest residue to the center. A bullet made the new president someone who embodied the structural force most committed to pushing that residue back to the margins. Johnson was not an isolated bad actor; he represented a real and powerful social force — the old white republic seeking to perpetuate its dominance after the legal form of slavery had been destroyed. The accident was that this force happened to hold the presidency.

III. Presidential Reconstruction: Seizing the Power to Define

Between Lincoln's death and the convening of the Thirty-Ninth Congress in December 1865, Johnson operated through a constitutional gap. The Thirty-Eighth Congress had adjourned; the incoming Congress would not meet for months. In that interval, he effectively implemented Presidential Reconstruction unilaterally: issuing amnesty proclamations that restored property rights to most former Confederate participants, appointing provisional governors, and authorizing states to reconstitute their governments. His instructions for North Carolina, for example, limited participation in the new political order to those who had met the state's 1861 voter qualifications and taken a loyalty oath — a requirement that excluded the newly freed Black population entirely.

The structural key is not the leniency itself but the question of who held the definitional power. Johnson positioned the president and Southern white elites as the primary definers of postwar political community, rather than the constitutionally elected body with the power to seat members and amend the Constitution. Congressional Republicans were angry not only at the pardons but at the result: Southern states were reconstituting governments that denied Black rights almost entirely, would restore full representation, and were sending former Confederate leaders back to Washington. When the Thirty-Ninth Congress convened, it refused to seat Southern representatives and formed the Joint Committee on Reconstruction — taking back the definitional power Johnson had seized in the interim.

This episode illustrates a recurring feature of the constitutional order: its rules always leave some gaps, and in crisis moments those gaps can be exploited to pre-empt powers that properly belong to more representative institutions. The gap here was the months-long interval between Lincoln's death and a Congress capable of acting. Congress's response — using its hard constitutional powers over membership and amendment to reclaim the initiative — showed the order's self-correcting capacity, but only after significant damage had been done.

IV. The Black Codes: Compressing Freedom Back to Servitude

The most immediate and explosive consequence of Presidential Reconstruction was the enactment of Black Codes throughout the former Confederate states. Mississippi's code acknowledged that Black people could sue and hold personal property, then restricted them from renting land outside towns, required all free Black people to have documented legal employment or residence, made labor contracts of more than a month enforceable by forfeiture of wages earned and re-arrest by officials, and empowered courts to apprentice unsupported Black children — with preference given to their former masters — and to arrest adults without regular employment as vagrants. South Carolina's code called contracted Black workers "servants" and employers "masters," permitted corporal punishment for disobedience, required licenses and fees for independent employment as artisans or tradespeople, and defined vagrancy so broadly as to capture nearly any Black person not in a plantation labor relation.

The structural meaning of the Black Codes is this: the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery as a legal institution; the Codes immediately demonstrated that abolishing the institution had not resolved the underlying residue. The old order could no longer own people as property — that specific form had been foreclosed. But it could replicate the functional content of that ownership through contract law, vagrancy statutes, and licensing requirements. A person was nominally free and actually compelled. The residue had not been absorbed; it had changed form. The two-sidedness of the residue meant that closing off one path of domination caused the dominating force to find another. You cannot resolve an irresolvable residue by attacking any single institutional expression of it; the residue will reconstitute itself in a new form as long as its underlying source — the refusal to grant full political membership — remains intact.

V. The Freedmen's Bureau: The Gap Between Acknowledgment and Payment

The primary federal tool for managing the transition from slavery to citizenship was the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, established March 3, 1865. The Bureau distributed food, clothing, and medical care; established schools; supervised labor contracts; and handled land issues, legal disputes, and family reunification for formerly enslaved people. Its work established hundreds of schools that became the foundation of Black educational institutions in the South.

But the Bureau operated under two permanent constraints: limited resources and enforcement capacity, and presidential hostility. Johnson vetoed the first extension bill in February 1866, calling it unnecessary. Congress revised and resubmitted; Johnson vetoed again in July. Both chambers overrode the second veto, and the Bureau continued operating for two more years. The fight over the Bureau was a core element of the larger Reconstruction conflict.

The Freedmen's Bureau's historical significance in this series' framework is this: it was the constitutional order's first large-scale attempt not merely to acknowledge the deepest residue but to begin paying the debt — building the institutions that would allow formerly enslaved people to function as actual citizens. Its limits were equally revealing. Without land redistribution, without sufficient military force to protect against violence, and without presidential support, the Bureau could stabilize the transition but not complete it. Acknowledging a constitutional debt requires only text; paying that debt requires the state capacity to enforce it on the ground. The gap between the acknowledgment (Thirteenth Amendment) and the payment (actual equal citizenship in southern communities) was vast, and the Bureau's limits made that gap visible. The constitutional order had run far ahead of its enforcement capacity.

VI. The Civil Rights Act and the Fourteenth Amendment

Congress moved to secure the gains through legislation and then constitutional amendment. The Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared that all persons born in the United States and not subject to foreign powers — excluding non-taxed Indians — were citizens, and that citizens of every race and color were entitled to make and enforce contracts, sue, give evidence, inherit, and purchase, lease, and hold property, with full and equal benefit of all laws for security of person and property. Johnson vetoed it, complaining it favored Black over white Americans. The Senate overrode 33 to 15; the House 122 to 41. This was a watershed: Congress had publicly demonstrated, on a major piece of legislation, that the president was not the final arbiter of Reconstruction's direction.

Recognizing that ordinary legislation could be repealed by a future Congress or challenged in court, Congress turned to constitutional amendment. The Fourteenth Amendment's provisions must be read section by section. Section 1 established birthright citizenship, prohibited states from abridging the privileges or immunities of citizens, prohibited deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process, and required equal protection of the laws. Section 2 recalculated congressional representation based on total population but reduced representation proportionally for states that denied adult male citizens the right to vote — a transitional mechanism, not a direct mandate for Black male suffrage. Section 3 disqualified from federal and state office those who had violated an oath to the Constitution by participating in rebellion. Section 4 affirmed the federal debt and repudiated Confederate debts and claims for emancipated slaves.

The Fourteenth Amendment's historical significance operates on multiple levels. It directly overturned Dred Scott's holding that Black people could not be federal citizens — a holding this series has tracked as an attempt to use judicial authority to permanently foreclose the deepest residue. The Constitution now answered that attempt with explicit inclusion. It placed equal citizenship and due process at the constitutional core rather than leaving them dependent on ordinary legislation. And its equal protection clause became, in later decades, the most generative provision in the American constitutional system.

One feature must be noted without softening. Section 2, in apportioning the political cost of denying suffrage, introduced the word "male" explicitly into the Constitution for the first time. In the midst of the fiercest constitutional struggle over racial inclusion, the axis of sex-based exclusion was not merely left unaddressed — it was more explicitly encoded into the fundamental law. The Seneca Falls Declaration of 1848 had named this gap; the Fourteenth Amendment deepened it. Two axes of the same unfulfilled promise were moving in opposite directions simultaneously.

VII. The 1866 Elections and Radical Reconstruction

The 1866 midterm elections were the pivotal moment of Reconstruction's political history. Johnson campaigned against the Fourteenth Amendment and congressional leadership, undertaking a "Swing Around the Circle" tour of northern states that went badly — he was drawn into undignified public arguments with hecklers in Cleveland and other cities. Republicans won veto-proof supermajorities in both chambers.

These results were not ordinary midterm fluctuations. They were a re-authorization by the electorate of congressional rather than presidential leadership on the central question of Reconstruction. In this series' terms: the constitutional order's mechanism for correcting presidential overreach — competitive elections — functioned exactly as designed. Johnson had used the presidential succession gap to pre-empt Congress's definitional role. The electorate returned a Congress capable of reclaiming it. The correction happened not through a coup or administrative reorganization but through the open verdict of voters.

The Reconstruction Acts of 1867 followed. They declared the ex-Confederate states (except Tennessee, which had already ratified the Fourteenth Amendment) to lack lawful governments, divided them into five military districts, and established the conditions for restored representation: male citizens over 21, regardless of race or prior condition, would elect constitutional conventions; new state constitutions must guarantee this suffrage; and state legislatures must ratify the Fourteenth Amendment. The Reconstruction Acts explicitly elevated Black men from a labor-force management problem to a constitutive element of reconstruction legitimacy. Political membership and constitutional restoration were now linked.

VIII. Impeachment: When the Branches Fundamentally Disagree on Who Belongs

The impeachment of Andrew Johnson was the extreme institutional expression of the conflict over Reconstruction's direction. In March 1867, Congress overrode Johnson's veto of the Tenure of Office Act, which restricted the president's ability to remove high executive officers without Senate consent. Johnson suspended Secretary of War Stanton in August 1867 during a congressional recess; the Senate refused to concur in January 1868, restoring Stanton; Johnson dismissed Stanton again on February 21, 1868. The House voted to impeach 126 to 47 on February 24, producing eleven articles. The Senate trial ran from March through May 1868, and three critical votes each ended 35 to 19 — one vote short of the two-thirds required for conviction.

Historians have offered two interpretations of the acquittal that are best held simultaneously rather than as alternatives. The first holds that the trial prevented Congress from converting impeachment into a tool for removing presidents on policy grounds, that the senators who voted against conviction were protecting the separation of powers and the independence of the executive, and that this preserved a constitutional norm of value beyond the immediate controversy. The second holds that focusing on the procedural principle obscures the substantive reality: the president acquitted was not an abstract official but one who had systematically obstructed Black citizenship, enabled the Black Codes, undermined congressional Reconstruction, and bought time for the old Southern order to reconstitute itself. Both interpretations capture something true.

What the impeachment most clearly illuminates for this series is the constitutional order's limits when its two branches fundamentally disagree on the most basic political question — who belongs to the political community. When that question is what divides president and Congress, the constitutional text does not automatically produce a peaceful answer. It pushes the conflict to the institutional edge, as far as the rules permit, without resolving the underlying disagreement. The structure can provide a stage for the conflict to play out without becoming open violence; it cannot, by its procedural mechanism alone, settle the substantive question of membership. The deepest residue, even after it had been written into constitutional text, remained unresolved in the form of the question: what does membership actually mean in practice?

IX. Conclusion

The years of Johnson and Reconstruction illustrate a specific kind of constitutional dynamics: acknowledgment followed immediately by pushback. The Thirteenth Amendment acknowledged the debt; the Black Codes demonstrated that acknowledging it and paying it were entirely different things. The Fourteenth Amendment pushed the acknowledgment deeper into constitutional text; Johnson's vetoes and Presidential Reconstruction attempted to prevent the acknowledgment from having operational consequences.

What the constitutional order demonstrated in these years was its capacity for self-correction through elections — not a guaranteed outcome but a structural possibility. Congress's Reconstruction legislation went as far as the veto-proof majority could push it. The electorate in 1866 produced that veto-proof majority. The order corrected its presidential imbalance through its open mechanisms, not through a coup. That is the non-closure dynamic visible even in this most contentious period.

What the Black Codes demonstrated was the residue's form-changing persistence. Destroy slavery as an institution and the underlying claim — that Black people do not belong to the political community as equals — reconstitutes itself in a new form. The Freedmen's Bureau's limits revealed the gap between constitutional acknowledgment and actual state capacity to pay the debt on the ground. The Fourteenth Amendment's Section 2 showed that even the amendment addressing racial exclusion simultaneously deepened sex-based exclusion. The impeachment trial showed that when the two branches hold fundamentally incompatible views on membership, the constitutional structure cannot resolve the disagreement — it can only provide a venue where the disagreement plays out to its procedural limit.

The rewriting was real. From the Thirteenth to the Fourteenth Amendment, the constitutional order moved the equality promise from deferred margin to constitutional core. But the gap between the text and the ground remained enormous, and the forces opposing the rewriting were not defeated — they were legislatively constrained. What followed was the test of whether those constraints could be enforced without the political will that had produced them, as casualties mounted, northern attention wavered, and the rewriting's opponents waited for the moment when the federal commitment would run out.
