第四篇：两条脊柱细说

Essay 4: Two Spines in Detail

Han Qin (秦汉)

前两篇画出了整条谱系的轮廓，也区分了权利的基础根据与请求权所产生的义务。这一篇要走进两条脊柱各自的内部：一项基础权利有多厚、边界画在哪里；一项承认性权利怎样获得制度效力、现实支撑与道德正当性，又会以哪些方式失灵。

一、基础权利的厚度

基础权利不是一条细细的底线，是一整层有厚度的东西——从最基础的生命、身体层面，一路延伸到更高的自我表达、自我导向的能力。一样东西要不要算进这一层，需要同时通过两项测试。

第一项测试：它必须保护主体存在、认知或展开自身的一个不可替代维度，而不只是指向某一种偶然的资源或工具。食物、教育与金钱是实现这些维度的条件；具体给付方式可以依赖制度，但当最低限度的营养、照护或教育直接关系到生命与能力形成时，它们也可以成为积极请求权的内容。第二项测试：剥夺这个维度或其不可替代条件，必须直接损害主体维持、形成或执行自身之法的能力。

关键的一点是：这层厚度谈的是不可替代的站位，不是眼下的功能状态。一个人因为疾病或伤残失去某些身体机能，站位并不随之减少；变化的是当下亲自行使能力的范围，以及它需要怎样的支持。站位保持完整，行使方式可以变化。

二、两种承认，两种边界

有两种经常被混在一起的"承认"，需要分清楚。一种是构成性的：它创造投票资格、任职身份等具体制度位置。另一种是确认性的：它承认一项不依赖这次制度决定才有道德根据的权利，例如废除奴隶制不是发明不被奴役的道德主张，而是停止侵犯并为它建立法律保护。基础权利的根据不需要构成性承认，但它的实现仍可能需要积极制度与他人的义务。

更准确的测试不是把所有其他人拿走——权利主张本来就会在关系中具体化——而是把特定制度拿走：如果支撑这项主张的道德理由仍然存在，它有基础层的根据；如果连它的意义都随投票程序、公司法或资格规则一起消失，它就是制度构成的承认性位置。这也画出两种边界："内在边界"说明哪些维度不能被外部用途重新定义；"相遇边界"则处理多个主体共享空间时，彼此的请求、自由与义务应怎样协调。

三、相遇边界为什么永远可以被追问

因为每个人的厚层在共享的处境里都会和别人的厚层重叠，具体一次相遇里边界到底该画在哪儿，没法在抽象层面一次性定死——它必须始终保持可以被逐案追问的状态。

处理一场具体争议，大致有两种模式：一种类似法庭——当真正需要一个有约束力的裁决、需要可执行的补救或预防性约束时，启动一个带强制力的裁决程序；另一种更像对等的相互核验——一种去中心化的核查，没有裁判，参与各方依然把彼此当作主体来相互检验。选哪一种，取决于当下真正需要的是什么（一个有约束力的了结，还是一种持续的、双方仍然彼此承认的核验），不是哪一种"更高级"。

四、三项运行条件与一道正当性门槛

一项承认性权利要成为有效、可运作的制度位置，通常需要三项条件：可以调用的制度形式，周围社群形成的稳定预期，以及足以执行和救济的真实能力。三项条件回答的是它是否有效、是否能够兑现，还不能单独证明它是否正当。奴役、隔离或世袭特权也可能拥有规则、认可与强制力；因此还需要第四道门：这项制度位置不得侵犯基础权利，并且必须能够向所有受影响的主体给出可公开检验的理由。

五、四种失效与一种迁移

四种失效需要分开。全面撤回：构成法律位置的有效来源退出，位置不再存在。空心化：制度文本仍然有效，社会也预期它存在，但执行与救济消失，权利只剩纸面。失去正当性：制度形式和强制力仍在，却无法通过基础权利与公共理由的检验；它可能仍是有效法律，却不再是正当权利。局部失效：它只在某些地区、群体或程序里无法运作。另有一种并非失效的变化——持有人迁移或权利承继：位置保持不变，持有人依法更换。

举两个例子帮助分辨：一部劳工保护法律仍然有效，却普遍得不到执行和救济，这是空心化；一项歧视性制度即使仍被多数人接受、也能被强制执行，仍可能因为侵犯基础权利而失去正当性。前者是效力与执行脱节，后者是有效性与正当性分离，两者可以同时发生，却不是同一种失败。

六、跨在两条脊柱之间的权利

有些常见的权利横跨两条脊柱。财产权就是一个好例子：栖身之所、维生工具和随身物品背后，可能有生存、身体安全与基本控制的基础根据；但这并不意味着完整的财产权制度天然存在。所有权、继承顺位、公司股权和知识产权的具体边界，都需要法律构成。拿走一个人唯一的毯子所侵犯的，首先可能是生存与身体完整，而不只是抽象的所有权。

权力落差是否被受影响者表达、拒绝或公开登记，会影响一段关系如何被看见，却不是侵犯成立的前提。一个人越是被当作物品、越无法回应，权利语言越有必要继续适用：沉默、昏迷、恐惧或完全受控都不能让受害者退出权利范围。极端物化不是权利分析的边界，而是它最需要面对的情形。

结语

基础权利这条脊柱保护的是完整站位及其不可替代条件，具体边界必须在主体相遇时逐案说明；承认性权利则需要制度形式、稳定预期与执行能力才能有效运作，还必须通过基础权利与公共理由的正当性检验。下一篇要问：这些制度位置在被行使时，怎样与权力发生接触？

The previous two essays sketched the spectrum and distinguished the ground of a right from the duties generated by particular claims. This essay moves inside the two spines: how thick a basic right is and where its boundary falls; how a recognition-based right acquires institutional validity, practical force, and moral legitimacy; and how each can fail.

1. The Thickness of Basic Rights

A basic right is not a thin floor line — it is an entire layer with real thickness, running from the most basic level of life and body up to higher capacities like self-expression and self-direction. Whether something counts as part of this layer requires passing two tests together.

The first test is whether a claim protects an irreplaceable dimension of a subject's existence, cognition, or development rather than merely one contingent resource or tool. Food, education, and money are conditions for realizing those dimensions. Their specific delivery can be institution-dependent, but minimum nutrition, care, and education can become the content of positive claims when life and the formation of agency depend on them. The second test is whether depriving a subject of that dimension, or of an irreplaceable condition for it, directly damages the capacity to sustain, form, or carry out a law of its own.

The key point is that this thickness concerns irreplaceable standing, not present function. Someone who loses bodily capacities through illness or injury loses none of that standing. What changes is the current range of personal exercise and the support required. Standing remains intact; modes of exercise can change.

2. Two Kinds of Recognition, Two Kinds of Boundary

Two kinds of recognition are often conflated. Constitutive recognition creates a particular institutional position, such as eligibility to vote or hold office. Declaratory recognition acknowledges a right whose moral ground does not depend on that institutional decision. Abolition did not invent the moral claim against enslavement; it ended a violation and established legal protection. The ground of a basic right does not require constitutive recognition, though realizing it may still require institutions and duties in others.

The better test is not to remove every other person — rights become concrete in relations — but to remove the particular institution. If the moral reason for the claim remains, it has a basic ground. If its meaning disappears with an electoral procedure, corporate law, or an eligibility rule, it is institutionally constituted. This also yields two boundaries: an intrinsic boundary marking dimensions that cannot be redefined by external use, and an encounter boundary where the claims, liberties, and duties of several subjects must be coordinated.

3. Why the Encounter Boundary Can Always Be Questioned

Because each person's protected sphere meets those of others in shared situations, the exact boundary in a particular encounter cannot always be settled in the abstract. It must remain open to justification and review case by case.

Handling one live dispute generally takes one of two forms: something court-like — when a binding decision, an enforceable remedy, or preventive restraint is genuinely needed, a procedure with real authority to decide; or something closer to mutual, peer-level verification — a decentralized check with no referee, in which the parties involved still test whether each continues to recognize the others as subjects. Which one applies depends on what is actually needed — a binding resolution, or an ongoing mutual check that both sides still recognize each other — not on which mode ranks "higher."

4. Three Operational Conditions and a Legitimacy Gate

A recognition-based right generally needs three conditions to become a valid and operative institutional position: an invocable legal or social form, stable expectations in the surrounding community, and real capacity for enforcement and remedy. These conditions establish validity and efficacy; they do not by themselves establish legitimacy. Slavery, segregation, and hereditary privilege can also possess rules, acceptance, and coercive force. A fourth gate is therefore necessary: the position must be compatible with basic rights and capable of public justification to everyone affected.

5. Four Failures and One Transition

Four failures should be distinguished. Withdrawal occurs when the valid source constituting a legal position is removed. Hollowing-out occurs when the law remains valid and people expect it to apply, but enforcement and remedy disappear. Loss of legitimacy occurs when institutional form and coercive force remain but the position cannot survive the tests of basic rights and public justification; it may still be valid law without being a justified right. Local failure occurs when a right becomes unusable only in certain regions, groups, or procedures. A fifth change is not a failure at all: succession or transfer of the holder, in which the position continues under a new bearer.

Two examples make the distinction clear. A labor-protection statute may remain legally valid while enforcement and remedies disappear; that is hollowing-out. A discriminatory institution may remain widely accepted and vigorously enforced yet lose legitimacy because it violates basic rights. The first separates validity from efficacy; the second separates validity from legitimacy. They can coincide, but they are not the same failure.

6. Rights Straddling Both Spines

Some familiar rights span both spines. Property is a good example. Shelter, tools of subsistence, and personal possessions may be protected by basic claims to survival, bodily security, and minimal control. That does not make a complete system of property natural. Ownership, inheritance, corporate equity, and intellectual property all require legal construction. Taking someone's last blanket may violate survival and bodily integrity before it violates an abstract title.

Whether an affected person can express, refuse, or publicly register a power imbalance changes how the relation becomes visible; it is not a condition for violation. The more completely a person is treated as an object and deprived of response, the more necessary rights analysis becomes. Silence, unconsciousness, terror, and total control do not remove a victim from the domain of rights. Extreme objectification is not the boundary of rights language but one of its most urgent cases.

Conclusion

The basic-rights spine protects complete standing and its irreplaceable conditions, with concrete boundaries justified case by case in encounters among subjects. The recognition-based spine requires institutional form, stable expectations, and enforcement to operate, and it must also pass the tests of basic rights and public justification. The next essay asks how these institutional positions come into contact with power when exercised.
