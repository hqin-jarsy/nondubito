第三篇：权利为何不以义务为本源

Essay 3: Why Rights Are Not Grounded in Duty

Han Qin (秦汉)

"每一项权利都对应一项义务"只有在把权利严格理解为请求权时才成立。霍菲尔德早已提醒我们，日常语言里的"权利"其实混合了四种位置：请求权对应义务，自由对应无请求权，权力对应责任承受，豁免对应无权变更。这个系列不需要否定这些关系。它真正要挑战的是另一个命题：权利之所以存在，是不是因为对面已经先站着一个负有义务的人？

一、根据朝内，义务可以向外

上一篇提到，所有主体彼此承认为目的时，每个主体的自身之法已经把别人考虑在内。这里的起点仍然朝内：主体不能把自身完全交给外部命令，而必须形成、检验并承担自己的行动理由。自我立法是基础性的要求；朝向他人的义务，则在多个主体共享世界、彼此提出具体主张时被推导出来。

因此需要区分"一项权利的根据"和"一项请求权的关系结构"。基础权利并不是因为某个义务人先承诺不侵犯才成立；但当它被具体化为"你不得奴役我"、"照护者必须保障这个孩子的生存"，对面就会出现真实的派生义务。说义务不是权利的本源，不等于说他人从不欠我们任何东西。

承认性权利也不只对应权力。一项投票权可能同时包含自由、请求、权力和豁免：选民可以投票，选务机构有计票义务，选票能够改变法律结果，普通人又无权随意取消选民资格。权力与制度让这些位置有效并可执行，义务则构成其中某些具体关系。两者不能互相替代。

这个系列还会用到第三个概念：给益关系。它指一个主体自愿提供某种利益，而接受方不因此自动取得要求继续给予的请求权。给益不是缩水的权利，也不能拿来覆盖本来就有义务存在的照护、受托或救助关系；第七篇会专门展开。

二、意志论真正面对的难题

意志论把权利的重要部分放在规范控制上：权利人能够主张、放弃或执行一项要求。睡着的人提醒我们，眼下没有行使意志，不等于权利已经消失；但这并不足以单独击倒成熟的意志论，因为睡眠只是暂时不行使，并没有消灭那个人通常拥有的控制能力。

更强的难题来自婴儿、长期无法独立表达的人，以及不可放弃的权利。他们可能从未具备、暂时没有或依法不能行使完整的控制能力，却依然拥有完整站位。这里最稳妥的区分仍是：持有权利、亲自行使权利、由他人代表或支持行使权利，是三件不同的事。意志可以解释其中第二件，却不能独自承担第一件的全部根据。

三、利益重要，但不足以单独说明站位

利益论并不必然采用市场式语言，也不必然要求一切利益都能加总。它最有力的版本主张：当一个存在的利益足够重要，足以要求他人承担义务时，一项权利就成立。这也正是利益论能够认真讨论儿童、动物和无法表达者的原因，而不是它无法处理的漏洞。

本理论与它的分歧更窄：利益可以说明什么值得保护，却未必已经说明谁以第一人称站在主张的位置上。一片湿地有生态价值，一只动物会受苦，一个孩子需要照护；这些事实都产生道德理由，但它们是否产生同一种权利、由谁主张、能否放弃，仍需额外的主体与关系分析。利益不是错误的词，只是不足以独自完成全部工作。

四、两条分割线应该交叉保留

消极权利与积极权利的区分仍然有用：它说明履行一项具体请求通常要求不干预，还是要求采取行动。不过这条线不能替代基础权利与承认性权利的区分。前者分析义务的形式，后者追问权利的道德根据是否依赖制度。

两条线会交叉。一项福利权可能包含与生存和身体完整相连的基础根据，同时需要资金、资格与发放程序这些制度构造；言论自由既要求国家不压制，也可能要求法院、选举制度或公共机构采取积极措施保护表达空间。承认义务的存在，不妨碍我们继续追问它为何成立、如何分配，以及制度用什么方式兑现。

五、历时展开：三组人权不是三段整齐年代

权利从来不是一次性定好、永远不变的清单。所谓公民与政治权利、经济社会文化权利、集体与团结性权利，常被归纳成"三代人权"；但这更适合作为分析分类，而不是一条全世界依次经过的历史时间线。三组权利长期重叠、彼此依赖，也常常同时受到承认或压制。

这个分类仍然提示了一件真实的事：新的处境会迫使旧有权利语言扩展，原本没有被命名的伤害和主张会获得制度形式。但扩展并不自动等于进步，也不保证后一组权利取代前一组。第六篇会再讨论这种开放而不必然上升的历史动力。

无国籍者把问题推到极端。阿伦特所谓"拥有权利的权利"提醒我们：一个人即使在制度上被所有国家排除，仍然有作为主体被重新纳入共同世界的主张。这项主张的道德根据不由某一国家先行授予，却只有通过他人、法律与政治共同体承担义务，才可能在现实中实现。基础根据与关系义务在这里不是敌人，而是前后相接的两层。

结语

修正后的命题可以说得更精确：权利不以义务为本源，但具体的请求权会产生或对应义务；自我立法提供基础根据，自洽地承认他人则推导出朝外的约束；自由、请求、权力与豁免各有不同的对应结构；消极/积极和基础/承认性是两条交叉而非互相取代的分割线。这样进入下一篇时，义务不再是被取消的概念，而是被放回了正确的层级。

"Every right has a correlative duty" is true only when right is used in the strict sense of a claim-right. Hohfeld famously showed that ordinary talk of rights blends four positions: claims correlate with duties, liberties with no-claims, powers with liabilities, and immunities with disabilities. This series need not deny those relations. Its real question is different: does a right exist because a duty-bearer was already standing opposite it?

1. The Ground Points Inward; Duties Can Point Outward

The previous essay argued that when subjects recognize one another as ends, each subject's own law has already taken others into account. The starting point remains inward: a subject cannot surrender itself entirely to external command, but must form, test, and own the reasons for its action. Self-legislation is foundational. Duties to others are derived when multiple subjects share a world and address concrete claims to one another.

We therefore need to separate the ground of a right from the relational structure of a claim. A basic right does not begin only after a duty-bearer promises not to violate it. Yet once it is specified as "you may not enslave me" or "this caregiver must secure this child's survival," genuine outward-facing duties appear. Saying that duty is not the source of rights does not mean that no one ever owes anything to anyone else.

Recognition-based rights do not correlate only with power, either. A right to vote may combine liberty, claim, power, and immunity: the voter may cast a ballot; election officials must count it; the ballot helps alter a legal result; and ordinary people lack authority to cancel the voter's status. Institutions and power make these positions valid and enforceable. Duties constitute some of their particular relations. Neither replaces the other.

The series will also use a third concept: a beneficence relation. Here a subject voluntarily confers a benefit without the recipient thereby acquiring a claim that the benefit continue. Beneficence is not a diluted right, and it cannot be used to redescribe care, trust, or rescue where genuine duties already exist. The final essay develops the distinction.

2. The Harder Problem for Will Theory

Will theory locates an important part of a right in normative control: the holder can assert, waive, or enforce a claim. A sleeping person reminds us that a right does not disappear whenever the will is not currently being exercised. But sleep alone does not defeat a sophisticated will theory, because temporary non-exercise does not remove the person's ordinary capacity for control.

The harder cases are infants, people who cannot independently communicate over long periods, and inalienable rights. They may never possess, temporarily lack, or be legally unable to exercise full control, yet their standing remains intact. The durable distinction is among holding a right, personally exercising it, and exercising it through representation or support. Will can illuminate the second; it cannot by itself supply the entire ground of the first.

3. Interests Matter, but Do Not Fully Explain Standing

Interest theory need not speak the language of markets, and it need not assume that every interest is commensurable. In its strongest form, it says that a right exists when a being's interest is sufficiently important to justify duties in others. That is precisely why interest theories can take children, animals, and people unable to communicate seriously, rather than treating them as embarrassing exceptions.

The disagreement here is narrower. An interest can explain what deserves protection without yet explaining who occupies the first-person position of a claimant. A wetland has ecological value, an animal can suffer, and a child needs care. Each fact generates moral reasons, but whether they generate the same kind of right, who may assert it, and whether it can be waived require further analysis of subject and relation. Interest is not the wrong vocabulary; it is simply not the whole account.

4. Keep Both Cross-Cutting Distinctions

The distinction between negative and positive rights remains useful: it tells us whether satisfying a concrete claim primarily requires non-interference or affirmative action. But it cannot replace the distinction between basic and recognition-based rights. The first analyzes the form of a duty; the second asks whether the right's moral ground depends on institutions.

The two lines cross. A welfare right may contain a basic ground connected to survival and bodily integrity while depending on institutional rules for funding, eligibility, and delivery. Freedom of expression may require non-interference, but it can also require courts, electoral systems, or public bodies to take affirmative steps to protect an expressive space. Acknowledging duties does not prevent us from asking why they arise, how they should be allocated, and how institutions should discharge them.

5. Historical Expansion: Three Groups, Not Three Neat Eras

Rights were never fixed in a timeless list. Civil and political rights, economic and social rights, and collective or solidarity rights are often grouped as three "generations." The scheme is better treated as an analytical classification than as a universal chronology. The groups have long overlapped, depended on one another, and been recognized or suppressed at the same time.

The classification still points to something real: new conditions force inherited rights vocabularies to expand, and previously unnamed harms and claims acquire institutional form. Expansion does not automatically amount to progress, nor does one group supersede another. Essay 6 returns to this open but non-teleological historical dynamic.

Statelessness pushes the problem to its limit. Arendt's "right to have rights" names the claim of a person excluded by every state to re-enter a common world as a subject. No particular state first grants the moral ground of that claim; yet only duties taken up by other people, laws, and political communities can make it effective. Foundational standing and relational obligation are not rivals here. They are successive layers.

Conclusion

The revised thesis is more precise: rights are not grounded in duty, but concrete claim-rights generate or correlate with duties. Self-legislation supplies a foundational ground; consistent recognition of others yields outward constraints. Claims, liberties, powers, and immunities have different relational counterparts, while the negative/positive and basic/recognition-based distinctions cross rather than replace one another. Duty has not been abolished. It has been returned to the right level of the analysis.
