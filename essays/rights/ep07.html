第七篇：权利与给益

Essay 7: Rights and Beneficence

Han Qin (秦汉)

第三篇区分权利与给益时，留下了两个问题。第一，主体自愿让某个对象受益，却没有让对方取得继续索取的请求权，这种关系究竟是什么？第二，婴儿、儿童与少年已经拥有权利，却未必能独立行使，站位与能力应当怎样随发展而连接？这一篇用"给益"而不是通常意味着法定权利的"权益"来回答。

一、给益定义给予，不定义接受者的全部地位

给益是一种自愿给予：给予者提供某项利益，而这一次给予本身不自动让接受者取得要求继续给予的请求权。接受者可以是人、动物或生态对象；但"正在接受给益"只描述这一段具体关系，不说明接受者除此之外没有权利、利益或法律保护。一位成年人接受匿名礼物，不会失去原有权利；一只动物得到额外照料，也不妨碍我们另行讨论它的道德地位。

因此，给益不能用来描述本来就负有义务的行为。父母为婴儿提供基本营养和照护，首先是在履行儿童的权利与照护者的义务，不是可随时撤回的慷慨；受托人依遗嘱或基金管理财产，也是在执行法律义务。只有超出既有请求、承诺与合理依赖的部分，才适合叫作给益。

二、关系可以单向，主体仍然可以拒绝

给益之所以单向，是因为接受本身没有生成一项要求继续给予的请求权，不是因为接受者必须没有回应能力。若接受者是主体，它保留拒绝礼物、设定条件和退出关系的全部权利；若受益对象无法表达，给予者仍受其他道德与法律规范约束。单向描述的是请求结构，不是把接受方降格成物品。

这一区分也解释了真正的培育为何不能靠"我是为你好"取得无限权力。帮助可以是自愿给益，但对方的同意、隐私、身体完整与既有权利始终独立存在。给予者不能拿一份额外利益交换对接受者全部生活的控制。

三、撤回给益何时会变成侵犯

在最纯粹的情形里，一次自愿、没有承诺也没有制造依赖的额外给予，可以停止而不构成违约。但只要给予者作出承诺、诱导合理依赖、垄断必要资源，或者本来就负有照护与救助义务，接受方就可能取得真正的请求权。此时停止提供不再只是"不慷慨"，而可能是违约、遗弃、歧视或对基础权利的侵犯。

第三方阻止一项给益，也要分别分析：它可能侵犯给予者处分资源或结社的自由，也可能伤害接受方另有根据的权利；但不能因为某件好事原本会发生，就自动把所有期待都升级成可强制执行的请求。

四、给益怎样转化为可主张权利

给益不会因为接受者终于能够说话才变成权利。转化通常发生在规范关系本身：承诺使将来的给予成为所欠之物，合理依赖让突然撤回产生责任，反复实践可能形成稳定预期，法律则可以把某种利益制度化为资格、程序与救济。接受者即使不能亲自表达，也可以通过代表或支持性机制持有并行使这项权利。

制度化仍要经过前文的两组检验：它是否有清楚规则、稳定预期与真实执行；它是否兼容基础权利，并能向所有受影响者给出公共理由。不是每项善意都应被强制延续，也不是每项长期给予都天然正当。

五、正在形成中的主体已经是权利主体

孩子的权利不需要寄托在"将来会成为完整成年人"这张期票上。婴儿此刻就有自身感受、依赖与发展连续性，是一个不能被当作工具的主体。随时间变化的，是它理解、表达和独立决定的能力，以及照护者应提供多少代表、支持与指导，而不是站位本身从零逐渐涨到一。

代表也不等于替孩子拥有权利。照护网络负有帮助实现权利的义务，并应随着孩子不断发展的能力，把控制逐步转为共同决定，再转为第一人称决定。年龄只提供粗略参考；不同领域、不同风险与不同个体会有不同速度，因此不存在某一天主动权突然、完整地一次性交还。

六、三个边界案例：动物、婴儿、少年

动物会感受、抗拒和选择；它们是否具有本理论要求的反思性自我立法，仍是开放的规范与经验问题。额外照料可以构成给益，却不能由此证明动物永远不可能拥有任何权利。婴儿已经持有生命、身体、照护与发展等权利，基本喂养不是纯粹给益；它不能亲自提出请求时，由照护者和制度代表、支持它行使。少年则随着理解和经验增长，在不同事项上逐步取得更强的独立决定权，监护人的角色相应从代为决定转向指导、支持与尊重。

结语：回到起点

给益不是打折的权利，而是一项没有自动生成持续请求权的自愿利益；接受给益也不抹去接受者原有的权利。正在形成中的主体已经是目的本身，变化的是权利如何被行使，而不是它是否值得被算作主体。把这两点分开，既能给慷慨保留自愿空间，也能防止照护者把义务包装成恩惠。

七篇下来，这个系列没有建立一份封闭清单，而是画出一组相互连接的层次：权利的基础根据、主体站位与行使能力，两条脊柱及其制度条件，权利与义务、权力和正当性的接口，限制与侵犯的判断，以及给益如何在承诺、依赖与制度化中转成可主张的位置。系列到此完结，但这套结构仍然必须对新的经验和反例保持开放。

Essay 3 left two questions open when it distinguished rights from beneficence. First, what kind of relation exists when a subject voluntarily benefits a recipient without giving that recipient a claim to continued provision? Second, how should standing and capacity be connected when infants, children, and adolescents already hold rights but cannot always exercise them independently? This essay uses beneficence rather than entitlement, a word that ordinarily denotes an enforceable right.

1. Beneficence Defines the Giving, Not the Recipient's Entire Status

Beneficence is a voluntary conferral of benefit that does not, by that act alone, give the recipient a claim to continued provision. The recipient may be a person, an animal, or an ecological object. But "receiving a benefit" describes only this particular relation; it does not imply that the recipient otherwise lacks rights, interests, or legal protection. An adult who accepts an anonymous gift keeps every pre-existing right. Extra care for an animal likewise leaves the animal's broader moral status an open question.

Beneficence cannot describe conduct already governed by duty. Parents who provide an infant with basic nutrition and care are first satisfying the child's rights and their own obligations, not dispensing generosity at will. Trustees administering a will or endowment are also performing legal duties. The term applies only to what exceeds an existing claim, promise, or justified reliance.

2. The Relation Can Be One-Way While the Subject Still Refuses

Beneficence is one-way because acceptance does not itself create a claim to continued giving, not because the recipient must lack a capacity to respond. A recipient who is a subject retains every right to refuse the gift, set conditions, and leave the relationship. When the beneficiary cannot communicate, the giver remains constrained by other moral and legal norms. One-way describes the claim structure; it does not reduce the recipient to an object.

This distinction also explains why cultivation cannot acquire unlimited authority by saying, "It is for your own good." Assistance may be voluntary beneficence, but consent, privacy, bodily integrity, and pre-existing rights remain independent. An extra benefit cannot purchase control over the recipient's entire life.

3. When Withdrawal Becomes a Violation

In the pure case, an additional benefit that involved no promise and created no dependence may stop without breach. Once a giver promises continuation, induces justified reliance, monopolizes a necessity, or already bears duties of care or rescue, however, the recipient may acquire a genuine claim. Withdrawal can then become breach, abandonment, discrimination, or a violation of basic rights rather than a mere failure of generosity.

Third-party interference also requires separate analysis. It may violate the giver's freedom to use resources or associate, and it may injure an independently grounded right of the recipient. Yet the fact that something good would otherwise have occurred does not automatically turn every expectation into an enforceable claim.

4. How Beneficence Becomes a Claimable Right

A benefit does not become a right only when the recipient finally learns to speak. The normative relation itself can change: a promise makes future performance owed; justified reliance makes abrupt withdrawal wrongful; repeated practice can create settled expectations; and law can institutionalize a benefit as a status, procedure, and remedy. A recipient unable to communicate personally can still hold and exercise the right through representation or supported decision-making.

Institutionalization must still pass both sets of tests developed earlier. Does the position have clear rules, stable expectations, and real enforcement? Is it compatible with basic rights and publicly justifiable to everyone affected? Not every act of kindness should become compulsory, and not every longstanding benefit is automatically legitimate.

5. A Developing Subject Is Already a Rights-Holder

A child's rights need not rest on a promissory note stating that the child will someday become a complete adult. An infant already has experiences, dependencies, and developmental continuity of its own, and cannot be treated merely as an instrument. What changes over time is the capacity to understand, communicate, and decide independently, along with the amount of representation, support, and guidance caregivers should provide. Standing does not rise gradually from zero.

Representation does not make the caregiver the owner of the child's rights. The caregiving network is responsible for helping realize them and, as the child's capacities develop, for shifting from substitute decisions to shared decisions and then toward first-person control. Age is only a rough guide. Different domains, risks, and individuals develop at different rates, so authority is never returned suddenly and completely on a single day.

6. Three Boundary Cases: Animal, Infant, Adolescent

Animals feel, resist, and choose. Whether they possess the reflexive self-legislation required by this theory remains an open normative and empirical question. Additional care can be beneficence, but that fact cannot prove that animals are forever incapable of holding any rights. Infants already hold rights to life, bodily integrity, care, and development; basic feeding is not pure beneficence, and caregivers and institutions represent or support claims the infant cannot personally express. Adolescents progressively acquire greater authority in different domains as understanding and experience grow, while the guardian's role shifts from substitution toward guidance, support, and respect.

Conclusion: Back to the Beginning

Beneficence is not a discounted right. It is a voluntary benefit that does not automatically create a claim to continued provision, and receiving it does not erase any right the recipient already holds. A developing subject is already an end in itself. What changes is how rights are exercised, not whether the subject counts. Keeping these points apart preserves room for generosity while preventing caregivers from disguising obligation as favor.

Across seven essays, the series has produced no closed list. It has instead connected the moral ground of rights, the distinction between standing and exercise, the two spines and their institutional conditions, the interfaces among rights, duties, power, and legitimacy, the analysis of limits and violations, and the transformation of beneficence into claimable positions through promise, reliance, and institutionalization. The series ends here, but its structure must remain open to new experience and counterexample.
