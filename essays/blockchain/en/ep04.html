Silk Road

Revisiting Silk Road through escrow, arbitration, and reputation: how a market excluded from formal institutions rebuilt a private institutional order.

I. January and February

Federal criminal records say Ross Ulbricht created Silk Road around January 2011. Academic research generally dates the beginning of its public operation to February 2011.

The dates do not conflict. One concerns creation; the other concerns the market beginning to operate.

That small difference is a clue.

It points to something pervasive in this history: different recorders define the same object differently, producing different dates, figures, and conclusions that can all be accurate.

Section IV will present the fullest instance.

What follows is more complicated than the previous three essays. It involves a life sentence, a presidential pardon, six deaths, and two federal agents later convicted of crimes. Each carries a different evidentiary status. Popular accounts have compressed almost all of them into a single sentence.

A rule must therefore be established first. This is not pedantry.

A court finding means a jury verdict, formal judgment, sentencing-stage factual finding, or conclusion upheld on appeal.

A government allegation means a claim in a complaint, indictment, prosecution sentencing submission, or Justice Department press release. Without a supporting judgment, it cannot automatically be treated as a court finding.

A research measurement means observations and inferences obtained through page scraping, feedback collection, laboratory testing, or surveys.

A platform record means the market’s own rules, forums, and contemporaneous archives.

These four layers must be separated because almost every widely circulated claim about this history substitutes one for another.

Roughly seven claims need examination: everything sold was drugs; it did $1.2 billion in business; Ulbricht was convicted of murder for hire; prosecutors proved the platform killed six people; Bitcoin made transactions untraceable; the two corrupt agents proved the whole case was fabricated; and closing it eliminated darknet markets.

The sections below address them in turn. They are not all entirely false. Almost every one, however, presents one evidentiary layer as another.

Separating layers is also an attitude. A person received life imprisonment, six people died, two agents were convicted, and a pardon followed. In writing about such events, precision matters far more than forcefulness.

The real subject, however, is not crime and punishment.

It is something absent from the first three essays: a market excluded from every formal institution grew a complete institutional order of its own.

The earlier constructs still stood beside formal institutions. The pizza purchase needed a credit card to reach the real world. Mining farms signed electricity contracts and paid bills. Mt. Gox had a company, bank accounts, and terms, and a national court ultimately handled its insolvency.

This case was different. Nothing could be counted on outside.

The market therefore had to rebuild those external functions internally, one by one. What it rebuilt is what the next sections examine.

II. Whom Do You Pay First?

The market faced three difficulties that ordinarily do not present the same problems in lawful commerce.

First, exposure of identity. Second, payment and seizure risks. Third, the absence of enforceable contracts between strangers.

The third was most consequential and remains the thread through the following sections.

A lawful online transaction rests on more than mutual trust. Behind it stand card chargebacks, consumer-protection law, police complaints, lawsuits, and court enforcement.

Participants in this market could not, in principle, ask a court to enforce an unlawful transaction.

Read that slowly, because it determines every design that follows.

A buyer pays and receives nothing. He cannot call the police, sue, or even publicly explain what he bought. His only recourse is whether the platform is willing to act for him.

The platform therefore had to build those functions itself.

First consider the two things it did not build.

Tor addressed location. Buyers accessed a hidden service through Tor instead of connecting directly to a conventional website. It made the server’s true location harder for clients to discover and prevented the server from directly receiving clients’ actual source addresses. It did not provide absolute anonymity or eliminate compromised endpoints, operational mistakes, exposed mailing addresses, or server discovery.

Registration was minimal. According to the FBI’s 2013 complaint, accounts generally required only a username and password, without real-name verification. A participant’s identity was therefore first a persistent pseudonym, not a legal identity.

Bitcoin addressed remote payment without conventional payment institutions.

The FBI complaint itself made a point often overlooked afterward: Bitcoin is not an invisible ledger. On-chain transactions are public. The real difficulty is linking an address to a real identity. Once that link exists, historical flows can be traced.

This case is an excellent counterexample to claims of untraceability. The appellate record says approximately 89% of the bitcoins obtained from Ulbricht’s laptop could be linked to flows through the Icelandic server seized by investigators.

The previous essay ended by observing that the chain can establish a transfer, while associating addresses with people requires something off-chain.

Here, the off-chain object was a laptop seized while open in a library.

The arrest illustrates the same point. Investigators used an administrator account already under their control to chat in real time with the suspect and induce him to open an administrators-only page. Once he logged in and entered it, agents moved immediately to prevent the computer from locking and encrypting. The seized laptop still displayed that administrator chat and back-end page.

The whole technical architecture was ultimately opened beside a person’s hands.

That also shows its real boundary. Tor concealed network location, not the table at which a person sat. The protocol reached the screen. The arrest took place outside it.

Then came what the platform actually built.

A typical transaction moved money from the buyer’s external wallet to an internal platform account, then into platform-controlled escrow. The buyer confirmed receipt, or the platform adjudicated a dispute. Funds entered the seller’s internal balance, from which the seller withdrew to an external address.

The crucial part was the middle.

The buyer did not send coins directly to the seller.

The platform provided deposit addresses and controlled the underlying wallet infrastructure. On ordering, payment entered escrow. Only after completion was confirmed did it become withdrawable seller funds, with commission deducted.

The platform was therefore not a noticeboard. It was a clearing intermediary and an intermediary of trust.

Christin’s independent scraping confirmed that escrow was central to ordinary transactions, not optional decoration. Buyers clicked to finalize after receipt, releasing payment. If they forgot, an order could finalize automatically after a specified period.

For disputes over nondelivery or misdescribed goods, administrators became third-party arbitrators, deciding whether escrowed funds went to the seller or returned to the buyer.

Ordinary e-commerce can reach beyond a platform to chargebacks, consumer law, police, and courts. Here, arbitration effectively replaced functions ordinarily shared by banks, contract law, and courts.

This also explains why account balances were a governance lever: the platform could freeze or confiscate balances of users it deemed fraudulent and ban their accounts.

It faced a structural problem, however. Pseudonyms can be recreated. Reputation history and seller entry costs therefore had to make switching identities costly.

The platform called its Bitcoin processing a tumbler. The purpose was not to change the protocol, but to prevent an immediately visible one-to-one link between the specific coins deposited by buyers and those eventually withdrawn by sellers.

Restraint is necessary here. The FBI complaint further characterized this arrangement as money laundering. That was investigators’ legal assessment, not a judicial conclusion established merely by the use of intermediate addresses. Technical facts and criminal-law judgments must be separated.

The platform also offered exchange-rate hedging. This addressed a commercial problem unrelated to anonymity: if delivery took several days, large bitcoin price movements exposed buyers and sellers to unexpected exchange-rate risk.

III. The Commission Schedule

The business model is clearest in its commission schedule.

Christin found an earlier flat commission of about 6.23%. After January 2012, a tiered schedule applied: approximately 10% on the first $50, then successive tiers of about 8.5%, 6%, 3%, and 2%, with about 1.5% on the portion above $1,000.

Using the actual transaction distribution, he calculated an average effective commission of roughly 7.4%.

The FBI’s 2013 material instead summarized commissions as generally around 8–15%, with higher percentages on small transactions.

The figures differ. The sensible approach is not to select one timeless rate, but to acknowledge changing rules between 2011 and 2013 and different periods covered by the study and the FBI account.

The schedule’s shape must be clear.

It was regressive.

That was not unique to this platform. Most per-transaction payment and trading systems have a similar shape because fixed processing costs differ little between small and large transactions. The point is simply what this schedule was and what it meant for different participants.

Someone buying $50 paid about 10% in that tier. Someone buying more than $1,000 paid approximately 1.5% on the excess.

No commentary is necessary.

Seller registration was not costless either. Early on, the platform auctioned a limited number of seller accounts. Later it moved to a fixed account fee or deposit-like entry barrier; archives contain figures around $500. Rules changed over time, so this cannot be described as one uniform three-year regime.

Its institutional purpose was clear: make disposable scam accounts expensive and attach sunk costs to pseudonymous identities.

In other words, it priced anonymity. A pseudonym became trustworthy not because its owner was inherently reliable, but because the owner had paid for it and would have to pay again for another.

This resembles the 39.99% in the second essay: a privately established rule without external enforcement that nevertheless worked because noncompliance meant losing the ability to keep doing business.

The difference is that the earlier rule-maker was a company that could be sued. Here it was a pseudonym whose identity no one knew.

The ratings system looked almost identical to lawful e-commerce. Buyers left one to five stars after completion, optionally with comments. Sellers accumulated public histories, and new buyers used them to choose suppliers.

Christin collected 184,804 feedback entries. Of these, 96.5% were five-star; four- and five-star ratings totaled about 97.8%; one- and two-star ratings about 1.4%.

This cannot be read as evidence that 98% of goods were objectively high quality.

The researcher noted selection bias in online ratings generally. An eBay sample had even shown more than 99% positive feedback. Scams after bypassing escrow or finalizing early, and unsuccessful transactions leaving no feedback, could also be missed.

The form resembled ordinary e-commerce, but the institutional burden was much heavier.

Ordinary star ratings mainly help customers rank options. Here they also performed part of contract enforcement: threatening lost future business to punish short-term fraud, reducing quality-information asymmetry where goods could not be inspected, and assisting dispute decisions.

They were also much more fragile. Pseudonyms could shed reputations or leave the market, and administrators had no outside oversight.

Now trace both ends.

A feature called finalize early allowed established sellers with good transaction records to ask buyers to release money before delivery.

Sellers gained faster working-capital turnover. Buyers lost escrow protection.

Platform rules explicitly warned of the risk.

The second essay’s three conditions apply again: calculable, selectable, and possible to leave.

Fees were public, hence calculable. Early finalization could be refused, hence selectable. A buyer could decline the transaction, hence exit was possible.

All three were present.

All were adjudicated by the same person.

The platform was custodian, judge, and fee collector, with no superior authority.

Those functions are deliberately separated in lawful institutions. Banks hold assets, courts judge, tax authorities collect, and regulators watch over them. Combining them in one person greatly increases efficiency, one reason this platform completed so many transactions.

The cost is settled on the day something goes wrong.

IV. Three Kinds of Number

Scale is especially easy to misstate because at least three measurement bases exist.

Begin with direct server-database figures. According to the FBI’s analysis, from February 6, 2011 to July 23, 2013, the system recorded 957,079 registered accounts, 146,946 distinct buyer accounts, 3,877 distinct seller accounts, 1,229,465 transactions, 9,519,664 BTC in merchandise sales, and 614,305 BTC in commissions.

Every figure requires a definition.

957,079 does not mean that many real individuals: one person could have several accounts. The 3,877 sellers appeared cumulatively across the period, not simultaneously. The final two figures directly add coin amounts from different historical moments.

The 2013 complaint converted all 9.519 million coins at the then-current price, producing approximately $1.2 billion in sales and $79.8 million in commissions. The complaint itself acknowledged large exchange-rate changes.

That calculation answers what all those historical coins would be worth at July 2013 prices.

It does not establish that transactions worth $1.2 billion at their actual transaction dates took place.

Later government material used values closer to transaction dates, yielding much smaller totals. In 2015, the Justice Department said the platform facilitated more than $200 million in illegal goods transactions and earned over $13 million in commissions. Academic work citing the government’s revised calculation gives approximately $214 million.

The $1.2 billion market and the roughly $200 million market are therefore not two databases. Largely, they are two valuations of the same transactions.

The judgment’s $183,961,921 forfeiture is a criminal forfeiture order, not a third precise revenue total. It concerns calculation of criminal proceeds; legal forfeiture and commercial gross transaction value are different concepts.

Independent research produced lower figures through transparent methods.

In 2012, Christin scraped the site almost daily and inferred completed orders from public feedback. He estimated about 7,665 BTC a day during the measurement period, approximately $1.22 million a month at contemporaneous rates, or $15 million annualized. Commissions were around $92,000 a month, or $1.1 million annualized.

These figures are much lower than later server data, unsurprisingly. The market grew rapidly between 2012 and 2013, and feedback-based methods missed hidden listings and transactions without reviews. The author regarded the estimate as tending toward a lower bound.

Active-seller counts show the same distinction between snapshots and cumulative totals. Christin observed approximately 564 active sellers on July 24, 2012. Across the entire scraping period, about 1,239 distinct sellers appeared, with only 112 present throughout.

The FBI later obtained 3,877 cumulative seller accounts.

564, 1,239, 112, 3,877. All four can be true.

Product composition presents a similar issue. Christin observed 24,385 distinct listings across about 220 categories in 2012. The largest category accounted for around 13.7% of distinct listings, and sixteen of the twenty largest categories directly concerned drugs or controlled substances.

There is therefore strong contemporaneous evidence for calling it principally a drug market.

His paper did not, however, provide a single drug/non-drug percentage across all approximately 220 categories.

The closest dated contemporaneous figure comes from a March 2013 snapshot: more than 10,000 listings, approximately 7,000 of them drugs. By listing count, that is around 70% against 30%.

Non-drug does not mean lawful. The remaining share included books, pornography, and forged identification.

A second trap is that listing counts are not sales volumes.

A bestseller sold for years and a listing that never sold count as one each.

Taken together, the section reveals something.

The same market was measured six times, yielding six sets of figures, all correct. The difference was not arithmetic error. Each measured something different.

The five measures in the second essay have reappeared in another setting.

This time there is another layer. Those five at least measured different aspects of the same object. Here, one measure does not measure the market at all. It measures Bitcoin’s July 2013 price.

Multiplying nine and a half million coins exchanged across two and a half years by one day’s price produces a statement about that day’s quotation, not how much business the market did.

V. Three Percentages

On the real-world side, the evidence supports opposing movements across different dimensions of risk, not a demonstrated overall net effect.

Begin with the most direct quantitative evidence for reduced risk.

In 2016, Barratt, Ferris, and Winstock analyzed Global Drug Survey data from 3,794 voluntary respondents who had used such markets during the preceding year. Median age was twenty-two, and 82% were male.

They compared the same respondents’ self-reported experiences buying drugs through different channels.

For these online markets, 3% reported threats to personal safety and 1% actual physical violence.

Buying from friends: 14% and 6% respectively.

Buying from known offline dealers: 24% and 10%.

Buying from strangers or open markets: 35% and 15%.

Respondents also expressed fewer concerns about adulteration and encounters with law enforcement online, while more often reporting lost money, long waits, or nondelivery.

The limitations must remain intact. This was not random sampling, but existing users volunteering for a survey. Data were self-reported. Conducted in 2014, it measured the broader market category, not only the original Silk Road. It could not show that moving a particular transaction online causally reduced violence. The paper’s term was association, not causation.

For quality, there is a rare set of laboratory evidence.

Spain’s Energy Control analyzed 219 voluntarily submitted samples from users of such markets in 2014–2015. In 200, or 91.3%, the main analytical result matched the substance buyers reported purchasing. In 141, or 64.4%, no additional adulterants were found. Among 103 cocaine samples, average purity was approximately 71.6%, and 51.5% contained only cocaine. The authors compared contemporaneous Spanish offline testing data: about 48% average purity, with only around 14% unadulterated.

The limitations are particularly important and must be stated fully.

Users submitted samples voluntarily. Researchers could not verify that all came from these markets or reliably determine the original country of purchase. Many categories other than cocaine had small samples. There was no offline control group matched by time, country, and purchase conditions.

Researchers explicitly noted that sellers might submit high-quality samples for promotional purposes.

The paper’s conclusion was that representativeness was unknown.

Higher purity is not a uniformly positive public-health outcome. It can mean fewer unknown adulterants and more predictable doses, but can also increase poisoning risk when users misjudge strength. Adulterants and high-risk substances remained in the sample. The authors therefore treated testing services themselves, rather than market reputation, as important harm-reduction tools.

The argument on the other side also has reasonable evidentiary foundations.

The internet lowers search barriers and offers access to substances unavailable locally. Greater choice may broaden the range of substances used. Postal delivery shifts enforcement risk from street meetings to packaging, delivery, and collection. Platform reputation cannot eliminate mislabeling, dangerous new substances, or sudden supply changes.

Research on platform texts also found that participants did not think they had escaped enforcement risk. They understood it as relocated to offline delivery points.

Risk did not disappear. It moved.

This sentence remains useful throughout the domain. Earlier essays showed several forms: preorders transferred the timing risk of rising difficulty to buyers; custody concentrated safeguarding risk in one exchange. Here, risk moved from the street corner to the mailbox.

Each transfer benefits someone. Beneficiaries and risk bearers are often different people.

The most important sentence here is this: these papers measure intermediate outcomes such as violence, purity, information, and use patterns. They do not causally estimate net deaths or net public-health benefits across society.

Thus “it reduced harm” and “it increased harm,” when stated as a single established overall effect, both exceed the evidence.

No verdict is rendered here.

That restraint is especially necessary because both sides have real evidence and real limitations, and do not even measure the same thing. One asks whether buying became safer. The other asks what happens when more of a substance circulates.

Both questions can have affirmative answers.

VI. Preponderance of the Evidence

At sentencing in 2015, prosecutors submitted material linking six deaths to drugs purchased through the platform.

There are three layers, with different standards of proof.

First, sentencing judge Katherine Forrest found sufficient connections to consider the deaths as harms at sentencing. She used the preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, meaning more likely than not, rather than the beyond-reasonable-doubt standard required for criminal conviction.

Second, defense medical experts identified missing information and multiple-drug factors in several cases. They said the deaths could not, to medical certainty, be attributed solely to goods purchased on the platform.

Third, the Second Circuit expressly stated that Ulbricht was not charged or punished for homicide. The deaths did not further raise his sentencing range; they illustrated the conduct’s risks and social harms.

Saying the platform’s drugs killed at least six people therefore expands the prosecution’s causal account into a determination the criminal court never made.

Conversely, saying the court completely rejected a connection is also wrong. The sentencing judge found a connection, and the appellate court allowed her to consider social harm on that basis.

This is the hardest distinction to maintain, and maintaining it is part of doing justice to the subject. Six people died. The sentencing judge found a connection under a preponderance standard. Defense experts said that, for individual cases, medical evidence did not support sole attribution here.

All three statements are needed. Omit any one and the account becomes inaccurate.

The murder-for-hire allegations likewise have three layers.

First, the New York jury tried no murder-for-hire count. The seven convictions included no murder, attempted murder, or murder for hire.

Second, Maryland federal prosecutors did separately charge an alleged attempt to arrange the killing of a platform administrator cooperating with law enforcement. That case never went to trial. Once the New York conviction was secure, prosecutors sought dismissal in 2018 on resource-allocation grounds. The case was dismissed, not resolved by a guilty verdict.

Third, at New York sentencing, Judge Forrest separately found under the preponderance standard that Ulbricht had commissioned five killings. The appellate record cites journals, chats, and bitcoin payments equivalent to approximately $650,000. The Justice Department explicitly stated that investigators had no evidence the purported killings actually occurred.

That sentencing finding exists. It is not a criminal conviction for murder for hire.

Saying the allegations were entirely media inventions conflicts with the sentencing record. Saying he was convicted of five or six murders conflicts with the judgment.

The convictions themselves were these: on February 4, 2015, a jury found him guilty on all seven counts, including drug distribution and conspiracy, internet distribution of controlled substances, continuing criminal enterprise, computer-hacking conspiracy, fraudulent-identification conspiracy, and money-laundering conspiracy.

At sentencing, two overlapping lesser-included counts were dismissed, leaving formal sentences on five counts. Concurrent penalties included two life terms and terms of twenty, fifteen, and five years, plus lifetime supervised release and forfeiture of $183,961,921.

The federal system has no traditional parole, so the life terms effectively provided no fixed release date.

The route to life imprisonment also needs explanation. It did not begin with an unconstrained judicial choice.

The presentence offense-level calculation already exceeded the sentencing table’s maximum and was capped at level 43, whose advisory sentence was life. The probation department also recommended life.

The judge then emphasized the large-scale, long-designed continuing criminal enterprise; its express aim of placing transactions beyond law enforcement; the fact that anonymity and technical distance did not diminish responsibility for an actual criminal market; and general deterrence, particularly for people considering replicating the business model. Online scale and technological distance were not mitigating factors.

The defense invoked the mechanisms discussed above: seller ratings, public forum discussion, drug-risk information supplied by a doctor, and potentially reduced violence from avoiding face-to-face contact.

The judge did not deny those mechanisms existed. She rejected treating them as sufficient to offset the platform’s overall unlawful nature and scale.

In 2017, the Second Circuit upheld the life sentence. It did not say life was the only reasonable outcome. It held that, given the case’s scale, the guidelines, sentencing findings, and the district court’s discretion, the sentence was not substantively unreasonable to the degree required for reversal on appeal.

VII. Two Agents

Two federal agents investigating the case were later convicted of profiting illicitly during that investigation.

This was not defense hearsay. There were two separate federal criminal cases.

Carl Mark Force IV was a DEA agent in the Baltimore task force. Operating under several online identities, he used his undercover position to solicit and divert bitcoins for himself. In 2015, he pleaded guilty to offenses including money laundering, obstruction of justice, and extortion under color of official right. The Justice Department said he misappropriated or diverted more than $700,000 in digital currency through his investigative role and sold Ulbricht information belonging to the government investigation. In October 2015, he received seventy-eight months in prison.

Shaun W. Bridges, a Secret Service agent on the same task force, used the account privileges of an arrested platform administrator to reset credentials and steal merchants’ bitcoins, then processed the funds through channels including Mt. Gox. In 2015, he pleaded guilty to money laundering and obstruction, received seventy-one months, and was ordered to forfeit more than $650,000. In 2017, he pleaded guilty to another theft involving government-controlled digital currency, receiving a further twenty-four months consecutive to the original sentence and forfeiture of roughly 1,500 BTC.

The use of Mt. Gox connects directly to the previous essay. The exchange was leaking funds while also serving as a route for a federal agent’s stolen proceeds.

These events overlapped. During the three years described previously, Mt. Gox was losing customer coins daily. In the same period, an investigator in this case used it to handle coins he had stolen.

One infrastructure was carrying two entirely different kinds of failure at once.

The defense’s resulting arguments raised substantive issues. Force’s misconduct was disclosed about six weeks before trial; some of Bridges’s conduct emerged fully only after trial. The defense argued that the investigation was tainted, potentially exculpatory evidence affected, and platform access might have enabled data manipulation.

The government excluded evidence traceable to Force from the New York trial’s evidentiary chain, did not make his undercover work a main pillar of conviction, and abandoned an additional murder-for-hire allegation involving his pseudonym in that trial.

The Second Circuit ultimately rejected the claim that the corrupt agents rendered the conviction unreliable. It found no evidence that they could have fabricated the extensive mutually corroborating journals, chats, keys, server database, and bitcoin links on the laptop. Nor did it find delayed disclosure material enough to change the outcome.

Two extremes must therefore be resisted.

The agents’ corruption was irrelevant to the case: plainly wrong.

Their corruption proved the core electronic evidence fabricated: unsupported by the court.

Seized bitcoins form another strand. In 2013, federal authorities first obtained roughly 29,655 coins from server-related wallets, then about 144,336 from Ulbricht’s devices. Contemporary government records totaled about 173,991. Documents vary by a few coins because of transfer timing and rounding, so “about 174,000” is preferable to false precision.

The government later auctioned them in four rounds. Independent auction tracking totals proceeds at approximately $65.84 million. Because the US Marshals Service did not publish every winning bidder or sealed-bid price, that is a public-record approximation, not an audited government proceeds figure.

Tim Draper is the first round’s undisputed prominent buyer: he publicly confirmed purchasing the entire batch of roughly thirty thousand coins.

The pardon came on January 21, 2025.

The president signed a formal instrument granting a full and unconditional pardon for the federal convictions described above.

The instrument did not retry evidence or declare the original judgment erroneous. It listed the convictions, sentences, and forfeiture, then exercised the constitutional pardon power.

The president’s separate political explanation on social media was an explanation of the pardon, not a judicial factual finding in the instrument.

One explicit public objection came from Senator Tim Kaine. In Senate remarks, he contrasted the pardon with the administration’s simultaneous description of drug trafficking as a national emergency, calling it a clear policy contradiction. That too was political assessment, not a legal challenge to the pardon’s validity.

Another legal misunderstanding must be avoided.

A presidential pardon is neither an acquittal nor an appellate reversal. It did not erase the jury’s historical 2015 findings or declare the Second Circuit’s judgment mistaken.

VIII. It Moved Elsewhere

A substantial longitudinal study examined where transactions went after a market closed.

Soska and Christin tracked sixteen anonymous markets between 2013 and 2015. They inferred transaction value from listing histories and buyer feedback, rather than simply counting advertisements.

They reconstructed the original market at roughly $300,000 a day in early 2013, more than $100 million annualized.

After its October 2013 closure, Black Market Reloaded and Sheep Marketplace rapidly absorbed some participants. Incomplete data for one prevented a precise total, but the researchers judged that the two largely filled the gap.

By late January 2014, aggregate observed market volume clearly exceeded the pre-closure level.

A bitcoin theft at Silk Road 2.0 later caused another decline, while competitors including Agora and Evolution kept growing. Operation Onymous closed multiple markets in November 2014, causing a sharp fall. Within weeks, the data showed recovery to more than half the pre-operation level.

By the study’s end, observed markets reached a peak thirty-day average of roughly $650,000 per day, with ordinary levels around $300,000–$500,000.

The paper did not conclude that enforcement was ineffective. Its narrower empirical conclusion was that, at the ecosystem level, individual closures and major exit scams often caused substantial short-term shocks but did not permanently eliminate aggregate demand during that period. Transactions moved to other platforms.

The wording matters because it accommodates neither extreme. Enforcement was not useless: short-term shocks were real, and money trapped in escrow really was lost. Nor had demand been eliminated.

The result cannot mechanically be extended to the present. Payment currencies, direct trading outside markets, encrypted messaging, independent seller sites, and enforcement methods have since changed. Coverage across studies is not directly comparable.

For 2013–2015, the data offers a strong answer. For today, there is no single directly comparable figure with the evidentiary standing of that original server database.

Product composition also showed continuity, rather than one platform’s rebirth. A relatively concentrated platform gave way to an ecosystem of competing markets with frequent failures and migrations.

Later research therefore often describes Silk Road’s historical role as a template for market design, not a permanent monopoly.

Return now to the opening judgment.

A market excluded from every formal institution grew an entire institutional order of its own.

What it grew looked almost exactly like the institutions from which it had been excluded.

Escrow replaced banks’ third-party custody. Arbitration replaced courts. Ratings replaced credit reporting. Deposits replaced licenses. Commissions replaced taxes.

Exclusion from an institutional order does not eliminate the need for institutions.

A market builds its own. The resulting order is usually rougher, more concentrated, and has no authority above it.

That is the remainder’s shape here.

The rule must be stated clearly: remainder carries no value judgment. Calling something a remainder means only that it was not counted. It says neither whether it should have been counted nor whether it is good.

The market as a whole was a remainder of lawful commerce’s institutional order. Inside it, further remainders emerged.

Those excluded from this private order were buyers whose goods never arrived, who were defrauded after finalizing early, or whose purchases did not match their labels.

Whom could they approach?

The administrator.

The administrator had no superior, and there was nowhere to appeal.

This is the most concrete difference between the private order and the one it imitated. Banks have regulators, courts have appeals, credit reporting has dispute procedures. Here there was no second level.

Fair arbitration depended on the arbitrator. The platform genuinely needed to be roughly fair to keep its business alive. That was a real constraint, but a commercial constraint, not an enforceable right.

The distinction appears on the day it is needed. Commercial constraints protect people who can still bring business. Someone defrauded once who decides never to return is precisely the person that constraint reaches least effectively.

The roughly 9.5 million BTC is cumulative merchandise sales recorded in the server data, not proof of a one-to-one set of independent on-chain transfers. Transfers into and out of the platform can be inspected on-chain; escrow and internal balance changes also require platform records.

Converting sales into dollars can yield roughly $1.2 billion or more than $200 million, depending on the valuation dates. The approximately $184 million forfeiture order uses a separate legal basis; it is not simply the same total repriced for another year.

All three numbers are right, but measure three different things.

The chain records only how many coins, from which address to which address, and when.

What happened after a package arrived, who signed for it, and what happened to that person afterward: the chain says nothing.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
