Silk Road

A market excluded from public institutions rebuilt escrow, arbitration, reputation, and enforcement in private form.

Han Qin · 6 September 2026 · English rewrite

IN THIS ESSAY

  1. A supposedly institutionless market
  2. Trust changed address
  3. Power entered through the remainder
  4. Exclusion is not absence

A supposedly institutionless market

Silk Road is often remembered as Bitcoin meeting an illegal marketplace. Its more revealing feature is how quickly a market outside ordinary legal enforcement rebuilt institutional tools: escrow held payment, ratings summarized past trades, administrators arbitrated disputes, and commissions financed the system. Removing public institutions did not remove the need for rules.

Trust changed address

Bitcoin reduced the need to trust a payment processor with censorship power. Buyers still had to trust product descriptions, sellers, delivery networks, and the market operator. Escrow moved the decisive authority into a platform account. Reputation helped, but could be manipulated and could not inspect a parcel before it arrived. The system did not abolish trust; it relocated and privatized it.

Power entered through the remainder

Rules that looked automatic depended on administrators who could freeze funds, identify fraud, change policy, or abuse access. Law enforcement entered through people, servers, operational mistakes, and undercover relationships—precisely the parts the transaction protocol did not define. The market’s boundary was not where its ideology placed it.

Exclusion is not absence

When participants are shut out of one institutional order, they may construct another. The new order can solve genuine problems and reproduce old concentrations under less visible names. The question is therefore not whether a market has institutions, but who writes them, who can appeal, who can exit, and where responsibility goes when private rules fail.
