Ethereum

I. A White Paper That Kept Changing

The first problem in studying this history is unexpected: the document everyone calls the 2013 white paper was not a text fixed forever on its publication date.

Vitalik Buterin circulated an Ethereum proposal among a small network in late 2013. In 2015, he specifically posted a link to a white paper dated December 2013.

Yet the “original whitepaper” on ethereum.org today explicitly identifies 2014 as its publication year.

The ethereum.org project later investigated the version history. Earlier drafts did exist in December 2013. The GitHub version now treated as canonical was committed by Stephan Tual on February 16, 2014. Buterin continued revising afterward. The team judged the December 10, 2014 version closest to the final text with his personal involvement.

It is therefore historically unsound to project every sentence on the current website backward, word for word, into late 2013.

This itself characterizes the domain. On-chain records are designed to resist rewriting, but a newly written block does not thereby acquire absolute finality; the history the network accepts still depends on consensus rules. The words describing that chain continue to be revised. One is a conditional mechanism for tamper resistance; the other is normal document maintenance. Neither is wrong.

People usually remember the latter.

No one reads every block, whereas white papers are written for people. How something is understood depends on which document is most widely read, not which record changes least.

The version question is not a pedantic introduction. It directly affects three widely repeated claims.

First, did the original 2013 white paper introduce the phrase “world computer”?

Second, is the 60,102,216 ETH in the current white paper the actual figure calculated by the genesis-generation script?

Third, is the commonly cited premine of about twelve million coins for the team and foundation identical to the generator’s sale-total multiplied by 19.8%?

No to all three. The following sections examine them separately.

The point is not correction for its own sake. Each remembered version is tidier than the material itself: a phrase attributed earlier, figures made rounder or larger. Tidy accounts travel farther. This has already appeared several times in the series.

The $41 in the first essay and $1.2 billion in the fourth are examples.

The construct examined here is the series’ first general-purpose construct.

Earlier measures each measured one thing: hash power in the second essay, custodial balances in the third, commission rates in the fourth.

This time the object is different.

It measures execution itself.

Earlier measures dealt with things already there: hash power, balances, transaction amounts. Execution is something happening, and before it begins no one knows whether it will end.

Pricing such a thing is the real subject of the next sections.

II. Lack of State

The white paper’s primary problem was not that Bitcoin could not be programmed.

It was programmable, but the expressive power and state model of its scripting system were insufficient for a general-purpose on-chain application platform.

The paper listed four limitations: lack of Turing-completeness, value-blindness, lack of state, and blockchain-blindness.

Each phrase had a specific meaning.

Bitcoin Script deliberately lacked general looping mechanisms. It could not readily control arbitrary subdivisions of transferable value. An unspent output itself represented only spent or unspent status, not an internal variable a program could keep modifying across calls. The available on-chain context was also restricted.

The white paper did not claim Script was useless. Its judgment was that implementing withdrawal limits, decentralized exchanges, financial contracts, or persistent organizational state under those constraints required extensive specialized protocols.

At least three directly relevant approaches preceded it. Where each encountered limits determined what needed changing.

Mastercoin’s 2012 proposal, a second-generation protocol on Bitcoin, used bitcoin transactions as carriers for new currencies, assets, and trading rules. Miners confirmed underlying transactions; software understanding the new rules reinterpreted their higher-level meaning.

Its difficulty was that every complex new feature required extending the metaprotocol itself.

Colored Coins marked particular unspent outputs to represent off-chain assets such as shares, commodities, and notes. Buterin participated in related discussions before Ethereum took shape.

It demonstrated that one chain could carry asset meanings beyond currency. Those meanings depended on color-tracking clients and inherited the output model’s and scripting system’s expressive limits. It offered asset marking and tracking, not a shared execution environment with arbitrary persistent internal state.

Nick Szabo had already used and defined “smart contract” in the mid-1990s. His 1994 and 1997 writings discussed digital cash, cryptographic protocols, automated performance, collateral, and machine-executable transactional relationships.

This was a direct intellectual precursor. Gavin Wood’s later review in the Yellow Paper distinguished it explicitly: those works introduced smart-contract and cryptolaw ideas, but did not provide a concrete system in which an open consensus network jointly executed arbitrary contracts in this way.

The original material therefore did not emphasize being first to imagine smart contracts.

It emphasized unifying purposes scattered across those approaches on a chain with general code execution and persistent state.

The three approaches met essentially the same wall. Mastercoin had to extend its metaprotocol for each feature. Colored Coins marked assets within an existing output model. Szabo’s work supplied a developed idea without a machine jointly executing it.

What was missing was not an idea but a place where arbitrary rules could be put.

The cost of building that place is the next section’s subject.

The origin of “world computer” also needs correction.

In reliably verifiable primary material, Buterin wrote in April 2015 that Gavin Wood had begun using the phrase to describe the ideal platform.

In substance, the 2013–2014 vision was already a general-purpose globally shared state machine. As an exact attribution, however, putting “world computer” in quotation marks as original late-2013 white-paper wording is unsafe.

The technical substitution can be compressed into one sentence.

Bitcoin’s basic unit of state is an unspent transaction output: spending references and fully consumes old outputs, then creates new ones. Here, the basic unit is an account object associated with an address: spending directly changes balances and increments the sender’s nonce.

The key design change was not replacing outputs with a bank-like balance field.

It was making the contract itself an object with identity, balance, and internal state preserved across transactions.

Identity matters. A contract has its own address, can receive money, be called, and call others. These actions run according to code, without a person manually carrying out each step.

The third essay's sentence now turns another way. The chain checks signatures, not whether the signer rightfully owns the key. Here, a class of accounts runs code when called rather than initiating transactions with its own signing key. That does not mean a contract has no developers, beneficiaries, or administrative powers; such arrangements may be written into the code itself.

That is what the “lack of state” limitation sought to address.

III. Run Only What You Buy

To understand gas, first understand the EVM.

It is a deterministic execution machine in which all validating nodes must obtain the same result. Contract code compiles to bytecode. When a transaction triggers it, every node validating that block must replay the same execution and derive the same new state from the same old state.

The world computer is therefore not a cloud computer distributing large computational jobs among spare processors worldwide.

It is first a highly replicated consensus state machine. Many validators separately repeat the same consensus-relevant computation.

This immediately creates a problem.

A Turing-complete language can express an infinite loop. Even without one, programs can construct extraordinarily expensive recursion, storage, and calls.

Nodes cannot generally determine before execution when a program will stop.

This is the halting problem. It is not an engineering difficulty but a mathematically proven undecidability result. No general program can determine whether another program will halt.

The solution here was not to abandon Turing-completeness.

It was to price each execution step and limit in advance how much execution could be purchased.

Pause there.

A logically undecidable problem was circumvented by a mechanism capable of settlement.

If you cannot calculate when it will stop, let it run only as far as it has paid for.

The construct is doing something absent from the first four essays.

It does not solve the problem. It prices it.

Its approach differs from the earlier cases. It neither hides the problem nor pretends it is absent. It takes insolubility as given and builds a charging system on that premise.

The economics series contains distant relatives of this move. Its fifth essay’s self-verifying ledger closed by leaving some columns unquestioned. Its tenth essay’s discipline placed unanswerable questions outside its boundary.

This case is different. It neither evades nor excludes. It acknowledges that the problem has no solution and installs a meter on that basis.

The mechanism works as follows. Different opcodes, storage operations, and data processing consume different gas amounts. A sender specifies maximum gas and a price per unit. Execution deducts gas step by step. If it runs out, the call fails and the corresponding state changes revert, while fees for computation already consumed are not refunded.

An infinite loop therefore no longer means endless execution by network nodes.

It means the loop runs only until the transaction’s purchased gas is exhausted.

Three quantities often confused must be separated.

An opcode’s gas cost is the protocol’s relative resource price for a computation or storage operation.

The gas price is how much ETH the sender will pay per unit of gas.

The gas limit caps consumption by a transaction or block.

The first converts different resources into a common unit. The second creates a fee market between senders and block producers. The third sets a hard boundary on worst-case execution and block workloads.

So far, the design is elegant.

The remainder appears immediately, at the most important point.

In 2016, the network suffered a round of denial-of-service attacks. The foundation issued announcements and implemented protocol changes repricing a group of opcodes.

The lesson is that gas solves the structural requirement that resources have prices and execution have bounds. It does not mean every opcode’s specific price was correct from the outset.

Pricing makes an undecidable problem operationally manageable.

Whether the pricing itself is right cannot be verified on-chain.

The chain can verify gas consumed and money paid. It cannot verify whether a computational step should really cost that amount. The schedule was made by people, and people got it wrong.

The remainder therefore has a clear location. Not in executed code, which runs precisely, but in the price schedule. The chain can verify execution results, not whether the schedule is right, because no on-chain criterion establishes that.

The trade-off of Turing-completeness can be stated precisely. It gains loops, arbitrary branches, and persistent state, so the base protocol need not anticipate future applications. The price is that arbitrary programs cannot be statically guaranteed to halt, every validator repeats consensus computation, and processing, storage, and bandwidth require a sufficiently accurate gas schedule.

IV. 6,290 Coins

The presale must be examined in four layers: legal sale terms, promotional blog posts, the actual sale algorithm, and the genesis generator.

Several familiar figures diverged between those layers.

The formal window ran from July 22, 2014 at 22:00 GMT to September 2 at 21:59:59 GMT, forty-two days. The foundation blog gave the local ending time as September 2 at 23:59.

The price curve was: 2,000 ETH per BTC for the first fourteen days; a reduction of thirty per day from days fifteen to thirty-six, beginning at 1,970, then 1,940; and approximately 1,337 for the final six days. The generator used the integer 1337.

The advertised linear decline was therefore implemented more like daily steps.

On-chain aggregation generally puts funds raised at approximately 31,529 BTC. Contemporary dollar valuations commonly give around $18 million.

BTC is the original settlement unit. Dollar values depend on the date and price used and should not be treated as a precise fundraising total.

There are two conflicting figures for ETH sold.

The white paper on ethereum.org still gives 60,102,216 ETH.

Running the official genesis-generation script on the sale data produces 60,108,506.26 ETH.

The difference is around 6,290 coins.

The latter is correct because it reconciles exactly with actual genesis supply.

Pause there.

A chain renowned for tamper resistance has a genesis allocation recorded as a different number in the document describing it.

The chain cannot be changed. The document can, and has been.

This is not fabrication. The generator output reconciles exactly with genesis supply and anyone can rerun it. The white-paper figure remained from an earlier version and was not updated accordingly.

It demonstrates a simple point: where a number is written determines both how easily it can be changed and how easily it is remembered. Those tendencies often run in opposite directions.

The formal sale documents specified three conceptual pools: purchaser allocations, X; early contributors, 0.099X; and the foundation’s long-term reserve, also 0.099X.

The two nonsale pools therefore totaled 19.8% of sales, not exactly 20%.

Substituting the generator’s 60,108,506.26 yields this genesis structure: purchasers, 60,108,506.26 ETH, or 83.472454% of actual genesis supply; early contributors and foundation, 5,950,742.11974 each, or 8.263773% each; the two together, 11,901,484.23948, or 16.527546%; total genesis supply, 72,009,990.49948 ETH.

A difference between legal allocation pools and on-chain pools deserves separate attention.

The terms described two pools of 9.9%, for early contributors and the foundation.

The genesis script did not create two vesting contracts with distinct rules. It multiplied the total sold by 198, divided by 1000, and credited the amount to one foundation address.

The two 9.9% pools were therefore organizational and accounting allocations, not separate protocol-level time locks at genesis.

This also affects unlocking arrangements.

Presale purchasers received encrypted wallet files. Their ETH could not be used or transferred before genesis; only afterward did it become spendable mainnet balances.

The terms provided allocation at genesis for the two pools, without specifying an on-chain vesting contract. Some later secondary research says early contributors were reportedly required to hold for at least a year, while acknowledging that individual distribution arrangements and many personal addresses could not be established on-chain.

The publicly verifiable sale terms and genesis code do not establish an on-chain one-year lockup in primary material.

Any separate individual contributor agreement was outside those public presale documents.

Participant counts require the same discipline. Genesis data shows accounts produced by the sale, later parsed at roughly 8,890; other providers round to about 8,800.

That does not establish 8,890 natural persons. One person could create multiple accounts; one account could represent several people. The sale published no identity register.

Now trace both ends.

The project received approximately 31,529 BTC in operating funds and two pools representing 16.53% of genesis supply.

Buyers bore project-failure risk, complete immobility before genesis, and a timing choice created by the declining daily price curve.

The arrangement was clearly documented for anyone to read.

The three conditions from the second essay were not all present in the same way. Public pricing and allocation made the terms calculable; buyers could choose whether and when to buy during forty-two days. But declining to participate was a choice before entry, not an exit after purchase. The terms excluded refunds, and ETH could not be transferred before genesis. Once payment had been made, exit was materially restricted.

One thing differed from earlier essays.

The mining-equipment buyer in the second essay at least bought a machine intended to be manufactured. Here, buyers purchased computational rights on a chain without its first block.

At purchase, that machine was only documentation and code. The thing bought would first exist at genesis.

The white paper itself offered an argument for the two reserve pools, worth presenting intact.

It did not argue that the team deserved unconditional coins. It made a counterfactual comparison: without ETH reserves, the organization could obtain economically similar funding by selling more ETH and holding more BTC. Holding ETH instead gave it a direct stake in the network’s value.

The critical evidence also belongs here. One data provider reported that approximately one hundred presale accounts obtained around 40% of crowdsale ETH.

That remains account concentration, not proof that one hundred individuals owned 40%. One buyer can split addresses, and multiple addresses may belong to one entity.

Both accounts are presented here without commentary.

V. A Boardroom Brawl

The eight commonly listed cofounders are Vitalik Buterin, Mihai Alisie, Anthony Di Iorio, Amir Chetrit, Charles Hoskinson, Gavin Wood, Jeffrey Wilcke, and Joseph Lubin.

Di Iorio’s later recollection further distinguishes the formation: Buterin, Di Iorio, Hoskinson, Alisie, and Chetrit were the original five in late 2013; Wood, Wilcke, and Lubin joined in early 2014.

“Cofounder” itself was partly a designation consolidated after the project formed, not a signed list effective on day one.

The decisive organizational conflict occurred at a Swiss gathering in June 2014.

Later participant accounts agree on the central division. One side favored a for-profit company with equity, financing capacity, and clearer corporate governance. The other favored nonprofit support for an open-source core protocol, avoiding founders’ ownership of the protocol as corporate shareholders.

Buterin chose the latter.

Looking back in 2022, he described the division directly: many participants cared about making money, and that was the largest disagreement.

Hoskinson’s recollection emphasized governance and financing. He favored a more formal company, venture capital, and explicit decision mechanisms. He later described the confrontation as a boardroom brawl.

Both statements need evidentiary labels: recollections years later, not verbatim minutes of the 2014 meeting.

Hoskinson left the core project in June 2014.

Chetrit also left during this period. Evidence about him is substantially thinner. Later narratives describe both as being asked to leave, while other recollections emphasize that Chetrit’s involvement had already declined. Their motives cannot simply be treated as identical on that basis.

No detailed contemporaneous explanation from Chetrit comparable to Hoskinson’s was found in publicly verifiable material.

Di Iorio was not asked to leave in the same sense. He later redirected his attention toward Canada and his own projects. Lubin founded ConsenSys in 2014, building the chain through an ecosystem company.

Gavin Wood remained the foundation’s technical leader, contributing the Yellow Paper and client implementations. He later described leaving the foundation after about two years in that role and establishing the company then called Ethcore.

Wood’s departure therefore belongs to a separate phase around 2016, not the 2014 company-versus-foundation split.

The legal structure formed in July 2014 was also more complex than “they decided on a nonprofit foundation.”

The presale’s contractual seller was Ethereum Switzerland GmbH, a Swiss-law entity in Zug. Not all sale-related legal acts were placed directly in the foundation.

At the same time, genesis allocation reserved a long-term fund for the foundation equal to 9.9% of the quantity sold in the presale, or about 8.26% of actual genesis supply.

The actual arrangement therefore had two entities: a Swiss commercial entity conducting sales and related operations, and a Swiss nonprofit foundation supporting the protocol over the long term.

The disagreement produced not a pure nonprofit structure, but a separation of commercial operations from nonprofit support. Arguments often end this way: neither side simply wins; each obtains something, written into different documents.

The legal framing was deliberate.

The 2014 sale documents defined ETH as computational fuel and a software product needed to run the platform, not company stock, profit participation, or governance rights. They described payment for computation and storage, listing reasons to buy as developing or running applications, paying for application services, using other platform tokens, and supporting the platform.

They explicitly stated that holding ETH conferred no ownership or governance rights in Ethereum organizations and no right to a refund.

A key phrase was that this was not an investment solicitation.

The contemporaneous foundation blog was even more direct: ether was a product, not a security or an investment solicitation. It offered no voting rights and no guarantee of future value.

A project’s contractual declaration is not a securities-law determination by a regulator.

This distinction remains useful throughout the series. How a document describes itself and what it legally is are determined by different people. In this domain, the former is often quoted as the latter because it is neatly written and readily accessible.

VI. Setting Aside the Original Fundraising

The later US regulatory history cannot be reduced to “the SEC determined ETH is not a security.”

The verifiable timeline is as follows.

In July 2014, the project called ETH fuel and a software product, sold by a Swiss entity. That was a private legal characterization.

On June 14, 2018, SEC Corporation Finance Director William Hinman said in a speech that, given the network’s then-current decentralization, current ether offers and sales were not securities transactions.

Two qualifications are commonly omitted.

First, he explicitly set aside the fundraising accompanying ether’s creation before making that assessment.

Second, the SEC page specified that the speech expressed the author’s views, not a formal Commission rule.

It cannot therefore retrospectively establish that the 2014 sale had been cleared of securities-law issues.

During Gensler’s tenure, the SEC issued no Commission rule declaring ether permanently a security or permanently not one. Debate over proof of stake, staking, and the reach of investment-contract law returned.

On May 23, 2024, the Commission approved exchange rule changes to list spot ether products. The core analysis concerned strong correlation between spot and CFTC-regulated futures markets and the adequacy of surveillance arrangements.

That approval did not announce a universal rule that all ETH transactions would forever fall outside securities law.

Subsequent exchange filings placed such products within the commodity-based trust-share framework.

On July 10, 2024, the CFTC chair told Congress that a federal district court judgment had reaffirmed bitcoin and ether as commodities under the Commodity Exchange Act. Senior CFTC officials continued using that characterization in 2026.

Together, these do not form a neat sequence of formal nonsecurity status, formal reversal, then formal restoration.

They are parallel statements at different institutional levels, by different agencies, with different legal characters.

A division director’s personal analysis; Commission approval of a product category; a chair’s congressional reference to a court judgment; and renewed jurisdictional disputes during another period.

Their legal effects differ. They did not cancel one another out.

This is why the history is frequently misstated. People imagine regulation as one institution providing a single answer. In practice, several institutions ask under different laws, and no one is responsible for making their answers identical.

The result is strange: something has been sold for ten years and held by tens of millions, while different institutions still answer separately what category it belongs to.

What something is depends on which institution is asking, and for what purpose.

The economics series showed another version: the same thing has different identities in different ledgers; cooking for oneself does not count, hiring a cook does. Here, the same thing can have different properties across legal regimes: potentially a security under one law, a commodity under another. Both judgments can apply because they answer different questions.

For holders, however, the consequences differ profoundly.

What determines whether they may trade, how much tax they owe, and where they turn after a failure is not the thing’s intrinsic nature, but the institution governing the matter at the time.

VII. Five Thousand Gas

Frontier’s July 30, 2015 launch should not be read backward through the standards of the later mature mainnet.

Ethereum's own roadmap described a first-stage, minimally viable operating network for miners and developers—not a finished product ordinary users could safely use without difficulty.

One detail best captures its condition.

The initial block gas limit was deliberately hard-coded extremely low: 5,000 per block.

An ordinary ETH transfer itself required about 21,000.

For the first days, ordinary transactions could not fit into blocks.

The August 4 thaw began raising the target toward 3,141,592. The protocol allowed miners to adjust each block’s limit relative to its parent by no more than approximately one-thousandth. The foundation estimated at least six hours to rise from 5,000 to a level allowing ordinary transfers, and more than a day to reach about three million.

During its earliest hours, the chain deliberately prevented use.

This is worth remembering because it contrasts with many later launches. New systems usually race to attract users. Here, the chain ran first to establish that it worked, then opened the door.

The caution had a history. The exchange in Essay 03 operated for three years without continuous reconciliation. Essay 02 described equipment makers taking money from twenty thousand people before delivery. By 2015, the domain had accumulated enough accidents to learn from.

Freezing and thawing were part of a safe-start procedure.

Before launch, more aggressive protections had been considered: a later chain reset, paying only a small fraction of normal rewards, and centralized checkpoints. These were abandoned before the actual launch. Frontier was therefore a continuing real-value chain from the beginning, not a testnet scheduled for disposal.

The same announcement still emphasized that software development was far from finished.

There is more direct evidence.

When releasing Homestead, the foundation recalled that only then did it feel able to remove the strikethrough from “safe” on the website.

That word had been crossed out.

The subsequent stages were Frontier on July 30, 2015; Frontier Thawing on August 4; Homestead around March 14, 2016 at block 1,150,000; Byzantium on October 16, 2017 at block 4,370,000, reducing block rewards from 5 ETH to 3; and Constantinople with St. Petersburg around February 28, 2019 at block 7,280,000.

The original roadmap also listed Serenity as the next major stage. Unlike the preceding stages, it did not materialize as one discrete early hard fork. Projecting today’s mechanisms backward as a fourth upgrade already scheduled in 2015 is likewise inaccurate.

The toolchain’s maturation can also be checked.

In a July 2014 team Q&A, developers were told that high-level back-end languages included Serpent, Mutan, and LLL, with front ends interacting through JavaScript bindings. The team explicitly said tutorials and introductory resources would be improved after the codebase stabilized further.

This is direct evidence that the development environment was still changing rapidly at presale time.

Olympic, in May 2015, was the final public testnet before Frontier. The foundation offered a total of 25,000 ETH in rewards for stress-testing transactions, the virtual machine, mining, and networking.

By Frontier, at least Go and C++ implementations were serving as consensus clients.

In October 2015, however, a foundation security announcement warned that differences in their implementation of an opcode could cause consensus problems.

These sources establish two things simultaneously: multiple clients existed, and consistency across implementations still needed continuing repair.

Early developer counts lacked today’s explicit statistical definitions. One lower-bound indication comes from the July 2014 Q&A, where Di Iorio said dozens had worked on the project since it began.

That supports dozens of core and peripheral contributors, not a count of independent developers across the ecosystem.

This is the same kind of problem as the five measures in the second essay. To count people working on an open project, first define “working.” Does a code contribution count? A single issue? Client documentation? Without a definition, the number is meaningless. Once defined, it measures only what the definition can encompass.

That sentence has already appeared three times in different forms: decentralization in the second essay, who reads the ledger in the third, market size in the fourth. Here it is the number of people in an open-source project.

VIII. Two World Computers

Actual use after Frontier requires two measurement bases to be separated.

Transaction counts show how frequently the network is used. Contract counts show how many code-bearing addresses have been deployed.

Neither equals the number of independent, real applications with actual users.

Later whole-chain research estimated approximately ten thousand daily transactions in January 2016.

Luu and colleagues’ 2016 research examined the first 1,460,000 mainnet blocks and obtained 19,366 existing contracts. The paper noted that around 15,000 had been deployed in the preceding six months alone.

At minimum, within a year of launch, deploying one’s own code to a public chain was no longer confined to a few demonstrations.

But 19,366 cannot be interpreted as 19,366 distinct applications.

Later whole-chain topology research found high contract activity but notably low code diversity. Many contracts were direct or near copies of others.

Created-address counts therefore substantially exceed counts of independent codebases, products, or development teams.

This is the same measurement trap as listing counts versus sales in the fourth essay.

Early empirical work offered a more useful cross-section of what contracts actually did.

Bartoletti and Pompianu classified early contracts into categories including finance, notarization, games, wallets, and libraries. Later summaries note that, in their sample of 834 contracts, more than two-thirds concerned managing, pooling, or distributing funds.

This overlapped strongly with the white paper’s token systems, financial contracts, and autonomous organizations. It cannot represent all contracts on the chain, because the study relied on available, classifiable source code.

Other uses appeared that the white paper did not promote centrally but that suited permissionless programmable money. A 2017 study of the early chain identified 189 Ponzi-scheme contracts through source-code and bytecode similarity.

Their number cannot represent the entire network. They show that permissionless deployment of financial logic supports speculative and fraudulent programs as well as intended financial applications.

The rule must remain clear: remainder carries no value judgment. Being accommodated by the system does not make something good; spilling outside it does not make something bad.

Permissionless means the same thing on both sides: the system does not ask what you intend to do.

At the other end was an example close to the original vision. In 2016, The DAO combined autonomous organization, crowdfunding, and on-chain asset control at scale. Its contract vulnerability then became one of the chain’s first major security and governance events. In June 2016, the foundation published successive notices on contract security, vulnerabilities, and fork discussions.

The engineering assumption that contracts were immutable and code was the rule had already collided directly with real financial risk.

Return now to two readings of “world computer.”

Measured by the existence of public, permissionless arbitrary programs with persistent state, there were almost twenty thousand contract addresses by 2016. Finance, wallets, games, notarization, and autonomous organizations were all observable.

Measured by the suitability of general global computation for direct on-chain execution, the opposite fact existed from day one: all validators repeated consensus-relevant computation. The team itself acknowledged in 2014 that scalability remained unresolved and planned to move application front ends and most data distribution off the main chain.

The two evaluations use different definitions.

This is not wordplay. It determines whether one regards the chain as a computer for any program or a ledger for any rule.

Both descriptions are technically defensible, but create entirely different expectations. Under the former, congestion and expense are failures. Under the latter, they merely show demand exceeding capacity, and the ledger never promised infinite capacity.

Many later controversies ultimately arise from those expectations colliding.

Earlier constructs measured things outside themselves: hash power, custodial balances, commissions, stars.

This one measures execution itself. How much for a computational step? How much for a storage operation?

It prices execution because it cannot determine when execution will stop.

An unanswerable computational question becomes a question for which a fee can be charged.

The method has a premise worth stating: every important resource cost can be converted into one common unit. Processor time, storage, bandwidth, and state growth all become gas.

If conversion is accurate, the system operates. If it is inaccurate, underpriced operations become attack entry points. That is what happened in 2016.

This is the construct’s central action, and it has been highly successful. The chain has run for ten years without being stopped by an infinite loop.

The remainder occupies the same place.

What each step should cost cannot itself be verified on-chain. People set the schedule. It has been wrong, has been revised, and will be revised again.

The chain requires agreement on one thing: with the same starting state, inputs, and protocol rules, validating nodes must derive the same result to agree on the state. That is a requirement of deterministic execution, not a historical guarantee that client implementations never diverged over ten years. The consensus security alerts of 2015 show that maintaining agreement also requires finding bugs and repairing software.

It cannot guarantee two other things.

First, what each step of that code ought to cost.

Second, whether the result is what its author intended.

The next essay asks the second question, in one of the most expensive ways then possible.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
