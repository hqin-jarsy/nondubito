1. Sixteen Years

For the final essay in this series, the task is simple: record what has already happened, taken effect, been measured, and been disclosed.

No predictions.

Recording it is harder than it sounds.

The most common source of misjudgment in this field today is still not a shortage of numbers.

It is the same word being used for entirely different denominators.

That sentence has appeared many times in this series, but it occupies a different place in the final essay. It is no longer merely a methodological reminder within a section. Taking the two pizzas in 2010 as the starting point of this account of everyday transactions, sixteen years later it has become one of the findings running through the stories.

As long as there is no agreed way to measure something, debate about it cannot be brought to a close. That is precisely where most debates in this field remain stuck today.

“Stablecoin transaction volume” can mean at least five things: raw on-chain transfers; amounts after removing repeated hops within the same transaction; amounts after filtering out bots and high-frequency addresses; amounts a data provider classifies as economic activity; or payments for which both parties and the nature of the goods or services can ultimately be identified.

These five levels currently differ by one to two orders of magnitude.

The same problem runs through this entire account.

An exchange's market share can be measured by self-reported trading volume, a data provider's cleaned volume, spot trading, perpetual contracts, all derivatives, or open interest.

Ethereum staking concentration can be measured by protocol, custodian, validator operator, withdrawal credentials, or the entities actually running the nodes.

Mining-pool share is a share of coordination inferred from labels on recently produced blocks, not a measure of who ultimately owns the mining machines.

And a “holder” may be an address, an exchange account, or an individual estimated by a model.

For each number below, its denominator will therefore be stated wherever possible.

This is not merely caution; it is necessary. Many important misjudgments in this history came not only from calculation errors, but from comparing numbers with different denominators, dates, or definitions.

In Essay 7, the claim “eighty percent were scams” counted projects, not money. In Essay 13, energy figures came from different years and models. In Essay 17, the 120% used dollar claims fixed at depressed petition-date prices. In Essay 18, two figures reported for the same date used different measurement definitions and differed by approximately thirty-two percentage points.

Each figure has a source and a measurement definition; its precision still needs separate checking. Even if each was calculated correctly under its own method, that would not make them directly combinable in one account.

What was wrong were the plus signs and division signs.

One more principle must be established first: a transfer of value on-chain must not automatically be interpreted as an equal amount of real-world economic activity.

One issuer disclosed that on-chain transfers of its token reached $14.8 trillion in the second quarter of 2026.

That number can be checked transaction by transaction. It is real.

It includes trading, liquidation, decentralized finance, and internal treasury management, however. It does not establish that $14.8 trillion in payments for goods and services occurred during that quarter.

These differences in measurement also mean something else: this time, there is no way to produce a tidy-looking consolidated account of sixteen years.

The final section will return to what that blank space means.

This time, the remainder also lies within that blank space.

In the preceding twenty essays, the remainder lay between the chain and the world, between code and intent, between two chains, between a certificate and what it referred to. This time, it lies somewhere more ordinary: between numbers that cannot be added together.

These figures need not contradict one another. Each has its own method, sample, and source; some even come from the same institution and document. First establish what each measures, then decide whether they can be used together.

They simply do not measure the same thing.

2. Five Numbers

As of today, stablecoins are the application closest to having established substantial real, nonspeculative use.

Yet large on-chain flows and large payment volumes remain different things.

For 2025 and nearby observation windows, five frequently juxtaposed sets of figures can be found. Their chains, periods, and measured objects do not fully coincide.

Aggregating the chains it covers, one card network recorded more than $51 trillion in raw transfers over the preceding year. That number includes bots, internal activity at trading venues, and hops within contracts. It measures token movement on-chain, not payments.

After filtering out noise, the same organization arrived at approximately $10.2 trillion. Its method takes only the largest one-way amount in each transaction. For unlabeled addresses, it filters out high-frequency and high-value patterns exceeding 1,000 transfers or $10 million sent over the preceding thirty days. It also relies on entity labels.

The result is cleaner, but still includes centralized and decentralized exchanges, lending, minting, and burning. It still cannot be called payment volume.

Another data provider, using its own entity labels and filtering method, arrived at about $28 trillion in “real economic activity” for 2025. Its definition is broader than payments for goods and services.

A third organization's research removed unusually large amounts, bots, suspected bots, internal transfers, and intermediary routing, arriving at approximately $4.2 trillion, about 7% of the total volume it observed.

Further identifying bilateral payments within that $4.2 trillion produced a range of $350 billion to $550 billion.

This is currently among the closer approximations of a global lower-bound range for real stablecoin payments.

Secondary reports attributed an estimate of about $390 billion in payment-related flows to the Bank for International Settlements, similar in scale to that range.

An entirely different method, collecting payment companies' actual business data directly and matching them to on-chain transactions, produced an annualized figure of about $118 billion in August 2025.

Its coverage is incomplete, so it is a lower bound, not a global total.

Its nature is entirely different from the preceding figures. Those work backward from the chain. This one works forward from company accounts.

One starts with broad on-chain coverage and filters through models. The other starts with company records, allowing concrete business activity to be checked but leaving incomplete coverage. The $4.2 trillion and $118 billion differ arithmetically by more than thirtyfold. That is not a disagreement between fully matched payment estimates: coverage and classification have not been aligned.

Five numbers: $51 trillion, $10.2 trillion, $28 trillion, $4.2 trillion, and a range from somewhat over $300 billion to somewhat over $500 billion.

Nearby periods, different coverage, and the same broad class of on-chain activity reclassified by different methods.

Large differences alone do not establish that one calculation must be wrong. Separate sources do not guarantee that every calculation is entirely correct either.

None can represent the whole undertaking on its own.

What matters most to preserve is not one correct answer among them, but the range itself.

Which number is chosen shapes how the system is understood. The $51 trillion may suggest the scale of global payments; the $500 billion may suggest an emerging payments industry. But the first measures gross transfers and the second approaches identified payments. They are not simply large and small answers to the same question.

These accounts can coexist if their labels remain attached. Remove the labels, and two apparently opposite conclusions may simply be answers to different questions.

Which figure is cited therefore usually depends less on the evidence than on what the speaker wants to argue.

This is one of the field's hardest problems: both sides can reach opposite conclusions without lying.

The International Monetary Fund issued a particular warning in 2026 as well: frequently cited annual transfer volumes above $30 trillion and cross-border flows in the trillions include large amounts of trading-ecosystem activity, arbitrage, bots, and intermediary routing, rather than payments by businesses or households.

There is a reasonably credible sample on the business-payments side. An ongoing survey of payment service providers found that business-to-business stablecoin payments grew from less than $100 million a month in early 2023 to more than $6 billion a month by mid-2025.

The latter is equivalent to an annualized run rate above $72 billion.

It covers only the payment service providers included in the study whose activity could be confirmed, not a global total.

“Cross-border” needs another qualification.

A public chain generally does not reveal the legal locations of the paying and receiving businesses. Geographic attribution requires payment companies' business data, supplemented by information such as network addresses and time zones.

We therefore have a reasonably credible scale for business payments today, but no global total for cross-border business payments that can be independently audited in the way bank statements can.

Calling every cross-border stablecoin transfer a cross-border business payment would count exchange reallocations, capital movements, and personal funds along with them.

The remittance evidence is thinner. The research does identify a separate person-to-person category, much of which is interpreted as remittance-like use. One widely circulated figure is $18 billion, taken from a secondary account in February 2025.

The available primary material is insufficient to reproduce that figure fully. It can therefore be listed only as a secondary industry report, not an established global fact.

3. Zero Point Three and Eight Point Nine Six

The question this series has asked for twenty essays, whether costs have actually fallen, finally has a genuinely independent measurement.

In 2026, the Bank of Italy conducted a mystery-shopping experiment.

Researchers tracked a 200-USDC stablecoin sandwich: local-currency funding, purchase, transfer, sale, and withdrawal. Not every corridor reached a bank account; some unfinished-leg fees were estimated from published rates.

Ten directions linked Italy with Argentina, Brazil, the UAE, South Africa, and Japan. Japan was analyzed separately, leaving eight cost-table corridors. Late-March transactions used different sending and receiving exchanges to avoid internal transfers. Wise simulations were dated April 14, not the same days.

Its definition of cost went beyond fees alone.

Costs were the difference between dollar-equivalent amounts sent and received, divided by 200. Completed legs supplied observations; unfinished withdrawals required labeled estimates, not claims of actual bank receipts.

That includes funding, the purchase spread, on-chain fees, the sale spread, withdrawal fees, card surcharges, and exchange-rate differences. Everything.

This is precisely the last mile that more than a decade of cost discussions had been missing.

Previously, almost every cost comparison had set on-chain fees against the full cost of conventional remittances. That was not a comparison. It placed two different objects side by side.

The measured results were as follows.

Italy to Argentina: 0.30%. A simulation for a conventional remittance provider on the same corridor gave 3.20–3.80%.

Argentina to Italy cost 8.96%; Wise displayed 0.26–0.31%, but did not actually serve that direction. Its quote was not an available alternative.

Reverse the direction, and stablecoin costs change completely. Before comparing alternatives, ask whether the alternative is actually available.

The South African pair was closer: 4.58% outbound versus 5.14–5.15%, and 5.44% inbound, with no comparable quotation.

The UAE pair reversed the advantage: outward, an estimated 7.20% versus 2.24%; inward, 8.95% versus 1.02%. Card funding added roughly 3.8%. The outward withdrawal was not completed; its fee was estimated.

Italy to Brazil was 2.70% versus 2.20%, close, with stablecoins slightly more expensive. Brazil to Italy was 2.21% versus 4.68–4.89%, with stablecoins cheaper.

From 0.30% to 8.96%.

The same technology, the same month, eight corridors.

A thirtyfold difference.

This is not a comparison of different chains or years. The eight stablecoin corridors used similar technology within one month; separately dated conventional quotes were not a simultaneous, fully completed experiment.

The study also broke down the costs. The on-chain leg averaged only about 0.4%, falling as low as 0.01% in individual corridors.

The expensive parts were chiefly at the two ends: fiat funding, stablecoin conversion, and bank withdrawals.

The chain's own technical cost was indeed very low.

Most of the total cost users paid, however, was not paid to the chain.

That sentence can answer the question this series has asked twenty times.

For the two pizzas in Essay 1, the protocol charged no one, while someone paid with a card on the buyer's behalf. Sixteen years later, that card is still there. It has merely been renamed “funding.”

Another independent academic study reached a directionally consistent result: a $200 sandwich using two exchanges to convert dollars into stablecoins and then euros was estimated to cost about 1.6% end to end.

What the studies share is not a fixed stablecoin cost. It is that both put exchanges and on- and off-ramps back into the cost equation.

A 2026 BIS paper and a Federal Reserve research note from the same year therefore both explicitly observed that on-chain transfer costs can be very small, while the cost of a complete cross-border payment, once on- and off-ramps are included, may equal or even exceed bank or fintech channels.

The question can therefore be answered more narrowly today.

The cost of the on-chain settlement leg has become substantially lower.

End-to-end costs, however, show no consistent reduction across corridors. The advantage depends on the destination, exchanges, funding method, foreign-exchange regime, and local off-ramps.

In other words, this technology's cost performance is determined mainly by things other than the technology.

It is determined by the banking systems, exchange controls, card-network rates, and local exchange spreads at the ends of those eight corridors. These differ in every country, and none is under the technology's control.

Where someone sends money from and to therefore matters more to the price they pay than the technology used to send it.

4. That Three Percent

The 0.30% in the previous section deserves to be examined separately, because it supplies the most important explanation in this entire account.

Much of the reason that corridor was so cheap was not that blockchain had lowered the cost.

It was that, in the Argentine market at the time, stablecoins traded against local currency at a premium of about 3% to the official retail dollar exchange rate.

Someone sending money from Italy back to Argentina received more than the official exchange rate when selling stablecoins for pesos in the final step.

The apparent cost of the remittance thus became 0.30%.

Someone going in the opposite direction had to pay that additional 3% to buy stablecoins with pesos. That is why the reverse corridor cost 8.96%.

Now trace the two ends.

Where does that 3% come from?

It comes from local demand for dollars in Argentina. Many people are willing to pay 3% extra for a dollar-denominated asset because the local currency they hold is depreciating.

In a sense, then, those people paid for the money saved by the person remitting from Italy.

This is not an accusation. It is the ordinary expression of an exchange-rate premium in opposite directions.

It establishes something quite concrete: part of what is called “stablecoins saving costs” is actually the realization of a local price premium for crypto dollars.

The premium reflects local dollar demand, exchange restrictions, and market liquidity, rather than a return created from nothing by blockchain arithmetic. Inflation and capital controls are important context, but a price observation alone does not measure how much each contributed.

What blockchain did here was make the premium easier to realize.

Other evidence from high-inflation countries requires the same qualifications.

One compilation put Argentina's stablecoin transactions in 2024 at about $34 billion, identifying inflation, capital controls, and dollar demand as important context.

That $34 billion is a flow, not $34 billion held by residents, and not $34 billion all spent on goods.

For Nigeria, a 2026 IMF thematic publication reported approximately $59 billion in cryptoasset inflows from July 2023 through June 2024. Since 2019, the country had accounted for about 60% of stablecoin inflows into sub-Saharan Africa.

A data provider also recorded a surge in the region's on-chain activity to nearly $25 billion a month in March 2025, with Nigeria contributing substantially after a currency depreciation.

These figures support the existence of real stablecoin demand in regions with high inflation and constrained access to foreign exchange.

Causal attribution, however, must remain narrower than the slogans.

They establish that users are willing to obtain dollar-denominated assets through this channel.

They do not, by themselves, establish that the demand originates in blockchain itself. The underlying economic motives are first to hold dollars, move dollar value across borders, and avoid local-currency depreciation.

Blockchain contributes an accessible channel for issuance, transfer, and trading.

If local residents could already open dollar accounts at low cost, the technology's incremental effect might be entirely different.

Cross-country randomized or natural experiments addressing this point are currently lacking.

This gap recurs more often than any other throughout this history, for the same reason each time. To answer what a technology has brought about, we need to know what would have happened without it.

That counterfactual cannot be observed directly, but research can estimate it through experiments, natural experiments, or other methods under explicit assumptions. What is missing here is not knowledge forever impossible in principle, but reliable identification adequate to this particular question.

For much of the material cited here, association remains the more appropriate description, not a measured causal effect. Associations have value; they should not be made to claim an extra step.

Whether a causal statement holds still depends on the identification strategy, the controls, and alternative explanations not excluded. Ease of circulation cannot decide that question.

Today, more than 99% of all stablecoin supply is dollar-denominated. Two issuers together account for about 84%.

That fact itself suggests the direction of the answer. The store-of-value function here comes, to a considerable extent, from the dollar, not from any price-discovery mechanism.

Someone buying dollar stablecoins in Argentina chiefly seeks transferable dollar-denominated value. That is not the same as directly holding US cash or bank dollars: issuer, reserve, and redemption risks remain. The on-chain version may be chosen because that channel is easier to use.

The first point concerns demand; the second concerns the channel. Keeping them separate allows real uses to be seen without treating token risk as though it were simply the dollar itself.

5. At Which Layer?

“Decentralization” can no longer be answered with one number, because it means five different things at five different layers.

Bitcoin's three largest mining pools currently account for about 57% of block production. But pools do not tell us who ultimately owns the machines. This is a share of coordination inferred from labels on recently produced blocks.

In Ethereum staking, the largest protocol accounts for about one-quarter, while containing dozens of node operators within it.

An L2 network can settle on Ethereum while a single sequencer determines everyday ordering.

A stablecoin's consensus layer can be decentralized while its issuers are highly concentrated.

Centralized exchanges have yet another, entirely different mechanism of concentration.

There is therefore no single node count today that can answer whether the entire system is decentralized.

This is the same wall the series has repeatedly encountered. The five measuring sticks in Essay 2, the 63% and 94.8% in Essay 18, and the three axes in Essay 19 all concern it.

By now, the wall is no longer a measurement difficulty. It is a problem of definition. For “decentralization” to become a testable proposition, we must first specify what is being measured and at which layer.

In practice, that sentence is often omitted in both directions. Promotion omits it to make decentralization sound more complete. Criticism omits it to make decentralization sound more hollow.

Both sides leave out the same qualification.

Censorship resistance must likewise be separated into three layers: the base chain, stablecoin issuers, and on- and off-ramps.

One data provider's estimate for 2025 showed a substantial increase in activity associated with sanctioned entities. What it called state-driven sanctions evasion rose 694% year on year.

This shows that public chains can indeed be used to circumvent some restrictions in conventional finance.

It is evidence of behavior, however, not proof that every transaction successfully escaped eventual enforcement.

Meanwhile, real stablecoin payments often require exchanges and bank on- and off-ramps that verify identities. Many L2s also still have privileged sequencers.

“Blockchain cannot be censored” therefore has meaning only when the chain, self-custodied asset, and layer are specified.

A more defensible version today is that certain base-layer transfers of self-custodied assets are harder for a single intermediary to block than a bank account.

Not that the entire crypto-payments system is uncensorable.

The distinction is practical. The first statement concerns self-custodied transfers under specified conditions. The second extends that property to the entire everyday payment process. The route a user takes determines which layer's restrictions and identity requirements they encounter.

Using regulated exchanges and banks for on- and off-ramps is common and usually involves identity checks. That does not mean every on-chain transfer between wallets rechecks identity at both endpoints.

The marketplace in Essay 4 tried to operate around formal institutions. Today, a large-scale application such as stablecoins reconnects identity and compliance at issuance, redemption, exchanges, and bank channels. How tokens circulate beyond those endpoints still depends on the asset and network.

Institutional adoption requires distinguishing four things: issuance, settlement, custody, and acquiring and currency conversion.

In December 2025, one card network opened US network settlement in a particular stablecoin. As of the end of November 2025, its disclosed monthly stablecoin settlement volume already exceeded $3.5 billion on an annualized basis.

This does not mean a merchant receives stablecoins every time a consumer uses a card.

In 2025, another network allowed certain acquirers in selected regions to settle in stablecoins. Conventional card payments still generally result in merchants receiving fiat currency.

The structure these deployments reveal is therefore that the chain replaces one segment of bookkeeping and settlement, not that intermediaries disappear.

This also confirms the other half of Essay 4's conclusion. Then, the conclusion was that something excluded from one institutional system builds its own. Today, the second half can be added: once it grows up, the original system reaches over and brings it in.

The integration works by each side retaining the segment it handles best.

The chain keeps bookkeeping and settlement. Identity, custody, compliance, disputes, and the last mile remain with the original system.

Financial access is the clearest gap in this entire account.

The World Bank's Global Findex database still measures financial access through formal accounts, mobile money, payments, savings, and borrowing. It does not include self-custodied blockchain wallets as a global census measure directly comparable to bank accounts.

Hundreds of millions of cryptoasset holders worldwide therefore cannot be taken to mean hundreds of millions of previously unbanked people have entered the financial system.

Existing research can establish that some users have obtained functions resembling a cross-border dollar account.

What is missing is a global identification of how many previously accountless people obtained a reliable payment or savings instrument for the first time because of blockchain.

6. Three Cases

For thefts, failures, and compensation, the temptation most in need of resistance is to produce one cumulative dollar figure.

In a 2024 retrospective, one organization said nearly $19 billion had been stolen over the roughly thirteen years from 2011 through 2024. Its database covered multiple types of crime and security incident.

Using another definition, a second organization counted around 150 attacks in 2025, with $2.87 billion stolen. One incident alone accounted for about $1.46 billion, roughly 51% of its annual total. It explicitly stated that it covered exchanges, custodians, cross-chain bridges, protocols, and well-evidenced attacks on large individual wallets, excluding small, unverifiable individual cases.

The two figures use different definitions. They therefore cannot rigorously be added to produce a cumulative $21.87 billion through 2025.

That same year, a third data provider called the largest case one of the biggest single digital-asset thefts in history and estimated that hackers associated with one country stole about $2 billion during 2025.

Attribution methods and incident coverage differ, so that figure cannot simply be placed alongside the previous one and added to it either.

There is currently no industry-wide, auditable denominator for a global recovery rate.

The reasons are specific. Some assets are returned by attackers. Some are seized by law enforcement but not yet returned to victims. Sometimes the same number of tokens is recovered after their dollar price has changed. Some losses are later compensated from protocol treasuries. Others enter years of bankruptcy liquidation.

Combining law-enforcement seizures, on-chain recoveries, insurance payments, and bankruptcy distributions into one recovery rate has no accounting meaning.

The current status of three cases tells us more than any percentage.

For the firm discussed in Essay 3, the trustee has extended the deadline for base repayments, early lump-sum repayments, and intermediate repayments to October 31, 2026. The trustee said most principal repayments to creditors whose procedures were complete and problem-free had been made, while the case as a whole remained unfinished.

There is cash, Bitcoin, Bitcoin Cash, different options, and different claim statuses. Recovery rates differ entirely depending on whether they are calculated in original coins, historical dollars, or today's dollars.

No single percentage can summarize every creditor.

The firm discussed in Essay 17 completed its fifth distribution, approximately $900 million, on July 31, 2026. Cumulative percentages reached 105% for several claim classes, 103% for several others, and 120% for the convenience class.

The denominator of these percentages above one hundred is the allowed bankruptcy claim.

They do not mean the original number of coins has been restored, or that compensation is based on 2026 market prices.

A creditor can truthfully say both that they received 105% and that they have not recovered the economic exposure represented by their original coins.

Both statements can be correct. The first has the court-allowed dollar claim as its denominator. The second uses the market value of the coins had they been held until today.

A third firm announced its fourth distribution on January 22, 2026, totaling about $344.4 million, including approximately $257 million from a settlement. Official statements said this was expected to be the final distribution primarily in Bitcoin. Any subsequent distributions would be in dollars or stablecoins. As of August 2026, the claims portal remained open to creditors with unclaimed distributions or possible future distributions.

Because unfinished and future distributions remain, no final compensation percentage can be stated today.

More broadly, cumulative user-asset losses from exchange failures lack a database capable of doing four things at once: consistently classifying bankruptcy separately from attacks; consistently distinguishing customer property from general unsecured claims; using a consistent valuation date for coin prices; and tracking final distributions more than a decade later.

Without any one of these, purported global cumulative losses and recovery rates will mix different legal facts.

The absence of such global figures therefore remains part of the factual situation itself.

The series has said this many times. In its final essay, it needs to be stated more generally.

Lacking a complete database does not mean researchers have no authority to define their measures. A study can specify which cases count as bankruptcy or theft, the valuation date, and the follow-up period. The difficulty is obtaining sufficiently complete, comparable, continuously updated material and having users understand those definitions correctly.

In this field, no single institution can guarantee continuous, complete public reporting by every relevant actor worldwide under one common form.

Banks, courts, regulators, and bankruptcy trustees can each require some records, but cover different entities, jurisdictions, and procedures. Their records do not automatically combine into a global account.

That can be read in two directions. There is no single global center deciding everything for everyone. Yet after a failure, connecting accounts across institutions still requires case-by-case work. Openness and reconciliation costs appear together here.

The two readings describe the same property.

7. What Has Been Narrowed Down

Several debates common ten years ago now have answers that are noticeably narrower and more definite.

On-chain transaction volume is not payment volume. One institution reduced more than $51 trillion to about $10.2 trillion after removing some duplication and bots. Other studies identifying payment uses produced figures on the scale of hundreds of billions. Those are not another filter applied to the identical dataset; coverage and identification methods differ.

“As long as transaction fees are low, cross-border payments are cheaper than banks” is also fairly clearly untenable. Independent measurements put the on-chain leg at about 0.4% on average, while the entire journey can cost around 9%. On- and off-ramps are often the largest component.

For “blockchain eliminates intermediaries,” the material supports a recombination of intermediaries. Exchanges, issuers, banks, card networks, foreign-exchange market makers, identity-verification providers, and off-ramps still exist. The chain can replace one segment of bookkeeping or settlement. Major payment institutions' actual deployments display precisely this structure.

“One address means one user” can now be explicitly rejected. One individual can control many addresses, while one custodian's address can represent many users. Organizations estimating users therefore have to infer people from surveys and on-chain models rather than count addresses.

“All blockchain systems are inherently decentralized” now requires a layer-by-layer rewrite. Mining pools, staking, stablecoin issuers, exchanges, and L2 sequencers have entirely different concentration mechanisms. The issuer layer is especially concentrated.

“The principal value of stablecoins must be everyday retail payments” is broader than current evidence supports. One study defined retail as transfers below $250 and found stablecoin retail velocity of only about 0.08 in the fourth quarter of 2025, compared with US narrow-money velocity of 1.65. Meanwhile, uses in business payments, trading, value storage, and cross-border funds are more prominent.

“Regulation will ultimately mean either prohibition or complete liberalization” is clearly inconsistent with the facts by 2026. The EU, Japan, Hong Kong, and the UAE are operating licensing frameworks. The United States and the United Kingdom have enacted frameworks whose key provisions have not yet reached their effective dates. Singapore has existing digital-payment-token regulation, while the formal implementation status of its dedicated stablecoin framework still needs to be distinguished.

“After a centralized platform fails, on-chain transparency naturally guarantees users get their money back” is answered by the three cases in the previous section. Ultimate recovery still depends chiefly on how assets were segregated, bankruptcy law, courts, trustees, remaining assets, litigation recoveries, and claim-valuation rules.

On-chain traceability has not eliminated the bankruptcy-distribution process.

This deserves particular notice because it overturns one of the field's earliest intuitions: if every transaction is visible, it must always be possible to settle the accounts after something goes wrong.

The three cases show that visible money flows and settled accounts are separated by legal rights, technical tracing, and practical enforcement. Drawing the on-chain route does not make distribution complete itself.

Law settles an account through an entirely different set of things: valuation dates, priority, trustees, courts, and many years of time.

The debates that remain unresolved are chiefly not about whether blockchain has any use at all.

They concern how large those uses are, who bears the net costs, at which layer decentralization should be assessed, and how much of the observed real-world outcome can be attributed to blockchain itself rather than to the dollar, regulatory arbitrage, capital controls, or ordinary fintech.

There is still no single total covering all real payment uses. For 2025 and nearby windows, figures include $28 trillion, $4.2 trillion, a range from somewhat over $300 billion to somewhat over $500 billion, $390 billion, and an annualized $118 billion. Some measure broader activity, some identified payments, and one a payment-company sample's annualized run rate. The evidence rejects treating all on-chain flows as payments, but does not establish a precise global payment total.

The debate has moved from whether payments exist to how they should be classified.

That shift is itself progress. Ten years ago, the debate was whether the thing had any use. Today, it is how much use it has and how that usefulness should be measured.

The latter debate is harder, but closer to what a mature field looks like.

When a field first appears, people debate whether it can stand. Once it has established itself, the debate becomes how to measure it.

On how much stablecoins can lower cross-border costs, existing independent experiments support heterogeneity, not either side's universal proposition.

Whether stablecoins are a payment innovation or infrastructure for dollarization need not be an either-or question. Blockchain may be precisely the transmission technology for demand for dollarization. There is not yet enough causal research to identify whether, without blockchain, these users would have achieved the same result through cash dollars, banks, or mobile money.

Whether financial inclusion comes from the chain or from the point of access likewise remains unresolved. Real users have gained a channel for transferring dollar value that was previously harder to obtain. But no reliable bridge connects global financial-inclusion data with crypto-holder data.

Compared with research on transaction volume and token supply, studies of real-world employment, household welfare, business productivity, and price transmission remain markedly scarce.

This is the same observation the series has been recording since Essay 2. Nineteen essays later, it still holds.

What can be measured on-chain grows ever more precise. The number of answers to questions on the off-chain side has barely increased.

8. The Boundary Is on the Human Side

Sixteen years since the two pizzas in 2010.

From January 3, 2009, to this essay's research cutoff, Bitcoin's main chain has continued producing blocks. The chain in Essay 5, beginning with that block on July 30, 2015, with only 5,000 gas, continued operating after the Merge as well.

They have recorded and settled large numbers of transfers, with amounts precise to the smallest units specified by their protocols. The corresponding public-chain records can be checked again under those rules. The tens of trillions quoted above are dollar values under particular definitions, not tens of trillions of individual transactions.

Yet in writing this account, there is no grand total.

Not because there are too few data.

Because every number has a different denominator.

The $51 trillion is on-chain flow. The $10.2 trillion is transfers after filtering. The $28 trillion is economic activity as defined by one data provider. The $4.2 trillion comes from another. What can be classified as goods and services is $350 billion to $550 billion.

Nearby periods, different coverage and classifications, and readings one to two orders of magnitude apart.

Their differences alone do not establish error, and their definitions cannot be omitted when combining them.

The theft accounts are the same. Approximately $19 billion over thirteen years cannot be added to $2.87 billion over one year, because they do not count the same types of incident. Recovery rates have no denominator at all, for the reasons already given in the previous section.

Compensation accounts are even more so. A person can say both that they recovered 105% and that they did not recover their original coins.

After twenty-one essays, this is the most definite sentence the series can offer:

There is no consolidated account to hand over.

Not because of laziness, or because every source in the field is opaque. Public chains provide unusually verifiable ledgers, allowing many transfers to be recalculated from historical records. Exchange internal accounts, identities, legal rights, and real-world purposes do not thereby become the same kind of record.

The inability to produce that account is not itself a failed exercise in statistics.

It is the most solid finding of these sixteen years.

The reason is not statistical technique. Any institution willing to do so can define a measurement method and calculate a number. In fact, they already have, each in its own way. That is precisely where the five numbers came from.

The problem is that no single method can answer all the questions everyone wants answered.

A construct that can be closed produces a consolidated account.

This one cannot be closed.

A consolidated account requires everything added into it to be measurable by the same yardstick. From its first day, this system never intended to provide that yardstick.

The reason it cannot be closed is precisely what it accomplished: it made one segment of bookkeeping exceptionally precise, fast, and cheap, without asking who anyone is.

It does not ask who people are, so it cannot count people.

It does not concern itself with purpose, so it cannot distinguish a payment from arbitrage or a machine transferring funds to itself.

It does not recognize national borders, so it cannot say which transfer is cross-border.

These three points are not a list of defects. They are three consequences of the same design choice, and that choice is precisely what made the system possible.

If identity, purpose, and jurisdictional screening become prerequisites for every base-layer transfer, the system is no longer that original permissionless public ledger. Other systems can exist and operate across borders; they make a different set of arrangements for access, responsibility, and control.

Its inability to settle those accounts is therefore not because it has performed inadequately. What it accomplished required it not to record those things.

It has made the part suited to protocol rules increasingly precise. Many off-chain questions still lack an equally complete, directly comparable account. That does not mean no problem has been solved in sixteen years. Solving one kind of recording problem does not automatically settle another kind of human circumstance.

The remainder has appeared in many forms throughout this series.

The list a standard explicitly leaves outside its remit. Promises never written into code. Premises a particular syntax cannot yet express. Events unknown at quotation time and no longer on that screen by settlement. Correspondences requiring additional verification between chains. Distinctions merged away. Something brought back that is no longer quite what it was.

Today, it has taken its plainest form: numbers that cannot be added together.

What lies in that part?

What the two pizzas in Essay 1 were worth to the people then. What remained in the county after the farms in Essay 13 left. People in Essay 6 who have not claimed for years but may still appear. The person in Essay 11 whose attempted repayment did not reach a block while collateral was auctioned near zero. The young person in Essay 16 whose savings for a jeonse housing deposit were hit by losses.

And today, someone in Argentina willing to pay an extra 3% for a dollar-denominated asset.

The chain did not record them.

Not because the chain broke, or because someone hid them. It is because there is still no universally agreed way to record them.

Its original problem was narrow: allow strangers to transfer value without relying on a trusted payment intermediary to process every transfer. It did establish such a publicly verifiable route. That achievement does not require claiming that all third parties disappeared or that every use outperforms earlier systems.

Over twenty-one essays, this series has written almost entirely about things outside that problem: how pizzas are priced, where mining machines move, whether promises entered the code, whether anyone comes to an auction, whether the collateral behind a loan still exists, and which day's price is used to value the coins someone deposited.

None of these is that problem. Every one is something someone actually encountered.

Almost everything people really want to ask falls outside the range of what it can calculate.

That is not its failure.

It is normal for a tool to do only what it was built to do. What matters is not to claim that accomplishing one thing has solved everything.

Over these sixteen years, the part it can calculate has kept expanding.

In early 2009, the protocol first provided rules for transferring value: coins could move between addresses under those rules. That does not mean the genesis block itself completed an ordinary payment between addresses.

Later, it could settle the execution of a contract, the liquidation of undercollateralized assets, a price signed by multiple parties, or the official registration of a fund share.

Whenever it settles one more thing, that cannot be understood simply as technology growing stronger on its own.

Technical advances make previously impossible operations possible. What a settlement counts for still depends on people connecting rights, responsibilities, and conditions of acceptance.

People first establish the conditions under which code should execute; the chain then processes contracts under those rules. They first establish a token's dollar-redemption arrangement; it can then carry that kind of dollar-denominated payment. They recognize a transfer agent's record as the official register; it can then carry those fund shares. Native-coin payments do not require first equaling one dollar. Each additional use has its own agreements.

The boundary of further extension therefore does not lie on the technical side alone.

It is on the human side.

The questions still not fully accounted for today cannot be explained simply by saying the chain is not yet fast, cheap, or secure enough.

They remain unresolved because people have not agreed on which day's price to use, whom to count as one person, which transfers count as payments, how losses should be shared, or at which layer to measure decentralization.

People are still talking.

Every debate listed in Section 7 remains open. Each is more detailed, more specific, and closer to being answerable than the debates of ten years ago.

Sixteen years ago, the question was whether this thing could work.

Today, the question is how to measure what it has accomplished.

The latter question is harder.

But it is a question that can be pursued further.

The blank space is therefore not a wall.

It marks how far this system has reached for now.

That position has kept moving over the sixteen years since the pizza story. Each move may involve technology opening a new route; it also requires people to work out the rights, responsibilities, and conditions of acceptance along it.

The chain records quantities, addresses, times, and signatures, and verifies those records according to its protocol.

No one has yet told it how to record the rest.

The chain is still settling. What else it can settle awaits consensus among more people.
