Bridges

Cross-chain bridges do not move assets through chains. They make one chain accept a statement about another; failures occur between two verifiable ledgers.

1. Making B Accept a Statement

Between two independent chains, no coin actually travels across a network.

Chain A's consensus knows only A's state. Chain B's consensus knows only B's state.

What we call a cross-chain transfer of assets actually requires a cross-domain accounting relationship: the assets available to spend on A decrease, while new rights available to exercise appear on B.

The central question has therefore never been how to send the coins across.

It is why B believes that something really happened on A that is sufficient to authorize B to mint or pay out.

This may look like a technical detail. In fact, it is the whole subject of the next seven sections.

Chain B's own ledger does not automatically record events on A. It needs messages and evidence, together with a way to verify them. A relayer can carry the message; whether B should accept it depends on signatures, state proofs, or light-client verification, not necessarily an external committee's judgment.

Transmission and verification form the additional layer that must be built between the ledgers. That layer does not necessarily sit outside both chains' consensus execution.

A's consensus does not automatically perform B's acceptance checks. To verify an event on A, B needs an additional path using signatures, state proofs, a light client, or another mechanism. What B can check, which chain's security it relies on, and whom else it must trust depend on that path's design.

An additional security boundary appears. It matters to both sides, but the fact that each chain is operating normally does not by itself establish that the boundary is safely covered.

The remainder is already in place here.

The problem need not be a miscalculation in either ledger. Both records may pass their respective rules while the asset correspondence between them fails. The remainder here is the connection that does not establish itself merely because each ledger balances on its own.

This position need not correspond to a company or a separate contract. It may be a signer group, a light client, or proof-verification code. Its name and location change; its task does not: why should B treat a record on A as grounds for paying on its own side?

The most typical process works like this. A user locks 100 ETH in a bridge contract on A; a verification layer proves that lock to B; B then creates 100 wrapped ETH. On the return journey, the order is reversed: the 100 on B are burned before the locked assets on A may be released.

The same ETH has certainly not moved to another chain.

What exists on B is a cross-domain claim on collateral held on A.

Where trust resides depends on who proves that the lock really happened.

With a multisignature scheme or a validator set, B accepts the required number of signatures. The additional trust rests in the signers, the security of their private keys, and their signing software.

With threshold signatures, B accepts a joint signature produced by enough key shares to meet the threshold. The additional trust rests in whether those nodes are genuinely independent, how the shares are held, and who controls access to the servers.

With an oracle or an external committee, the additional trust rests in that committee.

With a light client, B accepts A's consensus headers and state proofs. Trust rests in the security of A's consensus and the correctness of the light-client implementation.

With a validity proof, B accepts a verifiable proof of state. Trust rests in A's security and the correctness of the proof system, circuits, and verifier.

Not one row in this table can be labeled “no trust required.”

The differences are which layer must be controlled to falsify that statement, what doing so costs, and whether anyone can identify the deception afterward.

This already explains why the value locked in a bridge is especially dangerous.

The 100 locked on A are the shared collateral for all 100 wrapped assets on B.

If B is allowed to mint 200, or an attacker is allowed to release 100 from A without a corresponding burn, that cross-domain accounting identity breaks.

The previous essay concerned a law that depended on people to enforce it.

This one concerns what lies between two chains.

A gap had already appeared in Essay 10: the chain does not know that a price is 65,000. It knows that someone said 65,000.

This time, the gap has moved.

It lies between two chains rather than between a chain and the world outside it.

In the public chains discussed here, records on both sides can generally be inspected, and each has a consensus mechanism resisting revision. Those protections have their own finality and reorganization assumptions. They do not automatically prove that every asset entry corresponds across the two chains.

Two individually verifiable ledgers still need a verifiable correspondence between them.

Every incident discussed in the next six sections occurs in that gap.

2. Four Models

The four commonly cited models do not actually operate at the same level. Listing them side by side as four kinds of bridge obscures the real question.

The second model does not have to create a wrapped claim each time. Suppose liquidity providers hold stablecoins on both sides: a user hands 100 units to the pool on A, and a liquidity provider or relayer on B immediately gives the recipient roughly 100 units.

The two ends of the money flow are these: inventory increases on A and decreases on B. The protocol subsequently replenishes the capital through rebalancing or net settlement.

One protocol's current fee documentation explicitly divides the user's total payment into two components: a liquidity fee and a relayer fee. The first compensates for the use of liquidity and rebalancing risk; the second compensates the relayer for transaction fees, the opportunity cost of capital, and risk.

The user's payment does not necessarily go to a bridge company.

This arrangement separates different risks. Trust in security still rests at the layer of final settlement and message verification. Trust in immediate fulfillment shifts to whether liquidity providers have the capital and economic incentives to perform. The pools on both sides bear price and liquidity risk. If the fast path fails, the system can often fall back on slower canonical settlement.

The statement that liquidity bridges are non-custodial therefore also needs qualification. They may not establish a permanent collateral reserve for wrapped assets, but liquidity providers do expose capital along the bridge's route.

The difference between the two models can be understood this way. With lock-and-mint, risk concentrates in the pool on the source chain: everyone's claims rest on the same collateral. With liquidity-based transfers, risk is spread across inventories on both sides, at the cost of someone having to commit capital to both sides for extended periods.

The first can concentrate many holders' backing in one place and expose a large shortfall at once. The second distributes inventory across both sides, but is not inherently limited to small, local failures. A liquidity network can also suffer a sudden large loss if final settlement or message verification fails.

What they share has not changed: someone must always tell one side what happened on the other.

What changes is only who supplies the capital, and where that capital is committed.

Atomic swaps are based on hashlocks and timelocks. One party locks assets on A, and the other locks another amount on B under the same hash condition. Revealing the preimage to claim the assets on one side gives the other party the same secret, allowing that party to claim the assets on the other. If time expires, each receives a refund.

Their distinguishing feature is the absence of a large, permanent bridge reserve backing all users.

An individual swap need not put funds into a permanent vault backing every user. With a correct implementation and valid timing assumptions, its counterparty exposure is easier to isolate from other swaps. A shared implementation defect can still affect many swaps at once.

The cost is that a basic hash-time-locked swap is not a general cross-chain messaging system. It also needs matching assets, liquidity, and timing on both sides. It is useful in some settings without directly replacing every bridge discussed here. The question is not only safety, but what it can do and how practical it is to use.

Trust rests principally in the finality and liveness of the two underlying chains, the correct implementation of hashlocks and timelocks, and the counterparty remaining online within the periods specified by the protocol.

The costs arise from the same property: the parties need matching assets and liquidity; differences in timing between chains must be handled; and a single hashed timelock cannot by itself solve general-purpose cross-chain messaging.

The light-client approach deserves especially close attention.

The destination chain maintains the other chain's light-client state, while permissionless relayers transport packets and proofs. A packet is accepted only after the other chain's consensus state and membership proof have been verified on-chain.

The relevant documentation makes an explicit distinction: relayers are responsible for liveness, not trusted arbiters of security. In principle, a failed relayer can be replaced as long as someone is willing to relay.

The “trustlessness” claimed for light-client bridges is therefore more precisely described as something other than an absence of trust.

It means no additional trust in a separate external verification committee. Security is reduced, as far as possible, to the consensus security of the two underlying chains and the correctness of the light-client and verification code.

Academic proposals have taken this a step further. One paper proposes verifying the state of another chain with succinct proofs, avoiding an external committee in its prototype, and reports proof generation in under 20 seconds and on-chain verification at under 230,000 gas.

These are performance experiments in a paper and a prototype. They cannot simply be presented as proof that bridge security has been solved in production.

Now separate the levels at which these four things operate.

Lock-and-mint is a method of asset accounting.

Light clients, multisignature schemes, and validity proofs are methods of verification.

Liquidity networks are methods of fulfillment.

Atomic swaps are a different set of settlement primitives.

Calling them all “four kinds of bridge” can obscure the real security question: who holds the ultimate authority to say that B may now mint, or A may now release assets?

A more reliable axis for analyzing a bridge than its value locked, speed, or fees is therefore this: first identify the component that can turn “something happened on the other chain” into “this chain may now pay.” Then ask for the smallest set of actors capable of controlling, falsifying, misleading, or upgrading that component.

Every incident in the following sections is a point on this axis.

3. Nine Does Not Equal Nine

Ronin shows precisely why looking only at a numerical threshold creates a false sense of security.

According to the company's own incident report, there were nine validator keys at the time, and a bridge withdrawal required five signatures.

Four of those keys were controlled by Sky Mavis itself.

In November 2021, to handle user demand and requests for fee-free transactions, Axie DAO had allowlisted Sky Mavis's access to one validator's signing path. The temporary arrangement was no longer needed by December, but the allowlist entry was not revoked.

After compromising Sky Mavis's environment and obtaining its four keys, the attacker used this remaining channel to get the DAO validator to provide the fifth signature on malicious withdrawals.

The nominal threshold was therefore five of nine.

The practical barrier was not compromising five independent institutions. It was four keys controlled by one organization, plus a signing route that should have been revoked but remained effective.

The reason the channel remained was mundane: it had been opened temporarily to solve a real operational problem, and nobody remembered to close it once the problem was resolved.

In Essay 6, a mechanism added temporarily to handle one incident later became the entry point for the next. The same pattern appears here: something temporary remained, retaining all the authority it had been given at the outset.

The cryptographic threshold was not broken.

The assumption of independence was.

The token quantities are the cleanest part of the record: 173,600 ETH and 25.5 million USDC were taken in two withdrawals on March 23, 2022.

Three dollar figures can all be valid: roughly $540 million uses prices on the day of the attack; roughly $615 million to $625 million generally uses prices around the disclosure date of March 29. The US Treasury used a figure close to $620 million, while the company announcement gave the original token quantities.

Another number deserves to stand on its own: the assets were taken on March 23, but the theft was not discovered until March 29.

Six days.

For those six days, the wrapped assets continued to trade on the other chain, serve as collateral, and count toward various measures of total value locked.

But the collateral backing them was already gone.

During those six days, both chains could continue recording transactions normally. Individually valid records did not mean that backing and claims still matched.

The shortfall was publicly discovered only six days later. That delay alone does not establish that nobody was assigned reconciliation or that nobody checked. It shows that the detection and response arrangements in place did not surface this anomaly promptly.

The third essay described exchange outflows that went undetected for years. Here the delay was six days and the setting differed, but the demand is similar: once records exist, a person or mechanism must still keep checking the relationships that should hold between them.

Here, one need not first obtain a private internal ledger even to ask the reconciliation question. Both sides have inspectable records. The challenge is connecting backing, minting, burning, and unlocking into an effective ongoing check, rather than stopping at the fact that each transaction passed locally.

As for the initial entry point, later media reports suggested targeted phishing disguised as recruitment materials. The original official incident report did not directly confirm this, so it must not be blended into the official account of the four-plus-one compromise.

Harmony set the threshold even lower.

The company's own investigation said the attacker obtained at least two private keys. Public security analyses commonly describe the bridge at the time as a two-of-five multisignature arrangement.

These statements need not conflict. If the threshold was two keys out of five, obtaining at least two keys can explain how it was met. The questions still worth asking are who held the keys and whether they were truly independent, not an invented discrepancy merely because the statements contain different numbers.

The seven-of-ten figure for Orbit comes primarily from post-incident analyses by security researchers and firms. The company's initial announcement confirmed only abnormal access and asset outflows; it did not establish this root cause in its first-party statement.

It should therefore be labeled a security firm's inference, not an official finding by the company or a government.

Together, these three incidents identify the variables that should actually be recorded.

The number of signers. The threshold. The number of independent organizations. The number of independent failure domains across hardware, cloud services, and key-management systems. And whether administrator or upgrade keys can bypass the threshold.

The first two are usually precisely what public projects find easiest to disclose.

The last three are the hardest to verify.

This is the same kind of problem as the five measuring sticks in Essay 2, but more specific.

Then, the problem was that a single object could be measured in five ways. Here, the easiest number to publish is precisely the one on which security depends least.

A bridge can display “five of nine” on the front page of its documentation while all nine signers reside in the same region of the same cloud provider. That violates no disclosure requirement, because nobody requires that fact to be disclosed.

More difficult still, even a willing party would struggle to describe it clearly.

Whether two companies are independent depends on their shareholders, directors, offices, operations teams, and whose network their machines connect to. None of these can be written as a contract parameter. None can be verified on-chain.

Yet these are precisely what determine how much that threshold is worth.

4. Fooling the Verifier

The nature of the Wormhole incident was the exact opposite of Ronin's.

Its central flaw lay in the verification path on Solana. The attacker supplied a fake instructions sysvar account, and the relevant legacy function failed to check that the supplied account was actually the system account.

The program was thus induced to believe that signature verification had already occurred and to generate a verified record for subsequent use. The attacker then invoked the wrapped-asset completion path and minted 120,000 wrapped ETH.

This case is particularly useful for distinguishing two kinds of attack.

One steals enough guardian keys to produce genuine malicious signatures meeting the required threshold.

The other never meets the threshold at all, but the verification code incorrectly reports that verification has already succeeded.

Wormhole belongs to the second category.

The evidence therefore does not support saying that the private-key system of its 19 guardians was compromised. The attacker targeted the boundary of signature verification.

Different sources valued the loss at roughly $320 million or $325 million at the time. In both cases, the measure was the then-current market value of those 120,000 tokens, not a balance of US-dollar cash in a company account.

A very clear imbalance appeared between the two ends of the funds.

The Solana side had 120,000 additional claims created out of nothing, while the Ethereum side had no corresponding addition of 120,000 ETH in collateral.

Jump promptly supplied 120,000 ETH, restoring full backing to those claims.

The recovery of attacker-controlled assets two years later through proceedings in courts in the UK and New York was a recovery for the party that had replenished the collateral, not a second round of compensation for users.

This distinction matters because it determines whose money the roughly 90% recovery rate refers to.

The replenishment itself also deserves to be recorded. It was not part of the protocol design, an insurance arrangement, or something promised in any document.

It was a company's decision on the day of the incident to put up 120,000 ETH and restore the broken accounting identity.

Had it not done so, holders of the wrapped assets would have been left with undercollateralized claims.

There was another failure of the same kind, this time on a native bridge.

In October 2022, the bridge between BNB's two native chains was exploited. The project itself confirmed that the attacker forged cross-chain proofs through a flaw in the underlying proof library, creating and withdrawing an additional two million BNB.

At market prices at the time, the nominal value minted was roughly $570 million to $600 million.

What the attacker actually managed to transfer out before the chain was halted was roughly $100 million to $110 million.

The $400 million to $500 million between those two figures was contained because validators coordinated to halt the entire chain.

A chain designed to run without interruption avoided a larger loss by stopping. This was possible precisely because it had few enough validators to coordinate quickly.

Describing the incident as a loss of $570 million in customer assets held in custody is therefore inaccurate as well.

The underlying action was this: a proof verifier accepted a forged state proof, allowing two million additional tokens to be issued without legitimate backing on the source chain.

This was the same class of failure as Wormhole, not a theft of signers' private keys.

Putting the two cases side by side reveals something important.

At Ronin, the protocol's rules were satisfied in full. The five signatures were genuine, verification was correct, and the contract executed according to its rules. The problem lay outside those rules: the five keys should not have been in the same place.

These two incidents were the opposite. The rules were not satisfied, but the contracts believed they were. The signatures did not exist, the proofs were false, yet the verification code reported success.

In the first, the contract correctly verified signatures made with stolen keys, but that check could not distinguish who was using them. In the second, the bridge's verification implementation accepted material it should have rejected. Neither should simply be restated as failure of the underlying chain's consensus.

For users, either failure can become the same urgent question: can the claim they hold still be redeemed in full? But collateral may have been removed, or additional unbacked claims may have been created. Not every incident means all backing suddenly vanished.

Before the risk becomes visible, a wrapped asset may still work normally on the other chain: it can be transferred, pooled, included in locked-value figures, or accepted as collateral. Those uses can look alike without making the underlying backing and verification risks identical.

A cross-domain claim may ordinarily trade only a small distance from the asset it claims.

An incident can widen that distance abruptly. Whether it narrows again depends on remaining backing, replenishment, and assets ultimately recoverable.

This makes such assets difficult to price. A wrapped asset may track its underlying closely in normal times. Once backing is insufficient or redemption blocked, its price depends on remaining collateral, possible replenishment, and expected recovery. A sharp discount is possible without the outcome being limited to full value or zero.

Looking only at balances, signature counts, or whether the contract still runs may fail to reveal this redemption risk in advance. Backing reconciliation, permission changes, and actual control also need examination. The risks do not always appear gradually in one convenient price field.

5. The Zero Root

Nomad was the large-scale disaster among these cases that required the least advanced skill, because a prior logic error had already removed the security condition.

During initialization for an upgrade in June 2022, a state value representing a trusted root was set to zero. The confirmation time associated with that zero value was therefore assigned an acceptable value. Meanwhile, any unproven message also defaulted to zero in the mapping.

The result was that the function deciding whether a root was acceptable returned true for zero.

Messages without valid proofs could therefore also be executed by the processing function.

One faulty upgrade in the control plane directly changed the meaning of verification in the data plane.

Once the first attackers had shown that the withdrawal template worked, anyone following needed only four steps: copy the data from a successful attack transaction, change the recipient to themselves, resubmit it, and wait for the bridge's routing contract to pay.

This explains why it was called a crowdsourced attack.

Hundreds of people did not independently discover a vulnerability. A publicly available successful example made the exploit cheap to copy.

It also explains something else.

In 2025, Israel arrested a suspect at the United States' request and approved extradition proceedings. US authorities accused him of playing a central role in the incident.

That is a government criminal allegation, not a court finding that he stole all the assets.

More importantly, even establishing criminal evidence against the first person would not automatically show that the same person controlled all subsequent copycat withdrawals.

As for the amount, the project later used a figure above $186 million, while security firms and the media widely used roughly $190 million.

These apply different prices and scopes to the same multi-asset event. Both should be retained.

This incident occupies a particular place in the series.

In the preceding fourteen essays, when a construct failed, someone generally still needed a particular capability: computing power, capital, keys, or code that others could not write.

This time, no special capability was needed. Being able to read a transaction, change an address, and pay the fee was enough.

Every on-chain transaction is public and carries its full call data. This was originally one of the system's proudest features: anyone could verify what any transaction did.

That afternoon, anyone could also do it again.

This is also why losses in this case are particularly difficult to count. The chain cannot distinguish how many addresses belonged to attackers, how many to passersby, and how many to existing users trying to get their own money out first.

Some of those who later returned funds did belong to that last group.

Individual accountability becomes harder. Identifying the person behind an address and establishing conduct and intent requires further evidence, while hundreds of addresses submitted similar call data in those hours. The later arrest and prosecution show that action was possible; they do not make every call one person's single act.

One attack can have hundreds of joint participants, most of whom had never met before and would never meet afterward.

This is precisely the other side of composability and openness: the same property that lets anyone verify a transaction also lets anyone copy it.

Tracing both ends of the money flow also takes on a distinctive form here.

In a typical attack, the party taking the money is someone who can be tracked, prosecuted, or negotiated with. Here, the money went to hundreds of addresses, a substantial share belonging to people who joined on the spur of the moment after hearing the news.

About $36 million was later returned voluntarily, while the remainder could only be handled pro rata against compensation claims.

The usually useful question—who holds the money—could not be answered here merely by finding one address. It had to be separated into many controllers, many acts, and many pools of assets, some recoverable and some still beyond reach.

6. The Cloud Account

The most dangerous thing about Multichain was not the threshold-signature algorithm.

It was the real-world structure of control.

Abnormal withdrawals from the protocol began on July 6, 2023. One analytics firm initially counted more than $125 million, nearly $120 million of it from the bridge serving one chain. Stablecoin issuers subsequently froze roughly $65 million at related addresses.

After further abnormal transfers were included, other organizations used a broader total loss figure of at least $210 million.

Neither figure is simply right or wrong. The first covers the initial major wave of unauthorized withdrawals on July 6; the second includes subsequent abnormal outflows across multiple chains.

In its own account on July 14, the project made several statements.

Its CEO had been taken away by Chinese police on May 21; the threshold-signature node servers ran under his personal cloud-service account; police had also seized his computer, phones, hardware wallets, and seed phrases; and the team subsequently found that its access to the node servers had been revoked.

The project also said that his elder sister later moved the remaining assets to safeguard them, and was then taken away by police as well.

All these descriptions of police actions and the purpose of the asset transfers have the evidentiary status of the company's own account, not a published judicial finding.

The on-chain record can establish that assets were moved from the controlled addresses under authorization valid to the system.

It cannot, on its own, establish whether the controller was an outside hacker, an insider, a family member, or a law-enforcement agency.

The analytics firm's wording at the time was also cautious: a possible hack or rug pull, potentially involving compromised administrator keys.

A 2024 judgment of the Singapore High Court provides extremely valuable off-chain material. It records the plaintiff's evidence about this control structure and notes its conflict with the decentralized architecture advertised in the user agreement, which was supposed to be beyond any one person's control.

The judge explicitly stated, however, that he had not made findings of fact on all the plaintiff's substantive allegations, including fraud and asset transfers, because liability had first been established through a default judgment.

The label 'threshold signature' therefore cannot substitute for a security review of the actual deployment. An algorithm requiring enough signing shares is one thing; one person controlling those shares through one account is another.

Five other questions are needed: which machines hold the shares; who owns the cloud account; who holds the recovery phrases; whether genuinely independent entities operate the nodes; and whether an administrator can replace them.

One detail on the Orbit side also requires precise wording.

In January 2024, the company accused a former head of security of weakening its firewall without authorization before leaving, filed a complaint, and sought a police investigation. It also said it was investigating another possibility.

This must especially not become a statement that the former security head caused the attack.

The material establishes only that the company made the allegation and sought an investigation.

Read together with Section 3, this section reveals a complete descent.

At Ronin, the nine keys really existed. They simply were not independent enough.

Here, the nodes really existed, but they ran under one person's account.

Threshold signatures require enough shares to produce a valid signature; under the relevant security assumptions, an attacker below that threshold cannot forge one alone. Ordinary signing does not require malicious behavior, however, and several shares may be physically, administratively, or legally controlled by one entity. An algorithmic threshold does not automatically become a threshold of independent institutions.

On-chain signatures alone do not reveal actual control. A conventional multisignature may list several signing addresses; a threshold scheme may appear as one valid signature under one public key. The chain may not reveal how many nodes participated, still less whether they all operated under one person's cloud account.

This section also leaves a blank unusual in this series.

Where the money went can be traced on-chain. Who authorized its transfer still has no judicially established answer. The company's account points in one direction; the analytics firm spoke at the time of a possible hack or rug pull; and the Singapore court explicitly said it had not made findings of fact on these allegations.

Three sources, three levels of evidence, and they do not point to the same conclusion.

This is not the first time the series has encountered such a situation, but here it is most complete: assets worth more than $100 million were moved, every on-chain step can be checked, and three years later there is still no finding one can cite establishing who authorized the transfer.

On-chain transparency answers where the money went. It does not answer, and was not designed to answer, who made it go there.

7. Three Denominators

Recovery rates cannot simply be ranked in one table, because there are at least three entirely different denominators.

One is the asset recovery rate: assets retrieved from the attacker's control divided by the assets originally stolen.

Another is the user compensation rate: the value ultimately paid to affected users divided by the recognized losses.

A third is the degree to which the protocol's solvency has been restored.

These can differ enormously.

A bridge can recover 90% of the assets without users receiving a cent, because the recovered money belongs to the party that supplied replacement capital. Users can also receive full compensation without a cent being recovered, because the company paid out of its own pocket.

Putting those two situations on the same recovery-rate leaderboard produces meaningless numbers.

These incidents show at least four different outcomes.

Poly relied mainly on voluntary returns by the attacker. The attack itself is also often misdescribed: it was not a leak of guardian private keys. The attacker acquired the ability to rewrite the guardian list through an earlier stage of authorization logic. The attacker later returned the vast majority of the assets in stages, while a stablecoin issuer froze roughly $33 million. The near-100% figure should therefore be described as control ultimately being restored over almost all the nominally stolen assets, mostly through voluntary returns by the attacker rather than law-enforcement recovery.

Wormhole relied on replacement capital, followed by asset recovery through court orders and technical execution.

Ronin relied on on-chain tracing, trading platforms, and freezes through international law enforcement. Users were compensated through the company's recapitalization.

Multichain relied on stablecoin-issuer freezes and subsequent insolvency and liquidation litigation.

Nomad, Harmony, Multichain, and Orbit all left substantial unrecovered exposure.

What determines whether recovery can actually be enforced is therefore not whether the address can be seen on-chain.

It is whether there is an off-chain point of intervention: a centralized exchange that can freeze an account; a stablecoin issuer that can blacklist an address; a front end or control system within a court's jurisdiction that can be ordered to assist; or an actor who can be identified, arrested, and subjected to asset seizure.

The strength of attribution also varies widely and must be assessed case by case.

Ronin has a formal attribution by the FBI from April 2022; Harmony also has a formal attribution from early 2023. Both are stronger than an analytics firm or media report saying that an attack looks like someone's work.

Nomad has criminal charges, but the entire collective episode of copycat withdrawals cannot be attributed to one person.

Attribution is considerably weaker for Poly, Wormhole, Multichain, and Orbit. Some attackers' identities have never been formally attributed in a public government statement. Some legal proceedings concern assets controlled by unknown attackers: establishing property rights does not establish a real-world identity. And certain claims about state-level actors can only be described as suspicions held by companies and the security industry.

One sentence deserves to stand on its own here.

On-chain transparency resolves part of transaction attribution, not the identification of people in the real world.

A blockchain can give strong answers about which address received the coins, what they were exchanged for, which exchange, bridge, or mixer they passed through, and which address they ultimately reached.

The limits of a mixer's effects are also clear: it disrupts a simple mapping between inputs and outputs, but it does not erase the on-chain transactions before and after. Analytics firms can offer probabilistic tracing. Actual freezes still depend on exchanges, issuers, or judicial authorities.

The claim that the money always remains visible on-chain is therefore only half true.

Between seeing assets and recovering them lie not only technical evidence, but reachable points of control and jurisdiction.

The cross-chain mechanism does not automatically supply those off-chain conditions. Recognizing that gap does not require attributing an intention to evade jurisdiction to every designer.

Successful recoveries used different routes: voluntary returns, issuer freezes, exchange cooperation, court orders, or action against identifiable people. On-chain tracing can help find a route without performing those steps by itself.

Finally, consider the two sides of the business itself.

Liquidity providers, relayers, and protocol treasuries receive small, continuous fees.

When a bridge becomes insolvent, the enormous, discrete tail losses fall on holders of wrapped assets, liquidity providers, parties supplying replacement capital, or token holders.

Small, continuous income on one side; enormous, discrete losses on the other.

This pattern has appeared several times in the series. In Essay 9, liquidity providers collected 0.3% per transaction while bearing inventory losses after prices moved. In Essay 10, oracle nodes collected fees per service request, while a single incorrect report could cause $100 million in downstream losses.

Bridges are the most extreme case: fees accrue per transaction in basis points; failure arrives in a single event measured in hundreds of millions.

Whether company capital or insurance reconnects the two is a question of contracts and governance in each individual case.

It cannot be inferred from the label “decentralized bridge.”

8. Between Two Chains

Several popular claims need to be rewritten.

“Cross-chain bridges are crypto's greatest security weakness.”

This formulation is too broad. The most frequently cited figure comes from an analytics firm: in August 2022, it estimated that 13 cross-chain bridge hacks had occurred that year to date, with roughly $2 billion stolen, accounting for 69% of all value stolen by that point in the year.

The denominator behind that 69% was the stolen funds included in the firm's hack dataset for that year to date. It was not the market capitalization of all crypto assets, all losses from crime, or cumulative historical losses from exploits.

Its later full-year statistics used a different scope: roughly $3.8 billion lost to hacks in 2022, about $3.1 billion of it from protocols, with cross-chain bridges accounting for 64% of that amount. The denominator for 64% is the $3.1 billion. It cannot be directly compared with the midyear figure of 69%.

By 2024, the distribution of hacks in the firm's data had changed: private-key compromises accounted for 43.8% of the year's stolen value, and centralized services became the more prominent targets in some quarters.

Calling bridges the greatest weakness is therefore well grounded in that particular 2022 dataset, not a proposition that holds identically across years.

“The more signers a multisig has, the safer the bridge.”

The material supports only a narrower statement: security depends on the number of independent failure domains that must be compromised to meet the threshold, not the length of the public-key list.

“Bridges are hacked only because of poor private-key management.”

This is too narrow as well. Ronin, Harmony, Multichain, and possibly Orbit do illustrate risks involving keys and the control plane. But Wormhole involved a signature-verification flaw, Nomad an initialization-logic flaw, Poly a cross-contract authorization flaw, and the native bridge a failure of proof verification.

A narrower formulation is this: major bridge losses concentrate at the boundary that determines who can make another chain accept a cross-chain fact. That boundary can fail through stolen keys, but also through verifiers, proofs, upgrades, permissions, or initialization logic.

“Light-client bridges are truly trustless.”

If “trustless” means requiring no security assumptions, the claim does not hold. They depend on the source chain's consensus and finality, the light-client code, and execution on the destination chain. If the source chain's consensus is itself compromised, a correct light client will faithfully accept the malicious state confirmed by that consensus.

If “trustless” is defined as introducing no additional external verification committee, then these designs do remove one layer of third-party trust compared with externally verified bridges.

“Native cross-chain transfers are necessarily safer than third-party bridges.”

Two meanings of “native” must be separated. If it means light-client-style verification, the advantage is the removal of an external committee. If it merely means that the chain's official team maintains a separate set of bridge validators or a multisignature arrangement, official status does not automatically eliminate additional trust.

The compromised native bridge is the most direct counterexample: it connected two native chains, yet a flaw in its proof-verification library allowed two million tokens to be created without collateral.

“Native” therefore describes an organizational and product relationship, not a formal security property.

Now return to the first section.

The essence of a cross-chain transfer has never been moving something from A to B.

It is getting B to accept a statement about A.

These incidents attacked precisely five different components of that statement.

Poly attacked who had the authority to rewrite the list of speakers.

Wormhole and the native bridge attacked how the statement was judged to have proof.

Ronin and Harmony attacked who could sign the statement.

Nomad attacked which statements counted as proven by default.

Multichain and Orbit exposed who actually controlled the infrastructure that made the statement in the real world.

Five kinds of attack. Five components of one statement.

These five failure points do not mean the underlying signature algorithms or hash functions were broken. They appeared mainly at the interfaces among permissions, key control, and verification implementations. Cryptographic engineering is certainly involved; the security of the primitives is simply not the security of the whole system.

In the incidents discussed here, the materials do not show a break of the underlying signature algorithms or an actual hash collision. The failures lay mainly not in the primitives themselves, but in how they were used and verified.

What failed was the entire arrangement through which the statement was generated, transmitted, and judged.

This applies across the field. Cryptography can guarantee that a signature came from a particular private key. It cannot guarantee who holds that key, or whether the code checking the signature was written correctly.

The previous essay concerned a law that depended on people to enforce it, and people could choose not to.

Here, B’s contract executes under its rules when a valid message arrives, a transaction is included, and the required conditions hold. That does not mean operators cannot pause the system or that every message necessarily arrives in time.

The question is where that statement came from.

In Essay 10, the gap lay between the chain and the outside world: the chain did not know whether a price was true. It knew only that someone had stated that price.

This time, the gap has moved.

It lies between two chains.

The public records on both sides can be checked under their respective rules, and each has mechanisms resisting revision.

A has consensus. B has consensus.

That does not automatically create a shared ledger proving the assets correspond. A light client or state proof can let B verify A within its own consensus execution. It still needs a correct verifier and still depends on the underlying chains' security.

That is where the remainder lies.

Inspecting A alone does not answer it. Whether A has a corresponding lock, and how much is locked, must be checked against the claims actually created on the other side.

Inspecting B alone does not answer it either. A minting record on B may be perfectly clear while adequate support from the source chain still requires verification.

It lies between two individually clear records, in the correspondence that must still be verified, implemented, and continually reconciled.

Bridges are different arrangements built to establish that correspondence.

The length of that stretch defines the business itself.

If nothing were needed between the two chains, there would be no need for a bridge. Precisely because something is needed, someone builds one. And every design answers the same question: who makes the statement, and why should it be believed?

After fifteen years, no design has eliminated that question. It can only be moved elsewhere: to a set of signers, a piece of verification code, a state proof, or the consensus of the two chains themselves.

Each move changes where failure can occur.

It has never made the problem disappear.

Both chains may still be producing blocks and accepting records under their local implementations. Passing locally does not ensure that cross-chain locking, minting, and backing correspond. Where the verification implementation is flawed, evidence that should fail may instead be accepted.

Yet they do not reconcile.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
