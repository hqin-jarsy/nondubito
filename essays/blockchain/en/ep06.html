The DAO

The DAO crisis separated code execution, participants’ intentions, node choices, and market naming into four sets of rules, leaving two chains still running.

1. Four Sets of Rules

The most common compressed version of this history is a single sentence: a smart contract was hacked, Ethereum rolled back to issue refunds, and ETC was born.

That sentence contains at least three inaccuracies. The following sections address them one by one.

More importantly, it makes the event smaller than it was.

What those three months in 2016 actually accomplished was to separate, in full public view, four sets of rules previously assumed to be the same thing.

What the EVM would execute.

What The DAO's participants had originally intended.

What new protocol rules Ethereum's nodes were willing to accept.

Which chain the market was willing to call Ethereum.

Before then, the answers to these four questions were assumed to coincide. Afterward, no one could make that assumption again.

Their separation was concrete. The same code ran on the same virtual machine under the same rules; almost all the participants considered the result something they had not wanted; someone therefore proposed changing a protocol rule to offset it; and whether to change it was ultimately decided separately by several groups subordinate to none of the others: client developers, miners, exchange operators, and people buying and selling the coin.

Four matters, four groups, four ways of deciding.

The remainder lies at the seams between these four sets of rules. It is not something none of them covered. Quite the contrary: it is the part all four covered, but answered differently.

First, consider what it actually was.

Christoph Jentzsch's white paper was titled “Decentralized Autonomous Organization to Automate Governance.” What it described first was not a company, but an implementation that encoded organizational decision-making rules in Ethereum smart contracts.

Its central concern was not crowdfunding either. It was delegation and agency: how to let contributors directly decide where pooled funds would go, reducing the distance between managers and owners of capital, while providing a minority with an exit that did not require submission to majority rule.

Both objectives mattered. The first addressed a centuries-old problem: people who entrust their money to someone else cannot continuously know what that person is doing. The second addressed another old problem: a minority in an organization can usually only submit or leave, and leaving often entails a loss.

Encoding both in a contract was a substantial ambition.

The construct in the previous essay priced execution.

This construct sought to set rules for decisions.

The framework for metering and bounding execution became a lasting part of the chain, while its specific price schedule still required revision. The DAO's system for governing decisions operated for less than two months.

The next seven sections unpack that difference.

2. Three Centers of Power

Slock.it was the concrete commercial force behind this abstract framework.

It was a German blockchain and Internet of Things startup, driven by Christoph Jentzsch, Simon Jentzsch, Stephan Tual, and others. Its idea was to connect smart contracts to physical objects so that locks, vehicles, and other devices could be rented and shared automatically.

It had already publicly prepared to submit its own first major funding proposal to The DAO.

A popular account therefore needs qualification.

“Slock.it created it and then had nothing to do with it” is wrong. “Slock.it controlled it” is wrong too.

The narrower account supported by the evidence is this: Slock.it wrote, deployed, and promoted the code, ran the main communication channels, selected the initial Curators, and prepared to become one of the first contractors. After deployment, however, Slock.it itself had no administrator private key that could arbitrarily move funds into its own account. Ordinary disbursements still had to satisfy the contract's voting and whitelist conditions.

Now consider those three centers of power separately.

At the proposal end, a contractor seeking funds first had to deploy the relevant contract, then create a proposal in The DAO specifying a recipient address, amount, debate deadline, and description, and pay a deposit. Ordinary proposals required a debate period of approximately two weeks or more. Only after the conditions were met would the execution function transfer funds according to the on-chain vote.

At the voting end, it was not one address, one vote. An address's DAO Token balance determined its voting weight on a proposal. Tokens used to vote faced transfer restrictions until the proposal ended.

This produced an incentive problem researchers identified at the time: those voting yes wanted to advance a profitable project; those voting no would have their tokens locked; those who simply abstained retained greater liquidity.

There was therefore no neutral, cost-free setting for direct democracy. Participation itself carried a cost, and that cost was asymmetric.

The Curator was the third center, and the easiest to misunderstand.

The white paper did not pretend Curators had no power. It expressly stated that the design gave them considerable power.

Curators controlled a whitelist of addresses eligible to receive payments from The DAO. Its purpose was to verify that an identifiable contractor stood behind a proposal and to prevent certain malicious contract structures. In practice, the single Curator address was implemented as a multisignature arrangement.

The initial public group comprised 11 Curators selected by Slock.it during The DAO's formation. Announcements at the time introduced them as prominent figures in the Ethereum ecosystem.

Gavin Wood, however, had resigned in May 2016, before the attack. The list of 11 was the initial roster, not the roster on the day of the attack.

The SEC's later description of Curator powers was stronger than the promotional material at the time. It found that Curators not only verified contractors' identities and code and maintained the whitelist, but could effectively determine whether an address qualified for inclusion among payable recipients. It also cited a Curator describing this as complete control over the whitelist. Curators additionally had special authority to lower quorum requirements gradually.

Conversely, a Curator was not a fund manager in the conventional sense. Whitelisting alone transferred no ETH; ordinary proposals still needed token votes satisfying the contract's conditions.

The more accurate structure is therefore this:

Token holders supplied the capital-weighted approval. Curators controlled access to the set of eligible payees. The contract supplied final execution.

None of the three alone amounted to arbitrary control.

This is precisely why the SEC later rejected the defense that token holders did not depend on others because they had voting rights.

Finally, there was the mechanism that later failed.

Splitting was originally a minority exit mechanism, not the conventional creation of a subsidiary controlled by a parent company. Holders could vote yes on a split proposal naming a new Curator; those wishing to leave could then enter a new DAO with their proportional ETH and accounting entries associated with historical reward rights.

In other words, a child DAO was a technical container for leaving the existing governance community with one's proportional assets. Its very purpose was to prevent the majority and the original Curator from trapping minority capital forever.

This also shows where the real distance between the white paper's ideal and practice lay.

The distance was not that the white paper promised complete decentralization and a Curator suddenly appeared in practice. The white paper already acknowledged that center of power and used splitting as a counterweight.

The real difference was that The DAO in practice added another layer of off-chain facts: authorship of the code, the website and communication channels, selection of the first Curators, and preparations to become one of the first major contractors itself.

3. The Extra Payment Bought No Votes

The crowdfunding window ran from April 30 to May 28, 2016.

The SEC's account was that approximately 1.15 billion DAO Tokens were sold, raising about 12 million ETH, valued at roughly $150 million near the end of the sale.

Dollar figures, especially, cannot be detached from the pricing date.

A contemporaneous security paper put the late-crowdfunding total at about 11.5 million ETH and $150 million; a later ethnographic study gave a value of about $250 million near the close.

This does not mean the contract conjured up another $100 million. It means the same ETH-denominated assets were revalued at market prices on different dates.

For studying the scale of this fundraising, ETH quantity is therefore a more stable unit than a retrospective dollar valuation.

Nor was the price curve a fixed 100 DAO per ETH.

The deployed code initially created tokens at the base rate, then increased the creation price daily, eventually reaching approximately 1.5 ETH per 100 DAO. The SEC accordingly summarized the sale price as roughly 1 to 1.5 ETH per 100 tokens.

An accounting detail is almost never mentioned here.

The extra ETH paid at later prices did not give buyers proportionately more voting power.

The code converted the portion corresponding to the base exchange rate into DAO Tokens, while the premium in the price curve entered a pool called the extra balance.

In other words, additional ETH paid and voting weight acquired were not the same variable.

Pause on that sentence.

Late buyers paid more, but their voting power did not increase in proportion to the extra payment.

This also explains why subsequent refunds had to distinguish the main DAO's base exchange of 1 to 100 from extra-balance entitlements.

The total raised itself has at least three distinct measurement bases.

About 12 million ETH was the SEC's rounded figure for the entire sale.

About 11.5 million ETH was the crowdfunding approximation often cited at the time.

And 11,898,333.9787 ETH was a state-based figure used by the later fork refund code. It included the relevant main DAO, child DAO, and extra-balance states at the fork, and cannot simply be treated as the exact cumulative receipts of the crowdfunding contract.

The three numbers belong alongside one another; none supersedes the others.

There is likewise no reliable count of individual human participants.

The SEC explicitly noted that subscriptions were pseudonymous: only Ethereum addresses were visible on-chain, without identity checks. One person could have several addresses, and one exchange address could represent many people.

Contemporaneous reports of more than 11,000 investors should be understood as approximate descriptions of addresses or participants. A later on-chain snapshot of the pre-fork state found approximately 22,092 accounts with nonzero DAO Token balances; another historical compilation gave 22,873 holding accounts.

Their measurement bases differ, and neither is a count of original crowdfunding buyers.

Concentration, too, can only be given as a time slice. Contemporaneous statistics on May 17, 2016, before crowdfunding ended, said the largest single holder had less than 4% of the total, while the top 100 addresses held about 46%.

That shows no single address held a majority of votes. It also shows that absence of a majority holder is not equal distribution of power.

What token holders actually acquired were several code-defined rights: balance-weighted voting, proportional participation in rewards when eligible, transfer of tokens once markets opened, and exit with proportional assets through splitting.

These were not ordinary shares in Slock.it. Nor did they automatically convey ownership of Slock.it, the Ethereum Foundation, or any Internet of Things intellectual property.

Now trace both sides.

During crowdfunding, subscribing addresses lost ETH, the main DAO and extra balance gained ETH, and subscribing addresses gained DAO Tokens.

During ordinary investment, the DAO's assets fell and an approved, whitelisted contractor gained ETH. Token holders received not an immediate return of principal, but rights to future rewards generated under the project contract.

The pooled funds of token holders therefore bore project-failure risk, while contractors could receive project payments once proposal conditions were met.

These were not the same bearers of risk and return.

This is the third encounter with the same shape in this series. In the second essay, presales shifted the timing risk of rising difficulty to buyers while manufacturers received interest-free working capital. In the fourth, finalizing early gave sellers turnover while buyers lost escrow protection.

This time, the pool bore project failure, while the contractor received payment once the conditions were met.

4. The Amount Was Zero

The attack is often rewritten as a teaching example: money was sent before the balance was updated, the order was wrong, and the attacker withdrew repeatedly.

That captures the principle of reentrancy, but not the actual call chain.

The dangerous path actually ran as follows.

First, the split function calculated how much ETH the caller should move, using the caller's balance, the split snapshot, and total supply.

Second, it first placed that proportional ETH in the child DAO and gave the caller child DAO tokens.

Third, it then entered the reward-withdrawal function.

Fourth, that function called the reward account's payment function.

Fifth, the payment function sent funds through a low-level call, executing the recipient's fallback function. The recipient was the attack contract.

Sixth, the attack contract's fallback function called the same split function again.

Seventh, at that moment the outer split function had not yet executed its final two lines: subtracting the caller's balance from total supply and setting the caller's balance to zero.

The second invocation therefore still saw the old, nonzero balance and calculated the same transfer amount again.

Continuing recursively, the same token balance could trigger multiple asset movements before the state was updated.

The violated security pattern is what is now called checks-effects-interactions: check first, update state next, interact externally last. More precisely, the problem was not confined to reward withdrawal. Splitting had already affected assets while the holder's old balance remained valid, and the reward path then gave the attacker a control-flow opportunity to reenter splitting.

A counterintuitive detail here is among the most important things to remember about the event.

Before the attack, Slock.it had reasoned that because the reward account had no rewards to pay, recursive withdrawal could not endanger the main principal.

That judgment was arithmetically correct. The withdrawable reward really was zero.

But the low-level call made with a zero amount still executed the receiving contract's fallback function.

The attacker never needed the reward. What he needed was control flow.

The judgment “the amount is zero, so it is harmless” missed something else: the amount transferred really was zero, but control was still handed over.

This was one of the costliest oversights in this domain, and its shape is worth remembering: quantity was checked; authority was overlooked.

The previous essay described how gas prices each execution step. It measures resources consumed, not the amount of money transferred. A zero-value call still consumes gas, and code executed by the recipient adds costs. Paying those fees does not make handing over control safe: the attacker wanted not the zero-valued reward, but an opportunity to reenter the split function.

The precise interval exploited lay between the split function giving proportional ETH to the child DAO and burning the original balance.

Proposal 59, commonly remembered as “Lonely, so lonely,” supplied the path needed for the malicious split. The attacker had waited through the split proposal's minimum debate period in advance.

This was therefore not an incidental discovery on June 17 followed by immediate execution.

The attack began on June 17, 2016. While it was still underway, the Ethereum Foundation issued an emergency announcement explaining that the attacker was exploiting a recursive-call vulnerability to move ETH into a child DAO and publishing the malicious child DAO's address. It also noted that the child DAO's time restriction prevented the ETH from immediately becoming ordinary ETH freely controlled by the attacker.

The first reliable order of magnitude was approximately 3.6 million ETH.

A signed letter later claimed the precise amount was 3,641,694, but its author's identity was never verified by an on-chain signature or equivalent means. It can therefore only be treated as a text purporting to come from the attacker, not as a determination of the attacker's identity or rights.

The dollar value depended on the pricing moment. Headlines saying $50 million or $60 million merely converted roughly 3.6 million ETH at different prices on June 17; they were not a second loss figure. ETH's rapid fall after the attack further separated valuations at pre-attack, mid-attack, and post-attack prices.

To ask how long it lasted and how many times it happened, one must first define an occurrence.

A single outer transaction could contain many layers of recursive internal calls, and the attack also used multiple malicious contracts and multiple outer transactions. The initial extraction on June 17 lasted several hours.

Outer transactions, recursive calls, and child DAO asset transfers are three different units. The available primary sources do not share one authoritative count of how many times the attack was executed.

Whether there had been warnings before the attack requires three distinctions.

On May 26, 2016, three researchers published a systematic critique of The DAO, discussing voter apathy, affirmative-vote bias, and mechanism-design attacks involving proposals and splitting, and recommending that investment be delayed. The next day, one of them publicly called for a temporary moratorium, which Vitalik Buterin publicly supported.

But saying that paper precisely disclosed the eventual reentrancy attack a month in advance is inaccurate. Its most prominent content concerned mechanism design and governance risks, not the complete call chain.

On June 9, Peter Vessenes publicly discussed recursive calls in contracts: an external call before a balance was zeroed could let the recipient reenter. This was directly related to the core technical nature of the eventual attack.

On June 12, Slock.it publicly responded, saying the framework's updated version protected against such calls, while also judging the current funds to be safe.

The historical significance is therefore not that they knew nothing about reentrancy. Quite the contrary: they already knew the general recursive-call risk.

The problem was their failure to recognize that the deployed contract could combine splitting and rewards to turn a general defect into a drain of the principal.

5. June 28

In the month after the attack, the most revealing issue is not the hard fork but why the soft fork died.

The Ethereum Foundation's first proposal on June 17 was a soft fork, explicitly marked “no rollback”: from a specified height, relevant calls reducing DAO or child DAO balances would invalidate the entire transaction, freezing the funds.

On June 21, volunteers began using the same splitting weakness to move large amounts of still-vulnerable ETH into white-hat DAOs they controlled. Historical accounts generally describe this as protecting the remaining roughly 70% of funds.

The nature of this counterattack needs to be clear.

It was neither a forced protocol-level transfer nor a police freeze. It used the same category of already-deployed contract behavior as the attacker to take the remaining assets before the attacker could. Technically, it was closer to a defensive exploit.

It also meant funds moved from the main DAO, collectively controlled by all token holders, into child contracts controlled by white hats. They assumed the operational risks of protection and eventual repayment, while acquiring substantial temporary de facto control.

On June 23, outside developers including Peter Todd publicly called for an actual expression of coin-holder views and criticized vague community consensus as insufficient authorization. This was an important prelude to the later dispute about representation.

On June 24, the Foundation published the soft-fork voting arrangements. One detail matters: miners signaled support through block gas limits, not through DAO Token voting.

Then came June 28, the real turning point.

A high-severity denial-of-service flaw was discovered in the client implementation.

An attacker could construct a transaction that would eventually touch the frozen state, making miners perform substantial computation before discovering that the soft-fork rule invalidated it. An invalid transaction would not pay the corresponding fee.

This created an attack surface for making miners compute almost for free.

The Foundation consequently asked the community to vote against the soft fork or disable the option.

Read this passage alongside the previous essay.

That essay described how gas addressed the structural requirement that resources have a price and execution have bounds. Its entire function was to make every step of execution paid for.

The emergency rule written to freeze funds created precisely a stretch of computation that was executed but not charged for.

A rule added to fight a fire became a new source of fire. And a fire of the same kind.

This appears for the first time in the series, and will return in other forms: a mechanism added temporarily to handle one accident becomes the entry point for the next. The reason is simple: the temporary addition has not passed all the tests required by the system it enters, yet has the same network-wide force.

The Foundation's later retrospective also disclosed potential data races in the hastily written soft-fork implementation that ordinary unit tests could scarcely catch. This directly encouraged the development of network-level simulation tools.

Once the soft fork left the main path, discussion concentrated on a hard fork or nonintervention.

On July 15, the Foundation formally published the hard-fork specification: at the start of block 1,920,000, ETH from listed relevant accounts would move into a refund contract.

The same announcement said CarbonVote's result at block 1,894,000 would determine the Geth client's default fork option.

Notice “default.” It was not compulsory, but in practice it determined which side the vast majority of nodes that did not change their configuration would follow.

6. Five Point Five Percent

CarbonVote was an expression of opinion weighted by ETH balances. Its figures make a textbook case in measurement definitions.

A preserved July 16 snapshot shows total participation of approximately 4,542,416 ETH, or 5.5% of the roughly 82.05 million supply then. About 3,964,516 ETH, or 87%, supported the fork, while roughly 577,899 ETH, or 13%, opposed it.

About a quarter of the supporting votes came from one address.

Those numbers can stand here without commentary.

Only one methodological point is needed: this was a balance-weighted expression of views, not a vote by persons. No mechanism prevented one person from using multiple addresses or temporarily moving coins in to vote.

The widely circulated 97% appeared in headlines around July 19 and referred to a later page and a different basis for counting participation.

Whichever figure one adopts, it cannot become “87% or 97% of Ethereum users voted for the fork.” Still less can it become 97% of nodes or miners.

On July 20, block 1,920,000 executed the state change. The Foundation described the fork as smooth and initially observed roughly 85% of mining activity supporting the forked chain—not a headcount of individual miners.

Now for the technical question most often misstated: was it a rollback?

In the narrow sense of chain state and protocol engineering, no.

Ethereum did not move its chain tip back before June 17, delete those blocks, and resume block production at an old height. The attack transactions remain part of both chains' shared history.

Instead, block 1,920,000 introduced a special consensus state rule moving specified account balances into the refund contract. The Foundation itself called it an irregular state change.

In terms of economic outcomes and political argument, however, “rollback” has a broader meaning: a subsequent protocol rule selectively offset the asset effects that earlier code execution had conferred on the malicious child DAO.

These two propositions can therefore both be true without contradiction.

It did not rewind the entire blockchain.

And it used protocol intervention to undo the attack's expected outcome in economic ownership.

The first describes the state-transition technique; the second expresses the normative sense of rollback invoked by opponents.

The two descriptions concern the same event at different levels. For most of the arguments over the following decade, one side used facts from the first level to refute the other's claims at the second.

The refund contract's principle was 1 ETH for 100 DAO Tokens.

On the day of the fork, the Foundation said approximately 4.5 million ETH had already been distributed to token holders, while another roughly 463,000 ETH remained under Curator control pending security review of refund tools for edge cases such as the extra balance.

“How much was recovered?” therefore requires at least three measurement bases.

For funds moved from the relevant state into the refund system at the fork, the Foundation gave approximately 12 million ETH, while an on-chain recalculation yielded about 12.002 million. This did not mean investors had withdrawn it.

Approximately 4.5 million ETH had actually been paid to holders on fork day. That was not the final cumulative refund.

And there is the figure in section eight: the portion visibly still unclaimed by its original rights holders in 2026.

Subtracting the third figure from the first does not produce a final withdrawal total. The main refund contract is a separate pool, and claims remain open.

7. Two Chains

Before block 1,920,000, what we now call ETH and ETC did not have different histories. They were one chain.

The divergence was this: clients supporting the state change accepted the exceptional balance transfer, while those opposing it continued validating blocks under the original state-transition rules.

The Foundation's client even expressly retained an anti-fork option and warned people remaining on the original chain about replay attacks.

Calling ETC a new chain created on July 20 is therefore true only in a naming sense. More precisely, the forked branch later retained the Ethereum name, the main development teams, and most hash power; the non-forked branch continued the original state and later adopted the name Ethereum Classic.

ETC's subsequent Declaration of Independence treated July 20, 2016, and block 1,920,000 as the formation of its political identity, placing immutability, fungibility, and the sanctity of the ledger at the center of its argument.

It listed three grievances: the soft-fork blacklist, CarbonVote's alleged lack of representativeness, and the hard fork's irregular state change.

This matters because the original opposition argument was not simply that the attacker should get the money.

Its claim was that even when an intervention's beneficiaries deserved sympathy, allowing the protocol to rewrite state selectively according to value judgments about particular transactions would itself damage predictable ledger rules.

Nor can the supporting argument be reduced to refunds at any cost. Supporters argued that the attack exploited an implementation defect rather than investors' shared commercial intent; the funds were still time-locked in child DAOs; a narrow remedy could let original rights holders claim at the original ratio; and the community, miners, clients, and infrastructure could openly choose whether to adopt it.

Placed side by side, the arguments reveal that the dispute was not really about whether to trust code.

Fork supporters could say that the protocol already allowed nodes to upgrade rules, and that the fork itself was code.

Opponents said that if the base layer could make exceptions for particular historical property disputes, application-layer promises of irreversibility were weakened.

The dispute was over which layer of code should carry the stronger commitment.

That is more useful than asking whether to trust code, because it permits a further question: should an application contract's promise be harder than the protocol rules that run it?

If not, any contract's irreversibility ultimately depends on its operators not changing the rules. If so, the protocol loses the ability to change itself, including to repair its own defects.

Each answer has a cost, and there is still no answer all parties accept.

Exchanges turned the theoretical minority branch into a tradable asset.

Poloniex announced ETC trading pairs in late July 2016. Conventional chronologies give July 24, while ETC's own history gives July 23 under a different time zone. That one-day difference should remain a timestamp qualification rather than be forcibly erased.

This was crucial. Before then, the non-forked chain could have nodes and miners, but whether its block rewards had an independent market price remained unclear. Trading pairs let miners directly compare coin prices and mining returns on the two chains for the first time; original holders suddenly had two tradable sets of balances as well.

The Foundation observed initial mining support of roughly 85% on fork day. That was not a proportion of individual miners, nor a permanent allocation: it does not establish that ETC would always retain 15% of hash power, since miners could subsequently switch with prices and difficulty.

A fundamental break in comparability came later: after Ethereum's 2022 transition to proof of stake, it no longer had proof-of-work hash power directly comparable to ETC's. Interpreting Ethereum's zero hash rate today as a lack of security is a category error.

As of late August 2026, two mining-data providers estimated ETC's network hash rate at approximately 160–163 TH/s from difficulty and recent block times. These are vendor estimates, not a directly recorded on-chain total, and should be given as a range.

Market capitalizations can be compared on a common basis. At the time of this research, the same provider put Ethereum at approximately $286.2 billion and Ethereum Classic at $1.115 billion, a ratio of about 257 to one using the same method. These are one provider's point-in-time values on one day.

Development activity has no equally clean single measure. Comparing contributors across the entire Ethereum ecosystem with commits in one ETC core-client repository would be seriously misleading.

Protocol trajectories can be described more securely: Ethereum continued extensive upgrades and eventually moved to proof of stake; ETC retained proof of work while following its own sequence of upgrades. Its code was not simply frozen in 2016 and abandoned, but neither did it follow Ethereum's entire path.

The proof-of-work chain with less hash power later paid a price.

ETC's official history records a successful large reorganization and double spend in January 2019, followed by three successful majority-hash-power attacks on July 31, in early August, and on August 29, 2020.

Coinbase produced node-level retrospectives on the first two 2020 attacks. It identified approximately 800,000 ETC in double spends, then worth about $5.8 million, in the first, and roughly 460,000 ETC, or $3.2 million, in the second. It explicitly said it was not a victim, but greatly increased ETC deposit confirmation requirements after detecting very large reorganizations. The first attack also exposed differences in client handling of extremely deep reorganizations, causing a temporary network split.

The third attack on August 29 reorganized more than 7,000 blocks. That figure comes from secondary mining-pool and media records and measures something different from the first two attacks' double-spend amounts.

The consequences went beyond price. Exchanges increased confirmation requirements, delaying funds' availability; ETC developers subsequently discussed and deployed client rules raising the cost of deep reorganizations.

Rather than decide for the reader, set out the facts: a chain whose political identity arose from opposition to discretionary ledger changes still had to investigate what additional resistance client rules should impose on an excessively deep competing chain when facing majority-hash-power reorganizations. Those rules also generated controversy within ETC.

8. No One Comes to Claim It

On July 25, 2017, the US SEC published its investigative report on The DAO under Section 21(a) of the Exchange Act.

Its central legal conclusion was that The DAO's tokens were securities under US federal securities law, specifically investment contracts. Using a virtual organization, smart contracts, ETH rather than dollars, or blockchain issuance could not remove a financing arrangement meeting the definition of a security from securities law.

The part about reliance on others' efforts matters most.

The SEC found that purchasers reasonably relied on significant managerial efforts by Slock.it, its co-founders, and the Curators: Slock.it created and promoted The DAO, maintained communication channels, selected the initial Curators, and publicly prepared its own first major proposal; Curators controlled the contractor whitelist and exercised substantial discretion over whether proposals could become executable.

Why did token holders still depend on others despite voting rights? The SEC reasoned that holders were numerous and dispersed, and could vote only within the prewritten protocol framework and among candidate proposals admitted by Curators.

What the SEC did not do must also be listed in full.

This was not a court judgment against anyone, nor an adjudication that anyone had violated securities law. The report expressly emphasized that point.

The Commission explicitly said it had decided not to pursue enforcement based on the conduct then known, without supplying a set of exculpatory reasons to unpack.

It did not finally determine whether The DAO was an investment company under the Investment Company Act.

Its main function was market notice and legal interpretation: federal securities law applies to digital-asset issuance and trading meeting its definitions, regardless of whether an organization is called a DAO.

“SEC found The DAO to be an illegal securities scam” therefore needs substantial rewriting. What the evidence supports is that the SEC classified these tokens as securities under this factual structure, while bringing no enforcement action and making no court-like adjudication of violations.

Now we come to the real-world section.

And this section cannot be written.

Identifiable organizations include Germany's Slock.it, a Swiss partner, and communities around several off-chain coordinating bodies. Observable market effects include ETH's sharp fall within hours of the attack news and exchanges' subsequent need to handle the fork, ETC listings, replay risks, and more complicated asset entitlements.

No reliable data answer these questions: how many people lost jobs, how many individuals in each country suffered losses, how much household wealth fell, or how financing access changed.

The fundamental reason lies in the design itself.

Subscriptions were pseudonymous, with no identity checks. Addresses cannot reliably be mapped to people, businesses, or regions. The DAO itself had no conventional employee roster.

The SEC investigation could establish Slock.it's corporate role, but could not reconstruct the global geographic distribution of ultimate beneficiaries from the chain.

One should therefore not multiply 11,000 or 22,000 addresses by an average loss to manufacture estimates of victims or regional effects.

That absence is itself part of the historical evidence.

After a crisis in a capital pool advertised as global, permissionless, and pseudonymous, on-chain asset flows are exceptionally easy to trace precisely, while off-chain social losses are exceptionally difficult to identify.

The second essay ended with electricity and money clearly represented in those public materials, while people's work and circumstances were not presented with the same completeness.

Here the gap goes further: money is recorded to the smallest unit, but on-chain addresses alone cannot reliably reconstruct every participant's identity.

This is not an oversight. It is the design.

Allowing participation through addresses without first presenting a real-world identity was an important design choice. It did not guarantee that no one could ever be identified. It meant that after a failure, the chain would not automatically supply a complete list of people harmed. Finding those people still required off-chain information and their cooperation.

These are two sides of the same thing. One cannot take only half.

Finally, return to the money.

By 2026, the refund process had still not technically closed its books.

Approximately 70,500 ETH remained in the historical extra-balance refund contract, with another roughly 4,600 ETH-equivalent in Curator multisignature and historical edge-case accounts. In 2026, more than 75,000 ETH of long-unclaimed assets were reorganized into a security fund, principally for staking, with its returns funding Ethereum security work.

The original main refund contract was not part of that fund and continued to honor the exchange of 100 DAO for 1 ETH. Extra-balance claims also remained open. The project said it had returned another 738 ETH to claimants since its 2026 launch.

Ten years on, people are still coming to claim.

Some may never come. The balances alone cannot tell us who will return.

This is the shape of the remainder here.

It is not unrecorded money. The money is recorded, while the rights holders remain absent for years. Some may never return, but an unclaimed address alone does not tell us who they are or why they have not come.

Their money is on-chain, precise to the smallest unit and visible to anyone. But who they are, where they are, whether they are still alive: the chain does not know and cannot know.

This is the reverse of the third essay. There, the data remained but no one read them. Here, the money remains but no one claims it.

The two situations share something: transfers and balances can be checked against on-chain rules, but doing so does not automatically supply the missing off-chain part.

An intervention made to provide refunds thus remains unsettled ten years later and has grown a new governance problem: how to handle long-unclaimed on-chain property while keeping the door open to rights holders who may still appear.

Return to the four sets of rules in section one.

What the EVM would execute: it executed it that day.

What participants originally intended: that was never in the code.

What new rules nodes would accept: votes, defaults, and upgrades decided that.

Which chain the market would call Ethereum: exchanges and its users decided that.

Four questions, four sets of rules, four ways of deciding.

July 20, 2016, eliminated none of those answers.

It turned the different answers into separate chains that could keep running.

Both chains still produce blocks today. On one, the money was refunded; on the other, it was not.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
