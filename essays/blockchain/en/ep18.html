The Merge

The Merge replaced the consensus mechanism and sharply reduced the chain’s energy use, while moving concentration, MEV, and censorship issues into staking and block construction.

1. The First Half and the Second Half

The most durable description of this upgrade, requiring the least rhetorical embellishment, is this:

Ethereum retained its existing execution state and virtual machine while handing block production, fork choice, and finalization to Beacon Chain validators, ending proof-of-work miners' role in this main chain's consensus. It ended the former proof-of-work mining issuance stream and the chain's own demand for large-scale computational energy, while moving security-budget, maximal-extractable-value, capital-threshold, concentration, and regulatory debates into a new set of institutions and market structures: staking, validation services, and block construction.

The first and second halves of that sentence rest on entirely different grades of evidence.

The first half concerns code. The second concerns the world.

By Episode 18, this series has repeatedly encountered layers of evidence. This instance is unusually clean: the dividing line falls in the middle of a single sentence.

The two halves were subsequently quoted as though they were one thing. After an upgrade, people remember its name. Under that name sit, on one side, verifiable protocol facts and, on the other, more than a dozen sets of measurements, each needing its own definition.

In circulation, the second half usually wears the certainty of the first.

Protocol materials directly establish the first half: what code was replaced, at which block height, and under what triggering condition. Anyone can check.

Every economic, concentration, and regulatory proposition in the second half must retain its own measurement definition and evidentiary layer.

The decline in energy use depends on the models used to estimate both sides. Concentration depends on which addresses are attributed to which entities. The severity of censorship depends on whether the denominator is all blocks or payload deliveries. The yield depends on whether priority fees and extractable value are included, and whether service-provider commissions and inflation are deducted.

These disputes are not at the same level. The first half can be verified. The second can only be measured, and each measurement must first say what it is measuring.

The task here is to separate those halves.

The remainder can already be located.

This time it is particularly clear: what was replaced did not disappear.

It changed its name, its bearer, and its method of measurement, and moved somewhere beyond the protocol’s authority.

Of those four changes, the last matters most.

As long as something remains within the protocol, it has a definition everyone must agree on. Once moved outside, who defines it and how it is measured become separate matters of dispute.

The next seven sections chiefly address disputes of that kind.

2. The Part That Was Replaced

The briefest accurate description is that the upgrade did not rewrite Ethereum’s accounts, contracts, virtual machine, and transaction execution into another chain.

It replaced the part of consensus deciding which execution block belongs to the canonical chain: proof of work gave way to the Beacon Chain’s proof of stake, which had already operated independently for nearly two years.

That is also the practical meaning of separating the execution and consensus layers.

Execution clients continued maintaining account state, running the virtual machine, checking transactions, and executing payloads. Consensus clients maintained beacon state, verified attestations, and ran fork choice and finalization.

After the Merge, an ordinary full node needed both clients operating together and communicating through an authenticated interface. A validator client held the signing keys and used that node pair to perform its duties.

The mainnet announcement specifically warned that the old miner-style arrangement, running only an execution client, was no longer enough.

The Beacon Chain ran first and was connected later.

At genesis on December 1, 2020, the activation threshold of at least 16,384 validators with 32 ETH each had been met. From that day, it independently handled validator registration, randomized assignments, attestations, fork choice, and finalization, while miners continued handling real accounts and contracts on mainnet.

Between 2020 and 2022, it was therefore a functioning proof-of-stake consensus system, but not yet the consensus engine for mainnet transactions.

The deposit path at the time is central to understanding the later rise of liquid staking.

Thirty-two ETH were sent from the execution layer to the deposit contract. The Beacon Chain recognized the information and placed the validator in an activation queue. Before withdrawals were introduced, that path was one-way at the protocol level.

The specification defined validator registration, activation, and voluntary exit. Exiting validation duties did not mean transferring principal back to a spendable execution-layer address.

What validators actually do also deserves a clear account.

Time is divided into twelve-second slots, with thirty-two slots forming an epoch. One active validator is selected to propose a block in each slot. Others are assigned to committees to attest to the chain head and checkpoints they observe.

Normal rewards come from performing these duties correctly and promptly. Proposing is an infrequent lottery event. Attesting is the regular, continuous work of the great majority of validators.

Two consensus layers operate together here. One mainly determines which current chain head to follow. The other gives epoch checkpoints justified and finalized status.

Under normal network conditions, a checkpoint takes about two epochs, or approximately 12.8 minutes, to finalize.

Finalization is not the same concept as six confirmations under proof of work.

Proof of work has no in-protocol finalization flag. The deeper a transaction is buried, the lower, ordinarily, the probability that an attacker can reorganize it with another chain of greater cumulative work. That is a probabilistic economic judgment.

This consensus layer instead produces finality as a protocol state. Conflicting finality would force at least a substantial share of validating power to violate provable slashing conditions.

This does not mean a finalized block is physically incapable of ever being changed.

It means crossing that boundary requires identifiable, punishable consensus violations on a large scale.

Ordinary penalties and slashing must also be distinguished.

Going offline or missing an attestation or proposal ordinarily first means forgone rewards or ordinary deductions. If the chain fails to finalize for a prolonged period, another mechanism progressively reduces the weight of nonparticipants.

Actual slashing targets provably contradictory signatures: signing two different block headers for the same slot, or making conflicting attestations. Slashed validators are forcibly exited and face an additional correlation penalty. Losses become more severe when many validators are slashed together because of a shared failure.

Downtime and double-signing are therefore different risks.

The operation at the moment of the Merge was narrowly defined.

First the consensus layer upgraded to recognize the Merge logic. Validators then monitored whether the execution layer reached the terminal total difficulty. On mainnet, that threshold was fixed as a twenty-three-digit number.

Once cumulative difficulty reached or exceeded it, the system stopped waiting for another miner to solve a proof of work. The next execution block came from a proposer selected by the Beacon Chain.

At approximately 06:42 on September 15, 2022, mainnet reached that transition point at block 15,537,393.

The protocol did not migrate user balances, redeploy contracts, or reset the chain’s history.

What changed was the authority over block production and fork choice.

The former difficulty field ceased to represent computational work. The corresponding virtual-machine opcode was repurposed to supply a source of proof-of-stake randomness.

It is therefore inaccurate to describe the upgrade as moving data from a proof-of-work chain onto a proof-of-stake chain.

The narrower account is that the existing execution state and history continued, while the Beacon Chain moved from a parallel consensus system to the canonical consensus engine for that execution layer.

3. The Bomb Was Moved Three Times

The timeline must distinguish development targets, actual testnet transitions, and mainnet protocol events, because the difficulty bomb was postponed repeatedly.

The Beacon Chain launched on December 1, 2020. A longer-running public Merge testnet launched in December 2021. In March 2022, the network officially described as the final purpose-built testnet before public-testnet merges launched and completed its Merge. On June 8, 2022, the first long-running public testnet transitioned. The second followed around late June and early July. In August, the last major public testnet merged. The August 24 mainnet announcement confirmed completion of all public-testnet transitions.

Mainnet’s consensus layer upgraded on September 6, 2022. The execution-layer transition completed on September 15.

The withdrawal path was not activated until April 12, 2023.

The delays are best not attributed to one defect.

The original proposals show a moving window. One in 2021 moved the bomb to that December, stating the aim of completing the upgrade beforehand. The next moved it to June 2022 and explicitly allowed another postponement if the upgrade was not ready. Another delayed it by approximately 700,000 blocks to mid-September 2022, to prevent the bomb from degrading mainnet block production before preparations were complete.

These materials do not support the account that developers missed the same fixed promised date three times.

They support this: developers used the difficulty bomb as a protocol mechanism to force the eventual departure of proof of work. Whenever implementation, client interoperability, and test coverage had not reached the threshold for a mainnet release, they moved the bomb first.

One testnet announcement was explicit: if testing exposed problems or insufficient coverage, those would be addressed before proceeding to the next testnet. Mainnet’s upgrade epoch and difficulty threshold would be set only after the public testnets were stable.

The testnets were not ceremonial rehearsals.

They were release gates for the mainnet date.

The upgrade required different implementations of execution and consensus clients to interoperate in pairs. The transition itself also involved two kinds of trigger, one based on an epoch and one on a difficulty threshold. Compared with an ordinary single-layer hard fork, this introduced a cross-client, cross-layer state machine.

That also explains why announcements in those years stated triggering conditions rather than exact clock times. The precise hour and minute of a cumulative-difficulty transition could be known only when it occurred.

Withdrawals were deliberately excluded from this upgrade.

Contemporary announcements explained why: to reduce the risk of the mainnet transition, withdrawals were left for a later upgrade.

For more than two years, from deposits in 2020 until withdrawals opened in April 2023, ETH could enter validators and accrue consensus rewards, and validators could trigger an exit from their duties. But the protocol offered no path returning principal and accrued balances to an ordinary execution-layer account.

Here it is important to trace both sides.

Principal being nonwithdrawable at the protocol level did not mean holders had no liquidity at all.

Liquid-staking protocols could issue transferable representative assets. Users thereby exchanged Beacon Chain protocol-liquidity risk for smart-contract, governance, operator, market-discount, and liquidity risks.

Only after withdrawals opened did the underlying ETH obtain a genuine protocol-level redemption path. Representative assets still offered convenience and composability, but the significance of the function they originally served changed.

What those two years created did not disappear with that change. The liquid-staking protocols that grew during the period later became the largest part of the staking structure. The protocol itself solved their original problem, but they were already there.

4. 2.62 Megawatts

The important thing about energy use is not memorizing 99.95%.

It is knowing that the number did not come from an electricity meter.

The foundation’s widely cited 2021 estimate itself called the exercise a rough estimate.

The proof-of-stake system being modeled was the already operating Beacon Chain; the transaction-executing mainnet still used proof of work. The author observed approximately 140,592 validators and 16,405 distinct deposit addresses, removed known exchange and service-provider addresses, and assumed an average home staker ran approximately 5.4 validators. A conservative home-node hardware assumption divided approximately 100 watts by 5.4 validators. Adding hosted operators produced an estimate of approximately 2.62 megawatts for the whole proof-of-stake network.

The article expressly acknowledged that the hardware actually used by every operator was unknowable.

It then compared that figure with another organization’s contemporary estimate of approximately 5.13 gigawatts for proof-of-work Ethereum.

Dividing 2.62 megawatts by 5,130 megawatts and subtracting from one gives approximately 99.949%.

That is where the 99.95%, quoted tens of thousands of times, came from.

The numerator was an assumption-based estimate. The denominator was another estimate from another organization.

Dividing two estimates yielded a percentage with two decimal places.

Those decimal places subsequently lived an independent life for four years.

It was a ratio of two estimates.

That does not mean the conclusion was wrong. Multiple models put the reduction in the network’s own energy use above 99%, very likely above 99.8% or even 99.9%.

The direction is undisputed.

The disputed points are the two decimal places and the impression of direct measurement they convey.

This is not pedantry. “99.95%” and “more than 99%” read differently: the former sounds measured, the latter estimated.

They describe the same evidence.

That is the remainder’s first form.

Proof-of-work energy use also required estimation: hash power could be inferred from block timing and difficulty, manufacturers published machine power specifications, and mining sites had electricity meters. Turning those sources into a global total still required assumptions about equipment mix, utilization, and location.

Proof of stake requires a different set of assumptions: how many validators share a machine, how much power it actually uses, and how hosted and home nodes are combined. Servers also have power specifications and electricity meters. The difficulty is completely mapping distributed hardware and workloads, not the absence of any measurable basis.

Energy use did not disappear. The model needed to estimate it changed.

More precisely, actual energy consumption plainly fell, and fell enormously. That is undisputed.

What changed was the calculation behind the number. Previously it required estimates of specialized mining machines and their electricity use. Now it also requires estimates of how many validation duties a general-purpose machine carries and how much additional power they consume. Both have checkable components and inputs that on-chain records alone cannot supply.

Precisely measuring network-wide validator energy use would require knowing how many machines actually run, their configurations and locations, and whether they perform other tasks. Validator count is not machine count. This information is not automatically recorded on-chain, and the material used here supplies no complete global hardware-and-energy census. Surveys and sampling are possible; lacking such a census does not establish that no institution has authority to collect information.

The network-wide total cited here is therefore still a model estimate, not a complete direct measurement of the network. That does not make it unimportant; it means an estimate should not be cited as a direct measurement.

5. Three Cash Flows

After the Merge, validator income was not one interest rate but three different cash flows.

The first was consensus-layer issuance. The payment was newly issued ETH, with dilution economically borne by all existing holders. Recipients were validators correctly performing attestation, proposal, and sync duties. This was the protocol’s security budget, not a direct payment by transaction users.

The second was priority fees. Transaction senders paid them, and block proposers received them. Base fees were not part of this income: base fees were burned.

The third was maximal extractable value. Its ultimate economic sources could be arbitrage spreads, decentralized-exchange users’ slippage, liquidation opportunities, or searcher profits. It was distributed among builders, searchers, and proposers.

There were also service fees. People entrusting ETH to liquid-staking protocols, exchanges, or professional node providers paid them. They account for an important difference between validators’ gross rewards and users’ net annualized returns.

That explains why annualized yields have historically declined yet occasionally spiked.

As total active stake grows, protocol issuance yield per unit staked declines. But when on-chain activity surges, priority fees and extractable value can increase suddenly, raising validators’ total returns for a period.

Verifiable historical snapshots do not form a fully consistent series. They should be placed alongside one another rather than selecting just one.

Around January 2023, a staking provider’s consensus-layer analysis for roughly 500,000 validators gave approximately 4.16%. By August 2023, a data provider calculated an average of approximately 4.4% for the top eight liquid-staking protocols since January 2022. In December 2024, another provider’s network-staking-yield measure showed 3.08% nominal and 2.73% real, the latter additionally deducting token-supply inflation. In August 2026, a benchmark annualizing the preceding twenty-four hours of net network staking rewards showed 2.6488%. At the same time, two exchanges advertised customer product yields of approximately 1.74% and 2.36%.

The last two are companies’ own figures, affected by commissions, geography, product structure, and methodology. They cannot establish that the network’s actual yield was only that much.

Extractable value is an especially uneven component of reward volatility.

Academic measurement from September 2022 to May 2023 found approximately 160,000 ETH paid to proposers through the block-building market. The top 20% of proposer payments accounted for approximately 72% of the total value paid.

One economic advantage of pooled staking is therefore not a higher nominal protocol reward rate for large holders.

It is the ability to smooth a highly skewed lottery across many validators.

The threshold for independent validation is another matter.

At the Merge, a typical validator had to lock in 32 ETH, maintain execution, consensus, and validator clients, and preserve network connectivity and signing-key security. September 2022 documentation recommended approximately 16 GB of RAM and a 2 TB SSD for a full node.

By 2026, official minimum guidance specified two or more CPU cores, 16 GB of RAM, a 2 TB SSD, and at least 25 Mbit/s connectivity, with higher recommendations for validation.

Hardware requirements change with state growth and protocol upgrades. There is no protocol-fixed price for the computer needed to run a validator.

“The rich get richer” also needs to be split into two claims.

The in-protocol version is too broad. Under the basic reward formula, an entity with twice the effective stake and equal performance has roughly twice the weight and expected rewards, giving it more in absolute terms. The protocol does not automatically award a higher percentage rate per ETH to a large holder. On the contrary, issuance yield per unit falls as total active stake increases.

If all participants earn the same percentage with the same performance and reinvestment behavior, proportional staking rewards alone do not mechanically increase large holders’ share of wealth.

The version concerning the world around the protocol has substance: the 32 ETH independent-entry threshold; fixed hardware and operating costs; institutions’ economies of scale in uptime and key management; enormous variance in extractable-value income; pooling’s smoothing of that variance; and builders’ scale advantages in exclusive order flow.

These can give larger participants more stable or higher risk-adjusted returns.

The counterargument also deserves a place. Pooling and liquid staking let small holders participate with far less than 32 ETH and provide liquidity for stake that would otherwise be locked.

It is therefore equally inaccurate to say that people with less than 32 ETH cannot earn staking rewards.

The cost is that small participants generally transfer some control, fees, and risks to a protocol, operator, or exchange.

That joins the two sides of this section: the lower threshold is real, and the method of lowering it is to introduce an intermediary.

6. Thirty Percent and Thirty Percent

Concentration is the area most easily misrepresented by one number.

First, what is counted is staked ETH attributed to an entity or protocol, not natural persons. A protocol can include many node operators. An exchange’s legal entity can use many validators.

Second, after 2025 the maximum effective balance of one validator could rise to 2,048 ETH, making validator counts and controlled ETH even less interchangeable.

Historical snapshots with limited comparability look like this.

On September 9, 2022, a few days before the Merge, one data provider’s address attribution put a liquid-staking protocol at approximately 31% and three centralized exchanges together at approximately 30%. Four entities already exceeded half, but the protocol cannot be equated with one node operator.

In late August 2023, a data provider summed the protocols it defined as liquid staking and reported a 43.7% share, while also listing approximately 11.3 million out of 26.4 million ETH. Those two rounded quantities divide to about 42.8%, not the reported percentage. Until the original timing and definitions are aligned, the three should not be presented as one exact calculation.

An academic literature review for the end of 2023 gave 37%, alongside approximately 28% for exchanges and approximately 15% for staking pools.

An address-label snapshot around April 2024 put that protocol at approximately 29.6% and five listed exchanges together at approximately 22.4%. That was a lower bound for those five, not a total for all exchanges.

On August 14, 2025, the protocol had fallen to 24.4%, compared with a late-2023 peak of approximately 32.3% in the same series.

An April 2026 academic measurement reported 38.8 million ETH staked, approximately 32% of a 121.6 million ETH supply.

One conflict should be preserved rather than repaired away.

The August 2023 measure was 43.7%; the end-2023 compilation was 37%.

Only a few months apart, the apparent decline is substantial.

This cannot directly be read as a net 6.7 percentage points of capital leaving in four months. Sources may differ on whether exchange-issued liquid tokens count as liquid staking and on how staking pools and liquid-staking protocols are classified. Actual shares and the total-stake denominator can also change. Without rerunning the original data, the entire difference cannot be assigned to one explanation.

Without rerunning both underlying address-label datasets, the figures cannot be joined into a time series.

Such conflicts have appeared repeatedly in this series. Earlier ones mostly arose because institutions measured different objects. Here, the object is the same, but there is no accepted way to draw its boundaries.

The one-third threshold also needs rewriting.

It genuinely matters at the consensus layer: a sufficiently large share of uncooperative voting power can obstruct finalization, while conflicting finality involves provable, slashable behavior.

But approximately 30% of stake attributed to a protocol does not mean one person holds every signing key and can instantly order them all to misbehave.

Capital aggregation, governance organizations, collections of node operators, and individual validator keys are different layers.

A narrower statement of systemic risk is that highly correlated capital, operators, clients, cloud infrastructure, or regulatory obligations can produce common-mode failures or coordination risks.

The key is not whether a dashboard displays one name.

It is how likely that portion of stake is to act together during a failure, censorship event, or governance conflict.

That probability cannot be read from the chain. It depends on contracts, corporate structures, data-center locations, cloud providers, client versions, and the national laws governing each operator.

A large exchange under unified custody and legal control should therefore not be compared directly with a protocol-plus-operators structure merely because each has a 20% share.

Empirical research itself has no single answer to whether proof of stake is more decentralized than proof of work.

A longitudinal study of the Beacon Chain concluded that Ethereum’s proof-of-stake voting power was roughly as dispersed as that of its proof-of-work predecessor, while emphasizing the substantial effect of liquid and custodial staking services on concentration.

Another cross-chain study, applying different metrics to 2022–2024 data, concluded that consensus power became more concentrated after Ethereum moved to proof of stake.

Their windows, entity groupings, and dispersion measures differ. They should be presented together rather than selecting one as conclusive.

The narrower conclusion supported by the material is that proof of stake removed concentration pressures associated with energy and specialized mining equipment, while shifting importance toward stake ownership, custody, staking pools, client distribution, block builders and relays, and governance.

Greater dispersion on one dimension does not imply greater dispersion across the system as a whole.

7. Sixty-Three and Ninety-Four Point Eight

Before the Merge, the core power over extractable value rested with miners. They won the right to produce blocks and could also order transactions themselves.

After the Merge, the proposer was a randomly selected ordinary validator, often without professional low-latency arbitrage, order-flow, or block-building infrastructure.

An outsourcing mechanism therefore emerged. Validators retained the right to propose and sign the final block, while outsourcing construction of the most valuable execution payload to professional builders.

This is generally called proposer-builder separation, but a qualification is necessary.

The arrangement deployed at the Merge was separation outside the protocol, not a mechanism natively mandated by Ethereum consensus rules. Validators could decline it and build locally.

The typical flow was this: searchers and users sent transactions or bundles to builders. Builders assembled complete execution payloads and bid to proposers for inclusion. Relays mediated between them, checked payloads and payments, and initially showed proposers only blinded block headers. Only after the proposer selected an attractive bid and signed was the payload revealed and broadcast.

Relays solved part of the trust problem while becoming new infrastructure intermediaries themselves.

Tracing both sides shows that this income was not created from nothing.

If a builder paid a validator 0.1 ETH, it might come from searchers’ bids for arbitrage opportunities, priority fees, or surplus generated through transaction ordering. The builder retained some and passed the rest to the proposer through its bid.

The traders bearing slippage, sandwiching, or liquidation were often not the same economic actors as the validators receiving proposer payments.

The market specialized rapidly after the Merge.

The same study found that the mechanism covered approximately 90% of blocks within about two months of launch. One relay exceeded 60% of relay share in November 2022, then fell to approximately one-quarter by the following May.

Another measurement a year later showed continued coverage near 90%, but the top three builders producing approximately 80% of those blocks.

An increasing number of relays does not automatically imply a dispersed builder market. Relays and builders are separate market layers.

At the August 2026 research cutoff, a dashboard covering a rolling window of approximately fourteen days showed roughly ten relays, thirty-six builders, 88.92% of blocks using the mechanism, and an average proposer payment of approximately 0.00649 ETH.

That is a short-window, live market indicator. It should not replace historical shares from 2022 to 2024.

The censorship controversy especially requires the term itself to be reconsidered.

Contemporary reporting on October 26, 2022, citing a monitoring page, said approximately 63% of Ethereum blocks were classified as compliant with one country’s sanctions list.

The number circulated widely that year.

Today, the same monitoring page uses a more explicit and different measure. It labels relays that declare they filter certain sanctioned transactions as censoring, then calculates their share of relay payload deliveries.

Its current page shows approximately 43.1%, with a historical peak of approximately 94.8% on October 26, 2022.

The same day yields 63% and 94.8%.

The chain’s history did not change.

The denominator did.

This is an especially clear shift in measurement definitions. Network activity for the same date appeared on two pages as percentages roughly thirty-two points apart, using different scopes and definitions. They should not be treated as precise measurements of a single denominator.

The former was the all-blocks measure used at the time. The latter is the payload-delivery measure reconstructed today.

The page also has a counting issue: the same block or payload may be delivered through multiple relays, so delivery share is not strictly equal to unique-block share.

Saying “43% of Ethereum blocks are censoring transactions” therefore stretches the metric beyond what it measures.

A second terminology problem is more fundamental.

A block being produced by a builder and its payload being delivered through a filtering relay are different facts.

Builders decide block contents. Relays decide which builders’ blocks they accept and which proposers receive them. Early political debate often labeled whole blocks using relay policies.

Without directly examining a builder’s inclusion policy and the transactions that a block could have included but did not, a relay label alone cannot prove the builder actively censored a particular sanctioned-address transaction.

The narrower statement supported by the data is that, in the months after the Merge, a large share of block-building traffic at one point passed through relays publicly stating that they filtered particular sanctioned transactions. That share subsequently fell substantially as neutral relays increased.

The historical 63% belongs to a particular period and an older measurement definition.

It does not mean 63% of validators refused to attest to blocks containing those transactions, still less that 63% of Ethereum transactions were censored.

The distinction directly affects systemic risk.

As long as other proposers and builders still include those transactions, relay-level filtering principally causes delays in inclusion.

Only if a large share of validating power additionally refuses to attest to or finalize blocks containing a class of transactions does the issue escalate from block-production policy to consensus-layer censorship and liveness.

The two cannot be described by the same percentage.

8. Where It Went

The regulatory timeline has also changed. It cannot stop at an agency saying staking is a security.

In February 2023, an exchange’s staking service was accused of conducting an unregistered securities offering. The company agreed to end the relevant U.S. service and pay $30 million. That was a settlement: it establishes acceptance of settlement terms, not a general precedent reached after a trial.

In June 2023, another company was sued over a similar service. That was an allegation, not a judicial finding.

By February 2025, that case had been withdrawn and dismissed with prejudice, barring refiling of the same claims. It ended without a merits ruling determining the service's legal character at the time.

In May and August 2025, a division of the agency issued two statements concluding that certain protocol-staking activities described in the documents did not involve securities offers and sales. Both were then staff views, not Commission rules. A commissioner publicly criticized how readily those generalizations could be mapped onto actual services.

In March 2026, the agency issued a formal interpretive document at Commission level, expressly treating protocol-staking activities under its described conditions as not constituting offers or sales under securities law.

That document carried greater authority than the earlier staff statements.

It still cannot be rewritten as “the United States has determined that all staking products are not securities.”

Its qualification was “in the manner and circumstances described.” If a staking service adds operator promises of returns, managerial efforts, asset management, or other arrangements, the object of analysis may no longer be bare protocol validation.

The supply claim likewise needs narrowing.

The official approximate pre-Merge figures were 13,000 ETH per day for miners and 1,700 for stakers. After the Merge, the miner stream stopped, reducing total issuance by approximately 88%.

The base-fee burn mechanism had existed separately since 2021.

Supply change therefore equals issuance minus burning. It does not equal 1,700 ETH per day.

At this research cutoff, a page calculating on-chain supply under its own formula showed a positive change of approximately 1.45 million ETH since the Merge, with annualized supply growth of approximately 0.30%.

Its calculation explicitly added execution-layer and Beacon Chain balances, then subtracted deposit accounting to avoid cross-layer double counting.

The page itself also called “ultrasound money” a meme and explained that supply falls only when burning exceeds issuance.

It is not a protocol monetary-policy term.

The actual on-chain series therefore does not support permanent deflation after the Merge.

Tracing both sides here can be done with a direct set of numbers.

Miners lost approximately 13,000 ETH per day of protocol issuance, priority fees, and block-level extractable value. They also no longer needed to buy electricity and continually upgrade equipment for this chain.

Validators gained mainnet execution-block proposal rights, priority fees, and extractable value. Their existing Beacon Chain issuance continued.

Nonstaking holders received no direct cash, but bore much less new dilution previously used to fund security.

For staking-service users, providers could take over validator operating risk, while asset custody, smart-contract, or governance risks fell back on the users.

The words “staking service” did not automatically place risk and income with the same actor.

Specific examples of mining companies’ off-chain changes are available. They cannot be extrapolated to the world.

One listed company stated in regulatory filings that, before the Merge, its approximately 25-megawatt GPU fleet mined Ethereum for daily revenue of approximately $120,000 to $150,000. Within a week after the Merge, the same equipment mined other proof-of-work coins and converted them, generating approximately $20,000 to $30,000 daily. Another filing described the average as falling from approximately $150,000 to $30,000, and revenue per kilowatt-hour from approximately $0.35 to $0.07.

That amounts to a self-reported revenue decline of approximately 80%. It remains the company’s own account. A filing’s presence in a regulator’s archive does not mean the regulator verified its attribution of operating results.

The example also shows why “all the mining machines became electronic waste” is inaccurate. At least this company’s disclosures show equipment first redirected to other algorithms rather than immediately scrapped.

There is no global miner asset-disposal ledger showing how many GPUs were sold to gamers, redirected to AI computing or other chains, or simply switched off.

The employment side is especially thin.

Listed-company filings can measure equipment capacity, coin production, revenue, and impairments. No credible global database isolates changes in mining jobs in 2022 attributable to this upgrade alone.

Token-price declines, tighter financing, and a reversal of the broader mining cycle occurred at the same time. Even a documented layoff cannot be described, without controls, as a specified number of jobs eliminated by this upgrade.

On this point, available primary material is conspicuously weaker than on-chain data.

That absence should remain visible.

Return now to the first section.

The first half can be checked against the protocol and block records. The second requires each economic, energy, and concentration claim to be examined separately, with its model, date, and measurement definition intact.

Everything discussed in the preceding seven sections is a facet of the same thing: what was replaced did not disappear.

Energy estimation acquired a different model. Concentration pressures moved. Extractable value formed a new block-building supply chain. Some censorship questions rested with builder and relay policies. Execution-block income on mainnet went to validators, while the issuance stream formerly paid to proof-of-work miners ended.

In each case, we can identify where it went.

In their new locations, each requires a fresh account of how it is measured. Some also become harder to see directly in public records.

This need not be attributed to concealment. The old system's electricity, machines, and hash power had no naturally complete inventory either. The new system adds staking services, builders, organizations, and contracts. Once the roles change, the old readings cannot simply be spliced onto the new ones: one must first ask whom each reading represents and what it includes.

Hashrate can be estimated using block timing together with difficulty. Identifying how much stake an entity controls requires evidence such as address labels. Both readings have their own inferential steps.

A mining site can have its meter read, and so can a validating node. The difficulty is extrapolating distributed samples to the whole network: how many devices run, how many tasks they carry, and how much electricity should be allocated to this chain.

Miners’ transaction ordering can be inspected block by block. Whether a block was censored depends on whether one is asking about the builder or the relay.

Ethereum's proof-of-work block rewards and fee income can be checked in block and state records; they were not paid through Bitcoin-style coinbase transactions. A miner's actual take-home income also depends on pool allocation, fees, and costs. A staker's depends on provider commissions and rewards from proposing, attesting, and other duties. Gross on-chain income and a person's net income are different on both sides.

The remainder is here.

It is neither something eliminated nor something newly created.

It is the portion of the same thing that becomes harder to see after changing location.

At approximately 06:42 on September 15, 2022, after block 15,537,393, this chain changed its consensus system.

The part it replaced happened to be the part easiest to verify.

The protocol divides time into twelve-second slots and thirty-two-slot epochs. Under normal conditions, a checkpoint reaches finality after about two epochs. Slots may be missed and finality may be delayed. A fixed time structure is not a certificate that every checkpoint finalized on schedule without an error for four years.

What it establishes is which block counts.

It does not establish who built that block, what is missing from it, or whether transactions left out should have been included.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
