FTX

FTX’s permissions, customer shortfall, criminal losses, and bankruptcy distributions are four different sets of accounts. Price risk was ultimately fixed to a single date.

1. Three Groups of People

Four numbers were later frequently used interchangeably, though they measure entirely different things.

$65 billion was the credit and borrowing limit configured for Alameda in FTX’s system. It does not mean the company actually took $65 billion.

Approximately $8 billion was the most common measure of FTX customer losses and the customer-funds shortfall in the criminal case.

Approximately $11 billion was the measure of losses and forfeiture used at sentencing after combining customers, equity investors, and Alameda creditors.

And $14.5 billion to $16.3 billion was the estimated distributable estate after bankruptcy asset sales, investment appreciation, litigation recoveries, and coordination of assets across jurisdictions.

That last figure, in particular, does not mean $16 billion in stolen customer cash was recovered.

Four numbers, four objects of measurement: a permissions parameter, a customer-side shortfall, a combined victim-loss measure, and an estimate of the bankruptcy estate.

Mixing them can produce both “the company stole $65 billion” and “the company ultimately repaid $16 billion.” Neither statement holds.

In the preceding sixteen episodes, the construct was always some kind of machine: computing power, a contract, a curve, an oracle, a mint-and-burn formula.

This time it was an internal ledger, together with account-level exceptions written into the trading system.

This is the least technical case in the series.

That is precisely its place in the series.

Each previous failure required an explanation of a mechanism: how the halting problem was priced, how an oracle brought outside events in, how a constant product converted inventory into price, how a mint-and-burn formula converted redemptions into issuance.

This time that is unnecessary. The mechanism was that one account was allowed to keep using assets while its balance was negative.

One setting.

By this point, the series has covered contract vulnerabilities, deceived verifiers, liquidations with no bidders, stalled oracles, and five modes of bridge failure. Each required some technical ingenuity.

This did not. It required only someone with permission to change the setting and nobody to check it.

The exchange in Episode 3 leaked for three years without discovery because nobody had been assigned to reconcile the assets. This was different: a complete risk-control system was operating normally, while this account had explicitly been placed outside its rules.

The skeleton of the case can be stated in one sentence. The next seven sections unpack it:

The people who benefited during the crime, those who bore the risk at collapse, and those entitled to asset recoveries after bankruptcy were not the same group.

The remainder lies along the two boundaries between these three groups. It is not simply money not yet traced. Later investigations reconstructed extensive fund flows in detail, but tracing those flows does not automatically erase the groups’ different positions in bearing losses.

It is the position of those two lines.

One line distinguishes criminal from lawful conduct. Courts had already entered convictions and sentences; appeals and other subsequent matters still required separate attention.

The other falls on November 11, 2022, and is administered through bankruptcy. For creditors, it determines a different matter that is also deeply personal.

Criminal proceedings address responsibility, punishment, forfeiture, and related issues. Here bankruptcy determines how claims across millions of accounts are valued and distributed, and how later token-price changes affect the parties. Neither process substitutes for the other.

2. Common Control

Alameda was not simply an FTX subsidiary.

The bankruptcy investigators divided the group into several parts: Alameda, the international business, the U.S. business, and the investment arm. There was cross-ownership, common management, shared offices, and frequent related-party transactions and transfers between them. Legally, they were numerous separate companies.

A capital-structure reconstruction early in the bankruptcy showed SBF indirectly holding approximately 75% of FTX Trading Ltd., with approximately 25% held by roughly 600 outside equity investors. A bankruptcy document for the parent of the U.S. business listed SBF at approximately 52.99%, Gary Wang at approximately 16.93%, and Nishad Singh at approximately 7.83%.

These percentages concern equity in specific entities. They cannot be added into a group-wide ownership percentage.

Two statements can therefore both be true: the companies were distinct legal persons; and their actual operations were extensively commingled.

The first concerns legal entities. The second concerns control, personnel, system permissions, and treasury management.

The bankruptcy investigation found frequent intragroup related-party transactions, often without the contracts, approvals, reconciliations, and related-party governance expected at an ordinary large financial institution.

For product lines, the quality of public material is considerably weaker than the later criminal evidence.

FTX was not publicly listed. It had no long-running public segment accounts comparable to peers’ annual filings. Investor material from 2021 said crypto futures contributed approximately 75% of revenue. That should be understood as the company’s presentation of product revenue to investors, not a statistic validated through regulatory filings.

Internal data later obtained by journalists put 2021 revenue at approximately $1.02 billion, compared with approximately $89 million in 2020, and 2021 operating profit at approximately $272 million and net profit at approximately $388 million. These figures come from internal financial material seen by reporters, not independently verifiable audited statements.

What actually connected exchange revenue, collateral, and internal balance sheets was the platform token.

When launched in 2019, its stated initial supply was 350 million tokens, with approximately 100 million circulating. Holding it provided trading-fee discounts, and it could serve as collateral on the platform.

More important was its buyback-and-burn mechanism. FTX said that each week it would buy and burn tokens using amounts equivalent to 33% of futures trading fees, 10% of net additions to the insurance fund, and 5% of fees from other uses, continuing until half the initial supply had been burned.

The token’s narrative value was therefore inherently linked to FTX’s own business revenue.

Buyers were purchasing more than a discount voucher. They were making an indirect bet on the exchange’s business.

That structure was not, by itself, fraud.

Buybacks and burns were widely treated as a virtue at the time. An exchange using its revenue to buy and burn its own token appeared to tie token holders’ interests to the platform.

It also meant that the higher the token’s price, the healthier the balance sheet holding it appeared. The healthier that balance sheet appeared, the more easily it could borrow to support the price.

The mint-and-burn mechanism in Episode 16 was another version of the same shape. There, the redemption asset and the asset under pressure were the same thing. Here, collateral and issuer belonged to the same enterprise.

In both, a system supported itself with itself. During an upswing, no problem was visible.

The problem arose when the token entered another balance sheet and began serving a financing function.

The report published on November 2, 2022, relied on a private document dated June 30. It showed approximately $14.6 billion in total assets. Among the largest individual entries were approximately $3.66 billion in unlocked platform tokens and another approximately $2.16 billion in platform-token collateral. Liabilities were approximately $8 billion, chiefly approximately $7.4 billion in loans.

The report itself explicitly cautioned that the document might represent only part of the picture, not a complete, audited consolidated balance sheet.

The claim that all its assets consisted of the token is therefore unsupported.

The narrower statement is that, in this possibly incomplete internal statement for a specific date, those two token-related entries alone totaled approximately $5.82 billion, about 40% of listed assets. The same token was also serving as collateral.

That created a high degree of circular dependence: a self-issued asset was also an affiliate’s asset and collateral for financing.

The $5.82 billion cannot automatically be understood as dollar liquidity available for sale at any time without market impact.

Selling required buyers. Those buyers’ willingness depended precisely on how well the issuing company was doing.

3. Three Different Things

To understand how customer money reached the other side, three things must be separated: bank accounts, internal ledgers, and account exemptions.

The first route was fiat deposits.

Early in its history, FTX struggled to obtain sufficient banking services. Some customers were instructed to wire dollars and other fiat currencies to bank accounts controlled by Alameda, while FTX’s internal ledger credited corresponding balances to the customers’ accounts.

Economically, the two sides looked like this: the customer gained a number on a screen and a ledger claim against FTX; the actual bank cash initially went elsewhere.

The group never subsequently established a system sufficient to segregate those balances strictly and reconcile them daily.

Traditional finance has a name for this control: segregation of customer assets. It is normally among the most basic obligations of a licensed institution.

The group’s regulatory coverage and actual segregation arrangements were not as comprehensive as those in a strictly regulated traditional customer-asset system. That does not mean most entities had no contractual, property-law, or anti-fraud duties at all.

The second route was exchange account permissions.

Ordinary customers could not withdraw without limit when they had no balance, and were subject to margin requirements and automatic liquidation. Alameda’s main account received exceptionally unusual permissions.

The bankruptcy investigation confirmed the much-discussed “allow negative balance” setting, which let the account continue using assets even with a negative balance. It also received an enormous borrowing allowance and exemptions from automatic liquidation.

One point-in-time comparison assembled by investigators showed a typical retail borrowing limit of zero, limits of up to approximately $150 million for certain preferred customers and market makers, and $65 billion for Alameda.

This bears repeating because it is among the most frequently misquoted numbers.

$65 billion was the system-configured borrowing limit.

It was not the amount actually borrowed.

The code history contains another frequently omitted detail. Investigators discussed an earlier permission allowing withdrawals within a borrowing allowance, and concluded that the later “allow negative balance” setting largely made the earlier permission redundant for withdrawal purposes.

Calling the entire mechanism a single secret backdoor button is therefore imprecise.

The evidence supports a set of exceptions explicitly written into trading and risk-control systems and specifically granted to one account.

It does not establish a hidden theft button that only one person could press.

This distinction excuses no one. Describing the mechanism as a button or account parameters does not absolve established misuse of funds. Criminal responsibility still turns on conduct, evidence, and applicable law.

It changes what an outside investigation should examine. A special button deserves scrutiny, but so do related-party privileges embedded in ordinary risk settings. Parameters within the system are not inherently invisible to an audit. Without complete permission tables, reconciliation, and related-party data, however, outside observers cannot easily discover them from the appearance of normal operation.

Trial testimony supplies much stronger evidence. Gary Wang admitted that, as a technical leader, he helped implement the special permissions and said they allowed Alameda to withdraw funds that did not belong to it. The appellate court’s 2026 review of the trial evidence also recorded his testimony about these permissions.

The court record therefore strongly supports an operating system design, rather than a theory reconstructed by the bankruptcy team through speculation.

Precision still matters when describing what the jury found.

The jury used a general criminal verdict form. It did not make separate special findings on a particular parameter, database update, or the $65 billion figure.

It found that prosecutors had proved all seven charges beyond a reasonable doubt. Account exemptions, bank deposits, and witness testimony supported those charges. They were not seven independent judicial findings of fact.

The amounts must also be separated individually.

$65 billion was the permissions ceiling. Approximately $13 billion mentioned in trial testimony for late 2022 was the scale of internal borrowing and negative balances at a particular time. Approximately $8 billion was the customer-loss measure. More than $1.7 billion concerned fraud losses to equity investors, and more than $1.3 billion fraud losses to Alameda lenders. More than $11 billion was the sentencing measure for forfeiture and combined victim losses.

They concern different victim categories and different measurement dates.

None can substitute for another.

Yet in circulating accounts they almost always do. The most common substitution uses the permissions ceiling to describe losses.

$65 billion is so large that nobody asks what it measures.

Configuring a limit is itself commonplace in trading systems. Setting it at that amount more likely meant simply: do not block this account.

4. Nine Days

The collapse was not a moment. Over nine days, a longstanding asset shortfall became an immediate cash run.

On November 2, the report based on the leaked document appeared. Its central revelation was not that FTX was already bankrupt, but the extraordinary concentration of related assets.

On November 6, Binance said it had received approximately $2.1 billion equivalent in stablecoins and platform tokens when it previously exited its FTX equity investment, and would liquidate the remainder because of recent revelations.

The $2.1 billion was the historical equivalent value of the exit proceeds, not $2.1 billion in platform tokens to be sold that day.

From November 6 to 8, the run accelerated. A message from SBF to staff later seen by journalists said customers had made approximately $6 billion in net withdrawals during roughly the seventy-two hours before Tuesday morning, compared with a normal daily scale of tens of millions.

That figure came from contemporary internal communication, not a post-bankruptcy account-by-account audit.

On November 8, the parties signed a nonbinding letter of intent under which Binance proposed to acquire FTX’s international business, expressly subject to due diligence.

It was never a completed acquisition contract.

On November 9, the prospective buyer withdrew, citing due diligence and reports about the handling of customer funds and investigations by U.S. agencies.

“Broke the contract within twenty-four hours” is therefore also inaccurate. The document was nonbinding from the outset.

On November 10, the Securities Commission of The Bahamas acted against the local entity, suspending its license and pursuing court liquidation proceedings.

On November 11, approximately 130 affiliated entities began reorganization proceedings. SBF resigned as CEO and was replaced by John J. Ray III.

The outflows during the night after the filing are among the easiest parts of this account to misstate.

Later review classified post-filing unauthorized third-party transfers as approximately $323 million from the international platform and approximately $90 million from the U.S. platform, totaling approximately $413 million.

Meanwhile, approximately $426 million was transferred to cold wallets controlled by the Bahamian regulator, and approximately $742 million was transferred by the debtors themselves into cold storage.

The three flows had completely different characters.

One was taken by outsiders, one was taken under regulatory control, and one was moved into the debtors’ own vault.

Public on-chain records showed large asset movements between addresses. Classifying them as unauthorized attacks, emergency cold storage, or regulatory control was work performed later by the bankruptcy team.

In the first hours, different analysts mixed them together, producing news figures of $400 million, $470 million, and $600 million.

This is a limit of on-chain transparency, of the same kind as the gap in Episode 15: a chain can show assets moving, but not who authorized the movement or why.

The claim that hackers stole $600 million on the night of bankruptcy therefore needs rewriting.

The narrower version is that approximately $413 million across the two platforms was classified as unauthorized post-filing transfers, while more than $1.1 billion moved that same night between accounts for other reasons.

The conclusion of this section matters more.

The run was the trigger, not the source of the underlying asset shortfall found by the court.

Only when customers collectively demanded external assets for their ledger balances in early November did FTX have to honor claims simultaneously against assets that had already been used elsewhere.

The jury’s later criminal verdict concerned years of prior fraud, not merely a tweet causing a bank run on a healthy institution.

The division of explanatory work is this: the tweet can explain the speed of the run.

It cannot explain why the assets were missing.

5. Three Kinds of Sentence

On November 2, 2023, the jury found SBF guilty on all seven counts.

Precisely, those were conspiracy to commit wire fraud on FTX customers and wire fraud on FTX customers; conspiracy to commit wire fraud on Alameda lenders and wire fraud on Alameda lenders; conspiracy to commit securities fraud on FTX investors; conspiracy to commit commodities fraud on FTX customers; and conspiracy to commit money laundering.

On March 28, 2024, the court imposed twenty-five years in prison, three years of supervised release, and a forfeiture order exceeding $11 billion.

The sentence did not depend on customers never recovering a penny. On the contrary, the judge expressly rejected using post-bankruptcy appreciation in investments and potentially substantial future recoveries to retroactively offset the original criminal losses.

This later became an important legal issue on appeal.

On June 12, 2026, the Second Circuit upheld the convictions and twenty-five-year sentence. One central part of its reasoning was that wire fraud was complete when customer property was obtained by fraud. An intention to return it later, or later appreciation in related assets, could not erase the original fraud.

Outcomes for the other central defendants varied greatly. They should not be reduced to greater guilt producing longer sentences.

Caroline Ellison pleaded guilty to seven counts in December 2022 and was sentenced to two years in September 2024. The judge expressly described her cooperation as extraordinarily important, while finding the scale of the crime still required imprisonment.

Gary Wang pleaded guilty to four counts and received time served, with no additional imprisonment, in November 2024. The court gave substantial weight to his early technical cooperation.

Nishad Singh pleaded guilty to six counts and likewise received time served in October 2024.

Ryan Salame pleaded guilty to two counts: conspiracy involving unlawful political contributions and deception of the Federal Election Commission, and conspiracy to operate an unlicensed money-transmitting business. In May 2024, he received ninety months, or seven and a half years.

He did not provide cooperating testimony at SBF’s trial as the other three did.

His two admitted counts concerned unlawful political contributions and unlicensed money transmission. They should be distinguished from customer-fund misappropriation and other counts admitted by others, without implying that he alone pleaded guilty to other kinds of offenses.

That also shows that responsibility in this case extended beyond the customer-funds channel.

In U.S. federal sentencing, the timing, scope, and evidentiary value of cooperation, together with assistance in recovering assets, can have enormous effects.

These sentences are not a table ranking the size of crimes. Cooperation mattered greatly, including who spoke first and who could provide otherwise unavailable evidence. Different charges, roles, and other sentencing factors cannot be erased from the comparison either.

The person who wrote the code supplied the system’s implementation, among the hardest layers for outside investigators to reconstruct independently.

The longest sentence for someone other than the principal defendant thus fell on the person who pleaded guilty to the fewest counts.

That deserves remembering: in cases like this, a sentence measures more than conduct alone.

One further common feature deserves recording. The three cooperating witnesses were senior executives who had themselves participated in the crimes and pleaded guilty.

Their evidence was therefore not the observation of uninvolved outsiders. It was participant evidence, mutually complementary across trading, engineering, and finance. The jury reached its seven verdicts after hearing defense cross-examination.

The account closest to the court materials is therefore neither “several subordinates all proved one man stole the money” nor “he did not know what the engineering team was doing.”

6. One Coin

The bankruptcy outcome did not return the coins.

It converted a quantity of coins into a dollar claim valued on November 11, 2022.

This is where the case differs most sharply from public impressions.

Assets found early in the bankruptcy were far smaller than the later final distributable estate. A crypto portfolio submitted to the court in September 2023 totaled approximately $3.4 billion, including approximately $1.16 billion in one principal asset, approximately $560 million in bitcoin, and approximately $192 million in ether.

By May 2024, the estate expected eventually to collect, convert to cash, and distribute approximately $14.5 billion to $16.3 billion.

There is therefore no contradiction in asking how an $8 billion shortfall could eventually produce $16 billion for distribution.

The two sides concern different times and different assets.

What went missing during the crime was liquid property customers could withdraw on demand. What was realized after bankruptcy consisted of equity investments, locked tokens, remaining cryptocurrency, litigation rights, and assets that later appreciated.

Realizing more dollars than the total claims does not mean sufficient customer reserves existed in November 2022.

The relationship between those statements needs to be clear because this is among the most common points used in the defendant’s defense.

How much could later be recovered and how customer funds were used at the time are questions at different dates. The appellate court’s 2026 decision refused to let later recoveries or asset appreciation cancel fraud that had already occurred.

Now to the decisive step.

On February 7, 2024, the bankruptcy court approved a digital-asset conversion table for voting and distribution, converting digital-asset claims into dollars. A creditor’s dollar claim equaled the number of tokens multiplied by the court-approved conversion price.

Bitcoin, for example, was valued at approximately $16,871.63 each; ether at approximately $1,258; another principal asset at approximately $16.25.

The valuation date was November 11, 2022.

That was precisely the period when FTX’s collapse pushed the wider crypto market toward its lows.

The debtors’ argument was that bankruptcy law had to fix different claims at a comparable legal date. Millions of accounts contained hundreds of tokens, derivatives, locked assets, and fiat currencies. Each claim could not remain indefinitely floating as a right to an asset’s market value. Nor did the estate still hold enough of the original currencies to return all customers’ coins in kind.

Opponents argued that the terms of service had emphasized customers’ continuing ownership of their digital assets. A customer holding one bitcoin therefore understood the property taken to be one coin, not an abstract claim worth $16,871.63 on that date. If the coin later multiplied in price, the estate could realize large dollar recoveries by selling appreciated assets, while original holders received principal and interest based only on the depressed valuation.

The dispute produced sharply different economic outcomes for creditors holding volatile tokens and those holding stablecoins or dollars.

The court ultimately accepted petition-date valuation.

That does not mean the court abstractly declared that all custodied cryptoassets had belonged to FTX from the beginning. More precisely, this reorganization plan approved estimation and distribution of customers’ digital-asset claims at their petition-date dollar value.

The distribution rounds can be listed individually.

The first, in February 2025, covered only the convenience class. In May 2025, distributions exceeded $5 billion, with international customers at 72% and U.S. customers at 54%. In September 2025, approximately $1.6 billion brought cumulative international distributions to 78% and U.S. distributions to 95%. In March 2026, approximately $2.2 billion brought the cumulative figures to 96% and 100%. In July 2026, approximately $900 million brought both international and U.S. customers to 105%, general unsecured and digital-asset loan claims to 103%, and the convenience class to 120%.

The denominators of 105%, 103%, and 120% are court-recognized dollar claims.

They are neither 2026 market prices nor the original number of coins.

Amounts exceeding 100% came from arrangements such as post-petition interest under the plan.

Now for the calculation on which this whole essay turns.

Assume someone held only one bitcoin at FTX and no other balance; the claim was allowed in full; they did not sell it to a third party; and they completed identity verification, tax requirements, and distribution-provider registration.

One multiplied by $16,871.63 gives an allowed claim of $16,871.63.

Because that amount is below the convenience-class threshold, it would ordinarily fall into that class. By July 2026, the publicly stated cumulative distribution rate for the class was 120%.

$16,871.63 multiplied by 120% equals $20,245.96.

Under those assumptions, the person did not receive one bitcoin back.

They received approximately $20,246.

The actual amount received could also be affected by tax withholding, jurisdiction, and distribution-provider fees. Distributions themselves were paid in dollars to designated providers. A provider might separately offer conversion of those dollars into cryptoassets, but that would be a new transaction after receipt of the dollar distribution, not an in-kind return of the original coins.

The practical distinction is concrete: if the person used the $20,246 to repurchase bitcoin, how much they could buy would depend on the price when the money arrived.

The receipt date depends on the person’s distribution batch, not the date when cumulative percentages are consulted. Convenience-class distributions began in February 2025; not everyone waited more than three years after November 11, 2022.

“FTX creditors recovered 120%” therefore needs substantial rewriting.

The narrower version supported by the material is that, by July 2026, publicly listed cumulative distribution percentages for certain allowed claim classes ranged from 103% to 120% of petition-date dollar claims. Individual receipt still depended on eligibility checks, the relevant batch, and actual payment.

That was not 103% to 120% of the original quantity of cryptoassets.

Nor did it mean full restoration relative to later market value.

The ratio exceeds one hundred directly because the plan adds interest and other amounts to allowed dollar principal. Depressed token prices at the petition date explain a different comparison: why a high percentage can still leave creditors short of the later economic value of their tokens.

Both were entirely lawful. Together they produced a highly misleading number when stripped of context.

It was subsequently widely quoted as meaning creditors had recovered everything, even an extra 20%.

7. That Day

Episode 3 discussed another bankruptcy. Comparing them brings us to exactly the same question.

Neither proceeding restored the original position. They cannot be reduced to a contrast between dollar repayment on one side and full, 100% repayment in original coins on the other.

The procedural difference was nevertheless substantial.

At FTX, original cryptoasset claims were locked into dollar claims at November 11, 2022 prices, then distributed according to dollar priorities with interest.

At Mt. Gox, claims also passed through Japanese civil-rehabilitation formulas and valuation rules. They were not repaid in full in original coins. But eligible cryptocurrency rehabilitation claims could ultimately receive part of their repayment in bitcoin and bitcoin cash. From July 2024, the trustee actually transferred those coins to designated exchanges for distribution to creditors.

The two proceedings therefore answered differently the question of who benefits from rising token prices during bankruptcy.

The FTX customer principally received a fixed dollar claim plus plan interest.

Some Mt. Gox creditors ultimately received part of their repayment in kind. That portion therefore retained exposure to the token price after delivery.

The institutional background in Episode 3 provides the exact comparison.

During that case’s bankruptcy-liquidation phase, claims were also to be converted into yen at prices at commencement. Had liquidation continued on that basis, the subsequent surge in token prices might have left the estate holding coins worth much more than all customer claims valued at the earlier date, with the excess going to shareholders.

That was a principal reason creditors pushed for civil rehabilitation instead.

Two countries, two legal systems, the same question: a person deposited coins, but bankruptcy handles claims. Who owns the subsequent price changes?

This comparison concerns the form of repayment assets and the allocation of price risk.

It does not say which proceeding achieved a higher recovery rate.

The point is only that both had to answer the same question, answered it differently, and thereby determined who bore billions of dollars in price risk.

Other sides of the money flows can also be observed directly from real counterparties.

One lender said in November 2022 that approximately $175 million was locked at FTX. A listed digital-asset company disclosed approximately $76.8 million in exposure, including approximately $47.5 million already in the withdrawal process. Being in that process did not mean on-chain settlement had occurred. Another disclosed $30.3 million, some in withdrawals submitted before the suspension but still pending.

A large exchange said it had approximately $15 million in deposits at FTX and no platform-token or lending exposure.

Another disclosure best illustrates the measurement problem: approximately $1 billion had been transferred to FTX cumulatively over the preceding year, but most had been recovered, leaving exposure below $10 million at collapse.

Writing that the company lost $1 billion at FTX would be a textbook measurement error.

One number was cumulative annual transfers. The other was exposure at the moment of collapse.

These figures establish something concrete: insufficient customer assets at the exchange did not remain a matter of prices on a screen. The exchange transmitted its credit risk to identifiable institutions.

One of them entered reorganization itself that same month. Attributing its bankruptcy entirely to FTX would also be too narrow: it had already been hit by another crisis and had received an FTX rescue.

Political contributions involve three more noninterchangeable measures.

SBF’s own 2022-cycle donations visible in Federal Election Commission records totaled approximately $40 million, mainly to Democratic candidates and related organizations.

Prosecutors used a broader measure, saying he and his coconspirators made more than $100 million in political contributions using customer funds. That included arrangements carried out in other people’s names.

The bankruptcy team separately estimated approximately $93 million in political contributions by the group and its personnel before bankruptcy.

The three figures have different subjects, sources, and scopes.

The partisan flows were not one-way either. Contemporary public records showed Salame contributing more than $24 million to Republican candidates and related political organizations in the 2022 cycle, and Singh approximately $9.7 million to the Democratic side.

Sponsorships produced a different misreading.

The arena naming-rights agreement was for nineteen years and $135 million. The esports naming-rights agreement was for ten years and $210 million.

Those were nominal commitments over the entire contract terms, not cash already paid. FTX failed in the second year.

Adding the face values of long-term contracts and calling the total customer money already spent on advertising makes two errors at once: treating commitments as payments, and assuming courts traced every sponsorship dollar to customer funds.

The criminal case proved that customer money funded group expenses, investments, and political contributions, among other uses. It did not provide dollar-by-dollar tracing for every sports-marketing contract.

Data on off-chain effects are comparatively poor.

The bankruptcy case allows precise observation of institutional claims, frozen accounts, canceled sponsorships, and wiped-out equity investments. There is no credible primary dataset measuring how much the incident reduced employment in a particular region or increased consumers’ living costs.

That absence of quantified social evidence should itself be recorded, not replaced by token-price declines or social-media attention.

The series repeatedly encounters this same problem: what can be measured precisely and what we most want to know are often not the same thing.

8. Two Frameworks

Two frameworks for understanding the case are supported by the material. They answer different questions.

The framework of conventional misappropriation and fraud has the most direct legal evidence.

SBF was not ultimately convicted of operating a bad blockchain protocol. His convictions concerned wire fraud, conspiracy to commit wire fraud, conspiracies involving securities and commodities fraud, and money-laundering conspiracy. Customers entrusted property to a service provider. Through false statements and secret transfers, managers used that property for an affiliated business, loan repayments, investments, and other expenses.

Replace cryptoassets with dollar-denominated securities accounts and the same structure could fall under conventional fraud law.

The framework of the crypto industry’s institutional structure explains something else: why this fraud could grow so large, and why ordinary controls did not reveal the asset shortfall for so long as they might at a bank or broker.

FTX placed an exchange, an affiliated market maker, an exchange-issued token, collateralized financing, global offshore entities, and highly leveraged derivatives in one network under common control. The same affiliate was simultaneously a related company, a major trading customer, a market maker, and a borrower with exceptional system permissions. The token was simultaneously an exchange-issued asset, a major asset of the affiliate, and collateral.

These mechanisms are not necessary components of traditional bank fraud. They substantially amplified the risks of related-party dealings and self-referential collateral.

Using a self-issued asset to support related-party borrowing binds together the risks of issuer, collateral, and borrower. Traditional finance has various constraints on related-party transactions, capital, and concentration. The question here is why the group’s consolidated supervision, disclosures, and actual controls did not connect those risks.

The two statements are therefore best given separate work.

“Conventional misappropriation” describes the criminal-law basis and ownership of funds.

“Crypto-industry problem” describes why market structure, custody arrangements, related-party dealings, exchange tokens, and regulatory boundaries allowed the crime to remain concealed for so long.

They are not mutually exclusive propositions.

Reducing the case to either loses important evidence. Calling it only conventional fraud does not explain why it grew so large undetected. Calling it only an industry problem does not explain what the courts actually found.

On auditing, primary evidence now goes beyond retrospective commentary. In September 2024, the SEC settled with an audit firm, explicitly describing one enforcement action as concerning negligence in FTX audits. Together with a separate auditor-independence case, the required payment was approximately $1.95 million.

The claim that FTX had audit opinions and therefore external auditors had proved its customer reserves safe is particularly untenable after that regulatory investigation.

Auditing financial statements and proving reserves and segregation customer by customer, asset by asset, were never the same control objective.

On regulation, “there was no regulation at all” is also inaccurate. The local entity held regulated digital-asset status, the group included licensed U.S. entities, and the regulator suspended the local license and pursued liquidation on November 10, 2022.

The narrower criticism supported by the material is that these licenses did not create unified, look-through supervision of the group’s pooled funds, related-party dealings, customer-asset segregation, and privileged account.

The media side is hardest to assess through a single administrative decision. What can be established is that the story that changed market judgment relied on a leaked private balance sheet, not one automatically produced by an existing public disclosure system.

A limited inference follows: while this remained a private multinational company, outside journalists lacked periodically published reserve, related-party, and segment-risk data of the kind available for listed banks.

That explains an information constraint. The heavy reliance of much early reporting on founders, investors, and company-supplied information is another issue.

The first has a structural aspect: there was no full set of periodic, publicly checkable reserve and related-party data comparable to that of a listed financial institution. That does not mean private companies have no disclosure duties at all. The second concerns judgment: without independent data, reporting should be more cautious.

Nondisclosure does not turn all earlier favorable reporting into proof of assets.

Return now to the three groups in the first section.

Those benefiting during the crime: the affiliate could continue trading, repay outside loans, buy investments, and meet group expenses. Some external lenders and investment recipients received cash early.

Those bearing the risk at collapse: customers who could not withdraw in time, and the institutions that left credit exposure on the platform.

Those entitled to asset recoveries after bankruptcy: creditors receiving distributions under a dollar-denominated priority system, funded by appreciation in investments originally belonging to affiliates, remaining tokens, litigation recoveries, and seized assets.

These were not the same people.

That fact comes closer to the substance of the case than any loss figure. Recovery rates, payout ratios, and forfeiture amounts measure money. Separating these three groups measures how money fell to different people at different times.

The dividing lines are two dates.

The first is the period during which the crimes occurred. It determines who should be responsible.

The second is November 11, 2022. It determines who bears the price.

The remainder lies in the latter date.

It is not something overlooked. As a unit of account, one coin remains one coin, and bitcoin can be measured to the satoshi. That does not mean the platform continuously retained every customer’s original coins in full.

It is a choice that had to be made: the day on which time would be pinned down.

This bankruptcy proceeding used petition-date dollar valuations to put claims across many accounts, tokens, and derivatives on a common basis. Whether another case returns assets in kind, and how it values and distributes claims, must be examined separately.

This is not a technical limitation. It is a requirement of the procedure itself: all claims must be comparable before their priority and proportions can be determined.

This is a legal choice about classification and distribution, governed by applicable rules and carrying consequences that can be debated. Not every bankruptcy must take the identical approach.

Its difference from the choice in Episode 3 is not a matter of strictness. It is whether holders retain a route to repayment in kind.

That choice determined who received the gains and losses in those assets over the next four years.

Over those years, the assets rose considerably.

Post-petition appreciation first appears on the estate’s side. Distributions to creditors under the plan are based on dollar claims fixed at the petition date, plus the applicable interest and other amounts—not an automatic restoration of their original token-price exposure.

Throughout those years, the chain kept producing blocks. Those coins moved normally at other people’s addresses, were recorded normally, and rose and fell normally.

In the bankruptcy ledger, they stopped on November 11, 2022, and never moved again.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
