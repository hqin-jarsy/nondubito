1. An Inventory Constraint

Constant product does not automatically discover the market price.

It is an inventory constraint.

In the model before fees, the quantities of the two assets in the pool multiply to a constant. A trader adds some of asset A and may withdraw only enough B to preserve that product; the fee-charging implementation requires an adjustment.

The curve's slope gives the marginal price. Immediately before a trade, the instantaneous price is approximately the ratio of the reserves.

A finite trade does not execute at that single price. It moves some distance along the curve.

The larger the trade relative to pool depth, the farther its average execution price departs from the pre-trade marginal price.

This is basic price impact. Interfaces commonly present fees, external market movements, and execution differences caused by front-running together as some form of slippage. Practical slippage should therefore not be equated completely with pure curve-generated price impact.

Consider a calculation, not a historical pool.

Suppose a pool holds 100 ETH and 200,000 USDC, with a pre-trade marginal price of 2,000.

Someone inputs 10 ETH. Uniswap v2's formula yields approximately 18,132.22 USDC.

The average execution price is about 1,813.22.

The pre-trade marginal price was 2,000; the actual exchange achieved 1,813.22.

That roughly 9% difference is not all a fee: the fee is only 0.30%, while most of the difference is price impact from moving along the curve. The pool’s inventory changes, but the whole gap is not LP net profit; external prices, subsequent arbitrage, and inventory valuation still matter.

The nominal fee on those 10 ETH is 0.03 ETH. It is included in the input, not paid separately.

Another mechanism is easily misstated. The contract does not immediately transfer that 0.30% to LP wallets. The full input enters the pool, with the fee retained in reserves, increasing the product. LPs own proportional pool shares, so fees appear as claims on larger reserves and are withdrawn with principal when liquidity is removed.

A swap's actual contract sequence is more specific than calling a formula.

The router calculates route outputs from pool reserves and checks the trader's minimum output and deadline, then sends input tokens directly to the first pool. The pool checks that output is nonzero and below reserves, then optimistically transfers output tokens. Nonempty attached data can trigger a recipient callback, the basis of flash swaps. The pool rereads actual balances, infers actual inputs from their difference from old reserves, adjusts for fees, and checks that the adjusted product is no smaller than before. Only then does it update reserves and emit events; otherwise the entire transaction reverts.

Why did this fit early Ethereum better than a fully on-chain order book?

A full on-chain limit order book must continually write orders, cancellations, amendments, and matches to the chain. Every change is a state transition requiring gas, block inclusion, and network-wide consensus ordering.

AMMs compress the market state needing maintenance into a few pool reserves and LP shares. Traders need not wait for a counterparty to post a precisely matching order.

It is an exceptionally clean compression. Market makers, order books, matching engines, and clearinghouses become a function and two numbers.

Market-making costs did not disappear.

They became LP inventory risk, losses to arbitrage, and traders' price impact along the curve.

The remainder is already visible. It is not missing money: every cent on the curve can be recalculated transaction by transaction.

The remainder consists of costs moved to new positions where no field displays them.

That sentence grounds the next seven sections.

2. Five Point Seven One Nine

The strict benchmark for impermanent loss is not whether an LP lost principal.

It is the difference between the LP position's market value and the value of simply holding the same original assets, given the same initial portfolio and final external price.

An LP can therefore make a dollar profit and still suffer impermanent loss: both assets rose, but holding them unchanged would have gained more.

Conversely, fees can exceed impermanent loss and produce positive net returns relative to holding.

Including fees within impermanent loss confuses distinct economic quantities. Calculating them separately is more rigorous.

For a fee-free 50/50 constant-product pool, let r be the final external relative price divided by its initial value. After rebalancing to that price, LP value divided by unchanged-holdings value equals 2√r/(1+r).

The figures are as follows. Every percentage below excludes fees, gas, token rewards, and taxes.

A 25% price fall produces a 1.026% loss; a 25% rise, 0.619%.

A 50% fall produces 5.719%; a 50% rise, 2.020%.

A 100% rise, a doubling, produces 5.719%.

A 200% rise produces 13.397%; a 300% rise, 20.000%; a 900% rise, a tenfold price, 42.504%.

The same 5.719% for a 50% fall and 100% rise is no formula error.

Remember this: the loss measures price ratios, while everyday intuition perceives percentage changes. Two measurements of the same thing can create opposite impressions.

Impermanent loss is symmetric in a price ratio and its reciprocal; everyday percentage rises and falls are not.

“Impermanent” also needs qualification.

If price can return to the original ratio, the shortfall against holding can shrink. Once the LP exits, it is realized.

Even before exit, it is already an opportunity cost in mark-to-market terms, not a loss that has yet to occur.

Empirical findings do not support a universal claim that LPs always profit or always lose.

A 2021 study reconstructed LP positions in 17 pools after v3 launched, aggregating fees and impermanent losses relative to holding. It found $199.3 million in fees, estimated losses of $260.1 million, and a net shortfall of $60.8 million. Those 17 pools were selected by size, asset composition, and data availability, not the entire market, and covered early v3.

Another study reconstructed actual v3 positions, ranges, and management. It concluded that stable or low-volatility pools could deliver simple but modest returns, while substantial returns generally involved narrower ranges, greater risk, and active management.

A third studied multiple pools in 2022–2023, calculating fees from on-chain swaps and simulating arbitrage losses with second-by-second external exchange prices. Fees failed to cover simulated arbitrage losses in several v3 pools, while measured v2 pools more often covered them; in the second year's sample, fees were approximately three times losses.

The third study especially highlights a benchmark issue.

Impermanent loss and loss-versus-rebalancing are not the same measure.

The former benchmarks unchanged holdings. The latter benchmarks a continuously, frictionlessly rebalanced strategy at external prices, principally capturing losses when arbitrageurs trade against stale AMM quotes.

Presenting the latter kind of paper as empirical measurement of impermanent loss changes its benchmark.

Actual LP net returns therefore require at least this decomposition: underlying asset appreciation or depreciation, plus fees and token incentives, minus loss against the chosen benchmark, gas, and rebalancing costs.

An interface's annualized fee rate cannot simply be called LP profit.

One detail deserves attention: the right side has six terms, while the interface usually displays only one, the only term certain to be positive.

This is especially true of concentrated liquidity.

v3 does not remove the curve. It treats each active range as a constant-product pool with virtual reserves. The same nominal capital can concentrate within a narrow price band, greatly increasing depth near the current price.

Once price leaves that range, the position no longer participates in trades or earns pool fees, and becomes entirely one asset.

It therefore increases depth supplied by given capital within a price range, not LP returns unconditionally.

Narrower concentration earns more fees per unit of principal while price remains in range, but also makes departure from the range easier.

3. Five Hundred and Twenty-Six Thousand

Three dates in liquidity mining must be separated.

On February 26, 2020, Compound Labs published COMP's governance design and initial allocation. A May 27 announcement expressly expanded distribution to protocol users. Academic literature usually dates launch to June 15, while Compound Labs' contemporary article dates the milestone June 16 by publication time.

These should not be forced into one uniquely correct date. Scheduled dates, Ethereum execution blocks, and publishers' time zones readily produce a one-day difference.

COMP's total supply was 10,000,000. The original allocation was 2,396,307 to Compound Labs shareholders, 23.96307%; 2,226,037 to founders and team, 22.26037%; 372,707 to future team members, 3.72707%; 4,229,949 to protocol users, 42.29949%; and 775,000 for other community governance purposes, 7.75%.

A technical distinction is often misstated.

The original scheme did not mint fresh COMP every block with unlimited inflation.

Total supply was predetermined. A reserve pool held 4,229,949 tokens for release into circulation by block.

Initially, 0.50 COMP entered the protocol per Ethereum block. Assuming roughly 15-second blocks then, this meant approximately 2,880 per day.

That was a block-time approximation, not a guaranteed calendar-day quantity.

The initial allocation had two layers. First, the 0.50 COMP was divided among markets according to interest generated. Second, each market split rewards equally between suppliers and borrowers, allocated proportionately to their balances.

A strong feedback soon emerged.

High-borrow-rate markets received more COMP, while earning COMP itself increased willingness to borrow.

Governance changed the rules. Academic reconstruction dates execution to July 2, 2020, replacing accrued-interest weighting with dollar borrowing demand across markets.

This was not abstract.

Contemporary on-chain analysis recorded BAT outstanding borrowing rising from approximately 209,000 to about 1.1 billion BAT between June 14 and June 30, 2020, while deposited BAT rose from roughly 7.4 million to 1.3 billion.

Using those rounded figures, outstanding borrowing ended at roughly 5,263 times its starting level—an increase of about 526,000%. This measures BAT units, not net inflows of dollar principal.

These were BAT quantities, not dollar TVL, and did not represent 1.1 billion BAT of newly created real-economy credit demand. Users could deposit, borrow, and redeposit the same type of asset to expand reward-eligible balances.

What matters here is the order of magnitude, not a decimal place. Expansion on this scale is a warning: a balance that can be repeatedly recycled cannot simply be read as an equal amount of outside capital or real credit demand.

The path was later reconstructed transaction by transaction.

A study of Compound state from May 2019 to September 2020 identified 2,141 accounts using recursive leverage, involving more than $600 million in supply, roughly half the sample's supplied amount.

The largest supplier recorded cumulative supply of $342 million and borrowing of $247 million. Tracing the loops, the authors estimated original capital of approximately $55 million.

About 16% of reported supply.

$342 million on the books; $55 million in principal.

That answers what the mechanism was doing economically.

It did not create ordinary lending income from nothing. It added a subsidy in preallocated governance assets to existing interest cash flows.

For the same period and principal basis, a depositor’s return is approximately interest divided by principal, plus the period’s market value of allocated COMP divided by eligible capital. Annualization must use a consistent period and method for both components.

A borrower’s net cost rate is approximately the borrowing rate for a given period minus the market value of borrower rewards over that same period divided by outstanding debt. The periods must match before the two can be compared.

If the second term exceeded the first, borrowing had a negative net rate: borrowers paid interest but received COMP worth more.

The interface's annualized return therefore needs at least two components.

One comes from trading fees, borrowing interest, and liquidation fees actually paid by other users.

The other comes from reserved governance tokens entering circulation.

For COMP especially, the correct phrase is release into circulation, not an increase in total supply.

Recipients gained COMP. On the other side, no current borrower paid an equivalent additional cash amount; the reserve pool shrank while added circulating supply diluted existing holders' scarcity within circulation.

Market price determined the subsidy's dollar value. The same 0.50 COMP per block could therefore generate very different annualized dollar returns.

A counterfinding must also be retained.

The shorter study closely connected June's spike with COMP's launch. A longer daily study from May 2019 to June 2021 converted net minting into dollars at daily prices and found that including relevant variables added little explanatory power for net deposit inflows.

“COMP caused a visible short-term jump in activity” and “COMP was the main statistical explanation for longer-term net capital inflows” are different propositions.

The studies do not truly contradict each other.

4. Governance Rights or Income Rights?

Protocol fees, governance-token value, and token holders' rights to protocol income are three things most in need of separation in that period's narrative.

For Uniswap v2, the actual 2020 swap fee was 0.30%, entirely retained in LP reserves while the protocol-fee switch was off.

v2 did provide for a protocol fee: governance could activate capture of approximately one-sixth of trading fees, commonly described economically as 0.05% to the protocol and 0.25% to LPs.

The crucial 2020 fact was that an existing switch did not mean holders were receiving money.

UNI's launch announcement conferred governance, treasury control, and control over that switch, which also had a 180-day timelock.

Subsequent history further distinguishes protocol fees from cash dividends.

Governance material for a 2022 switch experiment expressly said it did not determine eventual use of fee revenue or establish expectations of holder payments.

Following architectural changes approved in late 2025, official 2026 documentation showed protocol fees active on v2 and some v3 pools, directing revenue to a burn mechanism.

Burning indirectly affects remaining tokens' supply. It is not a contractual dividend payable to holder accounts in dollars or other assets.

For Compound v2, cash flows begin with borrowers: debt grows with the borrow index, supplier claims' exchange rates rise with distributable interest, and each market's reserve factor retains some interest in protocol reserves.

Research on the contract economics expressly found no explicit plan in its sample period automatically distributing these reserves to COMP holders.

COMP therefore cannot be described as a right to Compound profit dividends.

More narrowly, COMP controls governance capable of changing protocol parameters. Retained interest reserves and token holders' cash flows belong to different accounts.

SushiSwap supplies a counterexample, so the reverse claim that governance tokens never generate income is also wrong.

Forked from v2, its classic staking model left approximately 0.25% of the standard 0.30% with LPs and converted approximately 0.05% into SUSHI for the staking pool, increasing the staking receipt's SUSHI exchange rate as income accumulated.

Actively staking holders thus had an economic claim mechanism funded by trading fees.

Ordinary holding was insufficient. Staking was required.

Fee uses later changed through governance, so calling 0.05% permanent is also wrong.

Governance-token income rights must therefore be answered protocol by protocol, period by period, and operational state by operational state.

Allocations belong in the same capitalization table.

UNI's initial September 16, 2020, supply was one billion: 60.000% community, 21.266% team and future employees, 18.044% investors, and 0.690% advisers. The announcement described four-year vesting for the last three categories.

Within the community's 60%, 15% of total supply was immediately claimable by historical participants and 43% remained in the treasury.

The historical airdrop counted addresses, not persons: 49,192 historical LP addresses shared 49,166,400 UNI; 251,534 historical user addresses shared 100,613,600 equally, or 400 per eligible address.

One person controlling many addresses, or one contract representing many people, makes persons differ from addresses.

For COMP, shareholders, founders/team, and future team members together received about half, against 42.30% for users.

Evidence discipline matters: the original announcement expressly specified four-year vesting only for founders and team. Without another primary source, other categories cannot automatically become four-year lockups.

This is the same kind of work as the previous essay's 25 projects without identifiable hard-coded restrictions.

Finally, consider something in the code.

SushiSwap's initial distribution contract expressly contained an owner, a developer address, and a highly privileged migration function. Its source comments even stated that the owner held tremendous power, with ownership to be transferred to governance later.

Deployment as a smart contract is not synonymous with absence of administrative intermediaries from day one.

The distance between those sentences is this section's most concrete point. The first is verifiable: code is on-chain for anyone to read. The second concerns power: who holds the private key, when they intend to surrender it, and what they do beforehand.

Who actually controls the key, and why control is transferred at a particular moment, may not be independently established on-chain. A transfer of authority and some preceding actions can leave on-chain records; the controller’s identity and motives still require other evidence.

5. Four Parties

Viewed through money flows rather than promotion, a swap involves at least four groups of economic actors.

The trader supplies the input asset and bears three separable costs: explicit fees, curve-generated price impact, and price/ordering losses between signing and execution. The sending address also pays gas.

Minimum output specifies only the lowest acceptable receipt, not a guarantee of execution at the quote seen on submission.

The LP receives fees, not risk-free rent.

Trading changes inventory. When external prices move, arbitrageurs push the pool's marginal price toward them, mechanically making the LP sell the relatively appreciating asset and buy the relatively depreciating one.

Against unchanged holdings this is impermanent loss; against continuous frictionless rebalancing it is loss to arbitrage.

The same pool earns fees and bears adverse selection, but gains and losses come from different trade flows.

High cumulative fees alone therefore do not prove net LP profit.

The arbitrageur sees ETH worth 2,100 externally while the pool still quotes about 2,000, and buys cheap pool ETH until no positive return remains after gas, fees, and frictions.

Economically, arbitrageurs' gross profit mainly corresponds to LP losses relative to timely repricing.

Competing arbitrageurs pass part of it to ordering and block-production participants through priority fees and bundle payments.

It is therefore inaccurate to say all arbitrage profits come from ordinary traders.

Sandwiching is different. A searcher observing a trade that will significantly move pool price can buy before it and sell afterward, converting the victim's allowed slippage into profit and worsening that trader's execution.

The 2020 paper did not simply query a field for total MEV income. It identified measurable arbitrage bots, observed their fee bidding for ordering priority, and analyzed the gas paid.

It first established that ordering power has measurable economic value. It observed only one identifiable strategy class, not the whole universe.

Methodological differences are themselves part of the answer to MEV's scale.

A widely cited on-chain reconstruction identified sandwiches, liquidations, and arbitrage over approximately 32 months of Ethereum data, estimating about $540.54 million in identifiable extracted value.

That is not total user loss. It is dollar-valued returns from three opportunity classes its algorithm could classify.

A 2024 remeasurement noted false positives and omissions in existing heuristics, proposed profitability-based identification, and estimated approximately $675 million of extracted MEV over the historical period around the September 2022 consensus transition.

These numbers should not be treated as alternatives to choose between. Time windows, strategies covered, private-transaction visibility, detection rules, and deductions for gas and bribes all differ.

Concentrated liquidity provides another counterexample.

A v3 just-in-time-liquidity study identified 36,671 events from June 2021 to January 2023. Simulating the same swaps without them, the authors estimated average trader price improvement of about 0.139%.

This strategy earned more by placing temporary capital before a large trade, collecting fees, and immediately withdrawing.

The losing side was therefore more likely diluted passive LPs than the trader.

MEV is not a single number for theft from users.

The abbreviation contains at least three different things: arbitrage bringing external prices into the pool, facing LPs; sandwiches surrounding others' trades, facing those traders; and temporary capital capturing fees, facing existing passive LPs.

The affected groups differ. Adding them into one number hides that fact.

This helps explain the persistent confusion in MEV debates. One person calls it theft from ordinary people; another calls it a necessary cost of market efficiency. They may be discussing different categories.

Across these four groups, explicit payments and value transfers must be separated. Traders pay fees and gas and may lose value to searchers through worse execution; arbitrage profits can also correspond to LP inventory losses. Gas allocation depends on the protocol at the time: under Ethereum’s EIP-1559, the base fee is burned, while priority fees and some additional payments flow to the block-production and ordering side.

LPs additionally bear inventory risk, benchmark-dependent losses, contract risk, and concentrated-liquidity range-management costs.

Arbitrage both aligns pool prices with external markets and imposes adverse-selection costs on LPs, because pools cannot cancel quotes instantly when external prices arrive as professional market makers can.

Both are true.

6. Three Dollars

Address statistics are easiest to obtain. Individuals, companies, income groups, and geographic distributions are hardest.

They cannot substitute for one another.

Uniswap Labs' September 16, 2020, announcement reported, less than two years after launch, over $20 billion in cumulative nominal swap volume, more than 250,000 unique addresses, and 8,484 distinct assets. More than 49,000 LP addresses had supplied over $1 billion in liquidity and earned approximately $56 million in fees.

These were cumulative developer-reported statistics. In particular, 250,000 addresses did not mean 250,000 individuals.

Compound has stricter independent address-level research. A study of May 2019–September 2020 records found the largest supplier accounted for 27.4% of cumulative supplied funds and the top ten 49%; the largest borrower accounted for 37.1% and the top ten 59.9%.

A second study extending the window through June 2021 found something more cautionary.

Its data contained 356,800 depositing addresses.

Approximately 195,200 had made exactly one stablecoin deposit of no more than $3.

More strikingly, around 183,000 had deposited exactly $3.

183,000 addresses, exactly $3 each.

These addresses cannot simply be counted as an equal number of independent users. The repeated tiny deposits suggest a shared strategy. The author’s best conjecture was anticipation of a possible airdrop—not verification of the people or motives behind every address.

The seventh essay said that an address is not a person. Here the point goes further: one person can organize participation through many addresses, while counting them does not itself reveal why.

The authors classified them as micro-addresses and excluded them from the main address analysis.

After exclusion, 161,569 addresses remained. The paper’s small-wallet category comprised 99.7% of addresses but 16% of dollar deposits; its 256 large-wallet addresses accounted for 30.5%. That category excludes separately classified smart contracts. Ranked across all categories, the top 100 addresses accounted for about 75% of deposits and could include contracts pooling many people’s funds—not simply 100 wealthy individuals.

99.7% of addresses, 16% of the money.

Together, these figures do not show deception. They show that an inclusion narrative by address count and a concentration narrative by amount can both be true of the same data.

Borrowing was more concentrated. The same study identified about 22,289 unique borrowing addresses; the top 100 accounted for roughly 78% of loans. Most loans were stablecoin-denominated, with an average term of about 31 days.

The authors also explicitly could not identify owners of some large smart contracts, which might represent other protocols, private investors, or institutions.

Equating one contract address with one wealthy individual is likewise unsupported.

Research can observe some fund uses, not borrowers' real-world identities.

Rapidly redepositing borrowed tokens into the same or another protocol indicates on-chain leveraged farming. The narrower supported conclusion is that substantial sample activity displayed leverage, arbitrage, short-selling, or farming characteristics within the chain.

That does not establish that no one met genuine liquidity needs. On-chain data generally cannot observe ultimate use at centralized exchanges, OTC venues, consumers, or businesses.

For LPs, behavioral classification across 250 v3 pools found that the professional category's share increased with pool size and volume. In sample pools trading over $10 million daily, almost all liquidity and most fees came from classified professional LPs; pools under $100,000 daily more commonly had retail LPs.

These identities were inferred from on-chain behavior, not verified professions. The research measured market structure, not population.

Single-swap size distributions present a clear evidence gap.

The original 2020 material reviewed here richly covered cumulative volume, address counts, and LP counts, but supplied no full-market, publicly reproducible transaction-size distribution with explicit dollar-conversion timing and correspondence to human identities.

Later papers reconstruct transactions for swap costs, MEV, or selected pools. They do not resolve which real-world groups own the addresses.

More importantly, off-chain empirical evidence on lower financial-service costs or improved access for unbanked populations is scarce.

Studies observing wallets, contracts, loan maturities, asset destinations, and recursive lending cannot reliably map most addresses to specific people, incomes, countries, business financing costs, or substitution for banks.

Permissionless access for anyone is a protocol design claim, not a randomized experiment or regional financial-inclusion measurement.

Existing evidence strongly supports technical accessibility: addresses with compatible wallets and on-chain assets can invoke contracts without conventional exchange matching accounts.

It is plainly insufficient for the stronger socioeconomic claim that on-chain finance in a particular year demonstrably reduced household or small-business borrowing costs in a particular region.

That claim must also include crypto acquisition, gas, overcollateralization, stablecoin and exchange access, and wallet-security costs.

For the 2020 wave, this research found no off-chain causal measurement rigorously quantifying additional employment, basis-point reductions in small-business financing costs, or newly included regional populations.

The absence should remain visible, not be replaced by wallet growth rates.

7. Three Sets of Accounts

Several popular claims need rewriting. How to rewrite them is this section's subject.

DeFi eliminated intermediaries.

Rewrite: some protocols converted functions previously performed by exchange matching engines, brokerage accounts, or lenders' internal ledgers into public-blockchain smart-contract rules. They did not eliminate all intermediation; they relocated it.

AMMs genuinely need no conventional centralized order-book operator to match each trade, and lending protocols need no loan officer to approve each collateralized loan.

Trading may nevertheless depend on wallets and frontends, validators and block builders, node services, oracles, stablecoin issuers, wrapped-asset custodians, governance holders and delegates, LPs, arbitrageurs/searchers, and liquidation bots.

The code comment discussed earlier especially demonstrates that smart-contract deployment does not imply absence of administrative power.

Composability can also create intermediary dependencies absent from conventional narratives.

A study of 23 labeled protocols and 10,663,881 related accounts found trading and lending protocols highly central in the protocol-call network, with many calls nesting other foundational modules.

Someone apparently using one protocol may rely on multiple contracts and asset layers underneath.

TVL is capital invested.

Rewrite: it is a total-value-locked indicator produced by valuing selected protocol addresses and balances in dollars at a particular time under a particular method. Prices affect it, and composability, wrapping, and borrow-and-redeposit loops can count the same underlying economic value repeatedly.

A rise from $1 billion to $2 billion can therefore follow three very different paths: roughly $1 billion in genuine net inflows; unchanged tokens doubling in price; or collateral generating receipts or borrowed assets redeposited elsewhere, creating layered accounting.

The earlier example of $342 million on the books from approximately $55 million in original capital explains why recorded supply and external net principal are not interchangeable.

A 2025 working paper systematically inspected 939 protocols' methods: 10.5% used external-server data; it found 68 methods beyond standard balance queries and 240 identical queries repeated across protocols. In a 400-protocol case study, only approximately 46.5% reproduced public figures exactly or closely.

This measures methodological verifiability, not proof that the other 53.5% committed fraud.

Other researchers proposed tracking only ultimately redeemable underlying economic value. Their peak-period reconstruction estimated a gap of approximately $139.87 billion between TVL and redeemable value on December 2, 2021, with a ratio near two.

That is not a universal divide-by-two correction. It is a historical result for that dataset, derivative-token tracing method, and date.

High liquidity-mining returns are income generated by the protocol.

Rewrite: farming returns usually combine income paid by other users, such as trading fees and lending spreads, with subsidies from governance tokens entering circulation. Their funding sources, persistence conditions, and cost bearers differ.

UNI's specific 2020 program allocated 5 million tokens to each of four pools from September 18 to November 17, 20 million total, or approximately 83,333 per pool daily under the announcement's block-time assumption.

That LP return was a treasury token allocation, not an extra 20 million tokens paid by traders during those two months.

So liquidity mining is merely unsustainable subsidies buying growth.

The evidence supports two interpretations; the mechanism alone cannot decide between them.

One is bootstrapping: liquidity has network effects in young markets. Protocols distribute governance assets to suppliers, borrowers, or LPs, compensating early capital for contract risk, price risk, and opportunity cost through treasury assets and token supply. As depth, users, and integrations grow, genuine fee income can replace some subsidies.

The other is subsidy-induced activity: whenever reward yields exceed borrowing costs, LP risk, and gas, rational capital can repeatedly deposit, borrow, and redeposit the same money, inflating nominal activity, loans, and TVL. When rewards or token prices fall, that activity may lack independent economic demand.

The earlier 2,141 accounts and over $600 million in loops, and BAT's extreme expansion within weeks, directly evidence the second mechanism on-chain.

Both can be true. Subsidies may start genuine liquidity networks and create circular transactions profitable only while subsidies last.

Both can even occur with the same funds: someone genuinely needs liquidity while looping to enlarge the reward base. On-chain, it is the same transfer sequence.

To distinguish them empirically, peaks are insufficient. One must separately examine net deposit retention after rewards cease or fall, unincentivized volume, fee and interest income, user reuse, net capital after removing recursive leverage, and persistent fee income per subsidy unit.

8. The Fields Missing from the Interface

Return to section one's sentence.

The compression did succeed, more thoroughly than many critics acknowledge. It has operated for more than five years, without someone opening a counter each day for routine swaps. That does not certify every implementation as error-free.

Costs did not disappear. They moved.

Their new positions share one feature: they do not appear on the interface.

The interface shows a 0.30% fee.

That fee alone does not display the full cost. Price impact, LP impermanent loss, sandwiching, and gas belong to different accounts. Some interfaces separately show price impact and estimated gas; even then, an annualized fee figure is not profit.

This is not entirely deliberate concealment.

Some costs can be estimated before confirmation; others become known only at execution or when a position is closed. An estimate and a realized result are not the same thing.

Price impact depends on trade size and pool depth, both calculable at that moment, and good interfaces do calculate it.

Impermanent loss depends on the exit price, which lies in the future.

Sandwiching depends on whether someone sees you in the same block.

Gas depends on how many people are competing at that moment.

These four costs have different timing. Price impact and gas can be estimated beforehand but may change before inclusion; sandwiching and ordering depend on execution, while impermanent loss also depends on subsequent prices and the time of exit.

An interface must present them all in the same second or it becomes unusable.

It can display only the fields it can calculate.

That is the remainder's shape in this history.

It is not hidden by someone. An interface that can show only presently calculable quantities leaves it outside that domain.

The point is broader than it appears. Earlier remainders lay outside a standard’s jurisdiction, outside the code, or outside anyone’s assigned measurement. Here is a fourth kind: while the quote is on screen, some events determining the final outcome have not happened. When that outcome can be settled, the reader has already left that moment’s quote screen.

Finally, three sets of accounts.

The first is trading: traders' fees and execution losses, LP fees and inventory risk, and extraction by arbitrage and ordering.

The second is incentives: who receives newly circulating governance assets, whose relative share is diluted, and how much is not protocol operating income at all.

The third is capital stocks: original net principal, recorded balances after loops, and TVL at market prices.

Each has its own units.

Trading accounts use individual fees and losses. Incentive accounts use tokens and their daily price. Capital-stock accounts use principal, calculable only after unwinding the loops.

Mix them into one number and one obtains a narrative capable of supporting any conclusion.

Separate them, and less is certain, but each remaining claim stands.

The curve’s mathematical constraint can itself be checked clearly.

That point has appeared repeatedly in this series, especially cleanly here.

Anyone can recalculate inputs and outputs under the public rule. That reproducible constraint is a technical basis for continued operation—not proof that every piece of code and every block has always been error-free.

It calculates reserves.

An actual v2 swap first adjusts balances for fees, then checks that their product is at least the old reserves’ product. Only then are reserves updated; otherwise it reverts. That is the core of this check, not the contract’s only check.

It does not ask who you are, where the money came from, or the external market price. It asks only what remains in the pool.

The second essay ended by saying the chain recognizes hash power without asking who stands behind it. Here the same thing happens at another layer: it recognizes reserves without asking what happens beyond them.

It does not calculate why you came, how you fare after the swap, or whether someone moved first in the seconds between confirmation and block inclusion.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
