Liquidation

From Maker’s zero-bid auctions to protocol governance: why can a liquidation executed under the rules still leave failures of bidding competition and unresolved questions about who bears the loss?

1. Four Different Objects

Contemporary accounts of March 12, 2020, contained four figures at once: approximately $4 million, $4.5 million, $5.67 million, and $8.32 million.

They are not necessarily contradictory.

They measure four different things.

Contemporary reporting often used these figures interchangeably. The consequence was more than imprecision. It determined what the event was understood to be: a protocol loss, a group of people's loss of property, or an auction's failure to establish a price. These interpretations lead to three entirely different lines of inquiry.

If the protocol lost money, the question is whether its capital buffer was sufficient. If people lost property, the question is whether they can recover it, and from whom. If an auction failed to establish a price, the question is under what conditions the mechanism breaks down.

All three questions are valid, and all concern the same facts. They differ in where they direct attention.

These three questions subsequently followed three different paths.

The protocol’s path moved quickly: a week later, debt auctions began raising funds through newly minted MKR to fill the shortfall. A quick start does not mean the entire gap was closed within that week.

The auction's path took more than a year, with parameter changes followed by replacement of the entire mechanism.

The people's path reached September, when a vote involving only a few dozen addresses gave them an answer: zero.

The three paths’ different speeds already tell us something. The shortfall was a calculable number with an existing repair mechanism, which could be started a week later. Changing the auction mechanism involved parameters and code and took more than a year. Whether people should be compensated had no ready-made mechanism, so it was put to a vote.

One object is the triggering of a Vault's liquidation itself. Another is the quality of the auction's execution. A third is the bad debt thereby incurred by the protocol. A fourth is the residual collateral that Vault owners did not recover.

The sentence most in need of avoiding is therefore: Maker lost $8.32 million on Black Thursday.

That $8.32 million primarily describes the value of collateral acquired at near-zero prices. The debt the protocol failed to recover is another measure. The residual equity lost by Vault owners is a third.

The previous essay asked where prices come from.

This one asks what happens when action is taken on the basis of those prices.

The two must be read together, because only one step separates them: an oracle supplies a number, and a liquidation contract sells people's collateral according to that number.

This execution path did not require someone to approve, review, or pause each individual liquidation at discretion; governance could still have separate powers to pause operations or change parameters. The previous essay ended by observing that the chain checks whether a statement was made under the rules, not whether it is true.

Here we examine what the machine acting on that statement does once it has been made.

2. A Normal Liquidation

At the time, ETH-A Vaults had a liquidation ratio of 150%. A Vault became eligible for liquidation when the collateral price used by Maker put the ratio of collateral value to debt below 150%.

The parameter most easily misunderstood here is the 13% liquidation penalty.

That 13% is added to the debt the auction needs to recover. It does not mean that at most 13% of a user's ETH will be taken.

If the debt is D, the auction's target proceeds are approximately 1.13 times D.

A theoretical upper bound follows.

Suppose a Vault is triggered at exactly 150%, and the auction can sell at the prevailing price without slippage. The original collateral is worth 1.5 times D. Repaying debt plus penalty requires only 1.13 times D, leaving, in theory, 0.37 times D.

That is 24.67% of the original collateral value.

Maker's later compensation proposal used precisely this 24.67% as its theoretical upper bound.

The actual proportion returned can be lower, because prices may continue falling after liquidation, auctions may clear at a discount, and execution may be delayed.

The auction itself had two stages.

When creating an auction, the contract recorded the collateral for that lot, the target proceeds, the liquidated Vault's owner, the current bid, the latest bidder, the individual bid timer, and the overall auction deadline.

During the first stage, the collateral quantity remained fixed while bidders progressively increased the amount of DAI they were willing to pay.

Once someone bid the target amount, the auction entered its second stage. DAI then remained fixed, and bidders competed by accepting less collateral.

At the end, the winning bidder received the final collateral quantity; the unsold portion returned to the Vault owner.

The existence of this second stage is one of the most important points in the entire essay.

The contract deliberately included a stage for returning excess collateral to the person being liquidated.

This is not a retrospective interpretation. It is an intention embodied in code: liquidation was to protect both the protocol's solvency and the Vault owner's residual equity.

Several parameters mattered at the time.

The minimum valid bid increment was approximately 3%: a new bid generally had to exceed the previous bid by at least 3%.

The individual bid timer was ten minutes. Once a valid first bid had been placed, an auction could end if no higher bid appeared within ten minutes.

This was one of the central vulnerabilities in what followed.

For collateral lot size, contemporary accounts described ETH auctions as having a maximum or typical size of 50 ETH each. Proposals soon emerged afterward to raise that to 500 ETH.

For the absolute maximum duration of an entire auction, the primary materials obtained for this essay do not establish the specific production value on March 12 securely enough. Later parameters are therefore not retroactively presented as pre-incident settings.

The name “zero bid” also needs correction.

The code required a new bid to exceed the old one. Since the initial bid was zero, a literal bid of 0 DAI could not be the first valid bid under the contract's semantics.

Academic reconstructions likewise describe these auctions more cautiously as ending at near-zero DAI prices.

“Zero bid” should therefore be understood as contemporary community and media shorthand for an economically zero price or a tiny positive amount. Headlines alone cannot establish that every on-chain bid field was strictly zero.

Participants performed two distinct functions in this system. Anyone could trigger liquidation of an unsafe Vault, while auction participants used DAI to bid for collateral.

The system did not yet have the later explicit fixed-plus-proportional initiation reward. Its principal economic incentive came from acquiring collateral below its realizable market price.

What a participant actually calculated was therefore: the collateral's resale value, minus the DAI bid, gas, slippage, price risk during the holding period, and financing costs.

With normal markets, an uncongested network, and enough participants, competition would compress that profit.

That was the design's central dependence on a market-determined liquidation price.

A normal liquidation should therefore run as follows: the price updates; a Vault falls below 150%; liquidation sets a target covering debt and penalty; the auction starts; multiple participants bid up the DAI amount, ideally meeting the target; the second stage further reduces the collateral the winner can receive; settlement follows; DAI corresponding to the target offsets system debt; the winner receives only the portion needed to cover liquidation; and the remainder returns to the Vault owner.

We need to specify which link in this sequence actually failed that day.

It was not the contract's ability to identify a Vault below the liquidation threshold.

It was the expectation that multiple participants would compete promptly enough to bring the auction price to a reasonable level.

These two links are fundamentally different. The first is verifiable in code: whether the collateral ratio was calculated correctly is arithmetic. The second is not verifiable in code. It is a judgment about the outside world.

Yet both appeared in the same contract, looking like the same kind of thing.

3. Ninety-Six Point Eight and Twenty-Nine Point Six

All times below are UTC. Prices preferentially follow the hourly trajectory reconstructed from on-chain logs, because that represents the prices visible to the protocol rather than instantaneous exchange quotes.

That reconstruction shows that most liquidations clustered in the approximately eighteen hours from noon on March 12 to 6 a.m. on March 13. Its authors also explicitly note that the market fell first, with Maker's liquidation peak approximately an hour later, consistent with the designed one-hour delay.

Between 8 and 9 a.m., ETH had already fallen substantially from around $190, while the protocol still saw a higher, lagging price. Later discussion of compensation eligibility noted that the earliest auctions returning nothing could be traced to around 8 a.m., rather than the noon starting point used in some proposals.

From 9 a.m., pending transactions with effectively no prospect of inclusion increased sharply. Approximately thirty-five minutes later, their generation rate doubled again. Most offered around 5 Gwei when the market required more than 30 Gwei.

Between 10 and 11 a.m., the on-chain price chart remained around $160–170 while the live market continued deteriorating.

Between 11 a.m. and noon, the live price entered its steepest decline of the day. Average gas prices for the day approached 80 Gwei, roughly six times normal, while an hourly average briefly approached 200 Gwei.

Between noon and 1 p.m., the on-chain price began dropping sharply from above $160 to above $130. Large numbers of Vaults started crossing their trigger thresholds.

Precision matters here: the oracle did not simply stop reporting prices throughout. The designed one-hour delay, combined with untimely network updates, concentrated risk into discrete price jumps.

The sharpest distortion appeared between 1 and 2 p.m. Maker's price source at one point remained around $166 while its reference live market price was around $130. Once an update transaction entered a block, the protocol-visible price dropped more than 20% in a single step.

Many Vaults that had not previously met the on-chain liquidation condition suddenly became eligible at once.

Tools relying on successive price thresholds to protect Vaults automatically could also have their trigger points skipped in that single jump.

The hour from 3 to 4 p.m. was among the worst for auctions. The on-chain reconstruction shows nearly 800 auctions ending during that hour, with a near-zero median clearing price. The quantiles indicate that at least roughly three-quarters cleared near zero.

For 4 to 5 p.m., contemporary emergency records report that, during the initial two or three hours, almost only one participant was still triggering liquidations normally and bidding at economically zero prices. Others then gradually returned.

Those two or three hours come from contemporary community records and should not be rewritten as a precise number of minutes.

The crucial part is the count. Competition had not merely weakened: almost only one active participant remained. In an auction that relied on competing bids to form a price, scarcely a second competitor arrived in time during those hours. That does not establish how many natural persons stood behind each address.

From 5 to 11 p.m., auction competition recovered markedly for a time, with reduced price variance and more reasonable clearing levels.

This belongs in the account: the system was not completely immobilized all day.

Between 1 and 2 a.m. on March 13, however, a second wave of severe anomalies appeared, and the median clearing price again approached zero.

By around 6 a.m., the extreme concentration of failures gradually subsided.

Now consider the pair of numbers.

Before those two days, approximately 96.8% of auctions reached the second stage: they raised the target amount and could begin reducing the collateral awarded to the winner.

On March 12–13, that proportion was 29.6%.

The same code. The same parameters. The same second stage.

What changed was whether anyone outside came to participate.

These two percentages most deserve to be placed side by side. They compare not two systems, but the same system’s historical sample before the event with its performance over the two incident days.

Gas statistics also use several measures that must not be conflated. One organization reported a daily average near 80 Gwei and a peak hourly average near 200. Another, looking back later, reported transaction quantiles: over those two days, the highest-priced half exceeded 60 Gwei and the top 1% exceeded 400.

This is not a question of one being right and another wrong. An average, an hourly peak, and a cross-sectional tail quantile are three different things.

4. Six Causes

The primary materials support at least six mutually reinforcing mechanisms explaining why participants failed to bid prices up.

First, gas estimates lagged badly. Contemporary analysis reported that many automated programs based their estimates around the mempool's middle quantiles. When gas prices jumped abruptly, their bidding transactions failed to reach the front of the queue.

Second, Ethereum's sequential transaction nonces could block later transactions as well. One organization observed afterward that once an earlier low-gas transaction created a nonce gap, subsequent transactions could not bypass it even with higher gas prices.

Third, simultaneous auctions tied up DAI. Under the old mechanism, a bid committed funds until it was outbid or the auction ended. With thousands of auctions running simultaneously, a participant might see the profit perfectly well yet simply lack enough DAI to cover a new target amount.

The subsequently introduced system-wide ceiling on debt in active auctions directly addressed this exhaustion of capital through concurrency.

Fourth, rapidly falling prices created substantial adverse-selection and slippage risks within the auction itself. Even a participant with DAI could see prices fall several more percent between submitting a bid, winning, and selling the ETH back into the market. Contemporary emergency posts explicitly stated that some participants stopped operating because of slippage risk and long confirmation times.

Fifth, some participants' self-hosted nodes experienced synchronization or operational problems. The emergency meeting grouped high gas, node synchronization, and insufficient competition together as a tentative simple root cause.

This point must retain its original unconfirmed status rather than being rewritten as a single established fault.

That uncertainty is itself worth noting. Six years later, no verifiable count establishes how many participants failed to bid that day because their own nodes malfunctioned.

Sixth comes the explanation that later proved most controversial: one organization reported finding large numbers of low-gas, repeatedly replaced transactions intended to consume mempool resources, and suggested that a similar test might have occurred a few days earlier.

It did not, however, have evidence that these activities caused the price collapse itself.

A narrower statement is possible: its mempool data support the interpretation that artificial traffic may have aggravated existing organic congestion and given certain high-gas, near-zero bids a relative advantage.

Consider these six causes together.

None is inside the contract.

They concern, respectively, an automated program's gas-estimation method, Ethereum's nonce-ordering rules, the amount of DAI on a participant's own balance sheet, their assessment of a further price decline, the synchronization state of their machine, and the number of other people's transactions in the mempool.

Six conditions were spread across different layers and could reinforce one another. Parameters and tools could influence constraints such as capital and gas, but no auction function alone could guarantee that all six would be in place together during extreme markets.

Their only intersection was the same ten-minute window.

Those ten minutes were one directly adjustable time constraint in the contract. Afterward, the window was extended to six hours. A longer window gave a second bid more chance to arrive, but could not guarantee that it would.

The contract could see none of those six things. It could see only whether someone submitted a higher bid within ten minutes.

To the contract, no one submitting a higher bid was indistinguishable from everyone considering the current price reasonable.

This is one of the deepest structural problems in mechanisms such as automated liquidation.

An auction establishes a reasonable price because someone disagrees with the current quote. Yet the contract can observe only silence. It cannot distinguish silence expressing agreement from silence because no one is there.

This is the other side of the gap described in the previous essay. On that side, someone said something and the chain could not know whether it was true. On this side, no one speaks and the chain cannot know what the silence means.

Together, they are two expressions of the same problem: a contract can process only what has been submitted, while some of the most important information in the world it confronts is often precisely what was not submitted.

5. Seventy-Four Point Three Seven

The best way to understand the loss is to examine a case whose components can be checked individually.

One Vault owner publicly provided a complete set of parameters: 355.77 ETH in collateral, 35,613.38 DAI in debt, and a liquidation price of $143.01 displayed in his interface. The final amount returned was zero.

Recalculate from these numbers.

The target was 35,613.38 multiplied by 1.13, approximately 40,243.12 DAI.

At $143.01 per ETH with no slippage, approximately 281.40 ETH would need to be sold.

In theory, approximately 74.37 ETH should remain.

Under his stated price and assumptions including a sale without slippage, the entire 355.77 ETH cannot therefore be called an additional loss caused by the auction’s failure.

The theoretical residual under those assumptions was approximately 74.37 ETH.

This example helps clarify the 13% penalty. The owner was not originally supposed to lose only 13% of his 355.77 ETH: the 13% applied to the debt of 35,613.38. At his stated price, assuming a sale without slippage, the model would require selling about 281.4 ETH and leave a theoretical residual of about 74.4 ETH. That is not an independently established actual auction outcome.

The additional harm from the near-zero clearing price can be illustrated by the loss of that theoretical residual. The roughly 74.4 ETH figure still depends on the owner’s stated price and the sale assumptions above.

It was not the loss of 355.77 ETH compared with avoiding liquidation altogether.

This distinction excuses no one. It determines the number on which compensation, if offered, should be based. The later proposal did indeed use the residual-equity measure.

This also explains why $8.32 million cannot be equated with the compensation amount.

The former includes collateral that should have been sold anyway to extinguish borrowing and penalties. Compensation restoring a normal liquidation outcome would concern only the portion that should have been returned after a normal auction but was not.

Maker's subsequent compensation analysis put the maximum theoretical amount at approximately 2.88 million DAI, not $8.32 million.

A contemporary forum thread specifically registered affected owners. The following are publicly accessible first-person accounts. Vault numbers and collateral can be checked further on-chain, but claims about personal circumstances or the amount an owner should have received cannot automatically become findings by the protocol or a court.

One owner reported 109 ETH, with zero returned after liquidation. He said he operated a small GPU-mining business, was unemployed at the time, did not fully own all the ETH himself, and had planned to participate in future staking.

Another reported approximately 1,000 ETH, with only 2.99 ETH returned.

Another reported 118 ETH, with zero returned.

Another reported more than approximately 600 ETH, with a zero balance after liquidation.

Another reported 52 ETH, with zero returned, and said he had previously added another 10 ETH.

Another reported 105 ETH, with zero returned. He said he had tried to repay 1,200 DAI, but the transaction remained unconfirmed for a long time.

This last account cannot establish that timely inclusion of that transaction would necessarily have prevented liquidation.

It records, instead, what happened off-chain that day: someone tried to save his position, and his transaction did not enter a block.

Pause over that sentence: it makes unusually clear what a person was actually doing at the time.

The chain records collateral quantities, debt amounts, auction IDs, and clearing prices. Off-chain, someone spent those hours staring at a screen, assembling funds, sending a transaction, and watching it remain unconfirmed.

The chain has no field for that latter experience.

Two more owners supplied their own estimates. One reported approximately 579 ETH, a liquidation price around $178, and a final return of 55 ETH; he estimated that he should normally have received at least 234 ETH. Another reported 262 ETH, a liquidation price around $173, and a return of 13 ETH; he estimated that he should have received at least 63 ETH.

These are the owners' own calculations. Their posts do not provide enough debt information, so this essay does not force a theoretical residual calculation from collateral quantities alone.

Now trace the other side.

The near-zero-price winner received the ETH quantity ultimately determined by the auction, while paying an economically near-zero amount of DAI into the system.

The code required the first valid bid to be positive, so it would be inaccurate to say categorically that exactly zero was paid on-chain.

The winner also had to pay gas. To outrun congested transactions, that gas price generally had to be sufficiently high.

The conditions for winning during those hours were therefore quite specific: have DAI available, have a program that was not stuck, and be willing to pay a transaction fee far above normal. Few participants met all three conditions at the time, and that scarcity was precisely what made execution at such a price possible.

The materials available for this essay do not establish a reliable address-level total for the winners' gas expenditure. “Free” therefore cannot mean costless.

As to who the winners were, the evidence supports only a fairly narrow statement.

Maker said that day that initially there was only one participant. One organization later reported copycats. The complaint alleged that two automated programs won large numbers of auctions. Another organization described the 1,462 auctions using the plural.

Four descriptions, with four different time windows and definitions of the actors involved.

This research did not obtain a complete raw export linking every auction to its winning address, collateral quantity, and payment costs. It therefore does not identify any one address as the sole attacker, still less attribute an address to a particular natural person.

This is not a minor matter of wording.

An address's on-chain profit is a verifiable fact.

Whether that address represents one person, one bot, or several groups of people is a different proposition requiring a different layer of evidence.

6. Thirty-Eight Addresses

The protocol's bad debt and Vault owners' lost residual equity ultimately followed two entirely different paths.

Begin with the bad debt.

The white paper already provided that, when collateral auctions could not cover a Vault's obligations, the shortfall became protocol debt. It would first be offset against protocol surplus. If that was insufficient, debt auctions would follow: the system would mint MKR, bidders would buy it with DAI, and the DAI received would extinguish bad debt.

MKR was therefore designed from the outset as the protocol's tail-risk capital buffer.

On March 12, the emergency meeting explicitly chose not to trigger Emergency Shutdown immediately. One important reason was that shutdown might impose losses directly on DAI holders, whereas the community's understanding was that bad debt should first be borne by MKR holders through dilution.

This was a normative argument by governance participants, not a legal determination of creditor priority.

Debt auctions began on the morning of March 19. Each required the bidder to pay a fixed 50,000 DAI into the system, while bidders competed to accept progressively less MKR. In total, 106 auctions raised 5.3 million DAI and sold approximately 20,980 MKR.

There is a measurement trap here. A secondary report gave an average price of approximately 279.93 DAI per MKR. Dividing the aggregate totals, however, gives 5.3 million divided by 20,980, or approximately 252.62.

The former may be a simple average of the 106 clearing prices; the latter is the aggregate weighted average across the total quantity sold. They are not the same kind of average and should not be presented without specifying their calculation.

The source of funds is also clear. One investment firm publicly said it won 72 of the 106 auctions, approximately 68%, returning 3.6 million DAI to the system.

It was acting as a capital provider and auction buyer: it held less DAI and more newly minted MKR. Existing MKR holders, meanwhile, experienced dilution as total supply expanded.

That 3.6 million cannot be described as compensation to Vault owners. It entered the protocol's recapitalization channel.

Now consider compensation.

Supporters argued that a normal auction clearly intended to return excess collateral to its owner. That day's participant market, gas parameters, and ten-minute window had failed to do so. Governance capital should therefore cover not only DAI's backing but also users' residual losses from abnormal auctions. Some also emphasized that interfaces had long highlighted the 13% penalty, potentially suggesting a bounded liquidation loss.

Opponents argued that owners had chosen high leverage and allowed their positions to reach liquidation thresholds; market prices were still changing rapidly, so it could not be assumed that every position would clear at its theoretical liquidation price; and the protocol had never promised that auctions would return a fixed percentage. Compensation would also retrospectively shift market risk to the MKR holders of the time and establish a bailout precedent for future extreme events.

A middle position held that governance, participant infrastructure, the Ethereum network, and Vault owners themselves each bore some share of responsibility.

An early-April signal vote supported continuing toward compensation. Proposal documents recorded approximately 62,000 MKR in favor and 33,000 against.

That supported developing compensation, not the proposition that money had already been paid.

Subsequent proposals defined theoretical full restoration at 24.67% and calculated funding needs for several compensation levels. The highest scenario was approximately 2,880,324 DAI, equivalent under the proposal's assumed exchange rate to 11,522 MKR. Lower tiers required correspondingly less.

These were proposal scenarios, not actual disbursements.

The final formal compensation-choice vote, held September 8–22, 2020, offered several compensation levels, including zero.

Approximately 65% of voting power chose zero.

Only around 38 distinct voting addresses participated.

No commentary is added to that number. It simply establishes the scale of actual participation in the vote deciding whether hundreds of people could recover collateral.

That participation count alone neither establishes manipulation nor rules it out. To judge whether turnout was historically unusual would require a comparable participation series.

Governance therefore did not distribute this compensation from the protocol to the affected Vault owners.

Litigation is a separate matter and must be treated separately.

In 2020, one Vault holder filed a proposed class action against related entities. He alleged that Maker had misleadingly described risks including the 13% penalty, and that problems with the oracle, participant activity, and auction design caused him and other holders to lose all their collateral.

These were the plaintiff's allegations. They must not be presented as a court finding that Maker had design defects.

On February 22, 2023, a federal district court granted the defendants' motion to dismiss the second amended complaint, while allowing another amendment. The court's reasoning concerned the complaint's failure to satisfy the pleading requirements of the relevant claims adequately.

The ruling therefore neither proved that Black Thursday involved no defects nor established Maker's liability.

The case later settled. In February 2024, a federal judge approved a class settlement of approximately $1.16 million.

A zero-compensation governance decision therefore does not mean that every affected person ultimately received nothing in the real world.

That $1.16 million was off-chain litigation-settlement funding. It was not a later governance reversal of the 2020 vote, nor debt-auction compensation for Vault owners. A settlement itself also does not generally amount to a court determination of liability on the merits.

The question of who ultimately bore the losses therefore requires five separate accounts.

For ETH that normal liquidation would have sold anyway, the owner's collateral decreased while the bidder acquired ETH; the bidder's DAI extinguished the debt.

For additional residual losses caused by abnormal auctions, the owners whose collateral was auctioned near zero lost value while those winners gained it; governance did not later reimburse them generally.

For system debt unrecovered through collateral auctions, the protocol's balance sheet bore the shortfall, filled first by surplus and then by newly minted MKR.

For the debt auctions themselves, existing MKR holders bore supply dilution while new buyers exchanged 5.3 million DAI for approximately 20,980 MKR.

For the off-chain litigation settlement, defendant entities paid eligible class members. This was not an on-chain governance transfer.

Asking whether Vault owners or MKR holders bore the loss is therefore a false binary.

They bore losses at different levels.

Owners primarily bore the residual equity not returned by abnormal auctions. MKR holders, through the additional twenty-thousand-plus tokens, bore the dilution associated with recapitalizing the protocol's bad debt.

DAI holders were not directly subjected to a haircut through Emergency Shutdown at the time. Near-zero-price winners received an abnormal gain between collateral's auction price and market value.

Evidence on the real-world side is considerably scarcer than on-chain evidence. Beyond the accounts above, the class action, and the eventual $1.16 million settlement, this research found no systematic study reliably quantifying that day's effects on employment, local consumption, business costs, or financial access.

This should remain an evidence gap rather than a basis for inferring macroeconomic effects from a few personal accounts.

7. Every Change

The subsequent changes were not as simple as raising the minimum bid from zero to one. The actual problems spanned auction windows, transaction throughput, participant capital, participant incentives, auction format, and governance's ability to pause operations.

The individual bid timer was extended sharply from ten minutes to approximately six hours. This addressed the possibility that a low bid could win collateral at an economically zero price when congestion prevented a second bid within ten minutes.

Collateral lot sizes increased from approximately 50 ETH to approximately 500 ETH. This targeted the transaction storm created by thousands of simultaneous liquidations. The cost was a higher capital requirement per auction, potentially concentrating participation further.

A system-wide ceiling on debt simultaneously in auction was introduced. It directly addressed the exhaustion of participant capital when DAI was locked across too many concurrent auctions.

The range of eligible collateral was expanded. This addressed single-asset shocks and insufficient DAI liquidity rather than directly repairing auction competition. It also introduced new centralized risks involving issuers, freezing, and custody.

This change deserves separate attention: it explicitly introduced a new off-chain dependency. To make the system more resilient in extreme markets, it accepted assets whose issuers had to be trusted.

Any claim that collateral expansion improved safety therefore needs that qualification.

The most fundamental change replaced the original two-stage bidding process with Dutch auctions.

Several accompanying measures followed: four built-in shutdown levels could progressively prohibit new auctions through to preventing purchases altogether, allowing governance to pause parts of liquidation during oracle failures or clearly mispriced auctions without immediately shutting down the entire system; global and collateral-specific throughput limits made the absorptive capacity of participants and markets explicit parameters rather than assuming unlimited depth; fixed-plus-proportional initiation and maintenance rewards directly addressed the old system's heavy dependence on external participants without sufficient independent incentives to start and maintain auctions; and starting prices could include a buffer above the reference price, decline automatically over time, and reset when a maximum duration or price-decline threshold was exceeded.

The fundamental change was from repeated on-chain competitive bidding to a price function that declined by itself, allowing anyone to buy directly at a price they accepted.

This reduced dependence on one specific assumption: that at least two participants' transactions would both reach the chain within ten minutes.

Combined with settlement allowing collateral to be obtained, sold or financed, and repaid within one transaction, participants also no longer needed to hold large amounts of idle DAI beforehand.

None of this eliminated the underlying network and oracle risks.

If Ethereum had no adequate block space, purchase transactions still could not reach the chain.

If the reference price was seriously wrong, the buffer merely surrounded an incorrect benchmark.

If market depth was insufficient, a Dutch auction's price could only continue falling until someone was willing to buy.

What was added was greater control over failures and the ability to pause, not proof that automated liquidation no longer depended on markets and networks.

That distinction matters to understanding how mechanisms evolve.

Viewed together, the changes in this section fall into two categories.

One relaxes constraints: extending ten minutes to six hours, increasing fifty ETH to five hundred, and rewarding participants for starting auctions. These changes make the underlying assumption easier to fulfill.

The other acknowledges constraints: limiting debt in simultaneous auctions, setting throughput ceilings, and adding circuit breakers with graduated shutdown levels. These changes do something different. They acknowledge in advance that participants and markets have finite absorptive capacity and encode that finitude as a parameter.

The second category matters more than the first. The first still relies on the assumption. The second, for the first time, treats it as something that must be stated explicitly.

8. The Sentence That Cannot Be Written Into Code

What that day exposed was not merely that liquidation in one particular protocol could lose money.

It exposed automated liquidation systems' common dependence on several external conditions.

First is market depth: the assumption that, when large quantities of collateral must be sold, an outside market can still absorb them near the quoted price.

Second is settlement-asset liquidity: the assumption that participants can quickly obtain enough DAI.

Third is network capacity: price updates, collateral additions, debt repayments, liquidation triggers, and bids must enter blocks within an economically meaningful window.

Fourth is price freshness: the price triggering liquidation must not remain stuck across several market states and then jump abruptly.

Fifth is participant diversity and rationality: participants must not all use similar gas estimates, share the same software flaw, or depend on the same funding source.

Sixth is that market risk and execution risk are not highly correlated.

That day moved all six assumptions in the wrong direction at once.

They also reinforced one another. Faster price falls meant more liquidations; more liquidations meant more congestion; more congestion slowed price updates and participants; those delays made DAI scarcer and auction discounts deeper; deeper discounts increased losses for both the protocol and owners.

Every step in this chain was a consequence of other steps, and none was part of the designers' intention.

Later events showed that automated liquidation can fail in more than one way.

Abnormal liquidations at another lending protocol in November 2020 were associated with a sharp spike in its reference price source. Reports put their scale at approximately $88 million. That was closer to a price-source anomaly for collateral or debt assets suddenly pushing healthy accounts across a threshold, rather than an absence of auction competition.

Both events involved price sources, but the point of failure differed.

A subsequent study of another protocol's large liquidation episode in May 2021 found an even more severe gas environment than on that day in 2020: the highest-priced half exceeded approximately 100 Gwei, while the top 1% often reached 1,000–4,000 Gwei.

Yet it did not produce the same kind of outcome.

One important difference lay in the mechanism: one required repeated, mutually superseding bids within a short window; the other did not. This helps explain the outcomes without treating market and network conditions on different dates as identical.

The comparison shows that gas prices higher than in the earlier event do not, by themselves, imply inevitable near-zero-price auctions.

The outcome also depends on whether liquidation requires prolonged, multi-round competitive transactions and on participants' funding and execution arrangements.

This research did not obtain a sufficiently clean same-day panel of liquidations and bad debt with consistent definitions across protocols. It therefore does not assemble scattered later figures into an apparently comparable ranking.

In particular, the notional volume liquidated, liquidators' profits, protocol bad debt, and borrowers' residual losses must be distinguished. Datasets frequently measure different things under these headings.

Conflating them is precisely why the four figures at the beginning appear to conflict.

Return now to the second stage described in Section 2.

The stage returning excess collateral to the liquidated owner was written in code, readable by anyone, and had previously worked normally.

On those two days, seven auctions in ten never reached it.

The reason lay outside that stage itself. Reaching the second stage required someone first to bid the target amount. In most auctions during those two days, even that never happened.

The first stage stopped there, leaving the second no opportunity to begin.

In the auctions that cleared near zero, identification, triggering, calculation, auction initiation, and settlement could satisfy the code’s conditions without producing the competitive outcome people expected. Execution under the rules is not the same as a design without failures.

What failed was the assumption outside the code.

Someone will come.

The remainder's location is therefore clear.

It is not money that went unrecorded. Every ETH in that $8.32 million is on-chain and can still be traced individually today.

It is a premise that cannot be written into a contract.

This is the purest form of the remainder encountered in the series so far.

In earlier essays, the remainder was what standards left outside their jurisdiction, what never entered the code, what no one was assigned to measure, or what had not yet happened when calculation was possible. Here it is a sentence that cannot be written in the relevant syntax at all.

You can write that an auction lasts ten minutes.

You cannot write that a second person will arrive and bid within ten minutes.

Yet the entire design depends on the latter sentence.

The sentence cannot be written not because the language lacks precision, but because it describes something other than the system's internal state. It describes whether people outside the system will act at that moment.

A contract can specify its own behavior. It cannot specify other people's.

Across those two days, the contract ran more than four thousand auctions, settling under the valid bids and timing conditions applicable to each. In those that ended near zero, a highest bid that people found unacceptable could still satisfy the code’s closing conditions.

It could identify the highest bid, but that result alone could not tell it whether competition had brought the price there or scarcely a second participant had arrived in time.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
