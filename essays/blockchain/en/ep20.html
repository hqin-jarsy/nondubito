Real-World Assets

Real-world assets bring fund shares on-chain. They also invite identity, custody, correction, and legal claims back into the settlement system.

1. The Remainder of a Remainder

Episode 4 described something: being expelled from an institutional system does not mean that something no longer needs institutions. It builds a system of its own. And the system it builds has almost the same shape as the one it was expelled from. Escrow replaced banks' third-party safekeeping, arbitration replaced courts, ratings replaced credit reporting, security deposits replaced licenses, and commissions replaced taxes.

That was more than a decade ago.

This time, the movement is in the opposite direction.

Real-world assets have long occupied an awkward position in this domain. Traditional finance has not taken them very seriously, because their scale is too small and their structures too circuitous. The domain itself has not taken them very seriously either, because they are not pure enough: they need licenses, qualified investors, custodians, and a contract written off-chain.

In a place that defines itself by permissionless access, censorship resistance, and not asking who you are, something that asks who you are at every turn is naturally marginal.

The way it is marginalized is quite specific. Almost every metric this domain uses to assess a project works against it: its total value locked cannot match lending protocols, its trading activity cannot match decentralized exchanges, and its holding addresses often number only a few dozen.

Measured with this domain's own ruler, it has always been small.

It is equally small by traditional finance's measure: a few billion dollars is not a figure that demands discussion in an asset-management industry measured in trillions.

Neither side treats it as a protagonist. This is the usual position of a remainder.

It is also the most common way a remainder forms. Episode 2 described how a construct's most important property is precisely the one it cannot measure itself. Here is another version: something is seen as marginal because the ruler used to measure it was not made for it.

So it is the remainder of a remainder.

Blockchain itself grew out of exclusion from the formal financial system. And within it, one part has been pushed to the side by this domain's own ordering of values.

Now that part is moving from the edge toward the center.

What brought this about was not a change in preferences within the domain. It was interest rates.

When short-term Treasury yields returned from near zero to a level of 4–5%, something that could sit on-chain, interact with contracts and also earn Treasury income gained a clearer economic attraction. Episode 8 examined reserve income accruing to an issuer. Here the direction changes: if that income reaches holders directly, it adds a reason to bring the product on-chain.

What we will examine this time is what happened as it made that journey.

Twenty episodes into this series, this is the first time something has been moving back.

The previous nineteen episodes were all about movement outward: exclusion, overflow, being left outside jurisdiction, distinctions that had been collapsed. This time, the direction is reversed.

First, we need a method, because it determines whether the next seven sections can get the matter right.

The most important distinction is not whether an asset is on-chain.

It is exactly which layer of legal relationships the on-chain token occupies.

A regulator's staff statement in 2026 divided these into three types: some tokens themselves correspond to the registration of securities; some merely give the holder a security entitlement against an intermediary; and others are simply a third party's promise to pay a return linked to a security.

These three structures are entirely different on the day the intermediary goes bankrupt.

On an ordinary day, they look exactly the same on a screen.

This is a practical warning running through the history. The distinction matters especially on the worst day, but also affects ordinary transfer eligibility and redemption. A similar-looking balance screen does not by itself tell you which structure you bought. Product descriptions and legal documents still need to be read.

Before the exchange in Episode 3 collapsed, its screens displayed balances too. Displaying a balance did not establish full asset backing, much less identify the legal position of the claim.

2. The First Layer

Most financial real-world assets do not bring the underlying Treasuries, loans, or property deeds directly on-chain.

Instead, a conventional legal entity first holds the assets. The fund shares, limited partnership interests, notes, or beneficial interests issued by that entity are then represented as tokens.

Money market funds are the easiest category to misdescribe.

The Franklin fund is a government money market fund under the US Investment Company Act of 1940. It invests at least 99.5% in US government securities, cash, and repurchase agreements fully collateralized by government securities or cash.

A token represents one fund share, not one Treasury security.

The blockchain records fund shares. It does not change how the securities held by the fund are invested or held in custody.

BUIDL is slightly different. The fund is a British Virgin Islands limited company, offered as a pooled investment fund under US private-placement rules. The service provider contracted by the issuer is expressly designated in the agreement as transfer agent, registrar, and dividend-paying agent.

The token therefore corresponds to a securities interest in the fund. It does not make the holder the direct registered owner of every Treasury security. At the fund level, the allocation is to cash, short-term Treasuries, and repurchase agreements, and those conventional assets remain within the conventional securities custody system.

OUSG shows most clearly how thick the wrapper can be.

The issuer's own disclosures state that the issuing entity is a Delaware limited partnership. Upon investing, investors become limited partners in the fund, and each token represents one unitized limited partnership interest. Most of its portfolio is in turn currently invested in BUIDL, supplemented by other government money market funds, bank deposits, and stablecoins.

The full chain is therefore: the investor, then an OUSG limited partnership interest, then BUIDL fund shares, then Treasuries, repurchase agreements, and cash.

Four layers.

What moves on-chain is the investor’s layer of OUSG partnership interests, not each Treasury security underneath.

What people talk about is the last.

Every layer is real, every layer has a contract, and every layer can be examined. The distance between them is a distance that will be traversed layer by layer only on the day something goes wrong.

These layers are not a deliberately designed maze. Each solves a specific problem: the limited partnership governs investor relationships, fund shares provide for valuation and redemption, and custody establishes responsibility for safekeeping.

Each layer serves a purpose. Stacked together, they put more than one legal relationship between the on-chain entry and the Treasury security underneath. The particular route here runs from OUSG partnership interests through BUIDL fund shares to the underlying assets.

Those layers are not necessarily a design flaw, nor are the same four steps inevitable for every tokenized product. The Franklin fund described above uses a different structure. The questions are why this product is connected this way and what rights each layer gives its holder.

At each layer, the contracts, applicable law and enforceable obligations must be examined separately. Whether fiduciary duties apply cannot be inferred simply from a role’s place in this chain.

These are precisely the things that belonged to the system from which it originally spilled out.

Once all those steps have been taken, however, that entry can no longer be called the Treasury security itself.

The distance is greater in private credit. An on-chain token is primarily a record of an investor's holding in a pool or tranche. The loan agreements and security package are still enforced off-chain.

A loan token moving from one wallet to another therefore cannot automatically be equated with the lawful assignment of the loan claim from the former holder to the latter.

Whether that assignment occurs depends on the subscription agreement, note terms, transfer provisions, and the structure of the security agent or trustee arrangement. Many designs deliberately keep the underlying loans in a fixed entity precisely to avoid having to carry out a fresh loan assignment for every secondary-market trade.

The design has a practical rationale. Depending on the contract and jurisdiction, individual loan assignments may require notice to borrowers, updates to security registrations or renewed consent. Repeating those procedures for every secondary-market trade would make circulation much more costly.

The price is that the speed of circulation at the on-chain layer is deliberately separated from the ownership of rights at the off-chain layer.

The result is an odd state of affairs: a token changes hands three times in a few seconds, while the lender on the loan remains the same entity throughout.

The structure is most intuitive in real estate. A common approach is to have each property held by a dedicated limited liability company, with token holders becoming members who hold fractional interests in that company. In principle, each company holds only one property.

The owner shown in the land register is therefore always the company, not several hundred wallet addresses.

Rent and sale proceeds arise at the company level and are then distributed according to membership interests.

A narrower, more accurate version of the claim that real-estate title has been brought on-chain is therefore: the interests in the company that owns the real estate have been tokenized.

Whether a valid token transfer also constitutes a valid transfer of company membership still depends on the governing documents, state company law, and the official membership register. An on-chain transfer alone does not establish that title in the county land records has changed hands.

There is a rare exception among commodities.

The terms of one gold token state that each token corresponds to one troy ounce of London Good Delivery gold. Holders are the beneficial owners of that allocated gold, which a fiduciary institution holds separately for their benefit in compliant vaults. The terms further provide that purchases, transfers, and redemptions automatically trigger the reassignment of allocations to specific gold bars.

Here, then, we can say with relative confidence that an on-chain transfer, under the design of the issuance agreement, is accompanied by a reallocation of beneficial ownership of gold.

That does not mean the bars move inside the vault when a transfer takes place. Nor does it mean the holder directly becomes the sole legal owner in every internal record kept by the vault operator. Direct purchases from and redemptions with the issuer still require verified customer identity.

Trade finance generally uses a securitization-style structure: a special-purpose entity first takes on trade receivables, then issues notes or securities. The token corresponds to the rights under that security.

The simplification most important to avoid here is that buying a token gives you direct ownership of the exporter's invoice.

Taken together, these five categories make one thing clear.

The word custody means something entirely different in each.

Securities funds need securities custodians. Gold needs vaults. A legal entity holds title to a house. Loans and invoices need servicers, security agents, trustees, special-purpose entities, and, potentially, courts at the end of the process.

It is inaccurate to describe all of these as assets held in custody by smart contracts.

More accurately, what a smart contract holds is the instrument evidencing the rights. Who holds what lies behind that instrument, under which country's law, and whom to turn to when something goes wrong are governed by five entirely different sets of arrangements.

3. No Legal Claim Arises from It

The structures in the previous section may sound like technical compromises: existing law has not caught up, so a wrapper has to be added for now.

One document shows that this is not what is happening.

Franklin's prospectus states that its transfer agent maintains the official record of fund-share ownership through a blockchain-integrated system.

In other words, the chain is not a copy here. It has been incorporated into the formal registration system.

The same document then explains several things.

The mapping between personal identities and wallets is still kept in a conventional off-chain database.

The transfer agent can correct erroneous transactions, restrict transfers and manage wallet whitelists. Correction here does not mean deleting a transaction from an old block. The prospectus explains that an instruction can be added in a later block to update the registration state while retaining the original transaction history.

If an unrecognized erroneous transfer occurs on-chain, the party that receives the tokens in error does not thereby acquire a legal claim to the fund shares.

We should pause over that sentence.

A critic did not write this. The issuer states it explicitly in the statutory disclosure document provided to investors.

What it sets aside is the idea that an on-chain transfer unconditionally determines legal entitlement, not the protection against arbitrary deletion of past block history.

The on-chain state does not override everything.

This is the same boundary encountered earlier. Episode 1 distinguished an on-chain transfer from the pizza outside it. Episode 6’s fork dispute concerned how to respond to an executed state. In Episode 11, some auctions completed under the code without producing the expected competition. Recording what happened does not mean the chain alone determines what it counts as under other rules.

In those episodes, what happened on-chain had happened. The disagreement was only over whether to accept it.

Here the issuer states in advance that a recorded on-chain transfer does not necessarily give the recipient legal rights to fund shares. Whether it produces other consequences, such as restitution or liability, depends on the facts and applicable law.

The statement appears in a prospectus addressed to ordinary investors. It tells buyers beforehand how valid registration is identified after an error and who may correct it. The statement alone establishes neither that regulation played no part nor the issuer’s sole reason for including it.

A transfer completed on-chain, confirmed in a block and available for inspection may still be insufficient to give its recipient the fund interest. Whether the transaction occurred and what was legally acquired are different questions.

This echoes Section 204(a) in Episode 12. There, a transaction could carry a cryptographic signature without necessarily satisfying the requirements for a written copyright conveyance validly signed by the rights holder. Here the issuer states in advance that how an on-chain record enters the official register, and how errors are corrected, depends on the product documents and applicable law—not merely on the token having moved.

This is not a defect.

On the contrary, it is a condition of being able to accommodate this kind of asset.

For a registration system to hold pension assets over the long term, it must explain how errors, inheritance and court orders are handled. Keeping the old record is compatible with lawfully correcting the current state of rights.

Errors will happen: a private key is lost, an address is entered incorrectly, an exchange malfunctions, someone dies and the heirs cannot obtain the keys. Traditional finance has a process for each of these situations. The precondition for that process is someone with the authority to say that this transaction does not count.

This authority has long been treated as a defect in this domain. Yet it is a precondition for the operation of an entire system of inheritance, divorce, bankruptcy, and court enforcement.

After a person dies, shares can pass to heirs through succession procedures without a new signature from the deceased. Updating the register requires valid documents, authority and legal procedures—not merely someone with the technical ability to alter the book.

On-chain immutability is an advantage when transferring value between strangers.

For property rights lasting thirty years through inheritance, divorce, bankruptcy and court orders, an undeletable record of past events is not enough. The system must also record lawful corrections and later changes.

These products do not start by giving up an undeletable history. They put the authority to update registration, and the conditions for doing so, within law and accountable procedures.

Correction authority is not the only condition made explicit again. In the products discussed here, whitelists restrict entry, subscriptions and redemptions follow their business arrangements, investor qualifications limit participation, and off-chain identity databases connect wallets to real people. These restrictions are not identical across every tokenized asset.

Every one of these was something it originally used to distinguish itself from the old system.

4. $15.64 Billion or $11.1 Billion

Scale is the easiest part of this entire story to inflate, and there are two main ways to do it.

The first is to add numbers measured on different bases.

One data provider uses two concepts at once: distributed value and represented value. The former is closer to economic value already issued and distributed on-chain. The latter can include larger asset pools represented on-chain without necessarily having entered free circulation in their entirety.

For tokenized credit, for example, the same dashboard shows approximately $7.46 billion for the former and $34.99 billion for the latter.

Adding them together and calling the industry's size $42.45 billion is double counting.

The second is to mix outstanding stock with cumulative issuance. In principle, scale refers to the balance outstanding, still in existence, or measured at net asset value at a particular point in time, not the total amount ever issued.

Stablecoins should also be listed separately, rather than mixed into this table. Episode 8 already examined their own accounts.

This is not pedantry about presentation. Stablecoins are one to two orders of magnitude larger than this entire category. Including them would make any assessment of tokenized-asset growth depend predominantly on changes in stablecoins alone.

The two groups separated here also often have different rights structures. One principally promises redemption at a fixed face amount; another represents fund shares or other underlying rights. Episode 8 examined the former. Combining them in one total can obscure differences in income, risk and redemption, but this does not make every RWA a fund share.

Once the measurement basis is consistent, the Treasury and money market fund category can yield a relatively robust series at widely spaced intervals.

About $104 million in January 2023. About $917 million at the end of 2023. About $931 million at the beginning of 2024. About $5.6 billion in April 2025.

By August 2026, one data provider reported distributed value of approximately $15.64 billion. Another reported market capitalization of about $11.1 billion for its corresponding category.

The earlier points come from the same data provider, so they should not be presented as independent corroboration of one another.

The two 2026 figures reveal a genuine difference in measurement. One provider's asset registry includes a broader range of fund structures and distinguishes asset-level net asset value, blockchain, and tokenization type. The other's figure is closer to the circulating supply of the tokens it covers multiplied by their prices.

Each has its own coverage and method; traceability to a data provider is not mutual validation. Using 11.1 billion as the denominator, 15.64 billion is about 40.9% higher. The gap first reminds us not to combine the totals without their definitions.

A series for a single product is easier to check.

In investor materials filed with a regulator, one service provider gives quarterly figures for its own product: approximately $40 million, $483 million, $518 million, and $649 million for the four quarters of 2024, followed by about $2.3 billion, $2.8 billion, and $2.9 billion for the first three quarters of 2025.

Another page in the same materials gives the second quarter of 2025 as about $3 billion. That is rounding, not a separate asset pool.

The dates for Franklin are easier to verify: an April 2023 announcement put the fund above $270 million, and it was above $360 million at the end of March 2024. The product page showed total net assets of $753.24 million as of the end of June 2026.

These are statutory and product disclosures from the same issuer, with consistently defined dates. They likewise do not constitute cross-checking by independent data providers.

The two products are opposites in accessibility, and that tells us more than their size.

The private fund is offered in the United States under private-placement rules; it is not a retail fund. In a 2024 announcement, a competitor said that the fund's minimum initial investment at the time was $5 million, with a $250,000 minimum for minting and redemption. Because those figures come from a competing product's announcement, they can only be labeled as one company's account of another's terms.

Such secondhand accounts are common in this domain, but their place in the hierarchy of evidence needs to be identified. One company describing another's terms is not the same as that company stating its own terms.

Franklin's 2026 prospectus, by contrast, states that the minimum initial purchase for most accounts is $20, with no minimum for subsequent purchases. US individuals can open an account through an app.

Five million dollars and twenty dollars.

Both are called tokenized Treasury products, yet they serve two entirely different groups of people.

The former is a cash-management tool for institutions and other eligible investors; the latter also lets eligible ordinary individuals buy through a phone. An industry total covering both can add their net asset values without showing how different their participants are.

That total is meaningful when answering how large tokenization is. When answering how many ordinary people participate, it answers nothing at all.

5. Unlocking

Those trillion-dollar projections are, first and foremost, projections. They are not current asset balances.

The addressable market a service provider presented to investors in 2025 directly cited a consultancy's estimate of a roughly $19 trillion tokenization opportunity. For the provider, this was capital-markets marketing material: its revenue is tied to the amount of tokenized assets, the number of funds, and transaction volume.

Another consultancy's 2024 estimate was distinctly narrower: a base case of about $2 trillion in tokenized financial assets by 2030, with a higher scenario of around $4 trillion also discussed.

Its method assigned adoption curves to successive groups of asset classes. The 2030 estimate implied average compound annual growth of about 75% for the early categories, followed by an assumed slowdown.

The two figures differ by nearly a factor of ten, yet they do not contradict each other. The former is broader, includes more asset classes, and is being cited to prospective investors by an institution with a commercial interest. The latter is narrower, sets out its assumptions, and explicitly calls itself a scenario.

Which one you cite depends on what you want to say.

The contexts in which the two figures are cited have one thing in common: they are usually presented without the current actual balance alongside them. That current actual balance is the roughly ten-billion-dollar figure in the previous section.

This is not the sum of an already contracted issuance pipeline. It is a scenario model.

The statement should therefore be rewritten as follows: if several classes of financial assets migrate to tokenized representations at the high adoption rates assumed by consultancies, some models yield nominal outstanding stocks of several trillion dollars by 2030. Results can differ severalfold depending on whether the models include real estate, private markets, stablecoins, and deposits, and on their assumptions about adoption rates.

What really needs to be unpacked here is the word unlocking.

If an existing $1 billion fund replaces its registration system with a distributed ledger, statistics can already show $1 billion in tokenized assets even if the number of investors, the amount of capital, and liquidity have not changed.

No new billion dollars of capital has been created.

This is not splitting hairs. Whether the development is meaningful depends precisely on which of those two things has happened.

If it is the former, the value lies in efficiency: registering, reconciling, and transferring the same money becomes easier. That is real but limited value.

If it is the latter, the value lies in access: someone who could not buy before can buy now. That is a much larger claim, and it requires much stronger evidence.

Section 6 shows why existing material is insufficient to turn easier, smaller purchases in some products into a conclusion that access has broadly expanded for ordinary people.

The word unlocking merges two entirely different things: changing the way records are kept, and attracting money that was not here before.

The first can be measured and is happening.

The second requires separate evidence: which investors are new, whether funds were simply moved from elsewhere, and why those investors previously could not enter. Existing material does not yet measure those questions fully.

This distinction is central, because every subsequent argument about cost, liquidity, and access comes down to the same question: was that number moved in, or did it newly grow?

Every section has its version of the question. In the section on scale, it is the difference between distributed value and represented value. In the section on liquidity, it is the gap between a few dozen holding addresses and a minimum investment of a few dozen dollars. In the section on cost, it is the difference between the efficiency issuers anticipate and the five service fees actually being added at the same time.

6. Twenty-Four Hours

The claim that round-the-clock trading and instantaneous settlement have already been achieved needs to be separated into four questions.

Can the token move on-chain at any hour?

Can the two sides find counterparties at any hour?

Does a transfer immediately achieve full legal finality?

And can the cash reach final settlement at the same time?

Franklin's fund offers the most telling counterexample.

Eligible holders can transfer peer to peer on-chain, but ordinary subscriptions and redemptions are processed only during business hours on business days. The transfer agent does not match buyers with sellers either.

The issuer expressly states that peer-to-peer transfers do not amount to the establishment of a public trading market.

This sentence is also worth noting, because it too comes from the issuer itself. Being able to transfer something and having somewhere to sell it are two different things. The latter is not a technical problem.

OUSG describes its offering as round-the-clock instant minting and redemption, while the company's own documentation also specifies service-provider-dependent limits over a 24-hour period. Amounts above those limits must go through a non-instant request process.

BUIDL's instant redemption relies, at least in part, on liquidity providers exchanging it for stablecoins.

That addresses liquidity between on-chain assets and on-chain cash-like assets.

If the end investor needs dollars in a bank, however, the process still has to pass through the stablecoin issuer, banks, and fiat payment systems.

So the apparently fastest part on-chain is still connected to the slowest part.

The narrower version is therefore that some products have achieved round-the-clock on-chain token movement and near-real-time asset exchange within arrangements where identity checks and whitelisting have been completed in advance, with stablecoins and liquidity providers available.

That does not mean every product has secondary-market liquidity every hour of every day.

Nor does it mean that on-chain finality, finality under securities law, and finality for bank dollars occur at the same moment.

Episode 19 separated sequencing confirmation, data inclusion on Layer 1, state confirmation and executable withdrawals. The present questions span farther: whether a token can move, a buyer can be found, legal securities rights have transferred and bank cash has settled. They involve technical, market, legal and payment arrangements, not one clock.

Nor can a round-the-clock market be assessed solely by how long its smart contracts run.

A 2025 study compiled asset and address data and found that transfer activity, active addresses, and secondary trading remained very low for most of these tokens. The authors identified whitelists, regulatory access restrictions, concentrated custody, opaque valuations, and a lack of trading venues as the main obstacles.

This is an empirical observation about market liquidity, not a legal determination.

A related claim also needs rewriting: fractionalization automatically creates liquidity.

Technology and product design really can make the minimum holding very small. Real-estate interests costing a few dozen dollars, 0.01 ounces of gold, and $20 worth of fund shares are all real.

But smaller denominations only lower the minimum ticket size.

They do not automatically create market makers, two-way quotes, eligible buyers, or legal transferability.

Even if something costs only 20 dollars to buy, we must still ask who is eligible, where it can be bought and whether a counterparty exists when selling. Lowering the monetary threshold is an improvement; it does not establish that the other gates are open.

Yet both are often summarized in the same sentence: the barriers have been lowered.

The monetary threshold has fallen. Whether eligibility has changed depends on the product. For products still limited to qualified purchasers, a small denomination does not automatically remove that gate.

One fund currently has only a few dozen holding addresses and still requires qualified-purchaser status. The first describes observed participation; the second describes legal eligibility. A few dozen addresses means neither a few dozen natural persons nor that only a few dozen people worldwide are eligible.

The narrower version is therefore that tokenization can reduce the minimum accounting unit and the technical increment in which transfers can be made. Whether it expands the actual pool of buyers and the depth of the secondary market still depends on securities eligibility rules, identity checks, distribution channels, market making, and legal transferability.

7. What Governs When Records Conflict

If on-chain records and an off-chain register clash one day, which governs?

Four jurisdictions provide four different starting points for this question. None offers a general rule that blockchain takes precedence.

That is worth noticing in itself. Four jurisdictions, with four different legal traditions, point in the same direction on this question: first look at what the existing rules say.

In the United States, the starting points are Article 8 of the Uniform Commercial Code, the issuer's or transfer agent's master securityholder file, and the issuance documents themselves. If the chain is itself the master file, its records can directly perform the function of registration. If it is not, the token's state cannot be assumed to take precedence.

In the European Union, the relevant factors are authorization under the pilot regime, the applicable member state's securities, property, and company law, and the formal settlement and registration rules. When token records conflict with off-chain records, there is therefore no uniform blockchain-first rule. One must examine the law governing issuance of the security, the applicable member-state law, and the specific exemptions granted to the distributed infrastructure.

In Singapore, regulatory materials focus on bringing tokenized capital-markets products within existing frameworks for securities, funds, licensing, and investor protection, rather than creating a new property law that makes token ownership independent of rights in the underlying securities.

Hong Kong's 2023 circular is particularly explicit: tokenized securities are essentially conventional securities with a tokenization wrapper. Existing rules on securities issuance, distribution, fund management, and market conduct continue to apply.

It also requires intermediaries to investigate how token ownership is transferred and recorded, when on-chain and off-chain settlement becomes final, and what the custody arrangements are.

More importantly, the circular itself distinguishes bearer form from registered form.

For bearer-form securities using permissionless tokens on a public, permissionless network, the transfer of the token may itself change ownership. Tokens in registered form, however, must be assessed in conjunction with their registration system.

In other words, the regulator itself has already rejected the claim that all tokenized securities follow the same ownership rules.

We now come to the most important point in this section, and it is a gap.

Within the scope of this search of public materials in these four jurisdictions, no directly relevant published judgment was found with this precise fact pattern: an on-chain holder's claim to a lawfully issued tokenized fund or security conflicts with the issuer's or transfer agent's statutory register, and a court determines who obtains the security.

The relatively numerous related cases address something else: whether cryptocurrency is property, whether it can be held on trust, whether it can be frozen, and whether it can be traced.

That does not answer a conflict over the register of holders of a tokenized security.

The difference is concrete. The first kind of question asks whether this thing counts as property. The second asks which of two contradictory ownership records counts.

The first category already has many answers. For the second, this search found no published judgment directly matching the facts. Not finding one does not mean such a dispute has never been raised.

That gap does not establish that no sufficiently large conflict has occurred. Disputes may settle or produce no published judgment. The material reviewed here cannot exclude those possibilities one by one.

Current analysis of which record governs still has real foundations in statutes, regulatory materials and offering documents. The qualification is that this search found no directly matching published precedent testing their application to the specific conflict.

If someone brings an on-chain record and a conflicting shareholder register into court, a judgment may make that application more concrete. Until then, however, existing law and contracts should not be treated as providing no answers.

That includes every sentence in this section.

So we cannot write that courts have already confirmed the precedence of blockchain registration. Nor can we write the reverse, that courts have already confirmed that blockchain registration is invalid.

For now, the more direct grounds remain statutes, issuance documents, the design of the transfer-agent arrangement, and regulatory guidance.

The sentence in Section 3 is a concrete product disclosure: the issuer says erroneous token receipt does not itself create a legal claim to fund shares. It tells buyers in advance how this product handles registration and correction, but does not outrank applicable law.

Its value is that a party disclosed its arrangements before a dispute and buyers can read them before purchasing. The validity and application of the terms still fall under applicable law and competent authorities. Being written by the issuer does not give them greater final authority than a court.

8. What Was Invited Back

Before we finish, one more point must be made, because it is the part of this entire story most easily obscured by optimistic narratives.

A private-credit protocol's 2023 risk review showed that some of its real-world loans did not have the bankruptcy-remote, loan-by-loan segregated securitization structures investors might have imagined. They were loans on the borrowing companies' balance sheets, secured by pledges over specific receivables, cash, or physical assets, with direct recourse against the companies themselves.

In one case, the platform later disclosed that an unauthorized intercompany loan had moved part of the collateral beyond the reach of recourse.

Here, the on-chain token is primarily a record of the investor's holding in a pool or tranche.

The loan agreements and security package are still enforced off-chain.

What this shows is that the variables hardest to verify are all, precisely, off-chain.

Whether the company has transferred the receivables away. Whether the security interest is valid. What has happened between related parties. How much a court can ultimately recover through enforcement.

The chain can tell you exactly how many units you hold, every second.

It cannot tell you whether the things behind those units are still there.

This is the sentence in the whole essay most worth remembering. On-chain precision measures the instrument evidencing the rights. What people actually care about is what lies behind it.

In Episode 10, the gap lay between the chain and prices outside it. In Episode 15, it lay between two chains. This time it lies between an instrument evidencing rights and what it refers to. That gap is centuries old and has nothing to do with blockchain.

So the two sides need to be traced as follows.

Investors provide funds and receive the corresponding legal rights instrument. Within the product’s terms and technical permissions, it may also be transferred on-chain, used as collateral or combined with other contracts. Those capabilities must be checked product by product.

Issuers and service providers may receive tokenization, transfer-agency, fund-administration, integration and maintenance fees. Roles and charges can overlap, and not every product has them all. Listed charges form part of actual cost; they cannot simply be counted in advance as costs saved.

The people holding the instruments still bear the underlying asset risk. Off-chain servicers, trustees, and courts still perform the enforcement.

This is the answer here to the question every episode in this series must ask: those who bear the risk and those who collect the fees are not the same people. This time, it is not concealed. The issuance documents spell it out clearly.

That is also the greatest difference between this story and the preceding episodes. Many of the earlier failures arose because something had not been written down: an assumption, a precondition, a promise.

This time, almost everything has been written down.

The transfer agent's powers are in the prospectus. Redemption hours are in the prospectus. Minimum investments are in the terms. The revenue-sharing structure is in the contract. And the provision that an erroneous transfer creates no legal claim is in the body of the document intended for ordinary readers.

The question is not only whether terms were written down, but whether they are accessible and understandable and whether buyers actually see them. Disclosure matters, but does not guarantee understanding.

As for whether costs have actually fallen, the strongest primary materials currently available consist mainly of issuers' expectations about efficiency, service providers' business models, and published fund fees, rather than a matched before-and-after comparison that includes all costs.

One issuer speaks of expected operational efficiencies. The Bank for International Settlements says that much remains to be verified about gains in cost and efficiency.

The narrower version is therefore that automated registration, reconciliation, and settlement between tokens have identifiable mechanisms for saving costs. For the leading products considered here, however, public materials are insufficient to calculate complete net savings that include custody, transfer agents, compliance, legal work, the cash leg, and on-chain infrastructure.

Now return to Section I.

Episode 4 described how something expelled from an institutional system builds one of its own, and how the system it builds has almost the same shape as the one it was expelled from.

More than a decade later, this domain has finished building its own system.

Now it is inviting the outside system back in.

Across the different product structures examined here, transfer agents, custodians, investor qualifications and business-day arrangements reappear in different forms. So do the people legally responsible for errors, inheritance and enforcement. The same role does not perform all these functions in every product.

The remainder is in this very act.

It is neither eliminated nor spilled out.

It is invited back.

Issuers’ own business choices are part of bringing these arrangements in: entering regulated markets, obtaining the relevant authorizations, appointing service providers and specifying rules in offering documents. Choosing to enter does not make the resulting licensing, disclosure and investor-protection obligations wholly voluntary. They also arise from applicable law.

Yet what has been invited back is no longer quite what it once was.

These products modify expectations of unconditional entry, unidentified participation, round-the-clock subscription and redemption, and final entitlement determined by one on-chain transfer. They can retain an undeletable transaction history while lawfully updating current registration. Those two facts should not be compressed into a claim that immutability was surrendered.

There is no value judgment in this.

Changing those conditions is not necessarily a betrayal. To carry something else, a system must develop a form capable of supporting it. A form carrying long-term property rights must preserve records and explain how lawful corrections occur.

To carry pension assets, a register must handle errors, inheritance and court orders. To support fiduciary duties, it must identify who is responsible and to whom. These requirements can be layered onto a chain retaining its old history. Legal correction need not mean technical deletion.

Such an arrangement can be institutionally coherent. Property rights lasting thirty years through inheritance and court orders need valid legal procedures that can identify an erroneous registration and have subsequent registration reflect that determination.

The institutions and people with duties in that process should be identifiable and accountable, and subject to supervision or judicial review under applicable law.

The accounts simply need to be clear.

If adjusting the original access and control conditions enables it to carry real assets, that is a trade-off that can be examined. Both sides must be recorded, and preservation of history distinguished from lawful correction.

Public materials already specify many access restrictions, correction powers and service conditions. What is still missing here is a matched measurement of the net result: how much costs fell and how many previously excluded people gained entry. Existing product functions should be distinguished from industry-wide net benefits not yet fully measured.

What can be said now is that some costs are explicitly listed and some concrete conveniences exist. Once both sides are counted, the size of the net benefit and who receives it still require measurement.

The underlying public chain keeps producing blocks and recording transfers that satisfy contract conditions. Product contracts and service providers may check identity and eligibility before a transfer. An open chain does not make every product indifferent to who participates.

Behind the tokenized funds, gold, loans and property structures examined here are different registration, custody, servicing and enforcement roles. Some use a transfer agent to maintain a register; others assign duties to different legal entities. Who may correct a record, who holds the assets and who may decide a dispute must be checked against the specific documents and law.

The chain records the first layer.

Documents and declarations from the later layers can also leave on-chain records. But their validity, the continued presence of assets and the enforceability of rights are not automatically established by precision in the first layer.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
