Pizza

Beginning with 10,000 bitcoins exchanged for two pizzas, an inquiry into the distance between price, trust, and on-chain settlement that was there from the first day.

I. May 18

At 12:35 a.m. on May 18, 2010, someone using the name laszlo posted on the Bitcointalk forum.

The subject was “Pizza for bitcoins?”

The first sentence offered 10,000 bitcoins for a couple of pizzas.

The conditions that followed were very specific. He wanted perhaps two large ones, because he wanted some left over for the next day. The other person could make the pizzas and bring them to his house, or order them from a delivery shop on his behalf. There was only one essential condition: he would not have to order or cook anything himself. The food just had to reach him.

He listed familiar toppings: onions, peppers, sausage, mushrooms, tomatoes, and pepperoni. Plain cheese would also be fine. He specifically added that he did not want any strange fish topping or anything like that.

This was not a manifesto.

It was someone explaining what he wanted to eat.

It is worth getting that straight at the outset, because this series will encounter a great many manifestos. White papers, roadmaps, governance proposals, foundation announcements: each says what human problem it intends to solve. Yet this system first made real contact with the world through someone writing down that he did not want fish on his pizza.

That day, someone asked which country he was in. He answered Jacksonville, Florida, in the United States, and supplied a ZIP code. Someone in Europe offered to buy him Domino’s but did not know how to pay from Europe. He replied that many pizza places accepted online orders; he and his wife sometimes used Papa John’s, and telephone orders usually accepted credit cards too.

Then came three days.

For three days, no one managed to do it.

That interval is worth remembering. Ten thousand coins was not a small amount on the forum at the time; one participant supplied an indicative valuation of $41. But having a quote and getting pizza delivered were still different things. The three-day wait shows, at least, that remote ordering, payment, and delivery still had to be joined up before coins could become dinner.

There was discussion beneath the post during those three days, but most of it was technical: how to order remotely, how to pay, whether it could be done from Europe. No one asked whether it was worth it.

At 7:06 p.m. on May 21, he returned to his thread and asked whether he was offering too few bitcoins.

A user called BitcoinFX said that, if no one else wanted to do it, he could look into buying a pizza for him online. Then he asked a practical question: was laszlo really hungry, or did he just like pizza?

At 9:33 that evening, laszlo replied that he simply thought it would be interesting to be able to say, one day, that he had paid for a pizza with bitcoins.

The twenty-one events in this series begin with that sentence.

Notice the reason he gave. He did not say it would prove anything. He did not call it a historic step or an experiment. He said it would be interesting to be able to say it.

Later, the event came to mean many things. At the time, that one sentence was what drove it.

From here onward, each essay follows the same movement: something is established as a construct within which settlement becomes possible; the construct closes at a certain point; something it cannot accommodate emerges as a remainder; and another construct takes over. In the economics series, a cycle like this often takes decades or centuries, so only its middle can be captured. This domain is different. It can complete a cycle very quickly. The first cycle ran through the four days from May 18 to May 22.

II. 1,400 Coins a Day

First, consider where those 10,000 coins came from. That determines what the transaction actually amounted to at the time.

The payment was included in block 57,043. The block explorer records a difficulty of 11.85 and a block reward of 50 BTC. There were just two transactions in the block: the new coins issued to the miner and this payment.

Working backward from a difficulty of 11.85 and the protocol’s target of one block every ten minutes gives an equivalent hash rate of roughly 84.8 MH/s.

That figure needs a qualification. It is an equilibrium value calculated from difficulty and the target interval, not a measurement taken in a particular minute. Actual block intervals fluctuated greatly in 2010. Historical hash-rate curves reconstructed later with moving windows can differ markedly from it.

During the same month, laszlo published measurements for one machine of his own.

On May 10, he reported about 1.8 MH/s from an E8600 CPU running two threads, and around 3.3–3.8 MH/s from an NVIDIA 8800 GTS using OpenCL. His best combination, one CPU thread plus one GPU, produced roughly 5 MH/s.

One development machine: 5 MH/s. The entire network’s equivalent hash rate: about 84.8 MH/s.

Put those numbers together and the smallness of the Bitcoin network in May 2010 becomes clear.

To make the scale more explicit, one person's development machine amounted to around six percent of the network's equivalent hash rate. Its owner was the person who offered 10,000 coins for pizza on May 18 and reported the completed exchange on May 22.

This matters directly to the essays that follow. Decentralization is discussed constantly here, but it is not simply a yes-or-no label: we must ask how hash power and control are distributed. In May 2010, one person's machine could amount to a substantial share of the network's equivalent hash rate. That comparison alone, however, cannot tell us how many machines were running.

His involvement with GPU mining can be dated to the day. On May 2, in a thread about the Mac version, he said he was adding OpenCL support to increase output. Early on May 10, he released a Mac binary using OpenCL, explicitly saying that it used the GPU to generate bitcoins and providing measurements. That afternoon, in a dedicated thread, he announced a working OpenCL prototype. By May 25, he said the Windows and Linux ports still had technical problems and he did not want to release an incomplete version.

In other words, at least twelve days before the pizza purchase, he had publicly run and released a version capable of using a GPU for proof of work.

In Digital Gold, Nathaniel Popper reconstructed this period from interviews with laszlo and private correspondence. When mining on a CPU, laszlo had obtained at most roughly one 50 BTC block a day. After adding a GPU, he sometimes reached one or two blocks an hour. Popper singled out May 17, 2010: 28 blocks, or 1,400 coins.

Using Popper’s figure of about 140 blocks for the entire network that day, that was roughly one-fifth of the day’s blocks. But a single day’s block production contains substantial randomness. One-fifth cannot simply be treated as an exact share of hash rate.

At that day’s output, 10,000 coins works out to about seven days.

This is arithmetic, not provenance. The available public record does not match every input in the pizza transaction to a particular block on a particular day. What can safely be said is that laszlo described the coins as ones he had mined. Three pieces of evidence support one another: his GPU development record in May, the day Popper documented, and his statement when he withdrew the offer in August that he could no longer generate thousands of coins a day.

There was also a letter during this period.

Popper published a private email attributed to Satoshi and addressed to laszlo. It expressed concern about GPU mining spreading too early. If GPU clusters took over generation too soon, newcomers would lose the incentive of obtaining a few free coins with ordinary CPUs. Satoshi therefore hoped not to accelerate the GPU arms race deliberately.

The evidentiary status of that email differs from a forum post. It is private correspondence transcribed in a later publication, not a contemporaneous public archive. It does, however, fit the public timeline of laszlo’s GPU development that May.

The concern expressed in the letter deserves a closer look.

In the second year of his protocol’s existence, its designer was worried not that it might be attacked, but that it might exclude a particular group of people too quickly.

What worried him was precisely the direct consequence of greater efficiency. GPUs made mining more efficient. That was real, genuine technical progress. At the same time, as efficiency rose, fewer people could participate.

These are not a good thing and a bad thing. They are the same thing.

This is the first remainder to appear in this domain, and it appears at the very beginning. The measure established by the construct here is computational work: those who compute more receive more new coins. It is an exceptionally clean measure. It does not ask who you are, which country you live in, or whether anyone will vouch for you. Once it starts operating, however, it pushes out those who compute less, and being pushed out registers as nothing on that measure.

That was exactly what Satoshi’s letter worried about. The form of the concern was unusual: he was not afraid the measure would fail, but that it would work too well.

This layer will return throughout the next twenty essays. When a construct causes problems, people tend to look for what has broken. Yet some of the hardest cases in this domain arise precisely when it works exactly as designed. When something functions as designed but produces an outcome its designer did not want, there is no bug to fix.

III. Two Numbers

Now consider the price.

At 7:01:50 p.m. on May 18, a user called ender_x told laszlo in the same thread that 10,000 BTC could then sell for about $41 on BitcoinMarket.com.

That worked out to roughly $0.0041 per coin.

This is the source of the most widely repeated figure. It appeared four days before the transaction, in an offhand forum comment, based on a trading market that had only just opened.

There was another number, too.

On June 12, laszlo made his offer open-ended: as long as he had coins, he would exchange 10,000 BTC for two pizzas like these. In the same thread, he gave his estimate of the food side of the exchange: about $25 for two pizzas, or around $30 with a good tip.

There are therefore two numbers in this story.

41 and 25.

One is a market valuation of what he gave away. The other is the cost of what he received.

The number people remembered was 41.

No one had to plan this. The reason 41 was remembered is that it is a number that can change, whereas 25 cannot. The changing number can become a new story every year. The unchanging number can become news only once.

The point is not that someone lost out. It is that these two quantities do not measure the same thing at all. The 41 measures the side of the measuring instrument; the 25 measures the side of the thing itself. Every recalculation over the next fifteen years recalculated the 41 side. None recalculated the 25.

One way a construct handles its remainder is already visible here, and it is the easiest way of all. It does not deny that the other side exists. It simply leaves it alone. The number 25 has remained in the original thread, available for anyone to check, yet for fifteen years almost no one has taken it out and used it.

This is also the remainder’s most common condition in this domain. It has not been hidden. It is there, public, verifiable, never destroyed. It simply has never been included in a calculation.

One more point about that market.

BitcoinMarket was not invoked retrospectively to supply this story with a price source. Its developer, dwdollar, announced plans for it on January 16, 2010. In February, people were discussing how to treat bitcoin as a commodity and establish a real-time exchange rate through actual buying and selling. On March 17, he wrote that what appeared to be the first real trade had taken place around noon.

He also described its scale: nine registered users, three who had deposited funds, and four funded users in all when he included himself.

At the first reported real trade in March, those were the four funded users. The $41 was an indicative quote a forum participant read from the same trading site two months later. March's user count cannot therefore be treated as the market's size in May.

This is not to say the number was false. Under the circumstances, it was about as close to a market figure as anyone could get. The point is different: when a measure is first established, its markings often come from somewhere very thin. Once it stands, later users stop asking where those markings were originally read.

Four funded users.

So ender_x’s statement that the coins could sell for $41 is best understood as an indicative quote he saw at the time, not an executable price at which all 10,000 could necessarily have been sold at once with no slippage. Early markets had far less trading depth, and far less complete preservation of transaction-level records, than later exchanges.

Another common claim needs to be dismantled here: the $41 was not calculated with New Liberty Standard’s electricity-cost formula.

That formula did exist, and it is interesting. It estimated monthly mining costs using annual electricity consumption of roughly 1,331.5 kWh for a heavily used computer and an average US residential electricity price of about $0.1136 per kWh. It then combined those costs with the number of coins generated over the preceding thirty days to derive a cost per coin and an exchange rate.

In essence, it was a production-cost model, not a clearing price established by matching buyers and sellers.

By February 2010, the method had already been explicitly challenged in forum discussions. Someone pointed out that generation cost and market price were different concepts, and that a real market price should be determined by buying and selling.

The pizza purchase therefore took place during a transition from cost-based pricing to prices formed through trade.

The source of the 41 in the original thread is explicit: BitcoinMarket, not electricity costs.

Two pricing methods crossed paths here, representing two entirely different routes. Calculating from electricity asks how much the thing cost to make. Calculating from the market asks how much someone is willing to pay. The first ties price to production costs. The second ties it to other people’s willingness.

This domain later took the second route, and followed it thoroughly. The first will return in other forms later in this series.

IV. The Part on IRC

At 7:17:26 p.m. on May 22, laszlo wrote in the same thread that he had successfully exchanged 10,000 bitcoins for pizza.

The next sentence thanked jercos.

A link to photographs of the pizzas followed.

That evening Sirius called it a milestone. The following day, New Liberty Standard replied that the pizzas looked good.

May 22 became the anniversary because of this follow-up reporting completion, not the May 18 post making the offer.

Jercos was later identified as Jeremy Sturdivant.

Here is a detail that is both easily overlooked and especially revealing: there is no post on the forum in which he accepts the offer.

He explained why himself. He did not have a Bitcointalk account. The transaction was actually arranged on IRC, and the chat log was not preserved.

He and laszlo did not know each other in real life. They had encountered each other on Freenode’s #bitcoin channel, and perhaps #bitcoin-dev. In his own account, there was a degree of public reputation between them.

Pause over that sentence.

A system said to require trust in no one made its first real purchase of a physical object through a chat channel where the two participants recognized each other. The record of that exchange is now gone.

Both statements must hold for the account to be accurate. The on-chain segment needed no intermediary to approve the transfer for both parties or vouch for the ledger. The 10,000 coins moved under public rules, while the record's security still depended on the network, hash power, and the validation rules participants followed. Everything before and after that segment required people too: someone to accept the order, someone to pay by card, and a shop willing to deliver.

The protocol governed the middle segment, which happened to be the part least likely to go wrong.

That division of labor was visible from the beginning. On-chain records could be checked against the protocol, while delivery, custody, promises, and responsibility needed other arrangements. Many failures later in this series did not begin with the chain calculating a number incorrectly. That does not mean implementations never fail, or that the on-chain segment is the most reliable in every circumstance.

Consider the transaction’s actual structure.

Bitcoin moved from laszlo to Sturdivant. Using a conventional payment method, Sturdivant remotely ordered and paid for pizzas online. The pizza shop delivered the food to Jacksonville.

The shop never handled bitcoin at any point.

The familiar claim that Papa John’s accepted bitcoin does not hold for this transaction. At the merchant level, the payment arrived by credit card. Nor do the available contemporaneous records show that the shop knew, when it delivered the food, that a bitcoin transaction lay behind the order.

Sturdivant later described his greatest worry at the time.

He wondered whether the Florida delivery shop would have trouble fulfilling an online order paid for with an out-of-state credit card.

That is the detail most worth remembering in this essay.

The risk people actually worried about that day was not on the chain. It was in the credit card’s fraud controls.

This will recur throughout the series, in increasingly complicated forms: the risk people fear and the risk a system is designed to prevent are often different risks. The protocol guards against double spending, tampering, and the need for a trusted third party. On the evening of May 22, 2010, however, the one thing that might have stopped the transaction was whether a pizza shop’s payment system would accept an out-of-state card.

A sentence can be set down here for repeated use later: a system’s real boundary is not in its code, but at the places where it has to deal with other systems.

The on-chain side was straightforward. Block 57,043 contained only two transactions, with total fees of 0.99 BTC. Since it contained just this one ordinary transaction, one can infer that the transfer had approximately 10,000.99 coins in inputs, 10,000 in outputs, and a 0.99-coin fee.

That is an on-chain inference. The 0.99 coins cannot be described as pizza money received by Sturdivant.

For a transfer of 10,000 coins, laszlo paid a fee of 0.99 coins.

On the $41 valuation, that was less than half a cent. On a 2025 valuation, the same 0.99 coins is another number.

The fee column would later become one of the most important things in this domain. That day, it was a negligible rounding remainder. More than a decade later, it would determine who could transact on-chain, who could not, and whether a transfer was worth sending. The toll a construct charges is always very cheap when it first arises, so cheap that no one treats it as a separate column.

Finally, trace both ends.

At laszlo’s end: 10,000 fewer coins, two more large pizzas.

At Sturdivant’s end: 10,000 more coins, about $25 less, plus the inconvenience of ordering online for someone else and a little concern about fraud controls.

Both thought the exchange worthwhile at the time. Years later, both still said they did not regret it.

This needs to be precise, because the series will encounter many cases in which the two ends are unequal. This was not one of them. Risk and benefit fell on the same person: laszlo bore the risk of giving away 10,000 coins and received the dinner he wanted; Sturdivant bore the card-payment and fraud-control risks and received the coins. Both knew what they were exchanging. Both acted voluntarily.

Few transactions in the next twenty essays will be this clean.

The reason it was clean also needs to be clear. It was not that both people were virtuous. It was that the transaction was small and simple, each knew who was on the other side, and neither was bearing someone else’s risk.

Tracing both ends is effortless here, because the two ends consist of just two people. Both are still around, both have been interviewed, and both have explained what they were thinking. In the next twenty essays, the ends are often separated by hundreds of thousands of people and several layers of protocol. They still have to be traced; the effort required changes. Once even one of those three conditions ceases to hold, the later stories begin.

The next essay concerns the first condition breaking down: mining grows from one person’s machine into an industry’s buildings full of equipment.

V. He Later Spent Them

Sturdivant was not an outsider who happened to see a news story.

He had followed Bitcoin through IRC since late 2009 and had mined several thousand coins with a CPU miner on Linode. Through mining and trading, he had at one point held nearly 40,000 coins.

He later said that the transaction had seemed much like the smaller ones that followed: an ordinary exchange of money for goods or services. He recognized it as a visible precedent for using bitcoin in everyday trade, but believed that someone else would probably have accepted the offer if he had not.

Asked again in 2018, he put it more directly. He had never regarded bitcoin primarily as an investment. If his aim at the time had been to hoard coins, he might not have been in the right place at the right moment to complete the transaction.

He spent the 10,000 coins fairly soon.

In an interview with Bitcoin Who’s Who, he said they quickly returned to the economy, at roughly the point when they were worth around $400 in total. He used bitcoin like money in a checking account, rather than a long-term investment.

Cointelegraph’s 2018 interview says the coins were worth a few hundred dollars when he converted them into fiat currency for travel. Later media accounts described a road trip with his girlfriend.

These claims need separate labels. That he spent the coins relatively quickly is something he repeatedly confirmed himself. The figures $400 and “a few hundred dollars” are compatible, but not identical. The road-trip detail spread mainly through later reporting.

Now consider which pizza chain it was.

This is a rare point in the story where a participant’s memory directly conflicts with physical evidence.

The May 22 post linked to photographs. Preserved and republished copies are generally identified as showing Papa John’s. When discussing remote payment on May 18, laszlo had indeed mentioned Papa John’s as an example.

Yet when Sturdivant was specifically asked about the discrepancy years later, he said he regretted not keeping the chat logs. He clearly remembered Domino’s, he said, although the photographs showed Papa John’s.

The photographs support Papa John’s. His personal recollection is Domino’s. Historically, these cannot be collapsed into one fact.

The conflict is useful in itself. One of this domain’s most frequently heard claims is that everything on-chain can be checked. Within its scope, that is true: anyone can trace those 10,000 coins today, down to the satoshi. But the chain says nothing about which shop supplied the pizzas. For that, there is only a photograph and someone’s memory, and they disagree.

The part that can be checked and the part that matters are not always the same part.

Papa John’s certainly found out later. In a 2020 interview, laszlo said the company had subsequently given him store credit, a commemorative gold-colored pepper, and some small gifts, allowing him to keep ordering free pizza for a while. The company also publicly commemorated the transaction on social media in later years.

Those facts establish only that the company later incorporated the event into its brand history. They do not establish that the store knew about it on May 22, 2010.

Laszlo’s own descriptions changed over time, but not from regret to an absence of regret.

In 2013, he told a newspaper that bitcoin had almost no practical value then, so being able to exchange it for pizza was itself particularly cool. At that stage, his emphasis was the value environment of 2010.

In 2018, a reporter asked directly whether he regretted spending coins now worth about $80 million. He began by saying he did not. He then said he was pleased to be part of Bitcoin’s early history. At the time, he had been working on the Mac port, providing technical support, and writing other code. He had always wanted people actually to use bitcoin; buying pizza was one way to do that.

In 2019, he offered another explanation that would be quoted repeatedly. He had thought of it as free pizza. He wrote code and mined coins. An open-source hobby usually consumed time and money. This time, his hobby had bought him dinner.

In 2020, his account became more precise. He acknowledged that, of course, he wished he still had the 10,000 coins, while remaining glad he had taken part in that history.

Wishing he still had the coins today and repudiating the transaction he made then were never the same proposition in his account.

Keeping those propositions apart is essential to reading this history. Conflate them, and the story has only one lesson: if only he had known, he should not have spent them. In his own account, however, what he wanted was to make the system work for someone once, and he achieved that. What those coins were worth fifteen years later belonged to a different ledger.

Both ledgers are real, but they measure different things. One records what he wanted to accomplish that day. The other records what those same coins could later buy in other people’s hands. Judging a decision in the first ledger retrospectively by numbers from the second is one of this domain’s most common misreadings. It will recur in many forms later in the series.

VI. Recalculated Every Year

The story developed a remarkably stable pattern of circulation. Whenever the price reached a new level, someone multiplied 10,000 by the current price.

Those figures need not contradict one another. They often use different dates, exchanges, and sampling times. What must be avoided is rewriting a later revaluation as the dollar amount actually paid in 2010.

This is not pedantry. Projecting a later price back into 2010 amounts to saying he paid an enormous present-day sum that day. He did not. Using the single day's output above as an illustration, 10,000 coins amounted to roughly seven days of machine production—not proof that the payment inputs actually came from those seven days. What the coins could buy then cannot be answered by a later quotation either.

The multiplication also assumes those 10,000 coins never moved. In fact, they moved very early: Sturdivant spent them when their total value was only a few hundred dollars. The annual recalculation therefore values a holding that no longer exists.

Recalculation began early. Around June 4, 2011, users in the original pizza thread were already updating the value in real time. One calculated about $140,000; another soon wrote $170,000 in the same thread.

The same day, the same thread, two numbers.

When those numbers appeared, only a year and two weeks had passed since the exchange. Recalculation began almost immediately, rather than being rediscovered in some later bull market. From its second year onward, the equation has been continually recomputed.

Later figures included roughly $1.27 million on May 22, 2013; about $5.09 million in 2014; $20,509,958 in a bot’s report on May 22, 2017; roughly $365 million in May 2021 reporting; and about $1.1 billion in reports marking the fifteenth anniversary on May 22, 2025.

The name Pizza Day did not exist on the day of the transaction.

One very early explicit usage located in this research dates to July 4, 2011. In a thread discussing which holidays Bitcoin should have, a user called ribuck listed May 22 as a possible celebration alongside the genesis-block anniversary. Another user immediately said he liked the idea of a big pizza party.

In October and November 2012, people in the original pizza thread explicitly proposed making it a Bitcoin holiday, then discussed whether to call it Bitcoin Pizza Day or Laszlo’s Pizza Day. By May 21, 2014, participants in the same thread were already treating the following day as the established Bitcoin Pizza Day.

The available forum record therefore suggests an anniversary gradually consolidated by the community between 2011 and 2014. There was no proclamation, no formal organization, and no founder who can be identified without dispute.

Tools later automated the process. A 2018 report interviewed Merijn Bellekom, creator of the Bitcoin Pizza bot. He said he had written it as a joke in January 2015. Each day it collected prices from several exchanges, averaged them, and published the day’s pizza value for 10,000 coins.

Thus the familiar statement about what “that pizza” is worth today has its own history of circulation, separate from the original transaction.

A machine posting automatically once a day turned a one-time exchange into a continuing quotation. The quoted price no longer had any concrete connection to the two pizzas of 2010. Its only connection was a multiplier and the day’s market price.

Taken together, these facts reveal something.

An equation held once, on the evening of May 22, 2010. Over the next fifteen years, it was recalculated countless times.

It was always the same side that was recalculated.

Not once in those fifteen years did a recalculation ask how many people those two pizzas fed that evening.

There is no need to elaborate. He had already written what he wanted on May 18: perhaps two large pizzas, so that there would be some left for the next day.

One adult and his wife, a dinner, and leftovers the next day. That is the entire right-hand side of the equation. It did not change for fifteen years.

The left-hand side did not remain the same number for a single day.

VII. Four Kinds of First

Many claims about this transaction do not survive comparison with the primary record. They are set out together because the next twenty essays address events far more complicated than this one.

The first category is dates. Many accounts say he posted the pizza offer on May 22. The original offer was made on May 18. May 22 was when he reported completion.

The second is how the deal was made. A common account says jercos replied on the forum and accepted. No such post appears in the original thread. He says the deal took place on IRC and that he did not have a forum account.

The third is money. Saying that the pizzas themselves cost $41 confuses measurement bases. The $41 was someone’s May 18 market valuation of 10,000 coins using BitcoinMarket. Laszlo’s own June estimate for the food was about $25, or $30 with a good tip. The available record contains no surviving store receipt from 2010.

The fourth is the merchant. “Papa John’s accepted bitcoin” does not describe the transaction’s structure. Nor can one say it was certainly Papa John’s and both participants remember it that way: Sturdivant explicitly remembers Domino’s.

The fifth is the people. Some media described Sturdivant as British, although he has said he had never left the United States. Some versions say he delivered the pizzas personally, which conflicts with the distance between them and his own account. Other versions say he kept all 10,000 coins. That is the claim he has denied most directly.

The sixth is priority. Laszlo describes himself as having written the first GPU miner, and his publicly verifiable OpenCL measurements of May 10 are indeed very early. Some later mining histories call ArtForz the first GPU miner, or more specifically the person who established an early GPU farm in July 2010. Those accounts are in chronological tension. The safer formulation is that laszlo left a directly verifiable public record of a GPU prototype earlier than the July account. Later sources do not agree on whether the first person privately running GPU hashing code, the first GPU farm, and the first mature publicly available GPU miner were the same thing.

The seventh is the total. The claim that he spent about 100,000 coins on pizza that summer is a rough figure given years later. The original thread confirms the first 10,000 and subsequent pizza purchases at the same price. On August 4, he announced a pause because he could no longer generate thousands of coins a day as he once had.

The reason for that pause is a clue. He stopped not because he thought the coins had become expensive, but because he was mining fewer. For him, the 10,000 was still calculated in output, not market price.

The word “first” deserves separate treatment.

The underlying point of this section is quite specific. This event was only fifteen years ago. It was publicly documented throughout; most of its principal participants are still alive; original posts can be found for nearly every step. Even so, it has accumulated this many conflicting accounts.

Every event in the next twenty essays is more complicated.

The complications run in several directions. Two participants become hundreds of thousands. Ten thousand coins becomes billions of dollars. One transfer becomes thousands of lines of contract code. One forum thread becomes hundreds of protocols across dozens of chains. The one thing that does not become more complicated is the question: who bore the risk, who received the benefit, and were they the same person?

There are at least four different meanings of “first,” and they are not interchangeable.

The first on-chain person-to-person transfer came earlier: on January 12, 2009, Satoshi sent Hal Finney 10 BTC. That was a test transfer, not a purchase.

The first bitcoin-for-fiat exchange also came earlier: on October 12, 2009, Martti Malmi and New Liberty Standard exchanged about 5,050 coins for $5.02. That was currency exchange, not a purchase of physical goods.

Attempts at commercialization also preceded the pizzas. On January 24, 2010, a forum user called Sabunir offered an image worth about $1 for around 500 coins. That was an offer of a digital good; the materials retrieved here do not establish that it was completed. An auction attempt in March 2010 offered 10,000 coins for $50. Later accounts record no buyer.

The four categories are therefore the first on-chain interpersonal transfer, the first exchange for fiat currency, the first attempt involving digital goods or services, and the first publicly documented completed exchange for physical goods.

Among the early forum materials located in this research, no earlier case was found that met all four conditions before May 22, 2010: completed, settled in bitcoin, involving physical goods, and supported by verifiable contemporaneous records.

That statement describes only the evidentiary condition of surviving, searchable records. It cannot prove that early participants never made private exchanges for physical goods that went unreported or whose records were lost.

The loss of Sturdivant’s IRC log itself illustrates the substantial gaps in record preservation from that period.

This is an easily overestimated feature of the domain. Everything on-chain is public: that is true. But the chain records transfers, not negotiations. Who said what to whom, why they agreed, what they feared at the time: none of that goes on-chain. Block 57,043 remains forever. That IRC conversation is gone forever.

A construct records the portion it can define. The portion it cannot define does not get preserved simply because the chain is tamper-resistant.

This will repeatedly determine how far the later essays can go. What went on-chain can be traced to the satoshi. What did not often survives only as someone’s recollection years later, and memories can be wrong.

VIII. It Was Always Two Pizzas

Return to the equation.

10,000 coins for two pizzas. On the evening of May 22, 2010, that equation took the form of a specific completed exchange.

It did not depend on a mature market. The trading site had been announced four months earlier; when it first reported a real trade in March, it had nine registered users and four with funds deposited. In the pizza thread, an indicative quote was still an offhand comment. A separate electricity-cost pricing method had already been challenged months earlier.

It held because two people recognized each other in the same chat channel. One wanted pizza without having to order it himself. The other was willing to put it on a card for him.

Both later expressed the same thought: someone else could have done it. Laszlo said he simply wanted it to happen once. Sturdivant said someone else would probably have accepted if he had not.

Both said this lightly, but it was the first sign that the system was useful: whether something could be done no longer had to depend on precisely which two people were involved. That day, though, it still required these particular two people.

The thought is worth remembering because it lies at the heart of the system’s entire promise: whether you can accomplish something should not depend on whom you happen to know. That promise was later fulfilled many times and broken many times. When it was broken, the failure often looked much like this day in 2010.

What the white paper had proposed was direct payment between two willing parties, without relying on a trusted financial intermediary to process it.

The first time it actually bought something, it depended on two people recognizing each other.

That was not a design failure.

The protocol segment operated exactly as designed. Block 57,043, two transactions, a 0.99-coin fee: anyone can inspect it today, and no one has altered it in fifteen years.

The reason was that several steps still separated the part governed by the protocol from a person’s wish to eat pizza.

Someone had to accept. Someone had to know you. Someone had to be willing to pay by card for you. A shop had to agree to deliver. An out-of-state card had to pass the fraud controls.

The protocol governed none of those five steps. It governed how the 10,000 coins moved from one address to another once those steps were arranged, and that segment never went wrong.

Every one of the other steps required someone.

Most of the next twenty essays take place in those steps.

Looking ahead, they will be automated one by one: exchanges convert your money, wallets keep it for you, oracles supply prices, contracts liquidate positions, bridges move assets between chains. Each automation really does remove a person from one step. Each also creates a new position somewhere else, and that position still needs a person.

What this series follows is where that position moves each time.

Look once more at the two numbers.

The 41 side was recalculated countless times over fifteen years: $140,000, $170,000, $1.27 million, $5.09 million, $20.5 million, $365 million, $1.1 billion. Every year supplied a new number, and every year it became news again.

The 25 side was never recalculated.

It was always two pizzas. Enough for dinner, with some left for the next day.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
