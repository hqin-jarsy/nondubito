1. Three Axes

Almost every debate about Layer 2 networks comes down to three questions. None can stand in for either of the others.

This distinction has appeared many times in the preceding eighteen episodes. What is distinctive here is that each axis contains concrete arrangements that can be checked separately, while an answer on one axis cannot guarantee the answers on the other two.

In earlier episodes, what was hard to check was often what mattered most: one person's loss, employment in a county, or who stood behind a transfer. Here, contracts, parameters, and some fee flows really are on-chain. Actual key controllers, legal rights, and net business profits still require off-chain evidence. The three axes can be examined separately without everything on them being completely public.

The difficult part is not looking them up. It is avoiding treating three answers as one.

Treating three answers as one requires no deliberate attempt to mislead. All it takes is a convenient term.

What guarantees the correctness of the state?

Who actually controls the system?

Who ultimately receives the fees and cash flows?

The distinction matters because a chain can perform very well on the first axis while presenting an entirely different picture on the other two.

It can use validity proofs while retaining an upgrade key that takes effect immediately.

It can allow users to force transactions into the system while leaving everyday sequencing to a single operator.

It can generate substantial chain-level profits without giving its token holders any legal or contractual entitlement to those returns.

The term “L2” packages the answers on all three axes into a single label.

Hence one of the most common sentences in this field: this chain is an Ethereum Layer 2, so it inherits Ethereum's security.

The first half can be checked. The second is not a verifiable proposition. It is an average of three different answers on three different axes, and that average has no definition.

The next seven sections therefore have a very specific task: separate that average back into three numbers.

Once separated, they reveal something: different chains combine these three numbers in entirely different ways.

Some have the most robust proofs but leave the upgrade key in the hands of a multisig. Some have a working escape route while everyday sequencing still runs on one machine. Some have the highest profits without having a token at all.

Putting them side by side in a single ranking, ordered by one score, erases these differences.

Rankings themselves are useful. The independent organization that produces this one explicitly states in its methodology that its stage classification is a subjective assessment of maturity, not a rating of a project's overall security.

That qualification appears on its homepage. Most people who cite it quote only the stage number.

This is something the series encounters repeatedly: a number is created with a full set of qualifications and loses them as it circulates.

It happened with the reduction in energy consumption given to two decimal places in the previous essay, and with the 120% in the essay before that.

The remainder begins here.

It is not confined to one axis. Contracts, thresholds, and some money flows can be inspected item by item; actual control and legal arrangements require further checking. The easily missed move is compressing these different questions into a single label.

It lies in the gaps between the three axes.

This time the remainder is not information left out, but distinctions collapsed into one.

And collapsing them costs nothing. Making distinctions takes effort; merging them does not.

2. Four Kinds of Finality

A problem of definition runs through this entire subject: “finality” has at least four different meanings.

A preconfirmation issued by the sequencer.

Transaction data having reached Ethereum.

An L2 state root no longer being open to challenge.

And a withdrawal from L2 to L1 becoming executable.

Calling all of these “finality” immediately creates an apparent contradiction: if it is already on Ethereum, why wait another seven days?

There is no real contradiction. Publishing sufficient data and confirming a state claim are different things. The former gives others the material to reconstruct and check; the latter depends on the relevant proof or dispute mechanism. For an optimistic system, reaching the end of the challenge period without an unresolved valid challenge still depends on data availability, the proof system, and challengers. Elapsed time does not prove that no error could exist.

The first can ordinarily happen on a timescale of minutes. Under the optimistic route discussed here, the second must also pass through the relevant dispute process.

During that window, the transaction has not become uncertain. What remains uncertain is only this: if someone proves that the batch's state transition was calculated incorrectly, the contents of that batch must be processed again.

The distinction has practical consequences for users. Someone buys something on L2 and receives it. A few minutes later, the transaction data reach Ethereum, where anyone can inspect them.

But if that person wants to withdraw money to L1, what they must wait for is something else.

In this field, the same word covers both, though the counterparties bearing on the risk differ. In the first case, the counterparty is the seller. In the second, it is the chain's proof system and upgrade authority.

Users do not necessarily take that second route. Some bring funds in, use them on L2, and leave through a third-party bridge rather than the canonical bridge. The speed gained often relies on a different set of capital and verification arrangements.

For someone choosing that faster route, the much-discussed dispute window is not the waiting period they directly experience on that transfer.

The route they do take places trust elsewhere: in a bridge operator or a solver. Episode 15 described what can happen along that route.

The protocol specifications themselves make the distinctions. The OP Stack specification distinguishes three chain heads: one produced by the sequencer but not yet published on L1 is “unsafe”; once its data enter the canonical L1 chain, it is “safe”; subsequently, it becomes “finalized” as Ethereum reaches finality.

Arbitrum likewise distinguishes the soft confirmations in the sequencer's live feed from the state subsequently resolved through disputes on L1.

It is therefore best to describe a transaction's irreversibility in three layers.

The first is the sequencer's preconfirmation: the sequencer promises that it has ordered and executed the transaction. This depends on the sequencer not reordering transactions or failing and is a soft confirmation.

The second is data finality on L1: the original L2 inputs have entered Ethereum's finalized history. The correctness of the state transition still needs to be established.

The third is state or withdrawal finalization: the fraud-proof window has ended normally, or a dispute has been adjudicated. This depends on the proof system, upgrade authority, and Ethereum's own finality.

This breakdown follows the actual processes of the two protocols. L2 finality is not a single duration.

The green check mark a user sees in a wallet usually corresponds to the first layer.

The second layer often takes minutes; the third also depends on proof and dispute procedures. Interfaces differ in how they show those states. A green check mark alone cannot answer separately for all three layers.

This is a real interface-design problem: how to keep everyday use simple while explaining whether the user sees a sequencer confirmation, finalized L1 data, or an executable withdrawal. A simpler display should not turn different promises into one promise.

If an interface emphasizes the first layer's soft confirmation, that layer still depends on the sequencer's operation and promises. The sequencer may stop, reorder transactions, or fail.

3. Accept First, Challenge Later

The core of the optimistic approach is not to dispense with proofs altogether.

It is to accept a state transition first, then give other participants a window in which to prove it wrong.

Transaction data must still be published to Ethereum so that others can reconstruct the state, identify errors, and mount a challenge.

Fraud proofs therefore cannot replace data availability.

This is worth remembering because it is the aspect of this approach most easily explained backward.

“Accept first, challenge later” sounds as though verification has been eliminated. What it removes is only verification in advance. To make a challenge possible afterward, the original data must instead be fully disclosed.

In other words, the more you rely on others to correct errors, the more fully you must lay out the material for them.

This also shapes the cost structure: data need L1 availability guarantees, making data publication one of the major costs. EIP-4844 in the March 2024 Dencun upgrade introduced the blob data market and reduced this component. That was not the 2022 Merge discussed in Episode 18.

What became cheaper here was primarily the data-publication leg. That cannot simply be restated as every step of virtual-machine execution becoming cheaper as a result.

For an ordinary transaction, the path is as follows: the user signs; the sequencer receives and orders it; local execution produces a soft confirmation; the batcher publishes compressed transaction data to Ethereum; L2 nodes rederive the chain from data that have reached L1, moving the transaction from unsafe to safe; a state root or dispute game is submitted to L1; it can be challenged during the dispute window; only after that window ends and the relevant L1 block is finalized does the state commitment reach the final state permitted by the protocol.

Another distinction is needed here.

Data entering finalized L1 history does not mean the dispute period for a particular state root has ended.

For an ordinary transfer, a user may obtain sufficiently strong economic confirmation quite early. But if the goal is to withdraw assets from L2 to L1 through the canonical bridge, the protocol generally does not allow L1 assets to be released before the dispute period ends.

Arbitrum's documentation gives an assertion challenge period of approximately 6.4 days, usually appearing as an exit wait of about seven days through the canonical bridge. The standard OP Stack withdrawal process has likewise long used a challenge window of roughly seven days.

Different chains and newer proof systems can change the specific parameters.

Seven days therefore cannot be treated as a mathematical constant of this approach.

This matters in practice because seven days has become a label. Someone acting on the label might wait seven days when they could have withdrawn three days earlier. Or they might assume every chain uses seven days when the actual window on theirs is longer.

The real answer is in the contract. It varies across chains and across periods in the life of the same chain.

This is a recurring way to read this field: the slogans remain stable while the parameters keep moving.

Base illustrates precisely why current contracts need to be checked rather than inherited slogans repeated.

A dispute type it activated in 2026 accepts two different types of proof: with only one proof path, the adjudication window is five days; submitting both can shorten it to one day.

This is still a specific implementation parameter of that chain's current dispute system. It does not establish that all chains sharing the architecture have moved to five days or one day.

Users who bypass the sequencer are not necessarily left without a route.

OP Stack's forced-transaction mechanism actually uses the L1 deposit path. A user submits a deposit transaction directly to the portal contract on Ethereum, and L2 derivation rules must incorporate it into the subsequent chain based on the L1 inputs.

The forced path thus depends on L1 availability and derivation rules, not on whether the sequencer is willing to accept a transaction into its private mempool.

This comes closest to a promise anywhere in the essay. It depends on no company's goodwill, only on Ethereum continuing to produce blocks and the chain's nodes continuing to derive the chain according to the rules.

Its cost is waiting: transactions taking this route must queue for the delay fixed in the contract.

Arbitrum’s route is more explicit. Transactions normally enter the sequencer inbox; bypass messages can enter the delayed inbox on Ethereum. One configuration document cited here lists 86,400 seconds and 5,760 blocks. Delay parameters and decision rules vary by deployment version; actual force-inclusion conditions depend on the relevant on-chain contracts and documentation date.

Official documentation also describes a censorship-timeout mechanism that can shorten the wait when persistent problems are detected. Under the cited normal configuration and conditions such as L1 availability, roughly twenty-four hours is an important timescale for this force-inclusion path, not a guarantee that withdrawals finish within that period under every failure scenario.

Read this together with Section 5: the sequencer's delay is not unlimited, because the rules put a clock on the bypass route. Completing that route still depends on L1, derivation rules, and subsequent processes actually operating.

That is the distinction between the first and second axes in actual parameters. The control is real, and so are the bypass conditions written into the contract. Assessing the exit protection they give users requires reading those conditions together with their dependencies.

4. Prove First, Accept Later

The validity approach replaces waiting for someone to discover an error with first producing a validity proof that an Ethereum contract can verify, then accepting the corresponding state update.

Here, too, a condition is often simplified away.

A zero-knowledge proof establishes computational correctness. It does not automatically establish that users can recover the data needed to reconstruct the state.

Where this distinction falls matters.

A proof can tell you that, given these inputs, those outputs follow, and that the computation did not cheat. It does not tell you what those inputs actually were or guarantee that you can still obtain them.

If the data needed for reconstruction are unavailable, a state proof may still verify, while users lacking those materials cannot reconstruct their balances and exit paths from that proof alone. Proving a computation correct and making the required data available to users remain different things.

A system genuinely operating as a rollup therefore still needs sufficient data availability on Ethereum. Zero knowledge does not mean the data may not exist, and a validity proof does not necessarily hide every transaction detail. The particular system may publish transactions, state differences, or other material sufficient for reconstruction.

The two routes share this requirement: users need access to enough data to check and reconstruct state and use exit paths. The published form can differ; it cannot always be described as every detail of the raw transactions.

They differ only in how they establish that the state transition was calculated correctly. One waits for a rebuttal; the other computes a proof first.

One waits through a fixed window. The other waits for a machine to finish computing.

The fixed window is written into a contract for anyone to inspect. How fast the machine computes depends on its load that day and the condition of the company operating it.

A system genuinely operating in rollup mode therefore still needs sufficient data availability on Ethereum.

A typical transaction roughly follows this path: the user submits it; the sequencer orders and executes it; L2 issues a preconfirmation; batch and state data are published to the data-availability layer; the prover generates a validity proof; the verifier contract on Ethereum checks it; the L1 state contract accepts the new state root; and the Ethereum block containing that update is finalized.

The greatest difference from the previous approach is that this final part has no fixed dispute window specifically reserved for potential fraud challenges.

The wait instead depends on when batching and proof generation finish, when the proof is submitted to Ethereum, and when it passes verification and reaches Ethereum finality.

One observation covering late July through late August 2026 found an average proof-submission interval of about twenty-nine minutes on one chain, with state updates averaging about twenty-eight minutes.

That is an observation of a production system, not a service commitment guaranteeing withdrawals in twenty-nine minutes.

The distinction emerges on the day something goes wrong: an observed value can change, whereas a commitment cannot. Here, there is no commitment.

This has appeared several times in the series. In Episode 8, the redemption threshold was written into the terms. In Episode 11, the ten minutes were written into a contract. This time, the twenty-nine minutes appear only on a dashboard.

What the material supports about which approach offers faster withdrawals should therefore be stated as follows.

Canonical withdrawals under the first approach generally must wait for the dispute mechanism to reach an executable state. The second does not require waiting through a fraud-challenge period, but actual withdrawal latency still depends on proof aggregation, proof submission, state updates, L1 congestion, and the bridge contract's process.

The absence of a seven-day challenge period does not imply instant withdrawals.

The difference between the two approaches is not speed, but what they wait for.

One waits for no one to refute the claim. The other waits for someone to compute and submit a proof. The first duration is written into a contract; the second depends on how fast the machine runs that day.

5. Three Machines and One Company

What the sequencer controls most directly is the short-term entry and ordering of transactions.

It decides when the transactions it receives enter L2 blocks, their relative order, and whether to defer them.

If a chain has no effective L1 bypass, it can extend deferral into prolonged censorship. If force inclusion exists, it can generally delay only within the force-inclusion clock, not permanently prevent the transaction.

Ordering power also creates extractable value. The independent rating organization explicitly identifies the ability of centralized operators to use their position to front-run transactions as a risk on several leading chains.

A sequencer generally cannot, however, use ordering power alone to conjure an invalid withdrawal that passes the final proof system.

Under the validity approach, an incorrect state should not be able to generate a valid proof. Under a mature fraud-proof system, an incorrect state can be challenged.

What truly changes that boundary is the upgrade key.

If the entity holding the upgrade key can immediately replace the verifier contract, bridge contract, or proof logic, the protection that a sequencer cannot falsify state is entirely different from a protection that upgrade administrators cannot change the rules.

The first statement concerns who can do what under the current rules. The second concerns who can change the rules.

The fork in Episode 6 concerned that second question. It involved public discussion and choices among participants. Here, one must inspect each governance council, multisig, and timelock. Some powers can be exercised quickly; others leave users a waiting and exit window.

Most security narratives in this field concern the first statement.

That is because the first can be formalized, proven, and written into a paper. The second can only be listed: which addresses, how many signatures, whether there is a timelock, and who sits on the committee.

A list of such particulars cannot be compressed into a score.

This produces a characteristic imbalance in this field: the proven part is promoted repeatedly, while the enumerated part appears only on a page most people will never open.

As of late August 2026, once the number of machines is separated from the number of independent governing entities controlling them, the principal networks look roughly as follows.

Arbitrum One still relies on a single sequencer operator for everyday operation, while users also have the delayed inbox. Official documentation describes decentralized sequencing as a long-term direction. The current production path is not a permissionless sequencer set.

OP Mainnet uses a single sequencer to produce soft confirmations, and the documentation explicitly warns that those confirmations depend on that sequencer. L1 deposits provide a bypass.

Base has a single sequencer, which can be changed by a multisig through the system configuration. An L1 self-sequencing path is available during failures, with current parameters giving a maximum wait of about twelve hours.

ZKsync Era's block and state proposers remain permissioned. Not just anyone can replace the normal operator.

Scroll has a centralized operator, while users can self-sequence through L1 with a maximum delay of about seven days. Submitting new blocks still requires producing a proof.

Linea remains controlled by permissioned operators and currently has no working sequencer-failure escape route available to ordinary users.

Starknet is the example in this section that most needs a precisely worded description.

It now has not one sequencer but three running consensus. The 2025 upgrade replaced a single node with Byzantine-fault-tolerant-style consensus among three sequencers, which independently take turns producing blocks and require two-thirds to confirm.

The project itself explicitly explains, however, that because all three are operated and controlled by the same company, the protocol at that point was designed only for a failure model involving crashes and network delays, not a full Byzantine failure model.

The qualification comes from the project itself, and it is candid.

Designing for crash failures means this consensus protects against a machine going down or a network disconnecting, not against all three machines lying together. When all three belong to one company, lying together technically requires no collusion.

The 2026 roadmap continues to place permissionless validation and more complete decentralization in later phases.

This is therefore distribution at the level of processes and machines, not permissionless sequencing among independent organizations.

Three sequencers and three independent operators are not interchangeable counts.

This has the same shape as the five measuring sticks in Episode 2: something that can be counted precisely is not measuring what people think it measures.

Then, the count concerned mining-pool shares and concealed who owned the machines behind them. Here, it concerns sequencer machines and conceals who owns the company behind them.

In both cases, the number itself is entirely accurate. It simply does not answer the question people mean to ask.

6. One Hundred and Sixteen Minutes

Outages are not a theoretical risk. Each incident below has first-party incident material from the project itself. They are not presented as a comprehensive incident database.

On September 14, 2021, Arbitrum's sequencer was offline for about forty-five minutes. The network was still at a very early stage.

On December 15, 2023, the same chain's official status page recorded an incident lasting about eighty-eight minutes. A surge in inscription-style transactions caused failures and backlogs in the sequencer's feed and processing pipeline, interrupting normal sequencing.

On June 25, 2026, Base completely stopped producing new L2 blocks for 116 minutes. Its mempool grew until it eventually rejected new transaction submissions. The root cause was a defect in the journaled state used for block construction.

The next day, a second outage related to the same root cause lasted twenty minutes.

On September 2, 2025, Starknet's official announcement reported about nine hours of degraded or halted operation, with intermittent unavailability between 02:20 and 13:40. Two reorganizations occurred, rolling back approximately one hour plus twenty minutes of block activity. The summary described roughly an hour and a half of activity as unprocessed, with transactions requiring resubmission.

The public material on these five incidents reported neither resulting asset theft nor acceptance of an incorrect state by the final proof system. That common point matters, but it does not mean users suffered no harm from waiting, rollbacks, or missed transactions.

This deserves to be stated first, because it fixes the nature of this section. What follows concerns stoppages, not theft.

According to those incident reports, no one used these failures to steal funds and the final proof systems did not accept incorrect states because of them. Each nevertheless interrupted ordinary L2 operation, leaving users unable to submit or complete transactions as usual.

Two hours, an hour and a half, nine hours. For a chain marketed on continuous availability, these are not small numbers.

Nor is this confined to early experiments. Four of the five listed incidents occurred in 2023 or later, when these chains carried real funds and activity. The list establishes that the risk occurred; without a complete observation period and sample, it cannot establish its probability.

Traditional finance has a whole set of institutional arrangements for outages: service-level agreements, regulatory availability requirements, and post-incident compensation arrangements.

The incident materials examined here mainly provide technical explanations and remediation plans, without a corresponding itemized promise of user compensation. Whether particular services have separate SLAs or availability duties depends on contracts, entities, and jurisdictions. Their absence from an incident announcement does not prove all three arrangements are absent across the field.

The reports are often of high quality, clearly explaining causes, timelines, fixes, and follow-up plans. Technical explanation and user remedies are nevertheless different questions. Detail in the former does not automatically answer the latter.

The Base incident demonstrates one point particularly well: L1 security and L2 availability are different metrics.

The project said the integrity of the chain's assets was not compromised.

Yet no new L2 blocks were produced for 116 minutes, and ordinary submissions were eventually rejected.

In other words, proof and settlement security can persist while access to the everyday execution layer deteriorates substantially.

The Starknet incident shows that multiple sequencers do not guarantee freedom from outages either.

The three machines disagreed about the state observed through the Ethereum interface. Subsequent manual operations also produced conflicting blocks. The proof layer detected the inconsistency and prevented it from entering the final valid state, while the system performed two reorganizations to restore the correct chain.

Two things must be retained together here.

On one side, the correctness backstop worked: the proof system did not accept an incorrect state.

On the other, L2 activity users had already seen was still rolled back.

For someone who submitted a transaction during that hour and a half and saw it marked successful in the interface, the latter was what they actually experienced.

In the language of this field, both are usually covered by the same word: security.

The first is final-state security; the second is availability in everyday use. They are different metrics. Proofs and settlement can be anchored to Ethereum while daily sequencing still depends on L2 operations, with separate waiting conditions for bypass and recovery paths.

Restoring normal service during those 116 minutes depended in practice on the operating company's diagnosis and repair. That does not establish that outside help was impossible in principle. It identifies the layer on which ordinary use then depended.

7. Who Captures the Spread?

Operators receive three different things. They cannot all be called profit.

The first is the fee spread: users' execution and sequencing fees minus the settlement rent paid to Ethereum. This layer can be measured on-chain.

The second is the right to ordering and extractable value. In deciding transaction order, a centralized sequencer inherently controls an ordering resource that can be monetized. Arbitrum has already explicitly auctioned and monetized part of this right, and governance materials list this revenue alongside sequencer revenue.

The third is control over the protocol. This is not accounting revenue, but it has economic value. A multisig able to change the sequencer, fee parameters, verifier contract, or bridge holds platform-governance power.

These three things are entirely different in nature. The first can be booked. The second can be auctioned. The third appears on no income statement, yet determines whether the first two can be changed.

Base's sequencer configuration remains controlled by a multisig, while core upgrades require thresholds to be met in both of two councils. OP Mainnet upgrades require joint signatures from the Foundation and the Security Council.

Claims concerning the validator side need to be narrowed considerably.

EIP-4844 did shift substantial data-availability demand from ordinary execution data into a separate fee market. Official materials also explicitly anticipated a reduction in the cost of using Ethereum for data.

But all the money L2s previously paid to Ethereum is not the same as the revenue validators previously received.

After EIP-1559, base fees were already a different flow of funds from proposer priority fees and extractable value. Blobs also have their own base-fee mechanism.

A decline in L2s' L1 rent therefore cannot be presented directly as validators earning that much less.

A rigorous account of validators' losses would need to separately aggregate proposer tips, extractable value, execution tips on data transactions, and burned base fees across the periods before and after the change.

The narrow conclusion that can be established is this: rollup demand for scarce L1 execution data declined, so rollups no longer compete for the same execution-fee space as they did before March 2024.

Official materials at the time likewise described lower L1 fees as a possible secondary effect, not the upgrade's principal goal.

Now bring the two ends together.

Those bearing the risks and those receiving the returns need not be the same people.

Arbitrum's sequencer and ordering-rights revenues enter the DAO treasury. Users are the ones who ultimately hold assets in the bridge and bear withdrawal waits and bridge exposure.

Base users bear the risks of sequencer outages and upgrades. Chain-level profits are not automatically distributed to them in proportion to their balances.

This describes the contractual structure of rights. It is not a judgment about whether the distribution is reasonable.

In traditional finance, other arrangements generally supplement this structure: shareholders have dividend rights, depositors have insurance, and platform outages are covered by service-level agreements. None of these is part of the protocol here.

A company may provide them voluntarily, or it may not. Whether it does is likewise not written on-chain.

The token question is particularly resistant to generalization.

Arbitrum's sequencer and ordering-rights revenues explicitly enter the DAO treasury. Whether and how token holders receive economic benefits requires a separate act of governance and distribution.

Base currently has no native token at all, yet it has one of the largest chain-level profits among L2s.

Some OP revenue flows into a collective under an agreement. The collective's revenue likewise cannot be equated directly with automatic cash flow to each token.

The narrower statement is therefore this: L2 tokens may carry protocol-governance rights, incentive uses, or treasury decision-making rights. Whether they carry an enforceable economic claim on sequencer revenue depends on the specific fee vaults, treasury contracts, governance contracts, and distribution contracts.

Fee vaults, governance powers, and some distribution rules can be read on-chain. Whether they create an enforceable legal right also requires off-chain documents. Reading these materials begins with knowing which question one is trying to answer, not merely recognizing the token's name.

It cannot be inferred from the words “this chain's token.”

This is the same issue as the standard in Episode 7. There, an interface specified how to check balances and transfer funds without specifying what the token was worth. Here, a chain can generate substantial profits while its token has no contractual relationship to those profits at all.

On the real-world side, clearly observable off-chain effects concern mainly infrastructure availability, not employment, prices, or macroeconomic productivity.

Base's 2026 incident report explicitly said that the outage disrupted users, developers, and node operators and that submissions failed during it. Starknet's 2025 incident required some applications to have transactions resubmitted.

This research found no credible primary or academic data that causally identify lower L2 fees with changes in a region's employment or goods prices, lower business financing costs, or a quantified percentage-point improvement in access to financial services.

There is plenty of corporate promotion and industry narrative claiming that lower costs encourage adoption. That does not meet the standard of causal measurement.

This absence should itself be preserved. What can currently be measured precisely are on-chain transactions, addresses, fees, outages, and flows of funds. The evidentiary chain from these metrics to social welfare or real-economy output remains noticeably weaker.

8. Feeling Like One Chain Again

Several popular claims need to be rewritten according to maturity stage. How to rewrite them is the subject of this section.

L2s inherit Ethereum's security.

At the earliest maturity stage, the material better supports this version: the rollup anchors some data, state commitments, and proof verification to Ethereum, while users' asset security and ability to exit may still depend on permissioned proposers, administrators with immediate upgrade powers, or other trusted actors.

The existence of validity proofs is not the same as satisfying the walkaway condition. Some chains have on-chain data availability and proofs yet remain at the earliest stage. Others also have proofs but lack a force-inclusion route currently available to ordinary users when the sequencer fails.

At the next stage, the narrower statement is this: within the protocol's stated failure model, users retain a way to exit using L1 data, proofs, or escape routes even if the everyday operator acts maliciously. Governance bodies such as a qualifying security council remain an explicit trust boundary.

Only at a further stage can this be narrowed to: participation in the proof system is permissionless, ordinary upgrades must give users a sufficiently long exit window, and the security council's immediate powers are further restricted to intervention in emergency defects.

Even then, do not say all risks are equivalent to Ethereum L1. Sequencer liveness, the ordering of soft confirmations, bridges and solvers across L2s, and wallet and application-layer risks remain additional layers.

None of these four is guaranteed by Ethereum. They are precisely the things an ordinary user actually encounters every day.

The zero-knowledge approach is safer than the fraud-proof approach because it has no seven-day window.

This should become: validity proofs can establish the correctness of state transitions without relying on after-the-fact fraud challenges, so canonical withdrawals do not need the same kind of fixed dispute period. They still depend on proof implementation, data availability, upgrade authority, prover and proposer liveness, and bridge contracts.

The reverse claim also needs limits. Saying that the former approach is unsafe because state remains uncertain for seven days is equally excessive. Users' transaction data may already have entered finalized Ethereum history. Those seven days are principally a safety window for state claims and withdrawal executability, not a period in which users cannot even know whether their transaction exists.

Centralization has merely moved from L1 to the sequencer.

There is quite specific material supporting one side of this statement. Several major chains still depend on one sequencer operator for everyday operation. Some have no on-chain timelock for their core upgrade path. Upgrade administrators can even change proof and bridge rules.

But saying that L2s are no different from centralized databases discards the other half. Delayed inboxes, L1 deposit escape routes, permissionless fraud proofs or validity-verification contracts, and on-chain data availability exist precisely to set explicit limits on some operator powers. Without simultaneously controlling the upgrade keys and proof system, a malicious sequencer does not automatically acquire the ability to fabricate arbitrary final states.

A formulation closer to the material is therefore this: many current L2s concentrate high-frequency, low-latency block-production power in a small number of operators, while using Ethereum's data availability, proof systems, and escape routes to limit those operators' authority over final state. Upgrade councils and multisigs then reintroduce trust in governance to varying degrees.

How far centralization has shifted must be examined layer by layer across sequencers, provers, upgraders, and bridges. Counting nodes is not enough.

The three sequencers are the clearest example of this layered analysis. The machines and consensus processes have expanded from one to three, while all three remain controlled by the same company.

L2s have fragmented liquidity.

The technical facts supporting this are that different L2s have different state machines and finality domains. Stablecoins at an address on one chain and the same stablecoins at the same address on another are not one synchronized balance. Cross-chain use generally requires canonical messaging, third-party bridges, liquidity networks, or intents and solvers.

One piece of evidence deserves to be set out separately.

The Ethereum Foundation's interoperability work in 2025 explicitly stated its goal as making Ethereum feel like one chain again.

That sentence is important institutional evidence.

If today's L2s already had the atomic, synchronous composability found within L1, there would be no need to make “feeling like one chain again” a project's goal.

This is an uncommon kind of evidence in the series: an organization's publicly announced goal reveals, in reverse, the current state of affairs.

It is firmer than any third-party criticism because it comes from the party with the strongest incentive to speak favorably.

The sentence also identifies the price of scaling. Moving execution out relieved congestion while turning what had been one synchronous place into several places that need to connect with one another.

Equating fragmented liquidity with a permanent reduction in liquidity, however, goes too far. Bridges, solvers, and shared messaging layers can move capital across domains. They improve price discovery and capital routing, but often introduce additional inventory, rebalancing, verification, and counterparty-trust assumptions.

The narrower statement should be this: multiple L2s transform synchronous, single-domain liquidity into multiple asynchronous state domains. Bridges and intent solvers can economically reaggregate these pools, while the finality, inventory, and trust assumptions of cross-domain transactions remain different from atomic composition within a single L1 block.

For an ordinary user, the difference looks like this: the same wallet address has two separate balances on two chains. Using them together requires a cross-chain transaction, with its own waiting time, fees, and party to trust.

Now return to the three axes in Section 1.

Correctness, control, cash flow.

A chain can excel on the first axis: validity proofs, on-chain data availability, and state that anyone can reconstruct.

At the same time, it can be entirely centralized on the second: one sequencer and one upgrade key with immediate effect.

On the third, it can hand the fee spread to an entity entirely different from those bearing the risks.

No protocol requires these three things to reside with the same entity.

The remainder lies in the gaps between the three axes.

It is not a complete absence of evidence. Contract addresses, multisig thresholds, dispute windows, and some daily fee flows can be checked item by item. Actual key control, legal rights, and net profit still require off-chain evidence. Neither the inspectable details nor the limits of inspection should disappear.

It is the act of packing three answers into one term.

And that term is what most people buy, use, and discuss.

This is no one's fault. A person using a chain every day cannot reread its verifier contract, multisig membership, and fee vault each time. Documentation, independent assessments, and explanations can help. Those entry points must still preserve the distinction among the three axes rather than compress them back into an attractive label.

On June 25, 2026, Base stopped for 116 minutes.

During that same interval, Ethereum L1 continued producing slots. Approximately 580 slots did not solve L2's inability to produce blocks.

Ethereum's part worked exactly as promised.

One half worked as promised; the other did not. During those two hours, the significance of the two halves to a person was entirely disproportionate.

The people on that chain could not submit transactions.

L1 security and L2 availability have never been the same thing.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
