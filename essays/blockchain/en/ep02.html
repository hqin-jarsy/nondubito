From CPUs to Mining Farms

How GPUs, ASICs, mining pools, and hardware preorders transformed computing open to everyone into an industry highly concentrated in capital, energy, and geography.

I. A Gentlemen’s Agreement

On December 12, 2009, Satoshi Nakamoto replied to a forum post.

He suggested a gentlemen’s agreement to postpone the GPU arms race as long as possible.

His reason was specific. Ordinary people could still participate on relatively equal terms using CPUs. Once newcomers had to wrestle with graphics-card drivers and compatibility, the barrier would rise.

The timing needs to be clear.

This was not nostalgia for the CPU era after ASICs had become widespread. It was written before GPUs entered the network on a large scale. It was a prediction.

The private email to laszlo discussed in the first essay dates to May 2010 and expresses the same concern. The same person had raised it at least twice within six months.

Over the next five years, the process ran its full course in the direction he feared.

The remainder can already be named here. This design makes hash power commensurable: those who compute more receive the new coins. The measure is so clean it scarcely seems man-made. It does not ask who you are, which country you live in, or whether anyone will vouch for you. But once it begins operating, it pushes out those who compute less. Being pushed out registers as nothing on that measure.

That is precisely what Satoshi’s remark was concerned with.

The way he expressed the concern matters too. He did not say it would destroy something or propose banning anyone. His term was a gentlemen’s agreement: something entirely voluntary, with no enforcement mechanism. Someone who had designed a system to dispense with trust could, on this matter, only hope that people would behave honorably.

One judgment must be established here, because it determines how the next seven sections should be read: at almost every step of this process, someone was doing something entirely reasonable.

No step required malicious intent. None took place in secret. Each new development was posted on a public forum with measurements and an invitation to reproduce them.

This will matter later. A great deal of actual fraud subsequently occurred in this domain, but the industrialization of mining largely belongs to a different category. It was a process conducted almost entirely in public, with a defensible rationale at every step. The result was still what it was.

Another familiar account must be dismantled first.

The four-step progression from CPU to GPU to FPGA to ASIC is useful as a teaching diagram, not as a historical record. Primary sources show overlapping generations: CPUs remained in pools after GPUs arrived; early FPGAs did not necessarily have a lower purchase cost per unit of hash power than GPUs; Butterfly Labs began accepting ASIC orders before Avalon announced its product; and when Avalon actually delivered, Butterfly Labs customers were still waiting.

The four neat date ranges were imposed afterward. People at the time made decisions amid several lines advancing simultaneously.

This correction matters throughout the series. Later accounts almost inevitably divide a construct’s path into stages, because otherwise it is hard to explain. Yet that act of organization erases something: people did not know which stage they occupied, when the next would arrive, or what the machine they had just bought would become a few months later.

II. Two Orders of Magnitude

Begin with the hardware, because everything that follows rests on these numbers.

One easily overlooked point about CPUs is that a CPU’s hash rate was never a fixed hardware constant. In forum benchmarks from 2010, an AMD Phenom II X3 720 achieved about 7.2 MH/s with a common miner. Recorded results for an Intel i7-980X varied with software, thread count, and clock speed, from below 10 MH/s to roughly 25 MH/s.

The same chip, different software, several times the performance.

This will remain important. Hash rate looks like a physical quantity, like weight or length, but it is jointly determined by hardware, software, and configuration. The first thing the construct measures here is already not a purely natural fact.

Autumn 2010 is a reasonable dividing line for GPUs.

Forum material from September 2010 explicitly records ArtForz using about twelve Radeon HD 5770 cards to exceed 1 GH/s, while the whole network was estimated at only 5–6 GH/s.

One person, around one-fifth of the network.

And that person was using a dozen or so graphics cards available from ordinary computer shops.

This deserves emphasis because it overturns a popular chronology: mining concentration did not first appear with ASICs. It had already appeared in September 2010. At that point, however, such concentration could still be assembled at home from consumer graphics cards.

A low barrier and an absence of concentration are different things. In September 2010, the barrier was low enough that anyone could buy graphics cards, yet concentration had already occurred. Others were not excluded. They had not thought of doing it, or had thought of it and were unwilling to spend the money.

This distinction will recur throughout the next nineteen essays and become harder to draw. Between something everyone is allowed to join and something everyone can actually afford to join lie money, information, and a willingness to place an early bet on something whose usefulness is not yet apparent.

The single-card figures were these: a Radeon HD 5870 achieved about 299 MH/s in forum tests, had a launch price of approximately $379, and typical board power of about 188 W. That works out to roughly $1.27 per MH/s and 0.63 W per MH/s.

Against the 7.2 MH/s CPU above, that was a difference of about fortyfold.

The dollars-per-MH/s figures here are only for comparison, not a mining-business cost calculation. A graphics card is general-purpose equipment that can also run games. The dedicated miners that follow do nothing except compute hashes. The same number rests on different kinds of asset.

The FPGA stage is the easiest to misrepresent.

Open-source FPGA mining code dates back at least to May 20, 2011. Commercial boards followed. ZTEX’s USB-FPGA Module 1.15x offered about 190 MH/s at 8.5 W, costing around $460 for one board or $430 each in a five-board purchase.

That is about $2.42 per MH/s.

More expensive than a GPU.

FPGAs did not therefore defeat GPUs on the purchase price of a unit of hash power. At launch prices, consumer graphics cards were cheaper.

The order-of-magnitude change was in electricity. At 8.5 W for 190 MH/s, the FPGA used roughly 0.045 W per MH/s, compared with about 0.63 for the 5870: a difference of around fourteenfold.

This step matters more than it first appears.

From here, mining’s optimization target changed. The question was no longer whether desktop hardware was fast enough, but how many hashes each watt could produce and how much hash power could fit in one rack. ZTEX’s own sales thread explicitly discussed competitiveness in hash power per watt.

The transition from personal hobby to industrial facility happened when the metric changed, not when someone decided to build a factory.

ASICs pushed both directions to their limits.

Avalon’s second-batch preorder specifications preserve a clear set of industrial figures: more than 60 GH/s, 6.6–6.7 W per GH/s, $1,500 per unit, 600 units, and planned shipment between March 6 and April 5, 2013.

Using a conservative 60 GH/s, the capital cost was no more than $25 per GH/s, or $0.025 per MH/s. Total power was approximately 396–402 W.

Compared with the 5870 in 2010, purchase cost per unit of hash power had fallen about fiftyfold, while consumption fell from 0.63 W per MH/s to about 0.0066.

Three years. Two orders of magnitude.

Those were not two improvements to the same thing. One reduced the money needed to buy a machine; the other reduced the electricity needed to run it. The former determined who could afford to buy. The latter determined who could afford to operate. By the ASIC stage, the answers began pointing to the same people.

III. The Same Machine

The difficulty curve is the pivot of this essay.

First, the measurement basis. The equivalent network hash rates below are calculated as difficulty multiplied by two to the thirty-second power, divided by 600 seconds. They are the ten-minute steady-state hash rates corresponding to difficulty, not rolling estimates based on the actual number of blocks found in the preceding twenty-four hours. The latter fluctuate with random block arrivals. The two need not match exactly.

Several observation points follow.

In the difficulty period covering mid-to-late May 2010, difficulty was about 11.846, equivalent to approximately 84.8 MH/s.

On June 15, 2011, difficulty was 876,954.493513, equivalent to around 6.28 TH/s.

On November 28, 2012, at the first halving in block 210,000, difficulty was about 3,438,908.96, equivalent to roughly 24.62 TH/s.

In late December 2013, difficulty was about 1,180,923,195.26, equivalent to approximately 8.45 PH/s. At the beginning of that same December it was only 707,408,283. There were several large adjustments within one month.

On December 30, 2014, difficulty was about 40,640,955,016.58, equivalent to roughly 290.92 PH/s.

Four and a half years: from 84.8 MH/s to 290.92 PH/s.

There is another detail. Throughout 2013, difficulty increased thirty times and fell only once. Almost every two weeks that year, everyone already mining was pushed back one position.

That phrase must be translated into money. A machine whose payback period made sense at March 2013 difficulty was purchased on that calculation. Once delivered, it was pushed back every two weeks. At some point, a payback period of months became no payback at all. Its buyer had made no arithmetic error. He simply could not calculate the difficulty two weeks ahead, because that depended on how many machines other people bought.

What is truly chilling is not the curve itself, but placing the same machine on it.

A 66 GH/s Avalon, on the day it arrived on January 30, 2013, accounted for about 0.31% of the network. Mining alone, it would find a block every 2.24 days on average. A forum calculation that day, using 66 GH/s and difficulty 2,968,775.33208, estimated output at around eleven coins a day.

The same machine in late December 2013 represented about 0.00078% of the network: one block every 889 days on average.

The same machine at the end of 2014 represented about 0.0000227%: one block every 30,610 days, approximately eighty-four years.

Not one component had changed.

Other people had changed.

A machine's earnings depend on how much hash power all the other machines supply. Other industries have something similar, but rarely so directly. Another bakery does not slow everyone else's ovens; a new miner does not slow the old hardware either, but it dilutes that hardware's share of total hash power. While difficulty stays fixed, an old machine's theoretical expected block output per unit of time does not immediately fall for that reason. As faster network-wide block production leads to a later upward difficulty adjustment, the same machine faces a higher threshold and a lower expected output.

The construct’s action here is so clean that it scarcely looks like an action. It excludes no one. It merely raises the standard, using everyone’s own efforts as the basis.

Each new miner changes everyone's relative position. When difficulty catches up with the added hash power, the same amount of computation may earn fewer coins, including for the newcomer.

This is the first appearance of a structure that will return in many forms: an individual’s optimal choice also worsens everyone’s situation, including his own. No one can avoid it by declining to participate, because nonparticipation simply means being pushed out faster.

The protocol itself writes this curve. Difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks, working backward from a target elapsed time of fourteen days. When Satoshi explained it in February 2010, he used precisely that calculation: if a period took only 9.4 days, multiply the previous difficulty by fourteen divided by 9.4 to obtain the new difficulty.

No individual can decide it. No individual can stop it.

This is both the design’s most elegant feature and its hardest feature to deal with. The elegance is that no one can manipulate issuance or print an extra batch overnight. The difficulty is that, when the curve begins hurting people, there is no one to appeal to. The first essay described the construct here as self-executing code. This is its first concrete example.

Variance is another part of the story.

At the June 2011 difficulty, a 299 MH/s graphics card would find a block every 146 days on average. Under a Poisson model, the probability of finding no block at all in a month was about 81%.

At the difficulty prevailing at the 2012 halving, a 215 MH/s FPGA would find a block every 795 days on average. Its probability of finding none for an entire year was about 63%.

These probabilities follow solely from difficulty and hash rate. They have nothing to do with a miner’s skill.

Someone could do everything right, buy the best hardware then available, optimize the software, run it twenty-four hours a day, and receive nothing for an entire year.

Mining pools grew from this point.

IV. Twenty Thousand People

Begin with preorders to see the business model’s actual shape.

At the FPGA and ASIC stages, something changed that mattered more than the hardware: manufacturers could receive full payment before a chip had even successfully completed tape-out.

Butterfly Labs is the most complete case because it became a federal enforcement action and left court records.

According to the FTC’s later complaint, BFL began advertising the BitForce SC range in June 2012, at prices from $149 to $29,899. Consumers were told the products were in the final stages of development, with initial delivery planned for October 2012. Every order required full payment upfront, through PayPal, bitcoin, or wire transfer.

October 2012 passed without delivery.

As of April 1, 2013, no unit had yet been delivered to a customer.

By September 2013, more than 20,000 consumers who had paid in full still had not received their machines.

The FTC’s reasoning deserves attention. It was not a general complaint about dissatisfied customers.

It joined delivery time, difficulty, hash rate, power use, and investment returns into one causal chain. BFL’s own promoted earnings calculator required mining difficulty and delivery date as inputs. The FTC pointed out that, as miners multiplied and hardware improved, older equipment rapidly approached obsolescence. Receiving the latest equipment promptly was therefore essential to whether buyers could recover their investment. The complaint further alleged that equipment BFL eventually supplied was often already obsolete, mining only a fraction of the bitcoins it could have mined if delivered when promised.

Connect that account to the figures in the preceding section, and the force of the causal chain becomes clear.

In 2013, a delay of six months to a year did not merely mean receiving a package later. It meant the same machine moving from one block every two days to one every eight hundred-plus days.

In September 2014, the FTC obtained temporary court restraints, placing BFL's business under court control. The 2016 settlements provided for suspension of the roughly $38.61 million judgment against BFL and Sonny Vleisides after payments of $15,000 by BFL and $4,000 by Vleisides. The $135,878 judgment against Darla Drake would be suspended once she surrendered the cash value of bitcoins obtained using company machines. Suspension rested on inability-to-pay disclosures; misrepresenting financial condition could make the judgments payable. The orders also restricted future deceptive claims about product availability, profitability, and whether equipment was new or used.

Avalon was a different case and must not be collapsed into BFL’s.

Avalon publicly announced preorders on September 17, 2012. Its conservative plan was to finish production in January–February 2013 and begin shipping in order sequence in February. In fact, it announced shipments on January 20. The first unit went to Bitcoin Core developer Jeff Garzik. On January 30, photographs of receipt and operating verification appeared on the forum, with measured performance around 68 GH/s.

Batch #1 itself therefore cannot serve as an example of delivery delayed by several months.

But the Batch #2 preorder thread contained a remarkably direct statement: the company needed the money before the weekend to place orders for components.

That sentence says everything about the nature of preorders.

Buyers were not paying for goods. They were providing the company’s working capital.

This was not secret. The thread said so openly. Buyers could see it and still bought. The reason is understandable: earlier orders received machines earlier, and earlier delivery meant several extra weeks of mining before difficulty rose. Prepayment bought a place in line.

Now trace both ends.

Manufacturers received interest-free production finance, no inventory risk, and confirmation of demand.

Buyers bore the risks of final hardware performance, delivery timing, and rising difficulty while they waited.

All three risks were on one side. The money went to the other.

This is the series’ first asymmetry, appearing in its second essay.

A qualification is needed for fairness. Preorders are not inherently fraudulent. They are common in hardware, especially chips that require tape-out first. Without advance payments, a new company may be unable to produce its first batch. The problem is not simply who bears risk, but that the risk-bearing side receives no compensation proportionate to it. Buyers absorb the entire loss from delay, while timely delivery brings them no additional benefit beyond what was promised.

Another boundary must be kept.

The claim that manufacturers kept the best machines to mine first and shipped them only after earnings declined was widespread. The evidence needs its labels. The FTC's case concerned delay, nondelivery, and equipment obsolete on arrival; its 2016 settlement announcement also expressly described allegations of undisclosed use before delivery. The settlement required one manager to surrender the cash value of bitcoins obtained with company machines. A private plaintiff also alleged mining with equipment meant for customers. These materials do not amount to a trial finding that every delayed machine was secretly used to mine.

An allegation in a complaint is not a generally established fact adjudicated at trial.

“Company equipment was used to mine” and “all delayed customer machines were secretly used to mine” are different propositions. The available material supports only the former.

Maintaining that boundary does not excuse anyone. This domain later produced substantial real fraud. If allegations and findings are written interchangeably, sentences will lose their value when the series reaches cases that demand plain accusation.

V. Who Bears Bad Luck?

Mining pools offer a rare case in this series where the distribution of risk was openly priced and users could choose.

The original Slush Pool thread was posted on November 27, 2010. Marek Palatinus defined the problem precisely: GPUs had made solo mining difficult for others, and his own three CPUs had gone a long time without finding a block.

His arithmetic was simple. If one miner supplied 1,000 kH/s to a pool totaling 20,000 kH/s, and the pool found a 50-coin block every two days on average, the miner’s share would be approximately 1.25 coins a day. He explicitly contrasted small, continuous returns with waiting weeks or months for a single solo reward of fifty coins.

The pool found its first block, number 97,834, on December 16, 2010.

Almost immediately, a reply identified the crucial point: two machines at 1,000 kH/s and one at 2,000 kH/s have the same total expected success rate.

In a theoretical comparison with equal hash power, uptime, and other conditions, pooling does not by itself increase expected earnings before fees.

It changes the variance of their distribution and when payment arrives.

In a sufficiently large pool that keeps operating and paying normally, the probability of receiving nothing for a year can be far below the 63% solo-mining example. What the miner buys is smoother payment, not absolute certainty. Fees reduce expected net income, and risks such as operator nonpayment remain.

The subsequent evolution of reward algorithms was driven by arbitrage. That history reveals much about the construct.

The initial method was close to allocating rewards in proportion to work submitted during a round. Shares soon appeared. Miners did not have to submit only rare hashes satisfying the full network difficulty. They could submit proofs of work meeting a lower internal pool difficulty. These lower-difficulty shares measured each participant’s computational contribution. Only the hash that met the network target actually produced a block.

But simple proportional allocation created another problem.

In his 2011 analysis of pool rewards, Meni Rosenfeld explained that, if a round had lasted a long time and accumulated many shares without finding a block, the expected value of a newly submitted share declined under a proportional scheme. Miners could therefore join at the beginning of a round and leave once it grew old.

This was pool hopping.

Slush accordingly moved to a scoring method: newer shares received more weight, and older shares’ scores decayed over time.

Pause here, because a widespread claim is incorrect.

The claim that Slush used PPLNS from the beginning does not fit the original thread. Proportional allocation came first, followed by measuring work through low-difficulty shares, and later a score-based system that weighted recent shares more heavily. Scoring depends on share age and time. PPLNS depends on the most recent N shares, crosses round boundaries, and does not use the elapsed age of the current round as its settlement boundary.

Calling all of these “share-based” loses the 2011 debate about pool hopping and reward design.

Taken apart, the methods form a risk-allocation schedule.

Under proportional payment, miners bear the risk of an unlucky round.

With score-based and geometric methods, risk remains primarily with miners, while the arbitrage opportunity is closed.

PPLNS pays the most recent N shares rather than using block rounds as settlement boundaries. Adjusting N trades off variance against the time needed for rewards to mature.

PPS immediately purchases each valid share at its theoretical expected value, independently of when the pool actually finds a block. Bad-luck and reserve-funding risks therefore fall on the operator, so PPS usually charges a much higher risk fee.

DGM uses double-geometric weighting to redistribute variance between operator and miners.

These terms have explicit prices. Miners can bear variance themselves and pay a low fee, or have the operator bear it and pay more. Both sides know what they are exchanging.

Read this section beside the preceding one and a distinction becomes clear. Both concern risk allocation, but preorders are asymmetric while pools are symmetric. The difference is not who has a better conscience. Pool risk can be calculated and priced, and miners can switch providers at any time.

Calculable, selectable, and possible to leave: together, these give both sides better conditions for comparison and bargaining, and risk allocation a better chance of becoming fair. They are not a formula that guarantees fairness.

One or another will repeatedly be missing in the next nineteen essays. Sometimes risk is not calculable because no one knows what an asset is worth. Sometimes there is no choice because only one usable provider remains. Sometimes exit is impossible because money is locked in a contract for thirty days. The remainder spills out wherever a condition is missing.

Comparative material from 2011 put the fee for Slush’s score-based model at around 2%. By early 2014, GHash.io took another route: an explicit 0% pool fee, combined with CEX.IO’s trading and cloud-hash-power businesses. GHash itself identified zero fees as a major reason it had exceeded 40%.

Cloud hash power removed the machine itself from the customer’s view.

In 2014, CEX.IO defined 1 GHS as a unit of cloud hash power in GHash.io, tradable on its market. Holders bore the electricity, maintenance, and hardware replacement costs of operating the miners. In May 2014, the company publicly quoted maintenance costs of $0.00045 per GHS per hour.

In January 2015, following falling bitcoin prices and rising difficulty, the company suspended automatic cloud mining. Terms quoted publicly at the time allowed CEX.IO to stop mining with holders’ GHS when maintenance exceeded block rewards or mining was judged uneconomic. The company also said GHS remained the holders’ property and allowed them to opt back in.

This dispute has an entirely different structure from an undelivered miner.

Customers bought a right to hash power. Operating terms allowed the exercise of that right to stop when it was unprofitable.

The arrangement needs to be understood accurately. The published terms provided for suspending uneconomic mining; stopping alone therefore does not establish a breach, while questions about actual performance require separate examination. Turning something into a tradable unit is not the same as creating a right that is always exercised. Your GHS could remain yours while the terms allowed it to stop working when you most wanted it to work.

VI. 39.99%

The events of June 2014 are often told as a pool suddenly approaching 51%. The primary record shows a much longer and more interesting process.

On January 8–9, 2014, Blockchain.info’s short-window statistics already put GHash.io above 42%. A day later, as miners voluntarily moved to other pools, reported share fell to around 38%.

That same day, GHash.io issued a statement acknowledging that its hash power had exceeded 40% entering 2014, and announcing a temporary halt to accepting new independent mining farms.

The most valuable sentence in that self-reported announcement was another one: about 45% of the pool came from BitFury ASIC systems and approximately 55% from independent miners.

Remember that sentence. The next section depends on it.

Around June 12–13, 2014, short-window block statistics finally crossed the symbolic threshold. Contemporary reporting put the pool at around 51% of blocks in a rolling observation window. Some secondary reports using shorter windows gave a peak near 55%.

Strictly speaking, whether the peak was 51% or 55% depends on the time window and whether observed block share is equated directly with instantaneous hash power. The reliable common ground is that it did exceed 50% within a short window that attracted substantial attention.

The community did not immediately fork the protocol. Miners left, people publicly urged the dispersal of hash power, and decentralized pooling mechanisms such as P2Pool were discussed again. January’s decline from 42% to 38% had already demonstrated this form of voting with one’s feet.

In July 2014, following an industry conference and roundtable discussions, GHash.io publicly pledged to keep its future hash power below 39.99% of the network.

The number itself deserves attention. Not forty, not thirty-five, but two decimal places. What it conveyed was simple: I am just short of the line, and I know where the line is.

The pledge was voluntary governance, not a protocol-enforced ceiling.

No consensus rule would automatically reject the share that brought it to forty percent.

The community’s technical responses fell into three classes, addressing entirely different points of control.

The first left the hash function unchanged and altered pool structure. P2Pool replaced the centralized pool coordinator with a distributed share chain. During the 2014 GHash episode, Gavin Andresen was still publicly advocating this direction.

The second changed the protocol’s strategic incentives. Eyal and Sirer’s 2013 paper showed that, in their model, strategic mining advantages could arise below an absolute 51%. They also proposed a protocol modification under which a selfish-mining coalition controlling less than roughly a quarter of resources could not gain that advantage.

The third was the most radical: replace proof of work with an ASIC-resistant design. Other chains experimented extensively with that route. Bitcoin’s main chain did not use it to invalidate deployed SHA-256 equipment.

Why the third route was not taken deserves separate explanation.

Once manufacturers such as Avalon, BFL, and ASICMiner had invested in tape-out and supply chains, changing proof of work was no longer merely a software optimization. It would invalidate an existing stock of capital equipment all at once.

After 2013, debate over changing the hash function was therefore also a governance dispute over who should bear losses to existing mining capital.

The crucial point is that these responses were not three ways of expressing one decentralization parameter.

P2Pool targeted the pool coordinator. Protocol changes targeted the strategic structure. ASIC resistance targeted dedicated-hardware advantages. GHash’s 39.99% addressed voluntary restraint at the pool level.

Four methods, four different positions.

None could simultaneously solve the problems at the other three positions. The community did a great deal during these years. Each action could reach only one place.

This is one of a construct’s hardest situations. The problem is not that no one governs it. On the contrary, there are many people and many proposals, each correct at its own position. The remainder does not reside at any one of those positions. It resides between them.

VII. Five Measures

Now the real question can be asked: did mining become centralized?

The answer depends on the measure used. There are at least five.

Pool-based measurement is relatively accessible: coinbase labels, receiving addresses, and other clues can help estimate block attribution. Not every block can be reliably identified, however, and attribution does not directly establish ultimate control. On this measure, the brief crossing of 50% in June 2014 was a clear concentration signal; later studies of the top five pools also continued to find high concentration.

Its strength is a direct correspondence to something definite: which pool infrastructure publishes block templates and receives the coinbase.

Its weakness is that it groups thousands of independent miners, free to switch pools at any moment, under one label.

GHash’s own 45%/55% breakdown is contemporaneous evidence of that weakness.

Measurement by actual machine owner gives another picture, pointing in the opposite direction.

In 2019, researchers analyzing on-chain reward distributions from three major pools found that, within each pool studied, no more than about twenty recipient entities took over half of bitcoin rewards. Some miners operated across all three pools.

By this measure, pool pie charts may understate concentration among underlying economic actors rather than exaggerate it.

Address clustering has assumptions of its own, however. One recipient may hold funds for several clients, while one company may divide itself across multiple addresses.

Measurement by chip manufacturer captures a different power: control over efficient ASIC design, tape-out, and delivery schedules.

That concentration does not appear in coinbase data, yet it determines who first receives the next generation of energy efficiency. The months of delay in Section IV could change a miner’s network share by orders of magnitude. This is why.

Private manufacturers do not regularly disclose how much equipment they sell, how much they retain, or where their customers are. This measure therefore depends more heavily on estimates than pool data does.

Geographic measurement captures correlated failure risks involving electricity grids, climate, and regulation.

One particularly easy mistake is to treat the block share of a pool classified as Chinese as the share of physical hash power located in China. Pool servers, company registration, ASIC manufacturing, and customers’ actual machines may be in entirely different places.

One study reported that pools labeled Chinese by operator classification jointly exceeded 51% between March 2015 and February 2017. It measured pool classification, not the location of each device.

Measurement by ultimate controlling party comes closest to the question that matters, but is hardest to observe.

One company may own miners, operate a pool, manufacture ASICs, sell cloud hash power, and host other people’s equipment. Conversely, one large miner may connect to several competing pools at once.

There is no one-to-one conversion between manufacturer share, pool share, hosted megawatts, and the practical ability to coordinate an attack.

Five numbers, five kinds of power, distributed among different people, with no one holding all of them. This helps explain why arguments over centralization never resolve: two people can both be correct if they are using different measures.

Set the five measures together and the central point emerges.

The chain records many things within its defined scope in great detail: difficulty, each block's declared timestamp, coinbase destinations, and difficulty adjustments. Participants can check those records against the protocol. Detailed records do not mean that every kind of fact about the world has been measured accurately.

Yet its most important property, how decentralized it actually is, has no accepted measure.

The property most important to a construct is precisely the one it cannot measure itself.

This matters across the series, and not only for this chain. Constructs in the economics series also each have something they cannot measure accurately: national accounts cannot measure the vast area they place outside their own boundary; ratings cannot measure what they will trigger once written into hundreds of charters; the measure of systemic risk cannot measure someone who is not connected to others.

The difference here is that this unmeasurable thing happens to be the construct’s entire reason for existing.

The problem is not a lack of attempts. The reason is harder: measuring it requires answering a question the protocol cannot answer.

What counts as an independent person?

The protocol recognizes only hash power. It does not ask how many network switches the machines connect to, how many legal entities own them, how many people make their decisions, or how many electricity grids supply them.

Not asking is no design oversight. On the contrary, it is a condition of the system’s possibility. Once it asks who you are, someone must verify the answer, and that returns it to the starting point. All its effort has gone into making that question unnecessary. The price is that it can never ask it afterward either.

This is the remainder in this history. It is neither omitted nor hidden. It appears on-chain every day as hashes. It simply cannot be read by the chain itself.

VIII. Electricity and People

On the real-world side, American reporting by early 2014 already treated mining farms as a familiar industrial-facility problem, not an internet problem.

MegaBigPower founder Dave Carlson began with hobby mining in 2012 and moved to the Wenatchee area of central Washington in 2013. Contemporary reporting explicitly identified exceptionally cheap local hydroelectricity as the reason.

When a reporter visited in April 2014, two warehouses had become data centers containing thousands of boards, with large industrial fans continuously extracting hot air. Carlson was then planning another facility roughly ten times the original’s size.

The same report demonstrated that electricity prices and cooling were not separate factors. Once computing equipment is densely packed, virtually all incoming electricity ultimately becomes heat. Even in an already cool climate, large-scale ventilation remains necessary.

HashPlex also moved from temporary hosting space in Seattle toward a large data center east of the Cascade Mountains. By 2014, central Washington had both farms whose operators owned large numbers of miners and facilities hosting equipment for outside owners.

A contemporary European example was Boden, Sweden. In February 2014, KnCMiner began building a mining data center of about 10 MW in a disused Swedish army helicopter hangar roughly fifty miles from the Arctic Circle. Later industry material summarized the local advantages as cold climate, inexpensive electricity, political stability, and a highly reliable grid.

These advantages concern more than a chip's computational efficiency. A disused helicopter hangar offers large-span space and existing power connections. Fifty miles from the Arctic Circle, a cold climate can reduce the need for active refrigeration, but it does not eliminate ventilation and infrastructure costs. By this stage, geography and infrastructure were deeply involved in determining who could keep mining.

This section should now continue: how many local jobs the farms created, how much tax they paid, and what happened to local electricity prices and grids.

But that part cannot be written.

For 2010–2014, contemporaneous verifiable figures for local employment and tax revenue are markedly scarcer than figures for power and capital expenditure.

The Wenatchee report gave warehouse scale, equipment counts by order of magnitude, expansion plans, and Carlson’s own claims of investments in the millions of dollars and returns in the tens of millions. It supplied no employee count, local tax amount, or dedicated utility agreement. The 10 MW Boden facility presents the same problem: public material does not disclose its contracted electricity price per kilowatt-hour.

This was not journalistic negligence.

Early public accounts primarily described farms through megawatt loads, warehouses, cooling, electricity prices, and equipment investment. Without utility records, tax authorities, or company payrolls, megawatts cannot be converted into jobs.

Utilities recorded electricity; companies recorded money. In this set of public materials, people's work and circumstances were not presented with the same completeness.

The history therefore left an exceptionally precise record of electricity and money, and an almost empty record of people.

That is the remainder's shape here. Public accounts gave megawatts and dollars a stable place, without giving the people replacing warehouse fans an equally clear one. This does not mean companies had no payrolls or local employment statistics did not exist. It means the records that can presently be connected do not yet fill in the human column.

The gap is not filled by the chain’s immutability. The chain records the address that received a coinbase, not how many people were replacing fans in the warehouse behind that address.

This limit persists throughout the series. On-chain records are extraordinarily complete, but complete only within what the chain can define. Warehouse workers and the lost chat log in the first essay belong to the same category: they existed, but they were outside what the chain was meant to record.

Return to the gentlemen’s agreement of December 2009.

It did not hold, of course.

Nor could it, because it required everyone voluntarily to forgo a choice that benefited them and complied fully with the rules.

Viewed separately, it is difficult to say who was wrong at any step in those five years. Those improving efficiency improved efficiency. Those reducing power consumption reduced it. Pool operators solved a real problem for small miners who might go months without finding a block. Moving near cheap hydropower was the location decision any industrial project would make.

Even the twenty thousand people waiting for machines had made what seemed the most reasonable choice when they bought: pay earlier, join the line earlier, receive the machine earlier, start mining earlier.

Together, these entirely reasonable actions produced the 51% of June 2014.

A construct does not need anyone to do wrong. It needs only everyone, in their own position, to do what is reasonable for themselves.

The 39.99% of July 2014 was a promise. No line of code enforced it. No consensus rule rejected a block at the fortieth percentage point.

The chain itself never noticed what had happened in June 2014.

It kept operating toward an average interval of ten minutes per block, adjusting difficulty every 2,016 blocks and awarding the reward to whoever found a valid answer.

It could calculate difficulty to six decimal places.

It could not calculate how many machines finding those answers were plugged into the same switch.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
