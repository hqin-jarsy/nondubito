1. Six Things That Are Not the Same

Three sets of definitions must first be separated here. Otherwise, almost every account of success or failure will be talking past its subject.

First, legal tender is not a single switch.

The 2021 law did four different things at once: it gave Bitcoin legal-tender status; required economic agents, in principle, to accept it; allowed taxes to be paid with it; and made the state responsible for arranging instant conversion from Bitcoin into dollars and bearing the funding risk of that mechanism.

The 2025 amendments did not simply delete the words “legal tender” from the law. They dismantled its most compulsory elements.

That is why IMF documents can describe the reform as removing legal-tender status while El Salvador's amended statute still contains the term.

The two descriptions do not refer to precisely the same legal components.

Second, there are at least three different ways to count Chivo's users: downloads or installations, registered accounts, and people still actively using it.

In its public statements in 2021, the government used these terms interchangeably. It did not publish an independently audited administrative series that consistently separated installations, unique verified accounts, and monthly active individuals.

Later academic surveys primarily measured self-reported downloading and use. Those results cannot simply be added to, or treated as independent confirmation of, back-end account totals.

Third, the central bank's crypto-remittance category is “cryptocurrency digital wallets.” It is not “Chivo remittances,” or even, strictly speaking, “Bitcoin remittances.”

The central bank's methodological notes explicitly state that its data come from banks, remittance companies, federations, telecommunications companies, and digital-wallet companies, and that individual institutions' data are confidential by law.

The public tables therefore cannot tell us what shares belong to Chivo, other Bitcoin wallets, stablecoins, or other cryptoassets.

Taken together, these three distinctions establish the method for this essay.

Accounts are not individuals.

Registration is not active use.

Using a dual-currency wallet is not the same as using Bitcoin.

Crypto-wallet remittances are not Chivo remittances.

On-chain reserve addresses are not the balance sheet of the entire public sector.

And trust capital is not a net fiscal loss.

Six things that are not the same. The next seven sections are essentially an effort to explain each distinction.

Taken together, these six distinctions constitute the remainder in this history.

It is not in any one figure. Most can be traced to original documents, but each has definitions and limits. It lies between them: between accounts and people, registration and use, a statutory obligation and what happens at a checkout counter.

No one deliberately excavated these gaps. They arise naturally when different institutions measure the same undertaking according to their respective responsibilities. The government measures how many accounts it has opened. The central bank measures how much money has crossed the border. Researchers measure whether people still use the system. The Fund measures the public sector's risk exposure.

Four institutions, four measuring sticks, all measuring what they are supposed to measure.

Each measures the thing it is responsible for. None is responsible for reconciling them.

Episode 2 ended with public records describing electricity and money clearly, but not people's work and circumstances with the same completeness. Here another layer appears: government, the central bank, and researchers all keep records, yet reconciling their different measures still requires additional work.

The construct examined here differs fundamentally from those in the preceding thirteen essays.

Those constructs were built by developers, companies, or protocols.

This one has a law behind it.

There is a key difference between law and code. Once the required conditions hold and a transaction is submitted and executed, code can process it automatically under its preset logic. A written law still requires people to comply and practical arrangements for enforcement.

That gap is what we will examine here.

Here, the distance between the statute and actual use particularly deserves measurement. The law required economic agents to accept Bitcoin in principle, while exempting those without the necessary technological access; surveys could ask how many merchants actually accepted it. The two cannot be compared unadjusted as one hundred percent versus twenty percent. They still prompt the question: how does an obligation on paper reach a checkout counter?

2. Must Accept

The law passed on June 8, 2021, and published on June 9 did not merely say in Article 1 that Bitcoin could be used.

It designated Bitcoin as unrestricted legal tender with unlimited debt-discharging power. It also expressly left the existing Monetary Integration Law in place, so the dollar system was not replaced. Article 16 provided that it would take effect ninety days after publication: September 7, 2021.

The crucial provision was Article 7. Its verb was mandatory.

All economic agents were, in principle, required to accept Bitcoin when a purchaser offered it in payment for goods or services.

It was not up to individual merchants to decide whether to enable it.

That verb deserves a pause, because it determines the nature of everything that follows.

Most laws governing new payment methods establish permission: permission to issue, accept, or settle. This provision established an obligation.

A country can stipulate what money people may use. Stipulating what money everyone must accept is another matter.

The first opens a door for something. The second requires everyone to go through it.

The exception in the original Article 12 was narrow: people who plainly and demonstrably lacked access to the necessary technology were exempt from Article 7. The state was also required to promote training and access to that technology.

A small merchant's reluctance to use Bitcoin, or concern about its price volatility, was therefore not in itself a statutory ground for exemption.

On the tax side, the original Article 4 allowed taxes to be paid in Bitcoin. Article 5 exempted its exchange from capital-gains tax, as with other legal tender. Article 6 retained the dollar as the reference currency for accounting. Article 13 further provided that monetary obligations denominated in dollars before the law took effect could be discharged in Bitcoin.

The most easily overlooked parts of the 2021 design were Articles 8 and 14.

Article 8 required the state to provide a technological solution enabling people to transact in Bitcoin and, if they wished, to convert it automatically and instantly into dollars.

Article 14 required the state to establish a trust through the state development bank before the law took effect, guaranteeing that automatic conversion.

The flow of funds in the 2021 system can therefore be stated in one sentence.

The consumer pays in Bitcoin; the merchant is legally required to accept it; the merchant can choose to receive dollars immediately; and the public trust and government arrangements take on the conversion and liquidity side.

This meant merchants were not necessarily forced to bear the price risk of holding Bitcoin. If they chose immediate conversion, at least part of the price and liquidity risk shifted to the state-backed conversion mechanism.

Conversely, saying that merchants could accept Bitcoin without exposure to volatility did not mean doing so was costless.

Part of the cost and risk had moved from merchants' balance sheets to a public mechanism.

As a design, the arrangement made sense. For something whose price can move several percent a day to serve as an everyday means of payment, someone has to absorb that volatility in between. Asking hundreds of thousands of small merchants to bear it individually was plainly not going to work.

The question is not, then, whether the arrangement was reasonable. It is that, once written into law, this volatility had a designated bearer, and that bearer was the public purse.

The state took on this layer. Taking it on was itself the source of the fiscal figures in the sections that follow.

This is our first instance of tracing both ends of a transaction, and it is written into the statute.

3. Three Million or Four Million

Chivo is not a fully open-source national wallet with a transparent architecture. Public materials allow us to piece together a system involving multiple contractors, two currency balances, connections to both the blockchain and the Lightning Network, and ATMs, payment terminals, and an internal back end.

In a filing with US securities regulators, one company said that it had contracted with the Salvadoran government in 2021 to develop the ecosystem, its website, and the interfaces connecting it with the digital wallet and platform. The development contract was worth $4 million, and the project was completed in September 2021.

The company also deployed 200 white-label ATMs within El Salvador, ten at Salvadoran consulates in the United States, and another forty-five at other US locations, for a total of 255. It sold 950 payment terminals to the finance ministry as well.

Another company said it became involved in supporting the system later in 2021, providing front- and back-end software, mobile applications, mobile point-of-sale software, a merchant website, call-center software, and an administration console.

That account came from the company itself, and the public materials found in this research did not disclose its contract price. It cannot therefore be credited with building the entire system.

These materials also establish something quite practical: zero fees at the user's end do not mean the system has no transaction costs.

Software, ATMs, cash logistics, customer service, identity verification, and conversion are paid for on the other side. At least part of that cost is borne through government contracts and public funds.

Zero fees are a pricing policy, not a technological achievement.

This field has been repeating that lesson since the first essay. For the two pizzas in that essay, the protocol charged no one, while someone paid with a card on the buyer's behalf.

Now consider the most frequently cited numbers.

The sign-up incentive was $30 worth of Bitcoin. It was a one-time account-opening bonus, not a monthly subsidy.

The government's published numbers changed very quickly after launch.

On September 18, 2021, it said 1.1 million, using the phrase “already using.” The next day it said more than 1.1 million, but the phrase became “already installed.” On September 25 it said 2.1 million, mixing the language of accounts and users. On September 27 it published a figure of 2.26 million as of September 25. Roughly three months after launch, the government and vendors were saying around four million.

These numbers must be read according to what they count.

On downloads, there is no public, audited cumulative total of downloads to unique devices. The government once described 1.1 million as installations. A later national survey found that more than half of adults reported having downloaded it, but that is a survey proportion, not a back-end download log.

On registrations, the safest description is the account total publicly reported by the government. Given the sign-up incentive, complaints of identity theft, and the absence of a published deduplication method, this cannot simply be called four million distinct individuals.

On activity, the government has never published a continuous, auditable series of monthly or daily active users.

The most comparable independent evidence comes from a national survey in February 2022: of those who had downloaded the wallet, only about 20% continued using it after spending the $30 bonus. Even those who continued were mainly using its dollar functions. The proportion continuing to use Bitcoin was lower still.

Later IMF research offered another perspective: the wallet processed around 5,000 operational transactions a day, less than 2% of the country's daily debit- and credit-card transaction volume.

One measures people and the other transactions. They are not interchangeable.

This also explains why the figure of four million can coexist with very low everyday use. One counts accounts registered over time; the other measures continuing behavior.

When discussing failures and identity theft, verifiable records must be separated from allegations.

The government itself acknowledged errors during the first days of operation and said its technical team was continuing to fix them. A claim that the launch was fault-free would therefore contradict the official record.

More specific accounts of technical failures come from later litigation testimony and investigative reporting. A technician involved in development testified in a civil lawsuit that the system had to be taken offline about twenty-six hours after launch and that some bank-to-wallet transfers were delayed or failed. He also said the initial identity-verification provider encountered problems after roughly 150 registrations, after which verification controls were relaxed. He estimated that between one-tenth and one-fifth of registrations might have been fraudulent.

These are the testimony and estimates of a participant in litigation and a technical worker, not findings by a court.

The percentage cannot therefore be multiplied by four million and then by $30 and presented as a proven fiscal loss.

A human-rights organization received numerous complaints from people whose identities had been used by others to register. In December 2021 it filed a complaint concerning 131 specific individuals. Public materials from the same period also reported approximately 755 identity-theft notifications over a particular interval. Prosecutors opened an investigation, and the police also acknowledged related fraud problems.

The public materials do not, however, provide a final nationwide count of stolen-identity accounts established by a court, or the amount actually recovered.

An audit authority's acceptance of complaints and decision to investigate likewise establishes only that a formal review began, not that the alleged violations were proven.

4. Twenty Percent

Adoption rates provide the most reliable real-world evidence in this case because they do not depend on the government's back-end definition of an account.

The widely cited study conducted a nationally representative, face-to-face household survey in February 2022, with roughly 1,800 respondents, and was later published in an academic journal. Its strength was its proximity to the launch, only about five months earlier, allowing direct questions about when people downloaded the wallet, their behavior after spending the bonus, remittances, and merchants.

The main findings must be interpreted with their denominators intact.

About 68% knew about the wallet. That is awareness among all respondents.

More than half had downloaded it. This was self-reported, not taken from installation logs.

About 40% of downloads took place in September 2021, while new downloads in 2022 were already scarce.

Those who continued using it after spending the bonus represented about 20% of downloaders.

About 3% of all respondents had received Bitcoin remittances through it. About 8% had received dollar remittances, but that was the wallet's dollar function and cannot be counted as Bitcoin adoption.

On the merchant side, slightly more than 20% accepted Bitcoin. The share of businesses that had actually made a Bitcoin sale was 11.4%. Bitcoin and the wallet together accounted for around 5% of sales.

Here is the most important sentence in this section.

Article 7 of the law still said, at the time, that all economic agents must accept it.

Actual acceptance was only about one-fifth.

On one side was a statutory obligation in principle; on the other, actual acceptance around 20%. The distance was substantial. But the law also preserved an exemption for inadequate technological access, so every nonaccepting business in the survey cannot automatically be classified as breaking the law.

As the previous section explained, the exemption was reserved for those genuinely unable to access the technology. Inconvenience, volatility, and customers' inability to use it were not among its grounds.

The survey did establish that most businesses did not actually accept Bitcoin. How many fell within the statutory exemption, and how many failed to meet an applicable obligation, would require further individual and enforcement evidence. Roughly 80% nonacceptance was not an adjudicated 80% violation rate.

Yet this research found no record of anyone being penalized for refusing Bitcoin.

This is not an accusation that enforcement agencies failed in their duties. Requiring acceptance of a payment method and making it work smoothly at checkout counters nationwide are different tasks. The law did not simply require every business to install Chivo. Equipment, connectivity, personnel, and evidence of refusal all affect enforcement, and the available material does not measure each of those links.

What the construct encountered was not merely whether people wished to comply, but how the obligation was communicated and enforced, and what conditions actual use required.

The previous essay described how neither the destinations of those machines nor the incentives offered by local authorities were automatic. This case goes a step further: even the rule itself had no automatic path to execution.

The study also found that sustained adoption was more concentrated among younger people, men, the more educated, and those already participating in the financial system.

The IMF's summary of the relevant micro-level evidence similarly noted that adoption skewed toward younger people, men, and those who already had bank accounts. That did not fully align with the priority of bringing people excluded from conventional finance into the system.

Surveys in later years provide a comparison over time, though they do not measure the same indicator.

A university public-opinion institute conducted a national household survey in December 2022 across all fourteen departments, with a sample of 1,273 adults and a margin of error of about plus or minus 2.7 percentage points.

It asked not whether people had accounts but whether they had used Bitcoin for a purchase or payment in 2022.

About 21% said they had, 73.9% said they had not used it that year, and a further 5.1% said they had never used it.

Even among that 21%, use was sparse: about 31.2% had used it once, 12% twice, and 16.5% three times.

Once still counts as having used it.

That distribution says more than the 21% figure alone. Multiplying the rounded shares gives roughly 14.4% of adults using it at least twice during the year. Only when the threshold is narrowed to more than three uses does the share fall to about 8.5%. Having used it once and having established an everyday habit are plainly different things.

In the same institute's survey the following year, the share reporting use in 2023 fell to about 12%. Another survey for 2024 was reported at about 7.5%.

We can therefore establish a sequence that requires no value judgment: rapid registration driven by sign-up bonuses in 2021; more than half reporting a download in February 2022, but only about 20% of downloaders continuing after spending the bonus; about 21% using it at least once during all of 2022; about 12% in 2023; and about 7.5% in 2024.

These are not the same indicator and cannot be plotted as a strictly consistent downward trend.

The narrower conclusion they support is that, across different survey methods, later measures of everyday use were all far below the registration and download measures from the launch period.

That conclusion is enough, because the opposing sides in the debate cite precisely the two ends of this sequence: four million at one end, 7.5% at the other.

The actual cost to merchants, by contrast, is a gap in the evidence.

Legally, merchants had an obligation to accept Bitcoin during those years. The government's automatic conversion mechanism could significantly reduce the price risk of holding it. Merchants could nevertheless face operational costs for equipment, connectivity, staff training, scanning and confirming payments, bookkeeping, refunds, customer service, and troubleshooting.

The existing national surveys measured acceptance and nonacceptance well, but did not establish a credible national average for the dollar amount each business spent complying with the law.

The available material therefore offers no verifiable basis for a claim that mandatory acceptance imposed some number of hundreds of millions of dollars in compliance costs on small merchants.

There is survey evidence, however, that roughly eighty percent of businesses did not accept it in practice, even while the law required them to.

This also shows that actual enforcement and compliance fell far short of the statute's apparent reach.

5. Less Than One Percent

We first need a sense of how much remittances matter to this country.

The World Bank's regional statistics for 2021 put its remittances at about 26.1% of gross domestic product. Similar cross-border transfer ratios in later IMF macroeconomic tables were roughly in the 24–25% range for 2022 through 2024.

Because the two institutions' classifications are not identical, decimal figures from different sources should not be stitched together as though they formed a fully consistent series.

This means that even a one-percentage-point shift between channels involves a substantial amount of household cash flow.

What matters, then, is not the technical possibility of zero fees, but how much remittance activity actually moved to that channel.

The central bank's category is cryptocurrency digital wallets, not Bitcoin alone. The annual figures are as follows.

After the 2021 launch, early reports gave a figure of about $57 million. Later reporting based on central-bank data gave $44.55 million. The former was the sum of $32.3 million in October, $12.3 million in November, and $12.4 million in December. The latter came from subsequently revised figures.

The full-year total for 2022 was about $126.7 million, reportedly around 1.62–1.63% of remittances. Historical versions of the total remittance figure also differed, at $7.742 billion and $7.820 billion.

In 2023 the full-year total was $82.93 million, about 1% of remittances, down roughly 34.5% from the previous year.

In 2024 it was $85.50 million, a slight increase in value, while its share remained around 1%. Total remittances were about $8.4797 billion.

For 2025 the figure was $57.67 million, down another 32.5% year on year. The cited report gave the share as approximately 0.57%, alongside total remittances of $9.98791 billion. Dividing those two listed amounts gives about 0.58%, so the reported share and amounts should not be presented as a perfectly consistent exact calculation without clarification.

The first five months of 2026 produced a cumulative $29.25 million, or 0.69%. This is a year-to-date figure and cannot be compared directly with a full year.

The discrepancy between the two 2021 numbers is worth preserving, rather than “correcting” them into a single figure.

The central bank explicitly states that, under its statistical quality policy, it revises up to the preceding three years of remittance data each January. Final figures for 2021 cited at different publication dates may therefore come from different database snapshots.

More importantly, confidentiality rules prevent the central bank from publishing individual wallet providers' data.

This table therefore cannot establish that Chivo received $126.7 million in remittances in 2022, much less that all $126.7 million was Bitcoin.

It is simply the total for the crypto-wallet category.

There are also verifiable figures for how much conventional remittances cost.

The country is already dollarized, so its main corridor, from the United States, has no exchange-rate spread in the usual sense. In the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide database for the third quarter of 2025, the average total cost among sampled providers was about 3.50%, or $6.99, to send $200, and about 1.87%, or $9.33, to send $500.

This is only a cross-section of market quotations for that quarter, not a causal finding covering 2021 through 2025.

Lower fees today cannot simply be credited to the wallet. Digital competition among multiple remittance providers, promotions, payment methods, and withdrawal methods were affecting prices at the same time.

The question “Did remittance costs actually fall?” must therefore be separated into three levels.

The first is the posted price facing a particular user. Transfers and conversions within this wallet can carry no fee for the end user. For someone who would otherwise have paid a conventional remittance provider and can successfully complete the process, the explicit sending fee can indeed fall from a positive number to zero.

The second is the economic cost of the entire chain. Zero user fees do not mean zero resource costs. Someone still bears the cost of service-provider development, ATMs, cash replenishment, customer service, identity verification, liquidity, and exchange-rate risk. The 2021 law even explicitly required a public trust to support instant conversion.

The more accurate description is therefore that some fees were moved away from end users and shifted, at least in part, to public finances and the system of service-provider contracts.

The third is whether this causally reduced average remittance costs nationwide.

This research found no study making a matched comparison of the same types of remittances from the United States to El Salvador before and after 2021 while accounting for funding the wallet, bid-ask spreads, on-chain and Lightning Network fees, dollar conversion, ATM withdrawals, travel time, and government subsidies.

In 2021 the IMF estimated that, if the system were widely adopted, potential savings in remittance fees could reach about $54 million a year.

The document itself described this as a potential benefit. It was a scenario estimate, not a measured saving after the fact.

In practice, however, the channel's share peaked at only about 1.6% in 2022, subsequently stood at around 1%, and fell to 0.57% in 2025. In the survey, only about 3% of respondents said they had received Bitcoin remittances through the wallet.

There is direct evidence that a zero-fee channel existed.

There is no corresponding measurement supporting a claim that the country's remittance-receiving households consequently saved some enormous sum.

The distance between those statements is the distance that recurs throughout this history: between what something is designed to make possible and how much it actually achieves within a country.

6. One Hundred and Fifty Million Is Not a Loss

In late August 2021, the legislature approved about $150 million in trust funding, administered by the state development bank, for the conversion mechanism.

Public budget reporting from the same period also identified $30 million for sign-up bonuses and about $23.3 million for implementation and infrastructure. The frequently cited initial budget of approximately $203.3 million is the sum of those items and the trust capital.

One point requires particular care: trust capital is not expenditure equivalent to a current-period loss.

A dollar placed in a conversion trust still corresponds to an asset. A $30 bonus becomes a transfer payment when it is actually claimed. Software or maintenance payments to vendors are closer to fiscal consumption costs.

The IMF later summarized public-sector funding for the project at approximately 0.75% of GDP. That likewise cannot be read directly as a net loss, because it combines items of different kinds, including capital funding, bonuses, and infrastructure.

The following three statements are therefore not equivalent.

The government authorized resources on the order of $200 million.

The government actually spent about $200 million.

Taxpayers permanently lost about $200 million.

Public materials provide reasonably good support for the scale of the first statement. They do not automatically establish the other two.

Bitcoin purchases were initially disclosed through the president's social-media announcements, rather than through a transaction-by-transaction treasury ledger or audited statements.

On September 6, 2021, the eve of the law's entry into force, the government first announced a purchase of 200 coins, then total holdings of 400. It added another 150 the next day as the market fell. It subsequently added 150 on September 20, bringing the total to 700; 420 on October 27, bringing it to 1,120; 100 on November 26; 150 on December 3; and twenty-one on December 21, bringing the total to 1,391. On January 21, 2022, it added 410, at an announced cost of about $15 million; on May 9, another 500 for about $15.37 million; and on June 30, another eighty for about $1.52 million. In November 2022 it announced that it would buy one coin a day.

This sequence reconstructs the history of announcements. It does not establish an audited cost basis.

What is missing is execution times, exchanges or market makers, fees, slippage, custody transfers, internal transfers between government entities, and complete cash-payment records after the daily-purchase announcement.

Public data are therefore insufficient to calculate a precise weighted-average cost that would meet judicial or audit standards.

On-chain transparency improved after 2024. The government transferred a substantial portion of its holdings to publicly identified reserve addresses. In August 2025 it announced that around 6,274 coins had been distributed across fourteen new addresses, with no more than 500 per address, and provided a public dashboard.

The balances currently held at those designated addresses thus became verifiable on-chain.

As of August 20–21, 2026, one tracking provider listed a balance of about 7,748 coins based on the public addresses, worth approximately $597 million at the day's prices.

That figure comes from one data provider's point-in-time balances at labeled addresses and current market prices. It is not an officially audited balance sheet for the country's entire public sector.

This reveals an apparent contradiction.

The IMF's first review required that overall public-sector holdings not increase. Meanwhile, the government's public dashboard continued to show rising balances at its reserve addresses.

The two are not necessarily logically inconsistent.

An increase at on-chain reserve addresses can result from transfers within the public sector, address consolidation, or purchases by a particular entity. The IMF program metric may instead measure net exposure across the entire public sector, allowing another public entity to sell or reduce its holdings at the same time.

Without a complete inventory of public-sector addresses and a consolidated balance sheet, growth at a single reserve address cannot establish a breach of the condition. Equally, a commitment concerning the net position alone cannot establish that the government has stopped all on-chain purchases.

The question of how many hundreds of millions El Salvador has now made on Bitcoin therefore needs to be reframed.

Market value can be checked. Total historical cost, the perimeter of holdings across the whole public sector, realized sales, and transaction expenses cannot be fully checked.

On-chain transparency has therefore delivered only half its promise here. It has made holdings verifiable without making their acquisition verifiable.

A sentence from the third essay takes a different direction here. In that case, the data had been there all along, but no one read them. In this case, people are reading, but the half they need to read was never written down.

As for the volcano bond, the proposal announced in 2021 envisaged an issue of about $1 billion, partly to buy Bitcoin and partly to fund city and energy infrastructure. Subsequent digital-asset issuance legislation established a framework. In December 2023 the National Bitcoin Office announced that the National Digital Assets Commission had approved the bond, with a launch expected in the first quarter of 2024.

Regulatory approval, however, is only a step preceding issuance.

As of this research's August 2026 cutoff, no final issuance announcement, pricing book, settlement announcement, or outstanding-principal data matching the original planned scale had been found.

“El Salvador issued a $1 billion volcano bond” must therefore become: the bond was proposed and received regulatory approval, with a launch originally planned for the first quarter of 2024; no primary material confirming completed issuance had been found by the research cutoff.

7. Curso Legal

The amendments passed on January 29, 2025, and published on January 30 have a clear article-by-article correspondence with the original law.

Article 1 retained the term “legal tender” but added a qualification that acceptance by private individuals or wholly private legal entities was voluntary.

Article 3 was narrowed from allowing prices to be expressed in Bitcoin to allowing prices to be converted into Bitcoin.

Article 5 retained the capital-gains-tax exemption for conversion but removed the phrase “as with any legal tender.”

Article 7 removed the requirement that all economic agents accept Bitcoin and instead stated that private individuals and wholly private legal entities could accept it.

Article 11 was amended to provide that the central bank and financial supervisory authority would issue regulations within their respective statutory powers.

Article 12 was amended to require that the state's domestic and foreign monetary obligations be discharged in the currencies in which they were originally contracted.

Article 7 of the amending law directly repealed the original Articles 4, 8, and 9: payment of taxes in Bitcoin, the state-provided transaction and automatic conversion solution, and the authority to issue detailed conversion rules.

The most precise, narrow description is therefore neither that the law was entirely repealed nor that nothing changed.

The statutory text retained the legal-tender designation, while three core operational pillars of the 2021 system were removed: compulsory private acceptance, payment of taxes in Bitcoin, and a state-provided automatic conversion solution. Public-sector monetary obligations were also tied back to their contractual currencies.

The operative verb in Article 7 is now “may accept,” rather than the “must accept” of 2021.

There is another point: the consolidated text still retains the original Article 13, allowing pre-existing dollar debts to be discharged in Bitcoin. The amended text is therefore not a law comprehensively redrafted at the conceptual level with every remaining legal-tender provision removed.

Its connection with the IMF agreement is not merely inferred from the sequence of dates.

The staff-level agreement reached on December 18, 2024, explicitly required a reduction in the project's public-sector risks: private-sector acceptance would become voluntary; taxes would be paid only in dollars; the government would gradually withdraw from the wallet; public-sector involvement in related activities would be restricted; and transparency and regulation would be improved.

El Salvador then amended the law in January 2025. On February 26, 2025, the IMF Executive Board approved a forty-month arrangement worth approximately $1.4 billion.

Here there is a genuine conflict in how the reform is described.

The IMF's documents use a stronger summary than the Salvadoran statute, saying the reform narrowed the law's scope by removing Bitcoin's legal-tender status and making private use voluntary.

The amendments' own legislative rationale, by contrast, emphasizes improving applicability and ensuring Bitcoin's effectiveness and continuity as legal tender.

This does not mean someone is lying.

The IMF describes the substantive position in terms of compulsory acceptance, taxation, and public-sector functions. The Salvadoran statute retains the designation.

A name has been separated from its three supporting pillars. The pillars have been dismantled; the name remains.

This is worth remembering because it is an unusual situation in this field. Usually, promotion outruns substance. Here, two institutions have offered two equally grounded summaries of the same text.

Implementation after the amendments was not immediately completed in 2025 either.

The IMF's first review in June 2025 still required the government to exit the wallet and avoid an increase in overall public-sector holdings. On December 22, 2025, the IMF said negotiations to sell the government electronic wallet had advanced substantially, while a staff-level agreement on the second review had not yet been reached. Transparency, protection of public resources, and risk controls remained under discussion.

As of the August 2026 research cutoff, no subsequent announcement confirming completion of the second Executive Board review had been found. The wallet's completed privatization is therefore not presented as a fact.

Sovereign ratings likewise cannot simply be described as “Bitcoin caused downgrades, and abandoning Bitcoin caused upgrades.”

Rating agencies repeatedly identified financing needs, debt maturities, short-term domestic debt, market access, the IMF agreement, and policy credibility together as drivers.

In July 2021, one agency downgraded the country from B3 to Caa1 with a negative outlook. Its materials at the time cited deteriorating policy quality, high financing needs, and the law's negative implications for an IMF agreement and policy risk.

In February 2022, another agency downgraded the sovereign from B- to CCC, citing financing risks, constrained market access, and uncertainty over an IMF program. It explicitly said that making Bitcoin legal tender increased uncertainty.

Credit pressures eased after timely repayment of a crucial $800 million bond and debt buybacks in 2023. On January 7, 2025, the latter agency upgraded the country from CCC+ to B-, with a stable outlook, explicitly identifying lower financing needs, debt management, and the agreement reached with the IMF as the principal improvements.

On April 23, 2026, it affirmed B- with a stable outlook. The former agency subsequently upgraded its rating to B3.

Sovereign credit had indeed recovered from the extreme stress of 2022, while remaining speculative grade.

That trajectory cannot be attributed solely to rises and falls in Bitcoin's price.

8. No Penalty Record Found

Viewing these years' flows of funds as a system brings us closer to the real world than looking only at how many coins the government bought.

On the taxpayer and public-finance side are approximately $150 million in trust capital, alongside separate bonuses, infrastructure, software, and ATM contracts. On the wallet-user side are the $30 sign-up bonus and very low explicit transaction fees.

On the public conversion mechanism's side sit liquidity risk and part of the price risk when merchants choose immediate conversion. On the merchant's side sits the ability to accept Bitcoin without having to hold it for long.

On the government's side are the $4 million development payment, ATM deployment and maintenance, cash operations, and back-end upgrades. On the vendors' side is contract revenue.

On the conventional remittance providers' side, digital wallets displace some potential transaction volume. On the remittance-receiving households' side, explicit fees can be lower.

The actual share that shifted channels has already been given: a peak of about 1.6%.

Company filings contain another unusual detail. A 2023 filing disclosed that the government had advanced cash to the vendor to replenish ATMs: approximately $1.107 million at the end of 2022 and $273,000 at the end of March 2023.

This was not vendor revenue. It was operating cash for the ATMs and must be distinguished from service fees.

Other material compiled from disclosures put the maintenance price for each ATM at about $1,412.50 a month. Mechanically assuming that all 255 original machines operated for every full month would produce a theoretical annualized figure exceeding $4.3 million.

Machine counts, downtime, and contract changes all vary, however. That multiplication can provide only a sense of the scale implied by the contractual unit price. It cannot be presented as $4.3 million actually paid by the government in a particular year.

The merchant side presents the reverse problem: national surveys measured acceptance rates well but barely measured dollar-denominated compliance costs.

We therefore cannot currently say how much extra the average small food shop spent to accept Bitcoin.

Nor is there reliable nationwide micro-level research on employment, wages, or consumer prices that can causally attribute changes to this law.

That absence is itself a research finding. Filling it with tourism growth, economic growth, or a case study of a seaside town would mix in contemporaneous security policies, recovery from the pandemic, and the rebound in global travel.

Now return to the six distinctions.

Accounts are not individuals: four million is the account scale publicly reported by the government, without a deduplication method.

Registration is not active use: more than half reported downloading the wallet, while only about one-fifth continued after spending the bonus.

Using a dual-currency wallet is not the same as using Bitcoin: continuing users mainly used the dollar functions.

Crypto-wallet remittances are not Chivo remittances: the central bank's category includes other wallets and other assets, and individual institutions' data are confidential by law.

On-chain reserve addresses are not the balance sheet of the entire public sector: those 7,748 coins are the point-in-time balance at labeled addresses.

Trust capital is not a net fiscal loss: a dollar placed in the conversion trust still corresponds to an asset.

The six distinctions are really saying the same thing: every layer of this undertaking has a number, but the numbers do not measure the same thing.

The most important pair of numbers is in Section 4.

The law said that all economic agents must accept Bitcoin. Only those evidently unable to access the technology were exempt.

The survey measured actual acceptance at around one-fifth.

The distance between them was large. But that acceptance rate alone cannot determine business by business who fell within the technological-access exemption and who failed to fulfill an applicable obligation.

The public material located in this research contained no specific record of a penalty imposed for refusing Bitcoin.

In the earlier essays, code could execute automatically once its conditions were met and a transaction was submitted and included. Yet someone still had to trigger liquidation, someone had to bid, and the chain had to remain operational. Here there was an additional layer: merchants and enforcement institutions had to put a legal obligation into practice.

This time was different.

This time execution depended on people: on a merchant installing an app, learning to scan a code, being willing to spend two more minutes at checkout, believing that the number on the screen would still be there tomorrow.

And people can choose not to do it.

That sentence is the real conclusion of this entire history. It is not a criticism.

Reasons for not using a payment method at checkout are often simple: it feels slow, someone does not know how, fears a mistake, or finds that customers do not use it either. Each can matter in practice. The survey did not, however, decompose the roughly 80% nonacceptance rate into these reasons business by business.

On the statutory side, the stated exemption remained genuine lack of the necessary technological access. The nonacceptance rate alone does not tell us how many businesses qualified for it.

Thus, a law addressing economic agents in principle while retaining a technological exemption was followed, about five months after implementation, by a survey measuring actual acceptance at only around one-fifth.

For more than a decade, this field has been doing the same thing: writing rules into code, precisely because rules on paper depend on people to enforce them, and people do not always do so.

This time someone tried the reverse, writing something that had grown within code into a law on paper.

It encountered precisely the problem that had originally led people to write rules into code.

The result points to the same thing.

The chain continued operating with a target average of roughly ten minutes per block. A country writing it into law, or amending those provisions, does not automatically rewrite Bitcoin's consensus rules.

It does not know that a country once wrote it into its statute book.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
