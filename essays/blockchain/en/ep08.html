Two Paths for Stablecoins

Fiat reserves and crypto collateral redistribute the returns, risks, and redemption rights behind a non-interest-bearing dollar.

1. A Dollar That Pays No Interest

The most important difference between the three technical routes for stablecoins is not whether they use a blockchain.

It is which assets ultimately pay when a holder wants to turn a token back into a dollar, and who bears falling asset prices, liquidity risk, and the risk of waiting in line.

The first route uses fiat or traditional financial assets as backing. USDT and USDC belong here. Parity rests on the issuer's bank deposits, short-term government debt, repos, and money-market funds, with minting and redemption at par in the primary market. Failure modes include insufficient reserves, reserve-asset credit or market risk, unavailable banks or custodians, suspended redemption, and disconnection between secondary and primary markets.

The second overcollateralizes with cryptoassets; Maker's DAI is typical. Collateral exceeds stablecoin liabilities and is liquidated when its price falls through a threshold.

Overcollateralization does not mean risk-free. It converts credit risk into collateral-price, oracle, and liquidation-liquidity risk. The Bank for International Settlements makes the same point: a sufficiently large price shock can exhaust the buffer, favor early redeemers, and trigger collateral fire sales.

The third is algorithmic stabilization, adjusting supply by minting and burning another token without requiring an equal amount of external safe assets.

Its strongest counterexample is not an abstract model but Terra. One later regulatory finding deserves separate attention: recovery from a UST depeg in 2021 did not result solely from automatic algorithmic arbitrage. The SEC found that an institution, incentivized by the project, bought more than $20 million of UST, while the market was given the impression that the algorithm had restored the peg itself.

“An algorithmic stablecoin guarantees a dollar through code alone” is therefore not an adequate description of that episode's actual history.

The real machinery of fiat-backed coins is the primary market.

Take a $100,000 mint: the customer's bank account loses $100,000, the issuer or its banking system gains $100,000 in assets, and the issuer simultaneously creates 100,000 on-chain tokens, incurring approximately $100,000 in redemption liabilities to holders.

Redemption runs in exactly the opposite direction.

We can already state what the next seven sections follow.

A holder gives up a dollar of cash for a stablecoin that does not itself pay interest.

The issuer obtains a dollar of reserve assets and places it in government debt, repos, money-market funds, or bank deposits.

When market interest rates are positive, reserves earn income while the fixed redemption liability on the token side remains approximately one dollar.

The question becomes: who owns the dollar redemption claim, and who owns the income-producing asset supporting it?

At zero interest, the difference is almost negligible.

At an interest rate of 5%, an entire industry lies between them.

The remainder is already identifiable. It is not an overlooked asset. Quite the reverse: every cent of that dollar is present, recorded, and inspected by accountants.

The remainder is what the dollar earns after working elsewhere for a year. It never appears on the holder's side of the ledger, because that side always contains only one number: 1.

2. One Hundred Thousand Dollars

Any holder can take one USDT to Tether at any time and exchange it for one dollar.

That sentence needs rewriting, on the issuer's own published rules.

Under Tether's current terms, direct platform purchase or redemption generally requires identity verification and a verified bank account, subject to jurisdiction and customer eligibility. The minimum direct purchase or redemption is $100,000. Redemption costs $1,000 or 0.1%, whichever is greater; purchases generally cost 0.1%.

Circle follows a similar route with a different organization. Circle Mint serves institutional customers such as exchanges, institutional traders, wallets, banks, and applications. Current terms expressly restrict direct minting and redemption to eligible accounts in good standing. Someone acquiring USDC in the secondary market must also satisfy account eligibility and registration requirements to claim redemption directly from Circle.

Owning a token in the secondary market therefore does not confer unrestricted retail access to redemption.

Small holders usually exit through exchanges, market makers, or other intermediaries. Direct primary-market arbitrage is available to customers meeting account, identity, banking, and minimum-size requirements.

These are precisely the people who maintain the peg.

If USDC falls to $0.98 in the secondary market, an institution eligible for direct redemption can buy a million tokens for $980,000, submit them to the issuer, and receive close to $1 million in cash. Buying increases demand while burning the million tokens contracts supply; both push the price toward a dollar. At $1.01, the mechanism reverses.

This is the basic no-arbitrage relationship between primary redemption and secondary prices.

Tether's fee structure shows why that arbitrage has a band.

Suppose 100,000 USDT trades at $0.995. The apparent discount is $500.

But the minimum redemption fee is $1,000, before bank charges, wire fees, and the cost of tying up capital.

That price may therefore not trigger direct redemption arbitrage at the minimum scale. Larger scale reduces fixed cost per unit and makes primary arbitrage more effective.

This clarifies what a peg is. It is not a physical constant, but the result of people calculating that action is worthwhile. That depends on the discount, fees, bank opening hours, and available capital.

One sentence must therefore be precise.

The peg is not a mathematically absolute 1.0000.

It forms within an arbitrage band jointly determined by eligibility, fees, banking hours, asset liquidity, and market-making capital.

The width of that band depends on who qualifies to arbitrage.

The second essay's three conditions must be recalculated here. Calculability broadly holds: fees and terms are public. Choice holds too.

Exit, however, means entirely different things for holders of different sizes.

For an eligible customer meeting the minimum size, it is a channel operating under the terms and banking arrangements—not an unrestricted promise of instant payment. Account standing, bank availability, and any redemption suspension matter. Someone holding only a few hundred dollars cannot use that direct channel at that balance size.

This is not an accusation. Thresholds have practical reasons: each primary redemption needs banking, identity checks, and human processing, with costs largely independent of amount. Direct small redemptions are economically unworkable.

The point is the promise's exact scope. Direct redemption at par depends on eligibility, size, fees, and processing conditions; it is not available to every holder at any moment. Customers meeting those conditions can conduct primary-market arbitrage. Other holders mainly benefit from the secondary-market price that arbitrage helps maintain.

3. Twenty-Seven Point Six

Tether's reserve history cannot be reduced to “it once had no reserves, then it did,” nor can today's reserve table establish the position in 2017.

The early central claim was that every token was backed by one dollar in traditional fiat reserves. The CFTC's later formal order used those contemporary claims as its benchmark.

“Fully backed” today encompasses a much broader asset concept. It no longer means precisely one bank-cash dollar behind every token.

Several comparable points show the change in composition.

At March 31, 2021, commercial paper represented approximately 49.60% of total reserves, fiduciary deposits 18.36%, bank cash 2.94%, reverse repos 2.73%, and US Treasury bills only 2.23%. Secured loans accounted for 12.55%, and corporate bonds, funds, and precious metals together 9.96%.

At June 30, 2022, Treasury bills accounted for 43.45%, commercial paper and certificates of deposit 12.65%, money-market funds 10.25%, and cash and bank deposits 8.16%. Commercial paper and CDs fell from approximately $20 billion the previous quarter to $8.402 billion.

By end-2022, total reserves were $67.044 billion, Treasury bills accounted for 58.51%, and commercial paper and CDs had reached zero. Three quarters later, total assets were $86.385 billion and direct Treasury bills had risen to 65.53%.

Two years later, scale had more than doubled. End-2025 reserves totaled $192.878 billion, with $122.326 billion in direct Treasury bills, 63.42%, and roughly a tenth in overnight reverse repos. The table also included $17.450 billion in precious metals, $8.430 billion in bitcoin, and $17.044 billion in secured loans, together more than a fifth.

At June 30, 2026, total assets were $187.751 billion, total liabilities $183.642 billion, and the difference $4.110 billion. Direct Treasury bills were $114.961 billion, 61.23%; overnight and term repos together $25.619 billion, 13.64%; precious metals $18.838 billion, 10.03%; bitcoin 3.09%; listed equities 2.00%; and secured loans $13.454 billion, 7.17%.

This time series has three methodological traps that cannot be omitted.

First, the 2021 disclosure grouped many items into “cash, cash equivalents, short-term deposits and commercial paper,” a category accounting for 75.85%.

Calling that “75.85% cash” is wrong. Its largest component was commercial paper.

Second, after 2023 two formulations often appeared together: directly held Treasury bills, and direct plus indirect Treasury exposure.

Treasuries held by money-market funds, Treasury collateral in repos, and directly held bills differ in legal rights, liquidity, and accounting. The 2026 attestation listed bills and repo assets separately. Treasury collateral supplied by repo counterparties cannot be added again as an extra asset.

Third, circulating market capitalization, cumulative issuance, and accounting token liabilities are not identical. The 2026 disclosure gave a contractual redemption total of roughly $184.589 billion, while some company-held tokens were outside treasury wallets and accounting liabilities reflected a contractual redemption fee of 0.1%.

Multiplying on-chain token quantity by a dollar therefore does not mechanically equal reported liabilities.

The two regulatory sources also have different evidentiary standing.

On April 25, 2019, the New York Attorney General sought and obtained interim judicial measures under the Martin Act. Allegations included Bitfinex's inability to access approximately $850 million of customer and company funds entrusted to a payment processor, followed by a reserve-credit arrangement of up to about $900 million between the affiliates. The office then said at least approximately $700 million of Tether reserves had already been used.

An interim injunction and investigative authorization are not a court finding that the allegations have been proved.

The February 23, 2021, settlement is another layer. Announcing it, the Attorney General said its investigation found the claim of full one-dollar backing at all times untrue. It highlighted a period beginning in mid-2017 when normal banking access was unavailable and corresponding dollar reserves were absent at certain times. Cash used for one 2017 verification had entered the account only that morning.

These were the investigating authority's findings in settlement materials, not judicial findings after a jury trial. The settlement itself followed no merits trial.

Verifiable terms included an $18.5 million payment, cessation of relevant transactions with New York customers, periodic reports on customer-fund segregation and intercompany transfers, and public reserve breakdowns including related-party loans and receivables.

The CFTC's October 15, 2021, order had a different evidentiary status.

It was a settled enforcement order imposing a $41 million penalty. Formal findings addressed public statements from at least June 2016 through February 2019, finding the claims of sufficient dollar reserves misleading.

Across the approximately 26 months sampled, sufficient fiat reserves to cover circulation were visible in the company's own bank accounts on only 27.6% of days.

That number is very often misstated.

27.6% does not mean only 27.6% of USDT was asset-backed.

The CFTC measured the proportion of sampled days with sufficient fiat reserves in the company's own bank accounts, testing the contemporary public claim of complete dollar-reserve backing.

It cannot directly become the daily ratio of all assets to circulation, since the company also held other assets.

Precisely because full disclosure was absent then, these two questions cannot substitute for one another.

The same order contains another detail: before an accounting check on a predetermined date, Bitfinex transferred approximately $382 million to Tether, and that point-in-time balance was then used for verification.

This explains why a sufficient bank balance on one day cannot be conflated with a full audit covering a continuous period.

4. Attestation Is Not Audit

Here, more than anywhere in the debate, terminology needs precision.

The quarterly documents from 2022 to 2026 were titled assurance opinions under International Standard on Assurance Engagements 3000 (Revised).

The accountants expressly described a reasonable-assurance engagement covering specified financial data and reserves, conducted under a standard for assurance engagements other than audits or reviews of historical financial information. Their conclusions were limited to one date, provided no assurance for other dates, and did not extend the same assurance to the notes.

Tether's own schedule expressly said it was not the company's financial statements, but financial information extracted from accounting records.

Calling those quarterly documents “quarterly audits” before 2026 is therefore inaccurate.

The narrower wording is that Tether for years supplied independent reserve attestations, usually covering reserves and liabilities on a particular date, rather than audits of complete annual financial statements.

As of today, another formerly correct sentence has also become outdated.

On August 13, 2026, Tether announced that KPMG had completed its first full independent financial-statement audit for the year ended December 31, 2025, with an unqualified opinion.

That is materially broader in both subject and scope than the earlier point-in-time reserve attestations.

The source cited here, however, is the company's announcement of the audit conclusion, not the audit report itself. Its evidentiary level should therefore be stated as a company announcement relaying the auditor's unqualified opinion.

A numerical discrepancy also deserves preservation rather than an explanation supplied on the company's behalf.

The end-2025 reserve attestation listed surplus reserves of approximately $6.338 billion.

The August 13, 2026, audit announcement said audited statements showed reserves exceeding liabilities by $6.814 billion.

The difference is approximately $476 million.

Audit adjustments or differing definitions and scope may explain it. Without reconciling the complete audited statements and attestation schedule item by item, one cannot explain the difference away.

This section is really making a more general point.

Different technical meanings of “audit” can allow apparently contradictory claims to have been true in different periods.

Before the August 13, 2026 announcement, the precise point was that Tether had not announced completion of this kind of full annual financial-statement audit. Its public quarterly reserve attestations were not a substitute.

Calling the quarterly reserve attestations discussed here full audits of the company's financial statements remains inaccurate.

This is not wordplay. It determines what a document assures, for which date, and what it expressly does not assure.

The distinction matters throughout this domain, where almost every kind of trust is built on a document. Differences between documents are often more important than the difference between having a document and having none.

Between a reserve-only attestation confined to one day and expressly excluding other dates, and a full-year audit of complete financial statements, the gap is one of scope, not simply strictness.

5. Eight Percent and Thirteen Percent

One of USDC's largest structural differences from Tether was its earlier narrowing of reserves to cash, short-term government debt, and later an externally managed government money-market fund.

March 2023 demonstrated something else: high credit quality does not mean every dollar of cash can be withdrawn from banks immediately at all times.

Follow the timeline day by day.

On Thursday, March 9, Circle attempted to move funds out of Silicon Valley Bank. It later disclosed that the transfer had not completed before the bank entered receivership.

On Friday, March 10, the bank was closed and placed in receivership. Circle subsequently confirmed that approximately $3.3 billion of USDC cash reserves remained there.

The market's question was not whether an on-chain contract had failed, but whether those dollars would be available when banks reopened.

On Saturday, March 11, with banks closed and primary dollar settlement restricted while secondary crypto markets traded around the clock, USDC fell materially below a dollar. The widely cited low was about $0.88; another provider's aggregate low was $0.8726.

Some exchanges saw smaller trades at lower prices. Isolated trades in extremely thin liquidity should not be treated as the whole market's true low.

On Sunday, March 12, US authorities announced protection of the bank's depositors, making all deposits accessible on Monday.

On Monday, March 13, banks reopened, the $3.3 billion became available, and the price recovered toward par.

Now place two figures together.

Circle described the $3.3 billion as approximately 8% of total reserves.

About 92%, in other words, was not at that bank.

Uncertainty over that 8% led the market to discount the dollar by about 13%.

Together, these figures do not establish irrationality. They show that during that weekend no one knew whether the 8% would become a permanent hole, and everyone knew they could not immediately find out.

Was this a crypto-system problem or a traditional-banking problem? Primary material supports two simultaneous explanations answering different questions.

One is that the direct shock came from traditional banking. The $3.3 billion exposure arose not from a smart-contract bug, chain attack, or crypto-collateral crash, but from the issuer's deposit at a conventional commercial bank. Once authorities guaranteed full deposit availability, concern over that particular shortfall disappeared.

The other is that the depeg's magnitude still reflected stablecoin architecture's own liquidity problem. Tokens trade on-chain and on exchanges continuously over weekends; supporting bank accounts, wires, and primary redemption do not operate in sync around the clock. Ordinary secondary holders may also lack direct issuer redemption. Even if no bank cash is ultimately lost, temporary unavailability can produce a secondary-market discount.

There is no contradiction.

The first explains where potential loss came from. The second explains why a bank's weekend event could become a stablecoin market-price event within hours.

That second mechanism connects directly to section two.

If every holder had unrestricted direct redemption, this discount would have taken a very different shape. One reason it could widen to 13% was that those able to redeem at par also could not obtain bank cash that weekend.

Arbitrage is normally the system's most reliable channel. It depends on banks being open.

That weekend, the chain produced blocks normally and exchanges matched trades normally, while the channel's other end was closed.

Circle subsequently reorganized its reserves.

By March 31, 2026, approximately 86% of USDC reserves sat in the Circle Reserve Fund, with the rest in cash across several banks. The externally managed, SEC-registered government money-market fund held only US Treasuries with no more than three months remaining, overnight Treasury repos, and cash.

Transparency operated at two frequencies, which must not be conflated.

Reserve assets and minting/burning flows were disclosed weekly.

Accountants provided monthly third-party attestations on matters such as whether reserve value exceeded circulating supply.

Those monthly attestations were still not monthly audits of the company.

6. Sixty Percent

The business model is clearest in Circle's public financial filings after listing.

The revenue trajectory was approximately $15.4 million in 2020, $84.9 million in revenue and reserve income in 2021, $772 million in 2022, $1.5 billion in 2023, and $1.7 billion in 2024.

Yet 2024 net income was only approximately $155.7 million.

Between gross reserve income and shareholders' ultimate profits stood substantial distribution, transaction, personnel, compliance, and other costs.

Post-listing filings clarify the money flow further.

For the first nine months through September 30, 2025, total revenue and reserve income was $1.976 billion, against $1.201 billion in distribution and transaction costs.

The latter was approximately 60.8% of the former.

This cannot simply mean 60% of interest went to one distributor: the accounting line includes other distribution and transaction costs.

The first half of 2026 showed a similar relationship: approximately $1.395 billion versus $815.8 million.

What really matters is the publicly disclosed sharing mechanism.

Circle's annual report describes the current economics as based on net reserve income: third-party reserve-management fees and other items are deducted first, then allocation depends on USDC balances on Circle's platform, Coinbase's platform, and in the wider ecosystem.

Of remaining income generated outside the platforms, after approved third-party ecosystem incentives, Coinbase can receive approximately half.

Pause on that sentence.

Under this arrangement, even balances held outside the two platforms can send part of net reserve income to Coinbase, through a path holders may not know about. Where tokens sit and who holds them are different questions from who ultimately shares their reserve income.

That company is neither the issuer nor the bearer of the token's redemption liability.

To be fair, this money is not unearned. Distributors supply access, balances, and liquidity, real conditions for widespread use. The contract is public, in a listed company's annual report, available for anyone to read.

The point is the chain's shape: income originates at the reserve end, while allocation occurs farther from the holder.

Now answer how much the holder receives.

USDC itself passes zero issuer reserve interest to ordinary holders.

Circle's terms expressly reserve to the company interest or income generated by segregated reserves. The token's core claim is fixed-par redemption, not a share in reserve-fund returns.

Some exchanges separately reward USDC accounts. These are their own commercial programs, not an interest entitlement embedded in the token.

A platform's advertised reward rate and the issuer's reserve yield therefore are not the same concept.

The former is commercial income a distributor chooses to return to particular account customers, potentially changing by region, account tier, and policy.

Recipients are only a subset of all holders.

They have something in common: they keep their tokens on a platform. Withdraw to a personal wallet, and one gains complete private-key control but loses the reward.

This recurring trade-off in the domain is particularly plain in stablecoins.

Circle's financial filings also contain a verifiable interest-rate sensitivity.

At September 30, 2025, its analysis used an average yield of approximately 4.10%. A parallel one-percentage-point rate shift modeled a roughly $740 million change in reserve income over the following twelve months and about $375 million of distribution and transaction costs moving in the same direction.

This was not realized revenue, but a scenario based on balances at the time.

It directly shows that a substantial portion of additional income from higher reserve yields flows to partners through distribution contracts.

Tether does not disclose contracts at comparable granularity, but the reserve-profit outline is clear.

The company announced approximately $13 billion in 2024 profit, including roughly $7 billion from conventional Treasury and repo income and about $5 billion from unrealized gold and bitcoin valuation gains.

Calling all $13 billion reserve interest is therefore also inaccurate.

The 2025 annual financial result was approximately $10.106 billion, alongside about $10.855 billion distributed to shareholders as dividends.

Place the four parties together, and income and risk do not fully coincide.

Holders obtain a roughly one-dollar token, liquidity, and exchangeability. They bear issuer risk, secondary-market depegging, primary-redemption eligibility, and banking-channel risk, generally without direct reserve interest.

Issuers receive reserve investment returns and some fees, bearing reserve-asset, custody, banking, operational, regulatory, and redemption-liquidity risks.

Distributors receive reserve-related income under public agreements and may choose to reward end customers. They bear platform-operation and customer costs, but are not the ultimate obligor for token redemption.

Banks, money-market funds, and Treasury markets receive deposits, fund assets, or demand for government debt, bearing traditional-finance risks.

One evidentiary distinction remains: Circle discloses the distributor's intermediate share quite specifically, whereas Tether has not published comparably granular material permitting systematic quantification of global exchange and distribution-partner shares.

That gap is itself a condition of the evidence. Industry rumor cannot fill it.

7. Zero Point Seven One Basis Points

One real-world measurement deserves separate attention, because nothing like it has appeared earlier in this series.

The BIS constructed an identification approach using daily data from January 2021 to March 2026, then estimated local projections.

It found that approximately $3.5 billion in stablecoin inflows lowered three-month US Treasury yields by about 0.71 basis points that day, with a cumulative effect of around 4 basis points after ten days and a trough of roughly 5 near day thirteen. Effects were concentrated at the short end and stronger when Treasury intermediation was under stress.

This is the series' first measurable effect of an on-chain object on one of the most central off-chain prices.

In the first seven essays, the real-world section was either thin or negative: electricity, unemployment, death, losses.

This time, there is a measurable, two-way effect at the heart of traditional finance.

It does not mean each additional $3.5 billion permanently reduces rates by 4 basis points. This is a local effect under a particular shock, dynamic path, and identification framework.

The same mechanism reverses during redemption, with larger effects. A summary of existing research says reverse shocks can have absolute effects up to roughly three times the baseline.

Both ends must therefore be described.

Minting inflows prompt issuers to buy short-term debt, increasing Treasury demand.

Large redemptions require cash, which issuers may obtain by waiting for assets to mature, using repo funding, or selling short-term debt. Only the last directly adds outright selling pressure; the other routes depend on funding arrangements and liquidity conditions.

Tether's scale must likewise be separated by asset type. The June 30, 2026, attestation directly identifies $114.961 billion in Treasury bills and another approximately $25.619 billion in repo assets.

These cannot all be rewritten as approximately $140 billion of directly held US Treasuries.

Then comes payments, the essay's emptiest passage.

Summarizing existing measurements, the BIS put 2025 nominal on-chain stablecoin volume at approximately $35 trillion, against estimated payment-related flows of only about $390 billion.

The figures do not use identical classifications, so precise division is inappropriate. They do reliably show a gap of nearly two orders of magnitude between nominal transfers and identifiable actual payments.

An adjustment method explains why raw on-chain volume is so large. It applies at least three treatments: counting only the largest one-way transfer within a complex transaction to avoid internal contract duplication; filtering unlabeled addresses for high frequency and large volume; and using address labels to classify activity.

Adjusted volume is still not synonymous with actual payments. The method expressly retains centralized and decentralized exchanges, lending, minting and burning, and on/off-ramping. An adjusted figure can still contain trading, arbitrage, inter-exchange movements, and on-chain finance, rather than just purchases, wages, and supplier payments.

Evidence of use in high-inflation countries is relatively strong, but correlation still cannot simply be called remittances.

A cross-border study found, within its sample and model, that inferred cross-border USDC flows were roughly 50% higher and USDT flows almost 20% higher when the sending or receiving country had high inflation.

This is a statistical association for a particular period, not a claim that every high-inflation household worldwide buys stablecoins.

The most cited user-composition source is not a census either.

It was a 2024 survey of 2,541 self-identified crypto users across five countries. Published results showed about 47% citing access to dollar exposure, about 39% reporting international remittance use, and around 23% reporting stablecoin wage payments or receipts.

The denominator must remain: 2,541 surveyed crypto users, not all residents of those countries.

“39% of people in emerging markets use stablecoins for remittances” is therefore an unsupported expansion.

Independent evidence on cross-border costs is scarcer than industry promotion.

The World Bank's remittance-price database currently covers 367 corridors, 48 sending countries, and 105 receiving countries, measuring end-user total costs for small cross-border transfers.

It does not provide a stable global panel identifying which stablecoin a particular transaction used.

One therefore cannot directly read from it how much stablecoin adoption reduced global remittance costs.

This also means a common comparison needs separating: “stablecoin cross-border transfers cost cents, while conventional remittances cost percentage points” usually compares on-chain fees with conventional remittances' full end-user costs.

Actual user costs also include local-currency purchase spreads, exchange or OTC charges, dollar premiums, recipient cash-out, local exchange-rate spreads, compliance costs, and bank withdrawal fees.

Available public independent research has not covered enough real corridors with one method to produce a large-sample before-and-after comparison of all these items.

The absence of data matters more here than an industry average.

Transaction-size distributions are another conspicuous gap. On-chain amounts can of course be calculated precisely, but include exchange hot/cold-wallet movements, market making, bridges, and batch settlement. A median across all transfers answers how large on-chain transfers are, not how large actual individual payments are.

Together, this section presents the remainder's second shape.

The Treasury end is measured precisely, down to 0.71 basis points, with identification methods, confidence intervals, and reverse-effect estimates. The payment end is almost empty: nominal and real volume differ by two orders of magnitude, without an accepted classification bridging them.

The precisely measured end concerns effects on traditional finance. The poorly measured end concerns effects on ordinary people.

8. Narrow

Regulation changed fundamentally in 2025. As of today, however, enacted law must be distinguished from fully effective implementing rules.

The US GENIUS Act became law on July 18, 2025. It takes effect at the earlier of eighteen months after enactment or 120 days after the principal federal regulators issue final implementing rules.

As of August 2026, agencies were still issuing and consulting on proposed rules; the latest material therefore continued to give January 18, 2027, as the expected statutory date.

The law requires permitted issuers to maintain identifiable reserves of at least one-to-one against circulating stablecoins. The FDIC's proposed implementation specifies eligible assets: US cash, Federal Reserve balances, qualifying demand deposits, Treasuries with no more than 93 days remaining, tightly conditioned overnight repos and reverse repos, and registered investment companies investing only in those eligible assets. It also restricts pledging, rehypothecation, and reserve reuse.

The proposed rule also calls for monthly reserve-composition disclosures showing asset categories, average maturity, and custody location. Redemption would generally take no more than two business days, with specified situations such as significant redemption requests permitting an application to the FDIC for an extension.

And one further provision: the minimum redemption amount may not exceed one stablecoin, still subject to appropriate screening and onboarding.

Pause there, because it directly addresses section two's $100,000 threshold.

Interest is more direct: the law prohibits permitted issuers from paying interest or yield solely because a holder holds, uses, or retains a stablecoin.

It does not automatically equate third-party exchanges' self-funded promotional rewards with an issuer-conferred legal yield entitlement.

In the EU, tokens pegged to one official currency are generally electronic-money tokens under MiCA. Rules require issuance and redemption at par on demand, while issuers and relevant service providers may not pay holders interest for holding them.

In Singapore's framework, a particularly clear holder right is redemption at par within five business days of a valid request.

Hong Kong's licensing regime took effect on August 1, 2025.

Now compare these rules mechanically, without value judgment, with Tether's June 30, 2026, table.

Direct Treasury bills at about 61.2% and repos at 13.6% broadly align with those eligible-asset categories.

Gold at 10.0%, bitcoin at 3.1%, public equities at 2.0%, other investments at 2.8%, and secured loans at 7.2% do not belong to that set of permissible one-to-one reserve assets.

Whether such assets may sit within additional proprietary capital at a company subject to the law is a separate question. Assets exceeding liabilities do not automatically turn them into statutory one-to-one reserves.

Together, these regimes reveal a specific structural change.

From regulators' balance-sheet perspective, payment stablecoins increasingly must resemble narrow monetary liabilities: fully backed by short-duration, highly liquid assets, redeemable at par, with holders not directly sharing reserve income.

This differs from banks' traditional use of short-term deposits to make longer-term loans.

The final clause deserves separate attention.

Holders not directly sharing reserve income is now more than a contractual term. It is law.

Legislators' rationale is straightforward: if holding generates a return, the token looks more like a deposit or fund share, each governed by its own regulatory system. Prohibiting interest helps keep it outside those two regimes.

The first section's minimalist balance sheet has thus received legal confirmation.

Ten years ago, the non-interest-bearing dollar was a company's contractual choice. Today it is a statutory provision.

Both sides of the shadow-banking debate deserve presentation.

Supporters of the label stress three points: issuers offer the public monetary liabilities close to redeemable at par on demand; reserves invest in traditional financial assets; and issuers are not commercial banks subject to deposit insurance and bank-capital frameworks. The 2021 reserve table's extensive commercial paper, secured loans, and other investments especially involved money-market-fund-like liquidity and credit transformation.

Those limiting the label observe that narrow-reserve stablecoins need not perform banking's core activity: taking demand deposits to create long-term loans. A stablecoin genuinely backed entirely by short-term government debt and cash, without credit expansion, may be described more precisely as a tokenized narrow-bank liability than a shadow commercial bank.

Tether currently sits between the two.

Return to section one.

The reserve-backed payment stablecoins discussed here separate redemption at a stated face value from rights to reserve income. Holding the token itself generally does not confer a share of that interest. This is not a description of every algorithmic, overcollateralized, or separately yield-bearing stablecoin arrangement.

What changed is what the backing dollar bought. In 2021, almost half of Tether's reserves was commercial paper. In the 2026 material used here, Treasury bills and repos formed the main part, alongside gold, bitcoin, loans, and other assets.

Who shares the income changed too. In 2020, one issuer's entire revenue was $15.4 million. In the first nine months of 2025, the distribution-channel expense alone was $1.2 billion.

The direction has not changed.

What does not rise with reserve earnings is the stated one-dollar redemption face value—not a market price guaranteed to remain one dollar, or an on-chain balance field permanently reading 1.

How much the dollar beside it did elsewhere, how much it earned, and how many hands it passed through: no on-chain field records that.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
