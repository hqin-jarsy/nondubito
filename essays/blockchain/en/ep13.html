Hashrate Migration

How hashrate migrated across borders after China’s mining crackdown: block records support estimates of network hash power, but reveal little about electricity prices, jobs, and costs borne locally.

1. Not a Single Day

This episode lends itself to an excessively tidy story: China suddenly banned mining in May 2021, mining machines immediately shipped to the United States and Kazakhstan, the U.S. grid gained flexible demand, and local communities gained large numbers of jobs.

The primary materials do not support such a tidy version.

Inner Mongolia acted before May 21. In early 2021, amid controls on both energy intensity and total consumption, the autonomous region proposed clearing out cryptocurrency-mining projects, with exit in principle by the end of April and a strict ban on new projects. On May 18, its development and reform commission also established a reporting platform.

This round should be understood as an energy-policy-driven removal, not a nationwide criminal prohibition.

On May 21, the State Council's Financial Stability and Development Committee explicitly called, at its fifty-first meeting, for a crackdown on Bitcoin mining and trading.

That established the national political direction. The meeting itself supplied no implementation rules for disconnecting individual mining facilities.

On May 25, Inner Mongolia's development and reform commission issued eight draft measures for public comment. They established separate penalty pathways for industrial parks, data centers, captive power plants, and entities providing mining facilities with premises and electricity. The principal tools were withdrawal of preferential policies, credit-related sanctions, and restrictions on power supply.

June brought local implementation through different instruments. These documents differ substantially in public availability and should be treated separately.

On June 9, Qinghai's industry and information-technology authorities issued a comprehensive shutdown notice, prohibiting new approvals, ordering existing operations closed, and requiring inspections of mines established under labels such as big-data and supercomputing centers, along with entities providing them with premises and power. Reporters verified the internal document's existence with the department at the time.

That same day, the development and reform commission of Changji Prefecture, Xinjiang, issued a suspension notice for the Zhundong development zone. It required relevant enterprises there to suspend production for rectification by 2 p.m. that day and report the results.

The verifiable notice directly addressed Zhundong. It cannot by itself establish that all Xinjiang shut down simultaneously on June 9 under the same document.

Around June 11, Yunnan's energy bureau required clearance by the end of June, focusing on unauthorized dedicated lines, evasion of transmission and distribution tariffs and government levies, unauthorized power supply by generators, and electrical safety.

Its language was narrower than Sichuan's. It used unlawful and noncompliant electricity consumption as the enforcement instrument; the document itself did not announce an unconditional same-day shutdown of every mine.

On June 18, Sichuan's development and reform commission and energy bureau required screening, clearance, and closure by June 20 of 26 suspected projects identified by State Grid Sichuan. Central-government-owned generators in Sichuan and provincially owned generators were to stop supplying such projects immediately.

The genuinely unified national framework came in an eleven-department notice issued September 3 and made public September 24: strictly prohibit new projects; accelerate orderly exit of existing ones; prohibit mining disguised as data centers; strengthen energy and electricity controls; treat mining as an industry designated for elimination; exclude it from electricity markets; end fiscal, tax, and financial support; and implement these measures at different administrative levels.

A separate ten-department notice was signed on September 15 and published on September 24. It mainly addressed cryptocurrency trading, payments, and overseas platforms serving domestic residents. It was not the mining-exit document.

News accounts often combine them as “the September ban.” They should be separated.

A formulation closer to the documents is therefore this: after the May 21 policy signal, local authorities rapidly curtailed physical mining facilities in late May and June through energy-consumption controls, power supply, and administrative inspections. The September 3 notice institutionalized those earlier local actions as a nationwide prohibition on new projects and orderly exit for existing ones.

Another distinction is often flattened.

Before the ban, Sichuan was a characteristic wet-season mining region. Local energy authorities even examined, in early June, the effect of mining's exit on hydropower absorption.

Nevertheless, the June 18 order required clearance and shutdown.

The materials therefore do not support the claim that the central government removed only coal-powered mines while allowing hydropower mines to remain.

Laying out these dates reveals something needed throughout the next seven sections.

What was being shut down appears on-chain as one number: network hashrate. On the ground, it consisted of Inner Mongolia's captive power plants, Qinghai's supercomputing-center labels, Xinjiang's development zone, Yunnan's dedicated lines, and Sichuan's hydropower stations.

One number, five provincial-level regions, five enforcement instruments.

The remainder already appears here. The chain can see the number falling, but has no field recording the level at which the decline occurred, whose hands it passed through, or who bore its costs.

2. Sixty-Five or Ninety

This section begins with something many people do not know.

Network hashrate itself cannot be directly observed.

Public hashrate series are estimates inferred from the number of blocks mined and the prevailing difficulty, not a census of the world's mining machines.

Figures of 58, 65, or 94 on a particular day can therefore all be valid if observation windows, smoothing methods, and sampled block times differ.

At the scale of calendar-month averages suggested by public daily series, 2021 roughly took this shape: January around 145–155 EH/s; February broadly flat; March rising; April near a local high at approximately 165–175.

A rapid decline began during May, bringing the month to approximately 155–170.

June, when Sichuan, Xinjiang, and other regions shut facilities, fell to approximately 105–120.

July had the lowest monthly-average range, approximately 90–105.

In August, overseas restarts began showing clearly, bringing the average back to approximately 105–120; September was around 120–135; October around 140–150, approaching pre-ban levels; November around 155–165; and December around 170–180, with network-level recovery largely complete.

These figures are rounded to roughly 5–10 EH/s to show the shape, not manufacture false precision.

The lowest point is more sensitive than the lowest month.

One data provider's twenty-four-hour measure gave a local low around 65 EH/s near June 28, while observing an average block interval approaching 1,958 seconds at the time.

Other sources or windows could produce around 58. Longer-window averages could instead fall around 90.

They do not measure the same statistic, so none should be selected as the network's exact minimum hashrate.

Common estimates for April to early May before the ban were around 180. Whichever window is used, June and July therefore correspond to a short-term contraction of approximately half or more.

Both sides are clear here. This is the essay's first and only completely automatic redistribution.

The reduction in visible hashrate inside China corresponded to a temporary slowing of block production across the network.

When competitors switch off, a remaining miner immediately has a larger share of the network's hash power. Before difficulty falls, however, the expected coins produced by that miner's own fixed hash power per unit of time do not automatically increase: the whole network produces blocks more slowly. A subsequent difficulty reduction is what increases expected output per unit of time for the same hash power.

This redistribution came from the protocol's own difficulty-adjustment mechanism, not relocation subsidies paid to overseas miners.

No negotiation, no contract, no person handling the transaction.

The adjustment could be substantial. If network hash power remained at half its former level and difficulty subsequently fell by roughly half, a surviving miner using the same hash power at similar electricity costs could expect nearly twice the coin output per unit of time. That is an effect after difficulty falls, not an automatic doubling while waiting for the retarget.

The miner may not have moved, switched off, or replaced a machine. The change comes from other miners leaving and from the retarget that follows. How long the improvement lasts still depends on returning hash power, later difficulty changes, coin prices, and fee income.

Everything in the next six sections, by contrast, required someone's intervention.

Remember that contrast. The protocol's own redistribution was the only adjustment in this history requiring no negotiation, no contract, and no signature.

The adjustment follows the preset rule of one retarget every 2,016 blocks, applying the same difficulty to the same amount of hash power. The intended interval is about two weeks under normal conditions. When the network slows sharply, completing those blocks takes longer; adjustment is not guaranteed within fourteen days.

Where to move machines, where to obtain electricity, whether a locality would offer concessions, and where to recruit workers: none of these was automatic.

3. An IP Address Is Not a Machine

What the most widely quoted country map actually measures must be stated clearly.

There is no “mining-machine country” field on-chain.

Cambridge partners with several mining pools. The pools calculate geographic distributions from the IP addresses of miners connecting to their servers, then submit aggregated results. Cambridge does not directly receive every miner's raw IP address. It then extrapolates this pool sample to the whole network.

Sample coverage is not 100%. The methodological documentation gives approximate coverage of 37% from September 2019 to April 2020; 32% from May 2020 to January 2021; 35% from February to April 2021; 34% from May to August; 38% from August to November; and 33% from December 2021 to January 2022.

The U.S. figure of 35.4% should therefore be described as Cambridge's estimate of network hashrate share extrapolated from its mining-pool sample, not a U.S. government census of mining machines.

Several snapshots show the migration clearly.

In September 2019, China accounted for approximately 75.5%, the United States only around 4.1%, and Kazakhstan around 1.4%.

By April 2021, the last relatively complete pre-ban observations, China had fallen to approximately 46%, the United States had risen to around 16.8%, Kazakhstan to around 8.2%, and Russia to around 6.8%, while Canada remained in the low single digits.

The June snapshot showed China falling rapidly, the United States at 21.8%, and Kazakhstan at 8.8%.

In July, China's share in the sample was approximately zero.

The August snapshot showed the United States at 35.40%, Kazakhstan at 18.10%, Russia at 11.23%, and Canada at 9.55%.

That snapshot became the most frequently cited basis for calling the United States first and Kazakhstan second.

The January 2022 update showed the United States at 37.84%, China at 21.11%, Kazakhstan at 13.22%, Canada at 6.48%, and Russia at 4.66%.

The largest statistical warning here is not a decimal-place error.

It is that an IP address is not a machine's physical location.

Cambridge itself notes that VPNs, proxy servers, and mining-pool connection architectures can distort location. Ireland and Germany at times showed shares far exceeding their known physical mining industries, while anomalous IP distributions in certain provinces also required special treatment.

The most revealing evidence is China's own curve.

It fell almost to zero in the July–August sample, then quickly reappeared after September with an estimated share above 20%.

Physical mining machines could not have moved out of China on a massive scale in one month and then all moved back.

At least part of the rebound therefore reflects underground operations, IP obfuscation, or operations becoming visible again.

“China's entire hashrate moved to the United States” must consequently be rewritten: China's share in Cambridge's visible sample briefly approached zero in mid-2021, while the sample shares of the United States, Kazakhstan, Russia, and Canada increased substantially.

The database cannot match every formerly Chinese-located machine one-to-one with an overseas IP address.

It establishes a major rearrangement of global geographic distribution, not a complete inventory of machine relocations.

This is the same kind of problem as the five measuring instruments in Essay 2, but one level more specific.

There, the same object had five measures. Here, the object cannot be directly measured at all. Since the chain has no location field, location must be inferred from the origin of a connection, and that origin can be changed.

The most widely quoted map thus measures, in effect, the addresses from which miners are willing to connect.

It remains the best public material on the subject. The point is not that it should not be used, but that its qualification is almost always dropped when it is quoted.

4. Sold Like Scrap Iron

The U.S. share on a network chart can jump more than ten percentage points in a few months. The bottlenecks of physical relocation are far more complicated.

When Reuters interviewed mine operators, hosting providers, and industry participants in July 2021, their shared judgment was that even large Chinese miners who had found destinations in the United States, Russia, or Kazakhstan might need months to restart.

Overseas data centers lacked sufficient ready capacity in grid connections, substations, racks, cooling, and on-site construction.

Small miners might not be able to afford relocation at all.

Nor was logistics governed by a single price list.

Two main routes were available. Sea freight had the lowest unit cost, but global container rates were under pressure in 2021. Industry logistics estimates showed sharp increases that year in forty-foot container prices, while air freight could cost several times as much.

Air freight's value was compressing weeks of transit into days. A newer ASIC with packaging weighs more than ten kilograms; with thousands or tens of thousands of machines, weight rapidly turns air freight into a major cash outlay.

It would be inappropriate to state a fixed dollar cost per machine for shipping from Sichuan to Texas. Route, machine weight, full-container versus consolidated loads, insurance, destination port, timing, and tariff treatment by origin all alter landed cost.

The contemporary public materials located for this essay contain no industry-wide customs-declaration database.

Customs and final landed costs are among the migration's most poorly documented components.

Specific cases are more reliable than industry averages. One company announced in June that its first shipment of 320 machines had reached Kazakhstan, totaling approximately 18.2 PH/s. It said subsequent batches would bring the number shipped there to approximately 2,600.

These figures establish that physical machines did cross borders. They are one company's disclosures, not a basis for extrapolating to the whole industry.

A larger figure came from a journalistic investigation. In November 2021, a news organization tracked fourteen major mining companies and counted more than two million machines those companies had moved out of China after the ban.

This is often miswritten as “all two million Chinese mining machines moved out.”

Its accurate scope is the combined relocations disclosed by or traced to fourteen large companies investigated. It is neither China's original stock of machines nor a count including small miners without public records.

The clearest evidence explaining why many machines did not leave immediately is the secondhand market price.

When Reuters interviewed miners in Sichuan on June 25, one machine type that had sold for around RMB 4,000 in April and May had fallen to RMB 700–800 in June.

A mine operator described machines being sold like scrap iron.

At least some older machines were therefore liquidated at a discount domestically rather than bearing cross-border relocation costs.

How many were ultimately scrapped, and how many never left? The available materials require an uncomfortable answer.

There is no verifiable industry total.

Many secondhand transactions occurred off-market. Machines could be broken up and sold, transferred to underground mines, temporarily warehoused, or exported later. IP data cannot identify individual serial numbers.

The relocation of two million machines by fourteen companies is verifiable. How many additional millions were scrapped in China cannot presently be inferred from the primary materials.

That absence is itself an important fact.

It determines whether another question can be answered: who ultimately acquired those discounted machines, and what did they subsequently do?

Physical restarts faced a second layer of delay in electrical infrastructure.

One site that later became a major destination advertised total electrical capacity of approximately 750 MW when acquired in 2021. Developed capacity at the time was approximately 300 MW. Long-term fixed-price power actually secured under contract amounted to only 130 MW.

These are three different numbers: the site's planned electrical capacity, developed infrastructure, and power covered by a fixed-price contract.

They must never be combined into a claim that the company was already continuously consuming 750 MW in 2021.

5. Three Hundred Jobs

Rockdale, Texas, is a representative destination, but it did not spring from nothing after China's ban.

Whinstone began construction in the former aluminum-smelter industrial area in 2019. The local municipal development organization announced a first phase of 300 MW and 180,000 square feet, with planned initial capital expenditure of $150 million and approximately 200 new jobs.

After Riot acquired it in May 2021, the Chinese migration coincided with a site that already possessed heavy-industry electrical infrastructure.

The 200 jobs were initially a project commitment, not a final employment census.

The power-purchase arrangements can be checked individually. In May 2020, Whinstone signed a long-term agreement with a retail electricity company, initially covering 130 MW at fixed prices through April 30, 2030. It added 65 MW in March 2022 and 150 MW in November, eventually totaling 345 MW under fixed-price contracts. Consumption above the contracted quantity was purchased at prevailing spot prices.

So-called demand-response income must be separated into at least three cash flows to avoid serious misunderstanding.

The first is the resale of contracted power. When spot prices become sufficiently attractive, a company can choose not to consume its fixed-price electricity and have the retailer sell the corresponding quantity into the market, obtaining credits against future electricity bills.

The mining company receives that benefit, while the corresponding energy is not consumed by mining machines during expensive periods.

The second is ancillary services and controllable load. Grid demand-response arrangements allow loads capable of rapid curtailment to reduce consumption in response to instructions or market signals. One emergency-response service, for example, requires contracted loads to provide the agreed power reduction when deployed; typical products require a response within ten or thirty minutes.

There is no uniform statewide payment for every kilowatt-hour not consumed. Different products establish capacity prices through procurement or day-ahead ancillary-service markets, with prices varying by period and product.

The third is peak avoidance. The local transmission-cost allocation mechanism averages one system monthly-peak interval in each month from June through September. Large consumers that correctly anticipate and avoid these peaks can reduce part of their transmission-cost allocation in the following year.

This means paying less in future transmission charges, not receiving a shutdown check from the grid at that moment.

The claim that the grid contracts with mines to pay a fixed rate for shutting down whenever the weather is hot therefore needs rewriting.

The narrower supported version is that mines can monetize rapid load reduction through several market and contractual mechanisms, including ancillary-service capacity income, market resale of fixed-price contracted power, and transmission-cost savings from peak avoidance.

Each has different triggers, payers, and pricing mechanisms.

Two verifiable cases show what they actually did during extreme weather.

During the February 2021 winter storm, Riot had not yet completed its acquisition. A Whinstone spokesperson later told the media that the site began curtailing load on February 11, shut down entirely on February 12, and returned to full operation only on February 19.

This is the company's account to reporters, not an independent grid audit of its load every fifteen minutes.

The July 2022 heat wave provides a more quantified example. The company reported voluntarily curtailing 11,717 MWh that month and receiving approximately $9.5 million in power credits. It estimated that curtailment reduced its monthly Bitcoin production by approximately 21%.

Using the company's own measures, this works out to approximately $811 per curtailed MWh.

That ratio cannot be interpreted as the grid paying $811 for every MWh curtailed. The $9.5 million combines the value of contracted electricity, market resale, and returns from other power strategies.

December 2022 revealed the other side: mines themselves can suffer weather damage. Company disclosures showed that severe cold damaged one building at the site, leaving approximately 17,040 mining machines temporarily inoperable.

Mines can therefore provide flexible load during extreme weather while their own infrastructure is also vulnerable to extreme weather.

Now consider employment, wages, and local tax revenue. All three require separating commitments from actual outcomes.

Rockdale's most widely cited actual employment number is not the 200 planned jobs from 2019, but a later review of the local economy by a state fiscal agency.

A municipal administrator said the company then had approximately 300 direct jobs and estimated 900–1,200 indirect jobs. She also said Rockdale's 2022 sales-tax revenue was on course to exceed $1 million for the first time.

The latter figures are, respectively, a local official's economic-impact estimate and that year's revenue trend. They should not become “the mine created 1,200 jobs and paid the government $1 million in tax.”

Industrial history explains quite concretely why such projects were welcomed. Closure of the aluminum smelter and later the coal mine and power plant cost the area many heavy-industry jobs, while leaving high-capacity electrical infrastructure at the old site.

The 2019 announcement itself cited 200 direct jobs and demand for local contractors, hotels, and retail businesses as reasons to welcome the project.

This is the only place in this entire history where people on the ground come into view. A county that had lost its smelter and power plant still had substations and transmission lines, and now someone wanted to use them.

Precisely here, the figures are scarcest. Jobs have two versions, indirect jobs are estimates, wages are absent, and tax revenue is a trend.

Average wages are a conspicuous data gap.

The county and municipal materials verifiable for this essay provided job targets, direct employment counts, and early promotional claims of pay above the regional average, but did not simultaneously publish the company's local payroll and stable employee count.

Mining workers' average wages therefore cannot be reliably calculated. Substituting recruitment advertisements or company-wide average compensation changes the measure.

Tax concessions also need company-specific treatment. In 2018, another company at a nearby site received a ten-year local tax abatement. Local reporting described approximately 80% off property taxes for the first five years. On the project's valuation at the time, the county still expected an average of approximately $700,000 annually over ten years.

That company subsequently contracted sharply after cryptoasset prices fell, and planned jobs were not delivered at their original scale.

The case shows that tax-concession amounts are static calculations conditional on project commitments, not actual eventual tax revenue.

Still less can one company's abatement simply be attributed to the later company.

Finally, residential electricity prices: the essay's most important missing evidence lies here.

Within this research's scope, no peer-reviewed paper was found using the actual timing of Texas mines' entry for causal identification and directly measuring changes in Texas residents' electricity bills.

This is an important evidence gap, not a fact to be supplied by a model.

A well-known study closest to actual household bills concerns upstate New York, not Texas. It uses cryptoasset-price changes as a source of exogenous variation in miners' electricity demand and combines local electricity-market and billing structures to estimate spillovers. A newer version estimates approximately $204 million in additional annual residential electricity costs and approximately $92 million for small businesses. Related summaries commonly express the residential figure as approximately $88 per average household annually.

It is still labeled a working paper and should not be described as a settled peer-reviewed finding.

Another frequently quoted Texas figure is an approximately 2% increase in wholesale electricity prices for each additional 1 GW of mining load.

That study concerns wholesale prices, not residents' final bills, and its public version has long circulated as a preprint.

The distinction is crucial. Final household bills also include retail contracts, grid charges, fuel-cost recovery, regulatory arrangements, and amortization of debt arising from the 2021 crisis.

Wholesale price changes do not immediately pass through one-for-one to every household meter.

6. One Tenge

Kazakhstan best illustrates why migration was not a one-time event.

Cambridge's sample-based estimates show its global share rising from approximately 8.2% in April 2021 to 18.1% in August, briefly making it the world's second-largest destination.

The electricity system soon came under pressure.

In its 2021 annual documents, the national grid operator identified the large-scale deployment of mining data centers as one cause of that year's electricity-demand growth. It estimated known digital-mining load at more than 1,000 MW, excluding shadow mining.

That is the system operator's estimate, not a complete industry census aggregated from smart meters at every mine.

More importantly, it reported actual curtailment: 83.259 GWh in October 2021, 201.642 GWh in November, and 200.036 GWh in December.

These are not theoretically curtailable capacities. They are actual monthly restrictions on digital-mining electricity consumption listed in the annual documents.

The operator also explained that, following an October 1 meeting chaired by the deputy energy minister, it could reduce digital miners' scheduled consumption during energy or capacity shortages or when necessary to prevent accidents.

Both sides are clear in these data. Mines obtained cheap electricity and opportunities from incoming hashrate. The grid bore the capacity-balancing pressure of additional continuous load.

When the system lacked sufficient supply, policy prioritized curtailing mining rather than guaranteeing continuous machine operation.

Taxation also changed quickly. A tax amendment passed on June 24, 2021, imposed a digital-mining fee of one tenge per kWh of actual mining electricity consumption from January 1, 2022.

The government subsequently replaced it with a graduated schedule linked to electricity prices. From 2023, cheaper electricity attracted higher mining surcharges, reaching more than twenty tenge per kWh at the top end.

The policy logic is clear: miners benefiting most from low electricity prices were required to pay the highest dedicated charges.

January 2022 brought two further shocks.

First, a widespread internet shutdown began on January 5 during national political unrest. Monitoring organizations observed a sharp collapse in national traffic, and Bitcoin hashrate observed by major mining pools also temporarily declined.

Then, a January 21 grid notice obtained by the media required scheduled power supply to digital-mining entities to be canceled from January 24 through 31 unless system conditions improved.

This latter point currently rests primarily on an internal memorandum obtained by reporters and should not be treated as equivalent to a published regulation.

By January 2022, Cambridge's estimate of Kazakhstan's global share had fallen to 13.22%. Contemporary reporting interviewed several large miners considering moving equipment again, to the United States or elsewhere.

The fall from 18.1% to 13.22% cannot all be attributed to curtailment. Its denominator, global hashrate, was also increasing, and IP-based identification has errors of its own.

It is directionally consistent with contemporary reports of companies beginning a second relocation.

Read this section alongside Section 5, and a concrete contrast emerges.

Both destinations received the same machines. One offered long-term fixed-price power contracts and negotiable tax arrangements. The other initially offered cheap electricity, then added per-kWh charges within a year and prioritized cutting the mines off when the system tightened.

Hashrate itself does not recognize this distinction. It recognizes only electricity prices and difficulty. The relocated machines recognize it.

More precisely, their owners do. When they began considering a second move in early 2022, less than six months had passed since completing the first.

7. Cost per Coin

One of listed mining companies' most frequently promoted metrics is the cost of mining one coin.

It resembles a commodity-extraction unit cost, but companies' numerators and denominators differ substantially.

The narrowest cash measure typically adds direct electricity costs, hosting and facility-operation fees, and on-site labor and maintenance, then divides by coins self-mined during the period.

It generally excludes machine depreciation, impairment, headquarters expenses, interest, stock-based compensation, and financing costs.

A company saying its cash cost is $12,000 per coin therefore does not mean it remains economically profitable when the coin price falls to $13,000.

One set of disclosures illustrates this especially well. In one company's 2022 operating costs, depreciation in the mining segment alone was approximately $214.8 million. The company later reported total operating costs of approximately $631.9 million for the year.

Looking only at cash electricity costs makes this large consumption of assets disappear entirely.

Machines also have highly cyclical secondhand values. The fall from approximately RMB 4,000 to RMB 700–800 during China's ban is an extreme observed example.

After cryptoasset prices fell in 2022, machines already pledged as collateral for equipment loans again lost value rapidly.

Depreciation thus becomes more than accounting smoothing: it translates into insufficient collateral and financing risk.

What hosting contracts actually sell also deserves examination.

Hosting separates ownership of the mining machines from operation of the facility and power supply. Customers own the machines; hosts supply transformers, racks, networking, cooling, electricity procurement, monitoring, and repairs.

Profit allocation is not limited to charging a markup per kilowatt-hour.

One company's publicly filed hosting agreement provides a useful real example. Customers owed base hosting fees, infrastructure fees, and operating-service fees, plus a Bitcoin profit share. During the initial five-year term, that share was 15% of Bitcoin profit. The agreement defined Bitcoin profit as profit from coins mined at the relevant facility after agreed reasonable operating expenses.

This reveals how risk is distributed between the two sides.

Machine owners bear cryptoasset-price, difficulty, and equipment-depreciation risks.

Hosts primarily earn fixed or semifixed infrastructure and electricity-service fees. Where profit sharing applies, they also receive some upside from rising prices.

If electricity costs pass through completely, customers retain more power-price risk. If the host secures a long-term power-purchase agreement, it can earn additional returns from price spreads and load curtailment.

The vertically integrated example above goes further: it operates its own machines, owns the facility, and controls long-term electricity contracts. It can therefore earn not only from block production but also, during some high-price periods, from the value of not running its machines under those electricity contracts.

The explanation for the 2022 bankruptcy wave likewise needs narrowing.

One hosting company entered restructuring in September 2022 and sought to sell assets. Another filed a voluntary petition in the Southern District of Texas bankruptcy court on December 21.

The latter's restructuring documents show substantial secured claims arising from mining-equipment financing. Early in bankruptcy, it also obtained court approval for approximately $37.5 million in debtor-in-possession financing from existing creditors.

The allocation of risk is typical: in the bull market, capital providers treated expensive specialized chips as financeable assets. When cryptoasset prices, revenue per unit of hashrate, and secondhand equipment prices fell together, collateral values declined while electricity bills, hosting fees, and debt interest still required cash payment.

“Mining companies went bankrupt in 2022 because Bitcoin's price fell” is therefore still too broad.

The better-supported version is that falling cryptoasset prices, recovering network difficulty, and rising energy costs combined with earlier large-scale equipment purchases and leveraged financing, leaving some hosts and miners unable to cover fixed debt and facility commitments from operating cash flow.

Each debtor's precise trigger must be assessed from its own restructuring documents.

8. The Greatest Gaps, the Greatest Certainty

Why can the same year, 2021, produce completely different carbon-emissions figures? The explanation lies broadly in three stages.

First, estimate how many watts the network uses. Traditional models infer power demand from hashrate and upper and lower bounds for machine efficiency.

In 2023, Cambridge substantially revised its model. One reason was that the old method could leave retired older-generation machines in the hypothetical equipment mix for too long, systematically overestimating electricity consumption during some periods.

An annualized number quoted from the website as it appeared in 2021 may therefore not belong to the same series as a later estimate recalculating 2021 with the revised model.

Second, infer where mining occurs. Carbon models combine the mining-pool IP distribution described earlier with regional generation mixes.

Errors in country identification therefore pass directly into emissions estimates.

Third, determine which carbon intensity to multiply electricity consumption by. Cambridge later estimated that the average emissions intensity of Bitcoin's electricity mix rose from approximately 491.24 grams of CO₂-equivalent per kWh in 2020 to approximately 531.81 in 2021.

A peer-reviewed paper supplied a widely circulated estimate of annual emissions: the post-migration network might correspond to approximately 65.4 million tonnes of carbon dioxide per year. That is not the same unit as the per-kWh emissions intensity in the preceding paragraph. The authors' central explanation was that China's wet-season hydropower share exited while new hash power concentrated more in coal-heavy regions, raising average carbon intensity.

This was not measured at smokestacks. It was a model result combining machine efficiency, hashrate, geography, and local electricity mixes.

At the other end are industry surveys. In the fourth quarter of 2021, one industry council said its surveyed miners represented more than 46% of global hashrate, used approximately 66.1% sustainable electricity themselves, and implied a global mining sustainable-power mix of 58.5% when extrapolated.

That number measures the share of power meeting the council's own definition of sustainable, not directly measured carbon dioxide. Participation was voluntary, creating potential selection bias.

The three approaches can be presented alongside one another without choosing a side.

“Mining consumes otherwise-curtailed electricity” has a narrow version with evidence and a broad version without it.

The narrow version is that, before the ban, Chinese mining displayed substantial seasonal migration. Hashrate moved toward hydropower-rich areas such as Sichuan during the wet season and back toward Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia during the dry season. Cambridge's early mining-pool IP data observed that pattern, and Sichuan authorities did discuss hydropower absorption before the exit.

The broad version is that Bitcoin mines use only water and electricity that would otherwise be wasted, so their marginal energy consumption is zero.

These materials do not establish that version.

Establishing curtailment requires at least a counterfactual: absent the mine, would that same kWh at that time and grid node actually have been curtailed, rather than sold to households or industry, transmitted elsewhere, stored, or used to support some other new load?

Cambridge's IP data contain no such grid-node-level counterfactual. Nor do mining companies' power-purchase announcements.

The claim that mines provide grid flexibility needs to be separated into parts: technical ability to stop, and an economic willingness to stop under some conditions, do not establish that mines necessarily lower residents' final electricity bills.

The first is technical. Specialized chips form a load that can be switched off and restarted quickly, and grids already have mechanisms for admitting rapidly curtailable loads. The 11,717 MWh described above is an actual large-scale curtailment example.

The second is economic. A mine with a cheap long-term power contract may find that, when spot prices surge, monetizing contracted electricity without mining is worth more than continuing to mine.

It does not automatically follow that mines therefore reduce every resident's electricity price.

During the great majority of nonemergency hours, a new mine remains additional load. It increases required generation and grid-connection demand and may change the marginal generating unit and wholesale prices.

A third qualification is that flexibility is not unconditional.

A 2026 study of large flexible loads using hourly mining-load data found that miners did reduce demand as electricity costs rose, but the response magnitude was moderated by mining revenue per unit of hashrate.

When mining is especially profitable, in other words, the price or compensation threshold needed to induce shutdown changes too.

The real comparison is therefore between two counterfactual systems, not simply whether machines can be switched off.

One has mines that consume more power normally but can shut down in emergencies.

The other never had that additional load in the first place.

Actual grid-curtailment records answer whether mines are flexible within the first system.

They do not by themselves answer the second, welfare-related question.

Return now to the curve in Section 2.

That curve can be estimated again from block records and difficulty using a public method. From approximately 180 down to 65 and back to 180, others can inspect the window, smoothing, and calculation; different methods may produce different point estimates. What is reproducible is a bounded estimate, not a precise census of the world's mining machines.

The off-chain questions, meanwhile, each lack a denominator.

How many machines were ultimately scrapped?

What do mining workers earn?

How much did residents' electricity bills rise in a particular county because of mining?

How much electricity would otherwise have been curtailed?

Four questions, four gaps. And these are precisely the four things discussed with the greatest certainty in arguments about this episode.

The remainder's shape therefore differs from all twelve preceding essays.

It is not what was left outside jurisdiction, excluded from code, or not yet realized when calculation was possible. Measurement itself has divided it in two: one half has continually updated on-chain estimates with public, reproducible methods; the other asks about scrappage, wages, bills, and curtailed electricity without equally complete denominators.

This is not because someone hid it.

There is no serial-number-level scrappage database because many secondhand transactions happen off-market, and machines can be broken up, stored, or exported again.

Wages are not public because no document requires simultaneous disclosure of a particular facility's payroll and stable employee count.

Residential bills require causal identification. The paper accomplishing that studied another state and still labels itself a working paper.

Curtailment requires a grid-node-level counterfactual, and no institution maintains such counterfactuals.

Every gap has a specific technical reason.

Together those reasons say the same thing: what the chain can record and what the world wants to ask do not occupy the same coordinate system.

Episode 2 ended by observing that electricity and money had clear places in those public materials, while people's work and circumstances were not presented with the same completeness.

The material here makes that gap more concrete.

Electricity is recorded because the grid needs to know its connected load. Money is recorded because listed companies must report their finances. Block records and difficulty can also be used to estimate the hash-power curve; the network retargets difficulty every 2,016 blocks.

What remained in the county the machines left, and what the receiving county gained, did not automatically come together in an equally complete account that could be examined item by item.

During those two months, the chain continued producing blocks. Its target was an average of roughly ten minutes per block, but the abrupt fall in hash power also brought longer waits.

It left records of blocks and difficulty from which people could estimate how much hash power remained across the network.

It did not record whose hands that hashrate passed through when it moved.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
