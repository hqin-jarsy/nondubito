Mt. Gox

I. A Trading-Card Website

The domain mtgox.com was not registered for Bitcoin.

Jed McCaleb registered it in 2007 to build a market where people could trade online cards from a card game as though they were stocks. The name abbreviated that idea: Magic: The Gathering Online eXchange.

The project never took off. The domain sat unused for a time.

This origin is often treated as an amusing anecdote, but it describes the conditions of the time. In 2010, the barrier to starting a bitcoin exchange was low enough that an unused domain would do. No license to obtain, no capital requirement, no custody qualification, no one checking where customer funds were kept. That low barrier was crucial to early expansion. It also meant entrants did not first have to demonstrate their ability to safeguard other people’s property.

On July 18, 2010, a Bitcointalk user named mtgox posted that he had just set up a new bitcoin exchange.

The post explained that trades took place between members, with continuous online automated matching, quotations, and custodial accounts.

This provides a launch date directly anchored in contemporaneous evidence.

On March 6, 2011, McCaleb announced on the same forum that he was handing Mt. Gox to Mark Karpelès, known as MagicalTux. His explanation was straightforward. He had created it on a whim after reading about Bitcoin the previous summer, but continuing to run it demanded more time than he could provide. He specifically cited Karpelès’s abilities in website programming, systems administration, banking, and payment integration.

This was not merely handing someone a website password. Records in a later US civil suit state that, in early 2011, Karpelès-controlled Tibanne K.K. acquired 88% of Mt. Gox K.K. from McCaleb, who retained 12%.

The figure needs an evidentiary label. The 2020 federal court document was describing the litigation record at summary judgment. The court explicitly said the relevant facts were presented in the light most favorable to the plaintiffs, without endorsing their truth.

As for the sale price, the handover thread’s answer was that the agreement’s specific terms were confidential.

Karpelès later recalled roughly 3,000 customers when he took over. By 2013, he said, there were about 1.1 million active accounts from 239 countries and territories. These are his retrospective statements, suitable as indicators of scale, not audited figures.

From 3,000 to 1.1 million in less than three years. Throughout those years, the company’s custody practices largely stayed the same.

A widespread claim must be dismantled here because it determines how the next seven sections should be read.

The claim that Mt. Gox handled 70% of the world’s bitcoin transactions is seriously misleading without a measurement basis.

The sources actually offer several figures. An academic study of exchange data found its market share consistently above 80% from 2010 to 2012: a share of exchange trading within the study sample. A 2013 US Department of Homeland Security seizure filing quoted the company’s own figure of about 80%. Karpelès later told Reuters that it exceeded 90% at one point in 2013. The frequently repeated media figure of 70% generally refers to global exchange trading volume.

None is a share of on-chain transfers.

Mt. Gox was not validating or processing 70% of network-wide on-chain transactions. It once accounted for most observable trading volume on centralized exchanges.

This distinction is the essay’s first anchor: the chain is one thing; the exchange is another.

That distinction will be tested repeatedly in the next eighteen essays. Almost every incident initially prompts the reaction that Bitcoin has broken. Almost every investigation finds that the failure occurred off-chain. During these years, however, most people encountered the former through the latter.

This is a central thread of the series. In the pizza transaction, the on-chain segment worked exactly as designed, while every surrounding step required people. At Mt. Gox, those steps were bundled into a company: conversion, custody, matching, withdrawals, customer support. Bundling had value. It turned something requiring technical knowledge into something anyone could do by registering an account.

It also bundled the risks.

Bundling is not inherently bad. Banks do it too. The difference is that banks are accompanied by reserve requirements, deposit insurance, regular audits, regulatory reporting, and laws establishing payment priority when they fail. This company in 2010 had none of them.

II. A Hole in the Bucket

First dismantle the second claim: that 95% of Mt. Gox’s coins were always safely in cold storage.

The public historical record establishes no verified, stable long-term percentage. In discussing the question specifically, WizSec clearly stated that it lacked sufficient knowledge of Mt. Gox’s historical cold-storage practices to assume a particular ratio.

The operating model WizSec reconstructed from on-chain and internal material is more useful than a percentage.

Mt. Gox used pregenerated paper wallets for cold storage. When the hot wallet had a surplus, coins were gradually deposited into them. When the hot wallet ran short, staff manually scanned paper wallets to replenish it.

The crucial management problem was that no one monitored the total in cold storage or reconciled recorded balances with actual holdings.

Hence the image.

The hot-wallet private keys had long since leaked, while staff continued replenishing it according to procedure. The bucket had a hole. The person adding water did not know, and no procedure would tell him.

There is no malice in this picture, only procedure. The person adding water follows the manual. The manual does not say to count what is in the bucket first.

That instruction was probably not deleted deliberately. More likely, no one thought it necessary when the manual was written. The company was small, there were few coins, and everyone roughly knew the amount. By the time it became necessary, no one remembered how the manual had first been written.

This model explains the apparent paradox: a company can sincerely believe most of its coins are in cold wallets while losing them continuously for years.

WizSec itself describes this part as its understanding and a possible explanation, not an internal process established by a court.

Both things can be true. Coins really were held in cold wallets, and the hot-wallet side really was leaking.

They could coexist because one action was missing: adding the totals on both sides and comparing the result with what customer accounts said should exist.

Accounting has an old name for that action: taking inventory. It predates this chain by thousands of years.

The economics series’ second essay discussed Mesopotamian clay tablets; its fifth discussed double-entry bookkeeping. Both addressed the same problem: making a ledger checkable against itself. Here, a far more sophisticated recording system was operating, but the checking step had never been connected.

No new technology was required. Someone simply needed to be assigned the job and do it regularly.

That statement will remain true through the next eighteen essays. Most accidents arise not from a missing technology but from a missing role.

Half of the remainder’s location is already visible. It is not on-chain, where every transaction is clear. Nor is it in the database, where each account balance looks fine. It lies in the unowned gap between them: actual on-chain holdings on one side, recorded obligations on the other, with no one responsible for looking at the two figures together.

The beginning of the theft must also be separated into two events.

Around March 2011, WizSec reconstructed a wallet theft of approximately 80,000 BTC. The main server had been compromised, an unencrypted wallet.dat was probably copied, and coins moved to an address where they remained unmoved for a long time.

The September 2011 event was different. WizSec believes the main hot wallet’s private keys were again exposed through a copied wallet.dat.

A theft and a private-key leak are not the same thing.

A theft removes what is there at that moment. A key leak means that, from then on, attackers can take any coins arriving at those addresses whenever they wish.

The former is a number. The latter is a pipe.

That difference shaped the next three years.

It also explains why detection was so difficult. A single theft of 80,000 coins creates a sudden hole visible to anyone reconciling the books. A pipe produces no abrupt change. It simply removes some of each replenishment after a while. In an exchange with heavy daily deposits and withdrawals, ordinary flows can conceal such losses for a long time.

III. Three Years

By late 2011, WizSec’s reconstruction shows, the gap between expected and actual balances had quickly reached hundreds of thousands of coins.

By the end of 2012, within one strictly identified class of theft paths, WizSec had explicitly identified about 300,000 BTC leaving Mt. Gox between late 2011 and late 2012 without matching legitimate withdrawal records. They flowed to external addresses, including BTC-e and Bitcoinica.

By mid-2013, WizSec believes approximately 630,000 BTC spendable with the keys leaked in September 2011 had cumulatively been taken. Mt. Gox had essentially no spendable BTC left.

A historical limitation is necessary.

WizSec did not publish an audited annual theft table with exact calendar-year totals. The scale and sequence can safely be repeated. Dividing 630,000 evenly into 210,000 per year over three years has no basis in the original investigation.

How WizSec reached its findings deserves more attention than the conclusion itself.

It also reveals what reconstruction required.

WizSec matched deposit and withdrawal records from the Mt. Gox database leaked in 2014 against on-chain transactions. Using clustering methods such as common inputs, it identified more than two million Mt. Gox addresses. It then isolated transactions that moved from those addresses to outside addresses without corresponding legitimate withdrawal records.

One calibration anchor deserves separate attention.

In 2011, Mt. Gox publicly moved 424,242.42424242 BTC to demonstrate that it really held the coins.

Four years later, that transaction became a reference point for outsiders reconstructing its historical balances.

A public act intended to establish trust became a tool for investigating it.

In the intervening years, no one used it to check.

By mid-2013, almost no spendable coins remained. The exchange nevertheless operated for more than another six months.

It continued matching trades, collecting commissions, and displaying account balances. Those balances were real in the database. Nothing corresponded to them on-chain.

New customers arriving during those months deposited real money and received a number in an account whose backing had already drained away. This is the hardest part to write because it concerns particular people, and the available material cannot establish how many there were or how much each deposited.

Only one point can be stated clearly: by mid-2013, almost no spendable coins remained on-chain, yet the doors stayed open for more than six months. That interval can be identified.

Now trace both ends, because this is the essay’s most important point.

The benefit side is clear. Mt. Gox mainly charged both parties a commission on each matched trade. Standard fees for small or low-volume traders were about 0.6%, falling in tiers with the preceding thirty days’ volume to roughly 0.25% for very high-volume accounts. It earned matching commissions and related withdrawal and remittance fees, rather than primarily trading spreads on its own account.

The risk side is also clear, though no one could see it at the time.

Mt. Gox’s terms described ordinary trades as transactions between members and said the company would hold members’ deposited currencies and BTC and manage those assets on their behalf.

A contract saying “held on your behalf” is not the same as client-asset segregation in its modern sense.

In practice, a member’s balance was first an internal number in Mt. Gox’s database. BTC was pooled and moved among addresses controlled by Mt. Gox. No member had a continuously corresponding, legally segregated on-chain wallet.

The later bankruptcy and civil rehabilitation processes treated users as creditors, rather than allowing each to reclaim particular coins. Legal analyses of the case explicitly state that creditors had no right to separate out specific bitcoins.

What was deposited at Mt. Gox was nominally coins, but practically a company’s liability.

The difference became visible only when the company failed.

This is the source of the phrase later repeated throughout the domain: not your keys, not your coins. It was repeated after Mt. Gox because this was where the lesson was learned.

All the risk fell on depositors, who could not verify what they bore.

More importantly, they did not even know what they should verify. No mechanism told them how much the exchange actually held.

Compare this with the three conditions from the second essay. Mining-pool risk allocation was roughly fair because it was calculable, selectable, and possible to leave.

Two were missing here. Choice existed: other exchanges were available. Calculability did not: no one could calculate Mt. Gox’s actual holdings. After February 7, 2014, exit disappeared too. Withdrawals stopped. Your balance remained on the screen but could not come out.

IV. A Bug

On February 7, 2014, Mt. Gox suspended BTC withdrawals to investigate abnormalities. That announcement did not yet assign a specific cause.

The company formally attributed the problem on February 10. Its central claim was that a bug in Bitcoin software made it possible to alter transaction details.

The nontechnical explanation said such alterations could make a system believe a payment had not occurred when the coins had already been sent, creating a possibility of duplicate payment.

The original allocation of responsibility must be preserved: the company called it a bug in Bitcoin software.

It was publicly challenged that day. Bitcoin Foundation chief scientist Gavin Andresen’s response was titled to the effect that, contrary to Mt. Gox’s statement, Bitcoin was not broken. He explained that Mt. Gox’s problems arose from its highly customized wallet software, customer-service procedures, and failure to prepare for a known property.

Andresen did not deny that the property existed. The disputes concerned who should handle it correctly and whether it could explain the subsequently announced shortfall.

Both questions later received answers, not through argument. Contemporary comparisons answered the first; statistics two weeks later answered the second.

The property itself needs a clear explanation.

Early Bitcoin transaction identifiers were hashes of serialized transaction contents. For an unconfirmed valid transaction, some signature encodings could be changed without changing which previous outputs were spent, who received the money, or how much they received. This produced a different identifier. A third party could do it without knowing the private key.

The danger was not that attackers could freely change the recipient or create coins from nothing.

A typical failure chain ran as follows. An exchange generated a withdrawal and recorded identifier A. Someone altered a malleable part to produce a semantically equivalent A-prime with a different identifier. If A-prime entered a block, the payment had succeeded. But a back end checking only A might regard the withdrawal as failed. A real loss arose only if the back end or customer support then paid again.

Decker and Wattenhofer emphasized that final link in their paper. Bitcoin’s core client tracked balances through unspent outputs. It did not assume money remained merely because its original transaction identifier had not confirmed.

Turning this property into withdrawal fraud therefore required an additional condition: an error in the exchange’s own withdrawal and accounting implementation.

Then came the paper’s measurements.

Across its whole sample, identified malleability conflicts involved approximately 302,700 BTC. Modified versions that ultimately won represented around 64,564 BTC. One principal attack form had a value-weighted success rate of about 21.36%.

Before Mt. Gox suspended withdrawals, identified attacks involved 1,811.58 BTC.

Applying that success rate gave roughly 386 BTC that might actually have been fraudulently paid twice before the suspension.

That number gains significance beside another. The whole sample involved 302,700 coins, but only 1,811.58 were involved before withdrawals stopped. Most malleability activity occurred after Mt. Gox had already closed the door.

The difference from the company’s initially reported 850,000 was three orders of magnitude.

The most chilling part follows.

After the February 10 announcement, malleability attacks across the network surged. On February 10–11 alone, 286,076 BTC were observed in related conflicts.

Mt. Gox had already stopped withdrawals.

An announcement described an attack. The attack then happened.

This is another form of the point in the second essay: a construct’s announcement does not merely describe the world. It enters the world and changes it.

There is an even colder reading. The announcement effectively turned a technical detail previously discussed largely among developers into instructions for a worldwide audience. Its authors did not intend that. The gap between effect and intention is one of this domain’s most common accident shapes.

For fairness, 386 BTC is not an absolute ceiling for the entire 2010–2014 period. The paper’s dataset mainly began in 2013. On-chain observation could establish whether an original or modified version confirmed, but could not show directly whether a particular company actually paid twice after encountering a changed identifier. The authors explicitly acknowledge these limitations.

Even with those qualifications, the orders of magnitude do not match.

There was also a contemporary comparison group. Exchanges including Bitstamp briefly took protective withdrawal measures amid network-wide malleability noise, then resumed.

The same protocol property, the same period: others dealt with it and reopened; this exchange did not. That was the factual answer to who should handle the property correctly.

V. ¥3.842 Billion against ¥6.501 Billion

On February 28, 2014, Mt. Gox applied to the Tokyo District Court to begin civil rehabilitation.

The announcement’s first page listed two figures: total assets of ¥3,841,866,163 and current liabilities of ¥6,501,119,371.

The difference was approximately ¥2.659 billion.

The second page was more specific. An internal investigation had found large quantities of BTC missing, then estimated at approximately 750,000 deposited by members plus about 100,000 belonging to the company, roughly 850,000 in total. The company considered theft exploiting system problems highly likely, while acknowledging that its investigation was incomplete. It also stated an approximately ¥2.8 billion discrepancy between cash actually held at financial institutions and recorded member deposits.

That ¥2.8 billion was a discrepancy between recorded deposits and bank cash. Without further evidence, it cannot simply be rewritten as a confirmed theft of ¥2.8 billion.

The document was much more cautious than subsequent news narratives.

It referred to several possible causes, including third-party intrusion, and said large volumes of transaction records needed analysis before the truth could be established.

In its formal filing, in other words, the company itself was no longer attributing the entire loss solely to the technical explanation offered two weeks earlier.

Another announcement followed on March 20.

After applying for civil rehabilitation, the company rescanned old wallets. On March 7, it found 199,999.99 BTC in an old-format wallet used before June 2011. The company said it had previously believed the wallet’s balance was zero.

It informed its lawyers, the court, and the supervisor. For security, approximately 200,000 coins were transferred to offline wallets on March 14–15. Including around 2,000 already known, the company then held about 202,000 coins.

The missing total was therefore reduced from roughly 850,000 to roughly 650,000.

Pause here, because this is easily read as good news.

The 200,000 were not stolen coins recovered from hackers by police. The company rediscovered them in an old wallet of its own.

A company could not find two hundred thousand of its own coins, searched for three years, and found them in an old wallet.

More accurately, it had not searched for three years. It went three years without looking, then rescanned past wallets only after applying for rehabilitation.

This is the same condition as the replenished bucket in Section II: no one was taking inventory.

Fiat-currency problems followed a separate line, which likewise cannot be labeled theft.

Mt. Gox’s dollar channels broadly worked like this. Members deposited through Dwolla. Its US subsidiary, Mutum Sigillum LLC, had a Wells Fargo account. For dollar withdrawals, money could move from a Japanese Sumitomo Mitsui account to Wells Fargo, then into the Dwolla system.

On May 14, 2013, a federal court in Maryland issued a warrant authorizing seizure of funds in a Dwolla account registered to Mutum Sigillum LLC and held by Veridian Credit Union. The application cited provisions concerning an unregistered money-transmission business.

The affidavit’s grounds included FinCEN records showing neither Mt. Gox nor Mutum Sigillum registered as a money-services business. It also said that, when opening the Wells Fargo account in 2011, Karpelès answered that the business was not a money transmitter. Investigators considered its actual activity to be receiving and transmitting customer money in the United States.

Precisely stated, the US seizure concerned compliance. It was not a 2013 US government finding that Mt. Gox had stolen customers’ bitcoins.

This distinction is the same kind of work as the four propositions in Section VII. A company can simultaneously have compliance problems, litigation, technical defects, management failures, and stolen coins. Each has its own evidence, chronology, and consequences. Compressing them into “it got into trouble” is satisfying to read and explains nothing.

Something else happened that month. On May 2, 2013, CoinLab sued Mt. Gox in the United States for $75 million. Its principal allegations concerned failure to transfer customer lists and software and system access as agreed, and continued direct service to US and Canadian customers in breach of exclusivity provisions.

These were civil allegations, not an established debt.

Together, however, the two events had a concrete effect: Mt. Gox’s fiat liquidity and banking channels were both squeezed during 2013.

The full position can now be set out. On-chain, almost no spendable coins remained. On the fiat side, US channels had been seized, its North American partner was suing, and withdrawals were backing up.

Its website continued operating, prices kept moving, and commissions kept being collected.

At the time, these looked like signs of life. In retrospect, they were precisely the things it could still do. Matching trades and displaying balances required no actual holdings, only a running database.

VI. Twelve Years

The Japanese proceedings followed a reversal that is easily misreported.

On February 28, 2014, the company initially applied for civil rehabilitation, hoping to preserve the company or explore restructuring.

That route did not continue. In April 2014, the Tokyo District Court moved the case into bankruptcy liquidation, appointing Nobuaki Kobayashi as bankruptcy trustee.

In November 2017, some creditors again petitioned for civil rehabilitation. At 5 p.m. on June 22, 2018, the Tokyo District Court formally commenced civil rehabilitation. Bankruptcy proceedings were stayed, and Kobayashi became rehabilitation trustee.

Why this mattered enormously to creditors with bitcoin claims requires explanation. It is the most concrete institutional distinction in the story.

During bankruptcy, BTC claims were converted to yen at the price when bankruptcy began, approximately ¥50,058.12 per coin.

Had liquidation continued on that basis, bitcoin’s later appreciation could have made the estate’s remaining coins worth far more than all customer claims valued at 2014 yen prices. Under the structure of company and bankruptcy law, the theoretical excess would fall to shareholders, not automatically be added to former bitcoin holders’ recoveries.

That was the principal institutional background to creditors’ push for civil rehabilitation.

Civil rehabilitation supplied a third structure.

The fixed valuation used for rehabilitation claims changed to market value when rehabilitation began: ¥749,318.83 per BTC and ¥97,481.19 per BCH. The plan also permitted part of eligible cryptocurrency claims to be distributed directly in BTC or BCH, rather than compulsorily settled entirely in yen.

The legal result was therefore neither “2014 prices” nor “today’s bitcoin price,” the two sides of the popular debate.

It did not freeze every coin at approximately ¥50,000 and pay only cash.

Nor did it reimburse the entire original coin balance in cash at today’s spot price.

Claims were determined using the plan’s fixed valuations and allocation formulas, distributed proportionately from remaining estate assets, with some cryptocurrency claims eligible for coins in kind.

Thus someone whose account once showed 10 BTC would neither simply receive ten times the 2014 yen price nor regain ten full coins.

The estate did not have enough coins to restore every historical balance one for one.

Read that slowly. Someone once saw ten coins on a screen. From some point onward, that number corresponded to nothing. Twelve years later, what returned was a formula-calculated share. During the intervening years, that person did not know what they actually held, because no one knew then.

Creditor counts also have two bases. The 1.1 million active accounts were exchange accounts. Only tens of thousands filed claims or were recognized by the trustee as having self-approved claims in the bankruptcy and rehabilitation proceedings. Reuters’ long-running investigation described approximately 24,000 actual creditors.

The repayment timeline follows.

On June 24, 2024, the trustee announced that BTC and BCH repayments were expected to begin in July. The first payments through certain designated exchanges took place on July 5. By July 16, more than 13,000 rehabilitation creditors had received coins; by July 24, more than 17,000; by August 21, more than 19,000.

On March 27, 2025, following payments in July–August 2024 and January–March 2025, more than 19,500 creditors had cumulatively received BTC or BCH.

On October 27, 2025, the trustee said that, except for a few special payment categories, base repayments, early lump-sum repayments, and intermediate repayments were largely completed for creditors who had finished all necessary procedures without problems. A substantial number remained unpaid because procedures were incomplete or processing issues persisted. The court approved extending the final repayment deadline from October 31, 2025, to October 31, 2026.

One caution is necessary: most batch announcements did not disclose the total number of coins transferred in that batch. A large on-chain wallet movement therefore cannot simply be treated as the exact amount actually received by creditors in a particular batch.

From February 2014 to the October 2026 deadline stated in the announcement.

Twelve years.

This is not simply a story of Japanese courts acting slowly. The case involved tens of thousands of people across more than two hundred countries and territories, a change of procedure and back, a redefinition of valuation, and a vote on distribution. At every step someone had to act, verify, and wait.

None of those steps could be automated. The valuation method changed because people pointed out that the old method would direct the outcome toward shareholders. Identifying that problem, petitioning, persuading the court, and obtaining votes from tens of thousands of people were all human work.

A system targeting one block every ten minutes had an accident twelve years before this essay's source cutoff, yet the human accounts were still not fully settled. In its 2025 announcement, the trustee extended the deadline then in force by another year.

On-chain settlement and settlement in the human world do not use the same kind of time.

VII. Four Propositions

Popular accounts of responsibility usually collapse into one sentence: Karpelès stole 650,000 coins.

The primary record offers four propositions with four different evidentiary statuses.

Separating them is not advocacy for anyone. They point to different remedies. Internal theft calls for background checks and separation of authority. External intrusion calls for key management. Management failure calls for regular audits. Accounting crimes call for external accounting and regulatory reporting.

Conflating them crumples four prescriptions into one.

The first proposition is established. On March 15, 2019, the Tokyo District Court convicted Karpelès of tampering with and unlawfully creating Mt. Gox private electronic records and account data. The case concerned fabricating nonexistent balances through internal account records. He received a two-year-six-month prison sentence, suspended for four years.

The second was not established. The prosecution’s more serious allegations of misappropriating customer funds did not result in conviction.

The third has no such finding behind it. No court judgment identified him as the hacker who stole approximately 650,000 BTC. That was not the offense for which he was convicted in 2019.

The fourth remains an allegation. In 2023, the US Department of Justice announced charges against Alexey Bilyuchenko and Aleksandr Verner. Prosecutors alleged that around September 2011 they and their coconspirators gained unauthorized access to a Japanese server holding Mt. Gox wallets and private keys, and caused approximately 647,000 BTC to move to controlled addresses between September 2011 and at least May 2014. Prosecutors also alleged laundering of more than 300,000 coins through other exchange systems.

That amount and period closely resemble WizSec’s on-chain reconstruction.

Its legal status must be stated fully: these are indictment allegations, not completed convictions. The Justice Department’s own page explicitly says a criminal indictment is only an accusation and defendants are presumed innocent until proven guilty.

Another common claim needs separate treatment. In 2017, WizSec connected address clusters associated with these coins to Alexander Vinnik. Its wording was careful: the investigation could identify him as an important figure in receiving and laundering flows. It did not thereby establish that he originally entered the server and copied the wallet file.

Distinguishing the four propositions is not pedantry.

Conflating an accounting crime with the theft of 650,000 coins obscures what actually happened.

For one or two consecutive years, an exchange failed to notice that its actual holdings differed from customer balances by hundreds of thousands of coins.

That is an asset-management fact independent of the hacker’s identity.

It is also a fact others can repeat. An accounting crime is not the same kind of thing.

The distinction is practical. A person deliberately falsifying records is unusual. A company without regular reconciliation is common. Focus entirely on the former, and similar accidents over the next decade will be treated as one surprise after another.

WizSec even criticized prosecutors’ selection of evidence. It argued that the internal trading bot later called Willy or Gox Bot could not be presented as the source of the 630,000-coin on-chain theft, because those coins could already be traced to external wallets. In WizSec’s reconstruction, the bot ultimately left negative balances of approximately 22,748 BTC and $51.6 million, totaling about $64.93 million at the February 28, 2014 exchange rate. It occurred later and could explain part of the fiat shortfall, not most of the on-chain outflow during 2011–2013.

Losses caused by management failure must still be distinguished from losses deliberately created by management.

Keeping these four propositions separate is work the series must continue doing.

It is also one of this history’s most practical legacies: what someone was accused of, what someone was convicted of, and what remains suspicion must be stated separately, because the remedies differ. This domain later produced genuine fraud established by courts. If allegations are treated as findings here, the sentences will lose their force when those essays arrive.

VIII. The Data Was There All Along

Return to the central fact.

Every movement of those 630,000 coins was recorded in the most public ledger humanity had ever made.

Permanently. Freely accessible. Open to anyone. Recorded at the moment it happened.

For three years, no one checked.

The scope of that statement needs care. There were no block-explorer address-label systems, on-chain analysis companies, or dedicated exchange-reserve watchers then. Those industries developed afterward. This is not an accusation that someone neglected an assigned duty.

The point is different: something can be entirely public and, in practice, unseen. Making it public and making it seen are two separate tasks.

The on-chain transfers WizSec later investigated were already available to inspect in 2012. That does not mean anyone then could have produced the same complete reconstruction.

But a qualification is essential, and it is the essay’s most important technical fact.

WizSec needed the database leaked in 2014 to associate on-chain addresses with Mt. Gox.

The on-chain data was complete. Understanding it required something off-chain.

The chain records transfers between addresses. It does not know who owns them, and does not intend to know.

“Does not intend to” matters. This is not an unfinished feature. It is a design trade-off: if the system does not ask who you are, it needs no one to verify your identity. The previous essay described the other side: because it does not ask, it cannot ask afterward either.

The story therefore cannot be dismissed by saying that people failed to watch the chain. Even if everyone watched every day, they would see transfers among numbers, not an exchange leaking money.

This is the domain’s deepest asymmetry: everything can be checked, but the key needed to check it is not on-chain.

That asymmetry will follow the next eighteen essays. The chain can prove a transfer occurred, a signature was valid, and an order was established. It cannot prove who transferred, whether that person had the right, or what promises stood behind the money.

Almost everything people need to judge lies in that second half.

The remainder’s shape is therefore clear.

It was not unrecorded. It was recorded with exceptional completeness, openness, and permanence.

What was missing was someone assigned to read it, and the off-chain information necessary to do so.

This is a new form of remainder, one that will recur. In the preceding twenty-three essays, remainders were often unmeasured, measured but ignored, or placed outside the boundary. Here is a fourth form: measured precisely, recorded completely, open to everyone, and read by no one.

The second essay ended with electricity and money much clearer in the public record than people's circumstances. Here the problem goes one step further: transfer records existed, but continuous reconciliation and the off-chain information it required were still missing.

Look again at the public transfer of 424,242.42424242 coins.

It was originally self-verification: a company voluntarily showing everyone that it held coins. It succeeded. Anyone could verify it.

That deserves full credit. The transaction was genuine, the verification method public, and anyone in 2011 could calculate it independently. It deceived no one.

The problem was never whether the coins existed that day. It was that, during more than a thousand days afterward, no one performed the same check again.

One-time public verification cannot solve persistent discrepancies between books and holdings. The domain took many years to make that realization a recurring institution.

Once institutionalized, the action acquired another name: proof of reserves. It still performed the same operation, allowing outsiders to reconcile the books once. The difference was its movement from a one-time performance to an obligation repeated periodically.

It still has not solved the whole problem. Proving how many coins you hold at one moment does not prove that you do not owe more to others. The domain would pay other prices for that half-sentence, in later essays.

Return to the 630,000 coins. They were not removed on one black Friday. Over three years, transaction by transaction, with valid signatures, they slowly flowed out through a ledger everyone could see.

The years described above separate the 2011 transaction from the practice becoming an industry convention.

The opening forum post contained one sentence, a dozen or so words saying something had been set up.

Three and a half years later, the company submitted a court page containing two yen figures precise to the unit. The latter exceeded the former by ¥2.6 billion.

In these outflow records, the chain accepted validly signed transfers under its own rules.

It recorded every movement of the 630,000 coins exactly as it happened: time, amount, sending address, receiving address. Nothing omitted. Still verifiable today.

It never considered those transfers wrong. Under its rules, all were valid: correct signatures, sufficient balances, compliant formatting.

It verified whether the signature was right.

It did not verify whether the hand producing that signature belonged to the key’s rightful owner.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
