NFT

NFTs precisely record who holds a token, but do not automatically deliver the image, copyright, server, or community's promises. Ownership thus separates into multiple layers.

1. Four Layers

This subject must be separated into four objects that cannot be used interchangeably.

The token and its on-chain state. Metadata and media files, whether on-chain or off-chain. Contracts or intellectual-property licenses accompanying the transaction. And the trading markets and cash flows that form around the token.

Many disputes talk past one another precisely because all four layers are called “NFT ownership,” or all four are called “NFT market size.”

The timeline also needs correcting first.

The ERC-721 proposal was created on January 24, 2018; ERC-1155 on June 17 of the same year.

2021 was indeed the year NFTs entered the mass market and art auctions and profile-picture projects expanded dramatically. But if a peak is mechanically defined as the highest month in some on-chain trading-volume series, the answer need not fall in 2021.

One data provider's market-fund-flow measure shows another large peak in January–February 2022. The provider explicitly associated one of those peaks with a new marketplace's launch. Subsequent research found extremely high proportions of incentive-driven self-trading on marketplaces that rewarded trading volume with tokens.

Calling 2021 “the NFT peak” is therefore more accurately a statement about the high point of public attention, primary issuance, and mainstream adoption, not necessarily the absolute peak of every uncleaned trading-value series.

Market size, especially, cannot be reduced to one number.

An academic study covering June 2017 through April 2021 counted 6.1 million transactions and 4.7 million NFTs, and reported that trading value in the first four months of 2021 already exceeded $2 billion. That measures transactions within its dataset, not the full year's global market, still less creators' income.

One data provider reported approximately $40 billion in cryptoassets sent during 2021 to addresses and contracts it classified as NFT marketplaces. A broader calculation from the same organization reported at least $44.2 billion. Neither is equivalent to the final net value of completed purchases and sales.

Another organization identified approximately $56 billion in activity, while emphasizing that a large portion was non-qualifying activity, including wash trades, contracts not attributable to projects, and financial collateralization.

Four numbers, four statistical objects.

Each figure has its own coverage and cleaning method. When someone describes the size of the NFT market, first ask which one they mean; different definitions do not independently certify each number’s accuracy.

Section 6 will show something more troublesome: some trades may be people selling to themselves. Providers differ in how they identify and exclude such activity, and no calculation of its share is universally accepted.

In the previous eleven essays, the things the construct sought to make commensurable were on-chain or could be brought on-chain: computational power, balances, prices, collateral, a transfer.

This time, it seeks to make ownership commensurable.

Ownership is something beyond its reach.

Ownership is not a physical quantity. It is a bundle of rights: whether you may copy, alter, monetize, or exclude others from using something. Contracts and law define those rights. Related texts or declarations can be recorded on-chain, but a token field does not automatically confer or enforce the whole bundle.

The remainder is therefore present from the first sentence. It is not an unrecorded transfer but that entire bundle of rights. The chain can record only its outermost layer: who currently owns this identifier.

This layer can be checked against the contract’s rules: which address currently corresponds to an identifier, and how it got there, can generally be traced transaction by transaction. That does not mean every implementation has been error-free for ten years, or that controlling the address automatically gives all legal rights in the work.

The other layers are not entitlements automatically promised by this standard’s owner field.

2. Two Hops

ERC-721's core object is an identifier with its own distinct identity and owner. The standard defines ownership queries, transfers, approvals, and associated events.

The part providing names, symbols, and URI queries is an optional extension.

The standard therefore does not require images, videos, or complete works to be written on-chain. It does not even require every contract to implement that extension.

ERC-1155's principal change is not a more sophisticated file format. It allows one contract to manage many token types simultaneously. Depending on supply rules, an identifier can represent fungible, non-fungible, or intermediate forms, and the standard natively supports batch transfers.

Issuing tens of thousands of game items or multiple-edition works therefore no longer requires a separate contract for every asset type, as earlier arrangements did.

The most precise description of an NFT's on-chain contents is this: the chain stores contract code, sufficient data to determine token state, and a history of transactions and events. Whether the work itself is on-chain is a separate question.

Note especially that the URI-returning function is a function. It can read storage or dynamically compute a string. It is therefore inaccurate to say categorically that a web address is always stored on-chain.

A typical structure involves two hops: the on-chain identifier points to a URI; that URI returns metadata; and the metadata points to the actual media file.

Three common implementations have entirely different persistence properties.

In a fully on-chain implementation, the function directly returns embedded data. The contract supplies the metadata, graphics, or generation parameters. Retrieving the same file then does not require the project's server to remain in business.

But if the contract has administrator-adjustable parameters, a proxy-upgrade mechanism, or dynamic generation logic, on-chain still does not automatically mean that content can never change.

An IPFS implementation points to a content hash. The content identified by the same hash cannot silently be replaced with another file.

Content addressing, however, is not permanent hosting. If no node continues storing the data blocks, or a gateway relied on by users stops operating, the content may still become unavailable.

The correct distinction is between two properties: immutable content identity and continued content availability.

A conventional-server implementation points to an ordinary URL, while the image in the metadata may point to a separate content-delivery network.

If the server shuts down, the domain expires, or the database is deleted, the token and ownership record remain on-chain, while wallets and marketplaces may display only a blank.

Conversely, if the issuer still controls the server, metadata or images behind the same URL may be replaced.

The joint report submitted to Congress by the U.S. Copyright Office and Patent and Trademark Office explicitly distinguishes a token from the work it references, noting that tokens usually point to a copy of a work rather than containing the work themselves.

Another contemporary primary source states this separation still more plainly. When a marketplace introduced lazy minting in 2020, its technical explanation separated on-chain issuance from metadata. An NFT might not even have been written on-chain before its first purchase or transfer.

An NFT appearing in an interface does not guarantee that a corresponding on-chain state change has already occurred.

As for how many early NFTs have ceased working, the empirical answer is not a single percentage.

One large academic measurement examined 12,353 contracts and 6,234,141 NFTs. It used multiple gateways and repeated access over a longer period to reduce the risk of mistaking temporary disruption for permanent failure. It found inaccessible assets in 25.24% of contracts and duplicate assets in another 21.48% of contracts.

The denominator is contracts. This cannot be rewritten as “25.24% of images are dead.”

Another persistence study followed a set of assets for approximately six months and found that around 45% of tokens failed the authors' initial persistence criteria. That is broader than a dead-link rate. It measures the authors' defined conditions and should not be paraphrased as “45% of images are permanently inaccessible.”

A third, narrower investigation sampled high-volume collections to examine storage patterns. Approximately 38.84% used IPFS, 31.68% centralized storage, 25.62% on-chain storage, and 3.86% could not be determined.

Even among prominent projects, then, “the data is on the blockchain” is not a uniform fact. The sample's concentration on leading projects also prevents it from representing all early NFTs.

All three studies differ in denominator, sampling period, access strategy, and definition of failure. Their results cannot be averaged.

The narrow answer to what happens when a link fails is therefore this:

The token usually does not disappear with the link.

What disappears is the ability of wallets and marketplaces to reconstruct a human-recognizable work from the on-chain identifier.

In practical consequences, this distinction is no word game. A surviving token means that the ownership record remains intact, transfers can continue, and history can still be queried. An inaccessible work means that, to most people, the token has no content left.

Something can remain intact in law and in the accounts while offering nothing in use.

3. Section 204(a)

Buying an NFT and acquiring copyright are two different transactions.

The joint report to Congress gives the clearest official formulation: transferring an NFT transfers possession of the digital token. It does not necessarily transfer a physical or digital copy of the underlying work, or its copyright. The token and copyright in the work are separate.

Even if metadata declares that a buyer owns a digital or physical copy, ownership of the copy does not itself confer copyright.

Several questions must therefore be asked separately.

Control and transfer of the on-chain token are usually automatic.

Acquisition of copyright in the work is not automatic.

Permission to reproduce the work is not automatic.

Permission to create derivative works is not automatic.

Permission to use the image commercially is not automatic.

Resale of the token is generally permitted by the contract and platform terms.

Exclusivity over the underlying image is not assured.

Those rights that do not arrive automatically with the token need another legal basis. It may be a copyright assignment or license; public-domain status, statutory exceptions, or rights the buyer already has may also matter. An ERC-721 transfer does not answer those questions by itself.

One legal requirement here gives the essay its sharpest point.

Section 204(a) of the U.S. Copyright Act requires copyright assignments and exclusive licenses to be in writing and signed.

As of the 2024 joint report, courts had not determined whether a smart contract alone satisfies that statutory signed-writing requirement.

An on-chain transaction can of course carry a cryptographic signature. The difficulty lies elsewhere: a private key’s signature on a transaction does not, merely because the transfer executed, establish compliance with copyright law’s requirements for a writing and valid signature by the rights holder.

The actual terms of leading projects differ enough to explain almost everything.

One profile-picture project's current license does not transfer all the issuer's copyright to holders. It grants a license: beyond personal, noncommercial use, holders receive an unrestricted worldwide license to use, copy, and display the purchased artwork and create commercial derivative works, for example on merchandise.

Holding the token may therefore bring extensive commercial-use rights. Those rights come from the company's contractual terms, not ERC-721 itself.

A sports-card project's terms take the opposite approach. They state that buyers fully own the underlying NFT and may sell, give away, or destroy it within the system's permitted operations, while the company or its licensors retain intellectual-property rights in the associated artwork.

Holders receive a worldwide, nonexclusive, nontransferable, royalty-free license primarily limited to personal, noncommercial use and display. Without consent they may not modify images, make commercial merchandise, promote third-party products, or undertake other commercial exploitation.

Once the token is sold, the license ends.

A third project uses the public domain. Its official statement places its artwork, marks, traits, and generated images in the public domain, available for anyone to use.

A holder can purchase the on-chain token and a position associated with the governance organization. Commercial use of the image is not exclusive to holders, because nonholders can lawfully copy and commercially use the same material.

A fourth arrangement was added later. For an older project dating to 2017, after another company acquired the intellectual property in 2022, the official history page described a new arrangement granting holders full commercial rights.

This establishes, at minimum, that those commercial rights did not arise automatically from the 2017 token standard. The rights holder subsequently changed the licensing framework separately.

Four projects, four models: broad commercial licensing, narrow noncommercial licensing, public-domain use, and commercial licensing for holders added later.

At the token-standard interface layer, they can be the same kind of thing. Their licensing arrangements and individual contracts do not thereby become identical.

What courts have and have not decided also needs separating.

In 2022, the Hangzhou Internet Court held in one case that minting an existing work as an NFT without permission implicated reproduction and online dissemination. It addressed minting, platform responsibility, and infringement, not which copyrights automatically pass to an ordinary buyer after purchase.

A 2025 U.S. Ninth Circuit decision addressed trademarks. It held that NFTs can constitute goods under trademark law and confirmed the plaintiffs' trademark priority, while reversing parts of the lower court's summary judgment on issues including likelihood of confusion.

It is an important precedent, but not a ruling on whether holders acquire copyright in the underlying image.

The joint report also specifically noted that relevant U.S. cases concerning matters such as whether minting creates a derivative work had settled or been withdrawn, leaving no direct adjudication. Nor had a court ruled on whether a smart contract meets the signed-writing requirement for copyright transfer.

At least under U.S. law, therefore, the contract text, rather than the token itself, remains the first material to examine when determining reproduction, adaptation, and commercial-use rights.

This section yields a practical conclusion.

On-chain ownership, file accessibility, and intellectual property are three independent dimensions.

They can combine in any arrangement. You may own a token whose image is no longer accessible and have no commercial-use rights whatsoever.

4. Voluntary

Technically, royalties have never been an automatic ERC-721 deduction.

The transfer function does not know whether a transfer is a sale, a gift, movement between one's own wallets, or a transfer inside a protocol. Nor does it know the sale price.

A research organization's analysis of on-chain royalty data makes the same point: one transfer function handles nonsale transfers too. This is the fundamental identification problem for protocol-level mandatory royalties.

ERC-2981 standardized something narrower. Created on September 15, 2020, the proposal's core function tells a caller: if this is a sale at this price, this amount should go to this address.

It standardizes royalty information, not forcible deductions from buyers' and sellers' accounts.

The proposal explicitly requires royalty payments to be voluntary. Whether a marketplace implements payment remains that marketplace's decision.

The actual technical path behind early promises of perpetual creator royalties was therefore: the collection sets a royalty; the marketplace identifies a sale; its settlement contract splits the payment; the seller receives less; and the creator receives the royalty.

It was not: a token moves, and the protocol unconditionally taxes it.

The policy changes can be set out chronologically.

In late August 2022, one marketplace made royalties buyer-selectable for most transactions, with a minimum as low as zero. The company said it needed to adapt as other marketplaces moved toward similar mechanisms.

On October 14 that year, another marketplace made royalties optional and temporarily waived its existing 2% marketplace fee. It called the decision difficult, emphasizing the conflict between traders' preference for low fees and creators' income.

On October 27, a third marketplace announced that creator royalties would no longer be enforced by default, though buyers could still pay voluntarily. It said zero-royalty marketplaces had changed user behavior and allocated part of its protocol fee to compensate collections.

Late in 2022, the largest marketplace moved toward an operator-filtering mechanism, allowing collections to restrict which operators could transfer tokens in order to compel marketplaces to honor royalties.

The nature of this step needs stating plainly.

It tried to move royalty enforcement from marketplace goodwill to restrictions on token tradability.

The cost was reduced freedom of composable transfer of the ordinary ERC-721 kind.

This enforcement design traded some composability for enforceability: to make specified resale payments harder to bypass, it restricted otherwise freer transfer paths. That added restriction is not an inherent power of ordinary ERC-721.

On February 17, 2023, the same marketplace temporarily reduced its own fee to zero. Collections without on-chain enforcement moved to optional creator earnings, with a default minimum of 0.5%.

From August 31, 2023, it stopped enforcing the filter for new collections and made creator fees optional, with existing collections transitioning through February 29, 2024. The company explained that the mechanism had failed to secure ecosystem-wide cooperation.

In April 2024, it added compatibility with a restrictive token design, allowing creators using that design to enforce creator earnings again.

Again, this shows enforcement being relocated to rules governing token transferability, rather than being a natural property of ordinary ERC-721.

How much creators actually received in royalties is another number with conflicting definitions.

On October 21, 2022, one research organization filtered an on-chain table for Ethereum NFT sales with positive creator fees and calculated cumulative creator royalties exceeding $1.8 billion from the sample's starting point through that date.

That does not cover every chain worldwide, nor is it all creators' net income. It is one data provider's classification and dollar conversion of creator fees on Ethereum.

The same analysis found that the top ten entities accounted for approximately 27% of royalties and 482 collections for 80%.

Collections associated with the largest entity collected approximately $148 million.

On November 30 that year, the largest marketplace announced that creators on its platform had received more than $1 billion in creator fees during 2022 to date; an earlier, more specific disclosure put the amount around $1.1 billion. The company also said approximately 80% went to collections outside that year's top ten.

These are company statements based on internal platform statistics, with a different denominator from the cross-market aggregation above. The amounts cannot be added.

The most verifiable post-policy-change decline is expressed in daily flows rather than cumulative totals.

A September 2023 account of a public dashboard reported that one marketplace's creator royalties fell from approximately $354,008 per day on June 28 to around $83,970, a 76% decline. Another fell from approximately $830,661 to approximately $31,229 per day, a decline of around 96%.

That decline incorporates shrinking trading activity, changes in cryptoasset prices, and changes in royalty policy. It cannot be attributed solely to platforms making royalties optional.

The supported history is therefore not that smart contracts invented perpetual royalties and platforms later deleted the code.

It is this: leading early marketplaces generally honored project-set royalties at settlement, producing cumulative payments on the billion-dollar scale. As traders migrated to zero- or low-royalty marketplaces, competition deprived that convention of enforceability. ERC-2981 itself had never guaranteed payment.

5. One Hundred Dollars or Less

What platforms actually collect must be separated from creator fees.

During the boom, the largest marketplace's standard model charged a 2.5% marketplace fee per sale. Its technical documentation still clearly distinguishes, in its fee examples, a 2.5% marketplace fee from, for instance, a 5% creator fee: the former is required marketplace consideration, while the latter may be creator consideration.

During the fiercest competition in 2023, it temporarily reduced its own fee to zero. That alone shows marketplace fees and creator royalties are different cash flows.

Another marketplace retained a 2% protocol fee when it changed its policy in October 2022. The new arrangement directed 0.5 percentage points to collections and creators, with the remainder continuing into the protocol's economic system.

Meanwhile, it distributed its own tokens to buyers and sellers according to trading volume.

Issuing those tokens is a trading incentive, not cash revenue paid by customers to the platform.

A third marketplace temporarily waived its previous 2% marketplace fee when switching to optional royalties.

A supposed “marketplace cut” must therefore be separated into at least marketplace fees, creator fees, and network fees. All three cannot be booked as platform revenue.

For these privately held companies, the boom did not produce a publicly audited annual platform-revenue series equivalent to listed companies' annual reports.

Multiplying trading value by a nominal fee rate can estimate theoretical gross fees, but fee holidays, private transactions, different chains and protocol versions, promotions, and revenue-sharing arrangements introduce errors. On-chain data providers can measure protocol fees; that is not corporate net income either.

Numbers obtained by multiplying trading value by 2.5% therefore cannot impersonate company revenue.

Issuers' revenue has at least three layers.

At primary issuance, the initial buyer pays assets to the minting contract or issuing address; the project and artist receive primary-sale revenue, and the buyer receives the token. The buyer may also pay network fees. On Ethereum, the base fee is burned, while priority fees and some other payments go to block producers; the allocation depends on the chain and transaction mechanism.

The primary buyer bears subsequent price risk, while the project has already realized its sale revenue.

Secondary royalties: a later buyer pays the sale price; the previous holder receives most of it; the marketplace retains its platform fee; and the creator receives royalties under the rules actually enforced.

One side of a royalty is creator income. The other is not money created for free by a blockchain. It is usually a reduction in the seller's net proceeds or, under some pricing arrangements, a cost shared through price between buyer and seller.

Subsequent products: a collection's brand may separately sell tokens, virtual land, game assets, merchandise, event access, or intellectual-property licenses.

The entities entitled to those revenues may even differ from the original issuer. The entire market capitalization of subsequent tokens, land transaction volumes, or branded merchandise sales cannot all be called creator income.

Writing both sides of a transaction makes the relationship clear.

At primary issuance, the buyer pays the mint price and receives the token; the issuer receives that primary-sale revenue.

On a secondary sale, the later buyer pays the price and receives the token. The former holder receives the price less platform fees and royalties; the platform receives its fee, and the creator receives the royalty.

The previous holder's realized return is therefore not selling price minus purchase price. It is selling price after platform fees and royalties, less purchase price, transaction fees, and taxes.

This also explains the distributional conflict in royalties. At a fixed sale price, higher creator income from resale means lower net proceeds for the seller. If buyers fully incorporate royalty costs into their offers, this also feeds back into the purchase price they will accept.

ERC-2981 does not determine that economic incidence. It returns only a suggested amount.

Now we reach actual creator income, where a negative finding must be stated explicitly.

This research found no reliable ecosystem-wide dataset that uses natural persons as its unit, reports both mean and median creator net income, deducts transaction fees, platform fees, and taxes, and removes duplicate project teams and multiple wallets.

A public chain can reveal what an address received. It cannot reveal whether that address is a shared treasury for multiple creators, whether one person has ten addresses, or what off-chain revenue splits, salaries, taxes, agent fees, and refunds apply.

That absence must remain an absence. Average sale price cannot substitute for average artist income.

One of the closest peer-reviewed sources studies an art platform. Researchers mapped more than 48,000 artworks, more than 15,000 artists, and their invitation, bidding, and purchasing networks.

They found markedly right-skewed creator earnings, with rich and poor clusters. Follower counts on the platform were significantly associated with total earnings.

The highest-earning artist cluster in the sample totaled approximately $2,703,171, averaging $6,766. In the randomized comparison, the highest cluster averaged only approximately $2,684.

These figures concern platform sales proceeds and network relationships, not creators' after-tax household income. They do not provide a median for all creators that can be directly paired with the mean.

In 2021, an artist also published a non-peer-reviewed analysis of primary-sales data. Her original account identified $100 or less as the largest price bracket, comprising approximately 34% of primary sales. Contemporary secondary reporting, citing the full dataset, put approximately 53.6% of primary sales below $200.

This rebuts the use of a few million-dollar sales to represent a typical NFT. It still measures individual sales, not each creator's net income.

She also noted that, given transaction fees and platform costs at the time, expenses could consume a large share of a low-priced sale.

How much an artist ultimately kept from an $80 work depended on the network congestion that day.

Some people did nevertheless achieve large sales through this channel that had previously been difficult to obtain for purely digital work.

An auction house's official account of one artist says he began selling work online at seventeen. In 2021, a sale of five works centered on his personal experience totaled more than $2 million; contemporary media gave the precise total as $2.16 million. The auction house also recorded a collaboration with a digital-fashion brand generating more than $3 million in sales in around seven minutes, and more than $50 million in cumulative market sales of related NFTs between 2020 and 2022.

This case also shows why sales cannot be equated with an artist's take-home income.

The $50 million describes market sales, not his personal after-tax cash income. The $2.16 million auction combined NFTs and physical works.

The narrow supported claim is that this channel enabled a still-young artist working primarily in digital media to enter the international auction system and achieve seven-figure artwork sales within a short period.

It cannot establish the counterfactual that he could not have earned that money without the channel.

6. Two Seventy-Eights

There is no industry consensus percentage for wash trading across the entire market. Differences between empirical results arise precisely from their definitions.

An early academic study examined the 52 highest-volume collections from January 2018 through mid-November 2021. Using address relationships and repetitive trading patterns to identify suspicious self-trading, it classified approximately 3.93% of addresses and 2.04% of sales as wash trading, inflating trading volume by up to approximately $149.5 million.

The authors explicitly treated the result as a conservative lower bound: when anonymous addresses share a controller, a demonstrable on-chain link may not exist.

Another research group extended coverage through January 2022 and used multiple detection methods. It found 5.66% of collections affected and estimated artificial volume of approximately $3.4061 billion.

It also distinguished two motives: washing prices upward to sell later to a third party, and washing volume to collect marketplace token rewards.

The latter was clearly more profitable. On the token-reward marketplace, successful reward-arbitrage operations averaged approximately $1.055 million in profit, with a success rate above 80%. Among attempts to inflate prices before resale, roughly half ultimately lost money.

Late in 2022, a data researcher applied more aggressive cleaning rules to Ethereum trading value, including a wallet buying from itself, two addresses repeatedly exchanging the same token, and an address repeatedly buying back the same token.

The rules flagged approximately 58% of 2022 trading value as wash trading, rising above 80% at one point in January. Two token-reward marketplaces were approximately 98% and 87% respectively.

These figures come from public on-chain filtering, not a peer-reviewed paper. They are best described as estimates under those cleaning rules, not fraudulent trading values established by a court.

In 2024, another research group applied transaction-network features and behavioral rules to more than 25 million transactions across four marketplaces. Again, platforms with trading incentives showed more anomalous activity: estimated wash-traded value was 94.5% and 84.2% at two marketplaces.

The direction resembles the earlier result, but the figures differ because of trading periods, marketplace coverage, and classification rules, among other factors.

Another paper compared direct and indirect detection methods across three large marketplaces, finding that approximately 30–40% of trading activity and 25–95% of trading value might involve wash trading. It found substantial variation in the performance of indirect filters.

Its contribution illustrates precisely this point: when the underlying fact of common wallet control cannot be directly observed, slight parameter changes can produce entirely different market-wide percentages.

One data provider used a narrower self-funding relationship, examining repeated NFT sales to addresses financed by the seller. It identified 262 participating address groups repeatedly doing this. In its estimates, 110 collectively gained approximately $8.875 million, while 152 collectively lost approximately $417,000, for a net gain of approximately $8.458 million.

That is a study of identified wash traders' profits and losses, not the market's overall wash-trading rate.

Set side by side, these results yield not a single proportion but a stable measurement pattern.

For ordinary marketplaces without volume rewards and leading early collections, strict rules identify only a few percentage points.

Once token-reward marketplaces and broader wallet-control rules are included, the proportion measured by dollar trading value can reach 80–90%.

There is no logical contradiction between the two sets of figures. Their samples are not the same market.

The trading-volume reward layer also needs separate examination. One marketplace distributed tokens to buyers and sellers based on daily volume over an issuance schedule spanning approximately 721 days. Its first phase scheduled 2,866,500 tokens per day in trading rewards.

A trader might therefore willingly pay transaction fees, marketplace fees, and royalties to trade repeatedly with themselves, provided the expected value of the token reward was greater.

The other side of the reward is not fresh consumer demand for NFTs, but newly distributed protocol tokens. If recipients sell their rewards for cash, the external money ultimately comes from market participants willing to buy those tokens.

A clearly bounded slice of evidence concerns later buyers' losses.

One data provider examining resales after initial minting found that approximately 78% of non-allowlisted buyers' resales lost money, and approximately 59% returned less than half the initial outlay. Approximately 78% of allowlisted buyers' resales were profitable, and 51% returned at least twice the initial outlay.

The same 78%, pointing in opposite directions.

Both sides of the money flow are clear. Allowlists let some people enter on more favorable issuance terms. If prices subsequently rose, their profits came from later purchasers willing to pay more. Meanwhile, projects earned primary-sale revenue and potentially secondary royalties.

Non-allowlisted or later entrants were more exposed to price risk after prices had already risen in the secondary market.

This still is not a complete answer covering all NFTs.

Observing resales alone omits losing positions that were never sold. Marking every NFT to a collection's floor price assumes that large quantities can all be liquidated near the same quote.

These assets' low liquidity makes realized losses, losses measured against last transaction prices, and floor-price marks three different statistical objects.

A collection's floor price falling 90% from its peak therefore does not directly imply that 90% of holders lost 90% of their cash.

7. What Needs Rewriting

Several claims need rewriting. How they should be rewritten is the subject of this section.

NFTs benefit creators directly.

The overbroad version says they remove middlemen and let creators earn automatically and permanently on every resale.

The narrower supported version is that they can allow issuers to sell tokens directly to wallets through contracts and open markets, and allow marketplaces supporting creator fees to route part of secondary-sale proceeds back to a designated address. By 2022, this mechanism had indeed produced cumulative payments on the billion-dollar scale on Ethereum.

But marketplaces, wallets, storage services, discovery and curation, and payment gateways remain intermediaries. Standards do not compel payment. Returns are highly concentrated. Many low-priced primary sales yield limited income after transaction costs.

A distributional qualification is also necessary: secondary royalties are creator income on one side and usually reduced seller proceeds, or a cost incorporated into the buyer's offer, on the other. They do not create additional aggregate social income out of nothing outside the protocol.

You are only buying a link.

This is too narrow and can be wrong.

A more accurate version is that you acquire control or an ownership record for the state defined by an on-chain contract and identifier, together with any additional licenses that a project's contract may attach to holding it.

In common implementations, the token does not itself contain the image file. The optional function returns metadata, which points to the image. That URI may be a centralized URL, IPFS, or fully on-chain embedded data.

“Only a link” therefore fails for fully on-chain implementations and omits the transferable token itself.

Official and empirical sources do support the claim that most NFTs do not store their underlying media files directly in on-chain state.

More importantly, even a permanently functioning link cannot establish copyright from a technical reference. The four projects in Section 3 provide the clearest counterexamples.

Royalties are in the smart contract, so they cannot be bypassed.

This should become: ordinary ERC-721 does not know whether a transfer is a sale; ERC-2981 provides only a quote; early royalties' stability largely came from voluntary marketplace enforcement during settlement. Later mandatory designs changed their point of intervention by restricting token transferability. They did not prove that the original standard had always been able to levy an unconditional charge.

The market was worth more than $40 billion in 2021.

This needs a sentence with a complete denominator. Three statements can simultaneously be true: one data provider observed approximately $40 billion sent to NFT marketplaces under its address classification; another calculation of related contracts reported at least $44.2 billion; and another organization identified approximately $56 billion under a broader activity definition, while itself classifying a substantial portion as non-qualifying.

On-chain volume is genuine demand.

This should become: on-chain records can tell us with high precision which addresses transferred how much by which routes. A transaction alone cannot establish that buyer and seller are two economically independent natural persons. One controller can own dozens of addresses. Trading-volume rewards, in particular, also turn trading value into an input for producing token subsidies.

NFTs brought ordinary artists substantial new income on average.

The available materials support only part of the first half. Particular creators did achieve sales through this channel rarely seen in the earlier digital-art market. Platform data also establish that many artists participated and received sale proceeds.

But industry-wide, person-level, deduplicated, cost-adjusted data are lacking for ordinary creators' average net income.

The narrower version should be: during a particular period, NFTs created new direct-sale and secondary-royalty channels for some digital creators, including verifiable high-income cases. Public data do not establish how much sustainable net income the typical creator received, and returns are clearly highly concentrated.

8. The Same Kind of Thing

Combining both sides of the money flows across the cycle reveals this structure.

Creators and issuers receive external funds first, at primary minting. Popular projects may continue collecting royalties on secondary sales.

Platforms charge transaction-based marketplace fees or issue tokens in exchange for trading volume and liquidity.

Early buyers entering through allowlists, low mint prices, or earlier secondary-market prices can realize capital gains during rising markets by selling to later entrants.

Later buyers pay the market prices already established. Whether they can subsequently exit depends on still-later buyers, liquidity, and fee costs.

When prices rise, all four groups appear able to make money simultaneously.

Projects already have primary-sale funds, platforms receive fees, creators receive royalties, and previous buyers obtain higher prices from new buyers.

Those realized incomes correspond to assets still held by subsequent owners and the risk of continuing to seek the next buyer.

Add trading-volume rewards, and nominal turnover may be amplified again by loops of platform token subsidies.

Including wash trades, movements between wallets under common control, and repeated turnover in market size thus misrepresents repeated transfers of existing assets as an equivalent amount of new external money entering.

Return to the four projects in Section 3.

A profile-picture token with a broad commercial license and a sports-moment token with only noncommercial display rights are the same kind of thing on-chain: a contract address, an identifier, an owner field.

One holder can print the image on merchandise and sell it. The other's noncommercial display license ends when the token is sold.

The public-domain project goes further: holders can use the image commercially, but so can nonholders, because the image is already in the public domain.

One standard, four kinds of rights.

The standard itself knows nothing about them.

Pause here, because this connects everything in the preceding seven sections.

The standard specifies how ownership is recorded, transferred, and approved. It does not specify where the work is stored, hence the three implementations with entirely different persistence properties in Section 2. It does not specify what holders may do, hence the four distinct licenses in Section 3. It does not know whether a transfer is a sale, hence the history in Section 4 of royalties moving from convention to optional payment.

Every blank was subsequently filled by an arrangement outside the standard: a server, a legal contract, a marketplace’s settlement logic. Settlement logic can run on-chain, and license texts can also be recorded there. How they may be changed depends separately on technical permissions, existing contracts, and applicable law.

The remainder's position is therefore clear.

This is not because token state is wholly inaccessible. Under the relevant contract’s rules, the owner field and transfer history can generally be checked. That verifiability does not automatically tell us the rights in the work beyond the field.

It is outside that field.

You can specify on-chain who currently owns this identifier.

You cannot specify what “owns” means.

That second statement must be assessed through a contract, a law, a judgment, or a license change made under valid terms.

Those bases can have on-chain records, but their legal effect is not determined by the chain alone. Some arrangements can also change under the governing contracts and law.

When they change, the owner field need not change with them. The address and transfer history may remain the same while the associated legal arrangement differs.

Commercial-use rights associated with a token may change through a valid license, contractual amendment, or legal judgment without changing the token’s owner field. That does not mean an issuer may arbitrarily revoke a license already validly granted.

The chain updates that owner field under the contract’s transfer rules.

It does not know what the person actually received once that field was changed.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
