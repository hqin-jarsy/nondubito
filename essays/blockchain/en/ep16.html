Terra and Luna

A nominal conversion formula is not a guarantee of getting a dollar back: minting, burning, liquidity, and halts expose different layers of Terra and Luna’s promise.

1. The Same Thing

The protocol documentation stated the central relationship directly: the market module allowed anyone to exchange $1 worth of LUNA for one UST, and vice versa.

Provided that both the chain and the oracle were working normally, this created two arbitrage paths.

When UST traded at $1.01, an arbitrageur could use $1 worth of LUNA to mint one UST within the protocol, then sell it on the market for $1.01. LUNA was burned, the UST supply increased, and the premium was pushed down.

Conversely, when UST fell to $0.99, an arbitrageur could buy one for $0.99, exchange it through the protocol for newly minted LUNA nominally worth $1, and sell that LUNA. This time UST was burned and LUNA was issued. The UST supply contracted, theoretically bringing its price back to $1.

The second path was the mechanical entry point to the spiral that followed.

What deserves particular attention here is where the arbitrageur’s one-cent profit came from.

The UST seller in the market received one cent less. The protocol issued LUNA to give the arbitrageur redemption assets worth $1. The arbitrageur then sold that LUNA to the market.

In practice, giving stablecoin holders a fixed dollar redemption value transferred the pressure to LUNA’s supply and buying demand.

Restoring the price therefore depended on more than the existence of arbitrage. At least four things had to hold at once: the protocol, oracle, and chain had to remain available; the market had to believe newly minted LUNA could still be sold near the oracle price; external demand for LUNA had to be deep enough to absorb the issuance; and net exits from UST could not exceed that capacity.

In quiet periods, all four held, apparently without effort. For more than two years, the mechanism therefore appeared to work very well.

They had one thing in common: each concerned the world outside. None was something the protocol itself could guarantee.

This has the same shape as the sentence in Episode 11. There, a contract could specify a ten-minute auction, but not that a second bidder would arrive within ten minutes. Here, the protocol could specify that one UST would buy $1 worth of LUNA, but not that the LUNA could be sold.

Under the native conversion mechanism, UST holders did not receive a direct claim against independent dollar reserves; they received LUNA within the same risk system. The foundation’s later use of external assets to defend the peg did not turn that native claim into a right to withdraw dollar reserves at par.

That sentence is the foundation of the next seven sections. It can be put more plainly.

Someone presents one UST for redemption. The protocol gives them LUNA. The first thing they usually want to do with that LUNA is sell it.

Every redemption thus does two things at once: it extinguishes a liability and introduces new selling pressure. That pressure falls on precisely the asset needed to settle the next redemption.

Essay 8 described two reserve-backed stablecoin routes. Their reserves consisted of external assets such as short-term Treasuries, overnight repos, and bank deposits, with an issuer that could be sued. Direct redemption still depended on eligibility and terms.

Here, the native conversion mechanism’s central support was the system’s own LUNA, not external reserves that every holder could directly withdraw at par.

The remainder was therefore in place from the first line of the design.

It was not an unrecorded sum or a hidden exposure. The accounts formed a complete closed system on-chain. Every UST burned and every LUNA minted can still be traced transaction by transaction.

The remainder was the condition of that closure: for the accounts to balance, someone outside had to remain willing to take what the system minted. No contract could write that condition into existence.

This remainder thus belongs to the same kind as the one in Episode 11: a premise that cannot be written into the construct, yet on which the entire construct depends.

The difference is that there the missing element was a bidder. Here it was a buyer willing to take the other side.

This case was harder, because bidding could at least be improved by adjusting parameters: change ten minutes to six hours; add incentives. There was no parameter that could make buyers appear.

2. One Hundred Million Was Not a Hard Cap

A widely circulated claim needs correcting first.

Terra did not have a simple, fixed $100 million daily hard cap that reset at a set time.

The market module used virtual liquidity pools, dynamic spreads, and recovery parameters. Greater exchange volume increased protocol fees and reduced the attractiveness of arbitrage. Later transaction-level reconstructions showed that arbitrageurs continued using native swaps until the fee roughly consumed UST’s market discount.

During the collapse, the project itself supplied a quantitative measure.

On May 11, 2022, Do Kwon supported a proposal to raise the base pool from 50 million to 100 million Special Drawing Rights and reduce the pool recovery period from 36 blocks to 18. He said this would increase minting capacity from approximately $293 million to approximately $1.2 billion.

This was effective throughput determined jointly by protocol parameters, recovery speed, and spreads, not a quota schedule with a clearly defined daily gate.

What increasing that throughput meant needs to be stated plainly.

It meant burning UST faster at the cost of issuing LUNA faster.

It did not mean finding new external dollar assets.

The proposal was therefore widening the outlet of a machine that had already begun leaking.

Its logic was not absurd: if UST could be burned more quickly, its discount might disappear more quickly. The cost was to concentrate dilution into a few days.

The day after the proposal passed, daily new issuance jumped from approximately one billion tokens to approximately 200 billion.

Increasing throughput worked.

It worked by accelerating burning and issuance together.

The chain of conditions producing instability did not require a particular attacker. Net redemptions alone could create it: substantial net selling pushed UST below its peg; discounted UST was exchanged for $1 worth of LUNA; the new LUNA was sold, lowering its price; redeeming the next UST required minting more LUNA; dilution and selling pressure increased; LUNA’s market capitalization and liquidity shrank further relative to UST liabilities; holders became less willing to wait; more redemptions followed.

Later research found that one point at which the run noticeably accelerated was when UST’s and LUNA’s market valuations began to converge.

The actual feedback in this chain was simple.

The lower the price, the more units of LUNA had to be minted. The more units minted, the harder it became to sell them at the previous price.

Once that feedback overwhelmed ordinary buying demand, something peculiar happened.

At the oracle price, one UST corresponding to a nominal dollar’s worth of LUNA was the conversion mechanism’s reference point. What was actually received also depended on dynamic spreads, parameters, and whether conversion was operating at the time.

The statement that the newly minted LUNA could actually fetch $1 in an external market began to fail.

The separation of those two statements is one of the most important points in this history.

A conversion mechanism based on oracle prices can continue applying its formula after its economic support has failed. Satisfying that calculation does not mean the received assets can actually be sold for the corresponding sum; market prices and liquidity must be checked separately.

It verifies the exchange ratio. It does not verify whether what is received can still be exchanged for something else.

The fork in Essay 6 concerned whether people should change the chain’s outcome. In Essay 11, auctions could settle under the rules without sufficient bidding competition. Here another gap appeared: continuing nominal conversion calculations did not guarantee that holders could recover a dollar’s purchasing power.

The promise itself was becoming worth less and less.

This does not mean the chain lacked warning indicators. Supply, conversion pressure, and reserve movements could leave observable changes. The nominal conversion formula itself simply could not decide whether the wider system could still bear that pressure.

3. 4.7 Times

Anchor’s own launch materials said deposits could earn a stable annualized return of about 20%. The principal economic sources were staking yields on borrowers’ proof-of-stake collateral, together with borrowing income. When actual income exceeded the target rate, the surplus entered the yield reserve. When income fell short, the reserve covered the difference.

Token incentives must be separated from the sources of deposit yield.

Borrowers did pay borrowing interest and provide collateral whose staking cash flows the protocol could capture. At the same time, the protocol distributed its governance token to borrowers as an incentive, allowing their effective net borrowing costs to fall far below the nominal rate.

That token issuance was another layer of subsidy. Its function was to increase borrowing demand, not to provide external cash directly to depositors.

The structural problem before the crisis was the severe imbalance in the scale of the two sides.

One retrospective on-chain reconstruction put peak deposits at approximately 14 billion UST, against only approximately three billion in borrowing.

Deposits were roughly 4.7 times outstanding loans.

That ratio is an important starting point for examining the yield gap, but it cannot by itself prove that no borrowing rate could cover it. To support the roughly 20% deposit promise, actual borrowing interest, collateral yields, and other income must be added together. Any remaining shortfall requires reserves or subsidies.

It is an arithmetic question, but actual yields and income must enter the calculation. It does not first require a judgment about anyone’s motives.

Public deposit and loan balances made that gap examinable. Establishing its size on every day before the collapse would require daily balances, income, and subsidy records, not just two figures close to the collapse.

That reconstruction came from an important Terra counterparty. It should therefore be treated as that counterparty’s retrospective data analysis, not an independent regulatory audit.

A set of estimates indicates the scale of the gap. An institution later cited a third-party estimate that Anchor’s actual yield in April 2022 was approximately 5.81%, against a target of approximately 19.46%.

The difference was approximately 13.65 percentage points.

That does not mean the project paid precisely 13.65% of each depositor’s balance in cash. It means that, at the estimated observation point, income generated by the protocol covered only a small portion of the target yield.

Mechanically applying that percentage-point gap to 14 billion in deposits produces an annualized shortfall of approximately 1.91 billion tokens, or approximately 5.24 million per day.

But 14 billion was the peak deposit figure, while 5.81% was an estimate for a different observation period. They were not simultaneous. The multiplication illustrates a running shortfall on the scale of millions per day; it cannot be written as an actual fixed daily loss of 5.24 million.

One contemporary community calculation measured approximately 4.2177 million per day at another point, using another date and method. It corroborated the same order of magnitude.

Two injections used to fill the gap can be verified.

On July 7, 2021, the project announced a contribution of 50 million SDRs, equivalent to approximately 70 million UST, to the yield reserve. On one side, the project’s stability reserve fund decreased; on the other, the yield reserve increased.

A February 2022 proposal said 9.5 million LUNA would be withdrawn and converted over five to ten days into approximately 450 million UST for the reserve. Subsequent community disclosures said the injection began on February 11 and finished on February 17.

Adding those announced amounts gives approximately 520 million UST in explicitly announced major subsidies.

Writing “$520 million” would still be imprecise.

The first amount was stated using the SDR valuation at the time. The second was, in substance, LUNA from the ecosystem treasury converted into new UST through the mint-and-burn relationship.

These were not matching dollar transfers from two external bank accounts.

The 450 million, in particular, cannot be understood as the founder giving away $450 million.

Converting 9.5 million treasury LUNA into UST turned treasury assets into new UST liabilities while sustaining the nearly 20% return on depositors’ statements.

When those UST were eventually redeemed together, the protocol’s promise required them to be absorbed through further LUNA issuance.

The subsidy thus traveled in a circle: treasury LUNA became UST; UST supported depositors’ recorded yield; and that UST ultimately had to be absorbed by issuing LUNA.

It returned to the same thing from which it had started, but in quantities larger by several orders of magnitude.

Who ultimately paid for that 20% therefore has a three-layer answer.

In accounting terms, the target yield was paid first from borrowing interest and collateral staking income, with the yield reserve covering the shortfall.

In funding terms, the project and foundation system replenished major gaps in the reserve. At least those two injections can be verified.

In terms of economic risk, early yield was supported by income-producing borrowers, collateral cash flows, and treasury subsidies. Tail risk ultimately entered the combined balance-sheet structure of UST and LUNA.

Those who withdrew early and converted into hard assets or fiat could lock in their returns.

Those who stayed and exited later bore the depegging and dilution.

That was the arrangement’s actual distributional shape. The point is not that someone stole the money. Part of the 20% had never been earned: it had been advanced. Those who came later would have to bear that advance.

4. Beforehand

The public warnings before the collapse were not retrospective inventions. Three kinds of material deserve to retain their original dates.

By June 2021, the Anchor community was already calculating how long the reserve could last. A June 13 forum discussion put it at approximately 4.3 million UST. Under the post’s assumptions, a minimum yield of 19.5% would consume approximately 23,000 per day, giving about 186 days. If deposits rapidly doubled, the model suggested its life might fall to approximately 23 days.

Ten days later, the forum recorded a decline in the reserve from approximately 4.3 million to 3.6 million.

These were contemporary community models, not audited figures. But the risk had already been clearly identified: deposits were growing faster than the borrowing side that generated income.

On November 25, 2021, a pseudonymous account published a scenario involving a wealthy attacker, estimating a capital requirement of approximately $1 billion and connecting liquidity in UST, Anchor, and Curve with shorting LUNA.

Do Kwon’s direct response three days later remains available. Among other things, he called it possibly the stupidest thread he had read that decade.

The warning closely resembled the eventual incident in its risk channels. It cannot, however, be described as a precise prediction of who launched an attack in May.

The events later observed did include suddenly thinner Curve liquidity, large UST sales, large Anchor withdrawals, and the subsequent explosion in LUNA supply. Existing on-chain research cannot infer from those transactions alone that the wallets shared one commander and followed that scenario in full.

By early 2022, the community’s warnings were more direct. A January 12 post estimated the reserve was declining by slightly more than $1 million a day and would run out in roughly fifty to sixty days at that rate. On March 22, a participant calculated from the previous fifty days that total value locked had risen from approximately $8.54 billion to $16.98 billion, while the reserve’s daily shortfall had reached approximately $4.2177 million. The participant explicitly warned that falling yields could trigger large UST redemptions and a loss of the peg even with reserves.

It would therefore also misrepresent the material to say that the product promised 20% forever from beginning to end and nobody tried to change it.

In March 2022, the community held lengthy discussions about a dynamic interest rate. One side argued that the fixed 19.5% was central to the product’s identity and that lowering it would drive capital away. The other argued that reserves were losing millions a day while borrowing income failed to keep pace, so maintaining the rate would require ever larger subsidies.

The approved proposal did not cut the yield immediately to a market rate. It linked the rate to reserve changes and adjusted it monthly, with a maximum monthly change of approximately 1.5 percentage points.

The forum explicitly recorded its passage on March 24.

The collapse came too quickly for that gradual rate-cutting mechanism to rebalance deposits against actual income.

Only a month and a half separated passage of the proposal from the first large withdrawal. At a maximum adjustment of 1.5 percentage points per month, reducing 19.5% to a level the protocol’s own income could cover would take nearly a year.

There was also a precedent more important than outside criticism, within the system’s own history.

UST had already lost its peg in 2021.

The later U.S. civil trial and criminal guilty plea addressed the same disputed fact: the project had presented that recovery as the algorithm repairing itself, while behind it there was a secret market-support arrangement with a high-frequency trading firm.

In 2024, a jury found the project and Do Kwon liable for securities fraud on this and other grounds.

The claim that the algorithm had recovered from earlier stress without outside assistance thus failed at the level of legal evidence.

This mattered more than any outside warning.

An external warning could be dismissed as bearish speculation, disputed, or ignored. The 2021 recovery was a stress-test report issued by the mechanism itself. Much subsequent confidence rested on that report.

What was later found misleading was the portrayal of that recovery as an algorithmic success without concealed outside support, not the fact that the price had recovered.

5. Nineteen Thousand Times

A day-by-day ledger of the collapse week must first distinguish two numbers: newly minted tokens during a day and circulating supply at a particular moment. Later reporting most frequently confused these two.

May 7. While preparing to move from one Curve pool to a new liquidity structure, the project withdrew approximately 150 million UST in liquidity. A large sale on the scale of approximately 85 million followed, rapidly leaving the pool overweight in UST.

That same day, two large addresses withdrew approximately 375 million UST from Anchor, followed by other large holders. Later research identified this date as the first clear wave of signals of a run.

LUNA’s circulating supply before the collapse was a little over 300 million. A commonly used point-in-time figure is approximately 340 million.

May 8. The foundation began directly deploying stablecoin reserves other than bitcoin. A later commissioned technical audit said it sold 26,281,671 USDT and 23,555,590 USDC on Curve to buy 50,200,071 UST.

At one point in the crisis, that pool was approximately 95% UST and 5% other stablecoins.

The side willing to take UST had become extremely thin.

May 9. The foundation council publicly voted to lend an OTC trading firm $750 million worth of bitcoin to defend the peg, while also lending 750 million UST. The subsequent technical audit said 52,189 bitcoin were actually transferred to the counterparty to purchase UST.

Academic reconstruction showed cumulative net withdrawals from Anchor had reached approximately six billion tokens by this stage.

That was far beyond what the liquidity pool could absorb.

May 10. On-chain reconstruction concluded that the foundation’s available defensive funds had largely been exhausted by this date. UST never subsequently recovered a stable $1 peg.

Approximately 40 million LUNA were minted that day.

May 11. The proposal received support, seeking to increase effective minting capacity to approximately $1.2 billion.

Approximately one billion tokens were minted that day.

Supply growth began jumping by orders of magnitude.

May 12. Validators halted the network at block 7,603,700. The official reason was to prevent governance attacks after LUNA’s extreme inflation.

Halting the chain temporarily stopped native redemption and on-chain transactions themselves. The claim that arbitrage could always restore $1 ceased to hold continuously even at the operational level.

Here validators actively halted a chain. That was not the same operation as changing state through the fork in Essay 6, nor the first halt in this series: Essay 15 already discussed BNB Chain’s pause. The immediate reason here was to prevent the enormous supply of newly minted LUNA from making governance control cheap for an attacker.

Both show the same thing: whether a chain can ultimately keep operating by its rules depends on what the people running it decide to do that day.

Measured as a flow, approximately 200 billion tokens were minted that day. Another point-in-time measure put circulating supply at approximately 176 billion. The difference was principally between flow and stock, and between cutoff times.

May 13. The chain was halted a second time at block 7,607,789, officially to develop a network reconstruction plan. In the surrounding tens of minutes, major exchanges successively delisted related spot trading pairs and perpetual contracts.

Approximately six trillion more tokens were minted that morning, while point-in-time circulating supply had exceeded 6.5 trillion.

From approximately 340 million to 6.5 trillion: an increase of roughly 19,000 times within days.

Pause over that sentence.

This was not an incidental consequence of a falling price.

It was precisely how the protocol settled UST redemptions.

In other words, the 19,000-fold increase was not a side effect. It was the machine working as designed.

For each UST it extinguished, it had to mint enough LUNA to take its place. The cheaper LUNA became, the more it had to mint. In the final days, it was using something almost worthless to settle a liability still denominated at face value.

Changes in reserves also have to be recorded on both sides.

Bitcoin fell from approximately 80,394 to 313. The 26,281,671 USDT and 23,555,590 USDC both fell to zero. BNB and AVAX were largely unused.

Conversely, the UST balance increased from approximately 697,344 to approximately 1.847 billion. LUNA increased from approximately 1.69 million to approximately 223 million.

Those last two lines make it look as though reserves increased.

What they actually mean is that the foundation used harder reserve assets to buy back large quantities of depreciating UST.

More tokens do not mean more economic value.

Those two lines looked attractive in the public disclosures at the time. Reserves had not fallen to zero. Holdings of some assets had even increased.

To understand what they meant, one had to know which tokens increased and which decreased.

This is a recurring interpretive trap in this domain: a table of quantities without the nature of the assets can make a complete failure look like a change in asset allocation.

Tracking both sides therefore yields this result: the foundation and project lost bitcoin, stablecoins, and substantial funds of their own; market participants exiting UST gained those more resilient assets; and the foundation and project gained large quantities of UST that subsequently traded at deep discounts, together with some LUNA.

The audit traced flows across many addresses and exchanges, supporting the claim that the great majority of bitcoin reserves really were transferred for defense of the peg.

It acknowledged, however, that it had not obtained transaction-by-transaction execution records from every market maker.

By December 2025, the bankruptcy administrator had sued that trading firm, alleging that approximately $1.5 billion of the bitcoin transfers lacked written agreements, oversight, and a complete accounting of their use, and seeking at least $4 billion.

Those were allegations in a civil lawsuit, not findings by a court. They show that the commissioned audit did not end the dispute over how the funds were used.

6. Those Who Came Later

There is no single total-loss figure that can be quoted without qualification.

Five are commonly cited: approximately $38 billion, a one-week market-capitalization loss reported by media using one data provider; approximately $40 billion, the scale of losses repeatedly used by the U.S. Department of Justice in charging, plea, and sentencing materials; approximately $45 billion, a summary of market-capitalization changes in some contemporary financial reporting; approximately $50 billion, a valuation-erasure measure used by one academic study; and nearly $60 billion, a combined valuation closer to the peak used by other studies.

They measure different things. What they share is that market capitalization itself is simply a marginal price at a particular moment multiplied by circulating supply.

Large numbers of holders cannot all exit at that price simultaneously.

None of those figures can therefore be described as that many dollars in cash invested and then entirely lost.

The Korean figure particularly needs unpacking.

In mid-May 2022, South Korea’s financial authorities estimated approximately 280,000 domestic holders with approximately 70 billion LUNA.

The easiest mistake is to multiply those 70 billion tokens by the pre-collapse price and call the result Korean retail investors’ losses.

That balance of 70 billion was measured after LUNA had already undergone inflation on the scale of tens of thousands of times.

The figure was so large precisely because the collapse had happened.

It was not the pre-collapse token balance at all.

Multiplying it by a pre-collapse price produces an absurdly large number that never existed at any moment.

Subsequent regulatory material put the residual value of holdings at one point at approximately 33.9 billion won, then equivalent to approximately $26.8 million, or around 0.08% of the country’s virtual-asset market.

That measured post-collapse residual value, not the principal these people had previously invested or lost.

The 280,000 also requires qualification: it came from trading platforms and regulatory aggregation. Public material does not establish that accounts across exchanges were deduplicated into unique natural persons.

What can be checked is approximately 280,000 domestic holders. There is no official figure of equivalent evidentiary standing for their combined losses.

At the beginning of the collapse, the Korean authorities themselves explicitly said it was difficult to establish specific losses under the legal and regulatory framework then in force.

Reliable measurement does show that losses were distributed unevenly between large and small investors.

A study using on-chain and exchange data found that large addresses exited first on May 7. Wealthier participants with more sophisticated trading behavior fled earlier and more decisively on average, and lost a smaller proportion.

Smaller investors exited later.

They were also more likely to buy during the initial price decline.

That sentence needs no adjective.

This also shows why aggregate market-value loss misses distribution. Earlier exiters transferred UST to later buyers or to the foundation’s and project’s defensive purchases. Earlier exit gave a better chance of receiving assets closer to one dollar; later participants often faced deeper discounts and more intense dilution.

The paper made another observation worth setting out separately.

Blockchain transparency allowed other participants to observe large holders’ actions in real time. The authors argued that this accelerated the run.

Pause here.

In the first fifteen episodes, on-chain openness was usually a good thing, or at least neutral. It allowed the stolen coins in Episode 3 to be reconstructed transaction by transaction, the curve in Episode 9 to be recalculated by anyone, and the destinations of funds in Episode 15 to be traced.

Here, it was an accelerator.

Visibility was symmetrical. The ability to get out was not.

The same on-chain data need not become an equally usable opportunity in different hands. Capital, tools, trading access, and response speed can affect whether someone exits while liquidity remains. That is not the same as saying large holders can always sell and small holders can only watch.

Transparency did make part of the ledger public to everyone. Publishing the same data does not mean there was no inside information, no latency difference, or no privileged channel; nor does it guarantee equal ability to understand and use the data.

Yet in a market undergoing a run, the same information means different things to different people.

For some people with tools, access, and adequate liquidity, the information could become an actionable signal. For others, it might be visible but difficult to act on in time. Position size alone cannot explain the difference.

Everyone can see. Not everyone can escape.

Existing material does not provide a distribution of exactly how much these people lost.

The obstacles are specific: one person can have many addresses; exchanges pool many people’s tokens in one wallet; on-chain records do not show fiat acquisition costs or users’ hedges on other platforms.

There is therefore reliable research on who exited earlier and whose proportional losses were smaller, but no reliable full distribution of absolute losses among natural persons.

Material reaching identifiable people is scarce and deserves to be recorded as it stands.

In contemporary Korean reporting, a 28-year-old employee in their second year of work said losses, including realized losses, were approximately 40 million won, money originally saved for a jeonse housing deposit. Another interviewee, aged thirty, said they had borrowed approximately 50 million won to invest in cryptoassets and lost about half.

These are personal accounts recorded by journalists, not bank statements or court-assessed losses.

They establish that losses reached housing savings and personal debt. They cannot be used to estimate an average loss.

They also show something an aggregate misses: what the money was for before the collapse differs from what remained afterward. Savings intended for a housing deposit could shrink to a balance no longer sufficient for that purpose. The need for housing remained.

That appears in no market-capitalization loss statistic.

This is also the boundary of the real-world evidence.

There is no official causal measurement, comparable in standing to the holder count, of the collapse’s quantifiable effects on Korean employment, household consumption, rents, house prices, or regional economies.

What can be confirmed concerns particular households’ savings, loans, and claims that later entered bankruptcy proceedings.

Measurement of off-chain macroeconomic effects is conspicuously thinner than research on on-chain prices and addresses.

That absence should be preserved, not filled with unverifiable generalizations such as “countless families went bankrupt.”

7. Three Numbers

Three stages of the U.S. civil case must remain distinct.

When the case was filed in February 2023, the claims were allegations. At summary judgment in December 2023, the court found the project and Do Kwon liable for unregistered offers and sales of the relevant securities. In April 2024, after a nine-day trial, a jury unanimously found both civilly liable for securities fraud.

The June 2024 final judgment comprised approximately $3.587 billion in disgorgement, approximately $467 million in prejudgment interest, and $420 million in civil penalties, totaling approximately $4.474 billion.

The media shorthand “a $4.5 billion fine” is therefore inaccurate. It was a combination of disgorgement, interest, and civil penalties.

More important is who receives the money.

The final judgment required Do Kwon to transfer assets worth at least approximately $204.3 million to the bankruptcy estate. The SEC agreed that it would take no distribution on its $4.474 billion claim until victim investors and other creditors had been paid in full through the bankruptcy process.

Saying that the project paid the SEC $4.47 billion in cash is therefore wrong.

On the criminal side, the status changed in 2025.

Do Kwon was arrested in Montenegro in March 2023 while using forged travel documents and arrived in the United States on December 31, 2024. On August 12, 2025, he pleaded guilty to one count of conspiracy to commit commodities fraud, securities fraud, and wire fraud, and one count of wire fraud, and agreed to forfeit more than $19 million in unlawful proceeds.

On December 11, 2025, a federal judge sentenced him to fifteen years in prison.

A description of roughly nine charges belongs to the indictment stage. His established criminal liability should be described by the two counts to which he actually pleaded guilty and the sentence imposed by the court.

On the Korean side, the cases still cannot be described as convictions.

In April 2023, prosecutors charged the cofounder and others with offenses including violations of the Capital Markets Act, fraud, breach of trust, and embezzlement. They alleged approximately 462.9 billion won in improper gains, while approximately 247 billion won in assets were subject to preservation measures at the time. The defendants denied the allegations.

Reporting as of February 2026 said the principal criminal cases begun in 2023 were still at first-instance trial.

In 2025, in proceedings concerning asset preservation, South Korea’s Supreme Court did not accept the argument that LUNA was a financial investment product under the Capital Markets Act.

That was not an acquittal in the principal case, nor a declaration that all virtual assets are not securities. It was a classification ruling in a specific preservation proceeding. It did make the prosecution’s route to establishing some liability under the Capital Markets Act more difficult.

The same event thus has two legal statuses in two countries: on one side, a civil liability verdict, criminal guilty pleas, and a fifteen-year sentence; on the other, core cases still at first instance, with even the asset’s classification still disputed.

Bankruptcy, too, does not mean compensation has been completed.

The project filed for reorganization in January 2024. The court approved a liquidation plan that September, after which a trust was established to address claims using remaining assets, transferred assets, and recoveries from subsequent litigation.

The publicly stated estimate of assets available for repayment was approximately $184.5 million to $442.2 million.

That was a projected asset-realization range under the plan, not a guarantee of what any individual investor would recover.

The filing deadline for crypto-loss claims was later extended to May 16, 2025. Administrator data showed approximately 16,640 claims submitted.

That is a count of submitted claims, not 16,640 approved unique natural persons, nor a count of people already paid.

Three numbers must therefore remain separate.

The $4.474 billion is a legal obligation. What the estate can actually realize is the asset side. The 16,640 is the filing side.

They are not the same number.

Mixing those three numbers has a concrete consequence: someone who lost money may read $4.474 billion as a pool waiting to be distributed. The amount actually available depends on estimated asset realization of roughly $180 million to $440 million, allowed claims, priorities, and the distribution plan. More than sixteen thousand filings are not a divisor for equal payments.

The two scales differ by more than an order of magnitude.

8. Still True on Paper

On why the collapse happened, the available material supports two sides that answer different questions.

On the mechanism side, it establishes that the protocol’s mathematics made net UST redemptions issue LUNA. The further LUNA fell, the more LUNA had to be issued for the same quantity of UST. Meanwhile, Anchor concentrated substantial UST in a deposit product and used reserves to subsidize a yield far above actual protocol income.

The empirical study described the collapse as a run: a few large addresses exited first on May 7, followed by other large holders. Its authors argued that the evidence did not support centrally coordinated manipulation by a third party as the principal explanation.

This still does not prove that collapse had to occur on May 7, 2022.

A reflexive mechanism can persist for a long time if demand grows, market capitalization and liquidity remain sufficient, reserves can intervene, and holders do not redeem simultaneously.

What the material actually supports is conditional instability: when net exits from UST exceed what the LUNA market and external reserves can absorb, and LUNA’s price falls with redemption, the mint-and-burn mechanism converts redemptions into ever faster supply expansion, producing a self-reinforcing decline.

That is narrower than “algorithmic stablecoins must mathematically go to zero,” and closer to the original mechanism.

What can be established on the attack side is also limited.

On-chain records did show highly concentrated, time-sensitive large transactions. One analytics firm divided the initial depegging into stages: a few large trades first broke the peg; the project and its supporters then attempted restoration with billions in buying; after defensive capital was consumed, the run spread.

Another firm’s on-chain research identified a small number of well-capitalized wallets that withdrew UST from Anchor, bridged it, and exchanged it on trading venues. Its public conclusion was not that it had found a hacker, but that a few wallets, possibly belonging to fewer entities, played an important role in the liquidity imbalance.

It explicitly rejected attributing the collapse to a single hostile attacker.

On-chain material therefore supports this: the initial liquidity shock was highly concentrated, and a few large holders exited rapidly during a weak liquidity window, materially affecting the timing of depegging.

It does not establish that the addresses belonged to one organization that had agreed in advance to mount a coordinated attack.

That latter claim requires evidence of identity, communications, trading intent, and a common plan, not merely adjacent on-chain timestamps.

This distinction appeared in Episode 15: profits earned by an on-chain address are verifiable, but who controlled it and whether the participants acted together belong to another evidentiary layer.

Here the distinction matters more, because it determines not just attribution but an entire set of conclusions about prevention. If attackers brought the system down, the target of prevention is attack. If it collapsed from within, the target is design.

By 2026, the evidentiary position had changed again. The bankruptcy administrator sued two trading firms, alleging that they had exited before the collapse through nonpublic information channels and profited from opposing positions. The defendants denied the allegations.

These materials mean it is now also too categorical to say that no professional institution traded early using information advantages.

They remain, however, the administrator’s claims in civil litigation.

A firm possibly learning of a liquidity move in advance and exiting ahead of others is a different proposition from that firm designing and launching a coordinated attack capable of destroying the whole system.

On whether regulators could have recognized the risk beforehand, the material supports two propositions that can both be true.

First, the economic risks were quite public. The mint-and-burn formula, the 19.5% yield, reserve figures, subsidies, and the 2021 depegging were not hidden code. The community was even calculating reserve depletion in real time. A regulator did not have to know when a large holder would sell to recognize the implications of combining heavily subsidized short-term liabilities, an endogenous volatile asset absorbing redemptions, and concentrated liquidity exits.

Recognizing risk does not establish that clear, enforceable legal tools then existed to require adequate external reserves for this product, constrain its promised yield, or stop it. That depends on jurisdiction, legal classification, and regulatory powers—not a comparison with a 100% dollar-reserve requirement that does not generally apply to banks either.

Subsequent Korean litigation showed that even whether LUNA qualified as a financial investment product under domestic capital-market law remained subject to prolonged dispute.

Return now to the first section.

The two routes in Essay 8 backed their stated redemption with external assets. Here the native conversion mechanism relied on the system’s own LUNA. The foundation’s separate deployment of external assets to defend the peg did not erase that distinction.

That produced the cleanest failure in the series.

While conversion was available, the protocol calculated the LUNA corresponding to UST at oracle prices, subject to dynamic spreads and other parameters. Validators later halted block production, and parameters changed. This was not uninterrupted one-dollar redemption.

“Worth $1” was calculated using the oracle’s quoted price.

From launch to collapse, the nominal calculation did not guarantee that holders would recover one dollar every time, much less sell the LUNA received for the same amount in the market.

The accounting reference point and the value actually realizable were not the same statement.

Once 340 million had become 6.5 trillion, however, that sentence no longer meant anything.

The remainder lay within the sentence.

Nor did the gap lie wholly outside the protocol. Spreads, capacity, oracles, and halts were all relevant conditions. Computing a conversion did not mean the system delivered the dollar outcome holders expected.

It lay between “worth $1” and “can be exchanged for $1.”

In March, the two could often appear close. During the collapse, nominal value and realizable market proceeds moved farther apart. Quotes did not all disappear; amid volatility, deteriorating liquidity, and restricted access, that nominal dollar became increasingly difficult to realize.

During those days, the chain both continued producing blocks and was halted by validators. Block production itself did not restore market support.

Executed conversions and newly minted LUNA left traceable ledger records. Being able to inspect them transaction by transaction did not mean that each preserved the dollar holders wanted.

It calculated quantities.

It did not calculate whether those quantities were still worth anything.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
