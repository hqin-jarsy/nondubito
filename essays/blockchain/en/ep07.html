The Chisel–Construct Cycle · Blockchain 07 / 21

ICO

ERC-20 solved how tokens interoperate, but did not establish why issuers should be trusted. The ICO boom mistook an interface for an institution.

1. An Interface

ERC-20 was proposed in November 2015 by Fabian Vogelsteller, with Vitalik Buterin as co-author.

Its stated aim was narrow: to establish a standard interface for fungible tokens in smart contracts, allowing wallets, exchanges, and other contracts to interact with different tokens through the same functions.

The standard centers on six functions and two events: total supply, balance, transfer, transfer on someone else's behalf, approval, and allowance queries; plus transfer and approval events. Name, symbol, and decimal places are optional supplementary information.

It therefore standardizes three things: how to check balances, move funds, and authorize a third party to move them for you.

That is why a wallet or exchange need not design a new protocol for every new token. If a contract follows the interface, the same calls can read balances, make transfers, and manage approvals.

What it does not prescribe deserves to be read out item by item.

Why the token has value. Whether supply should be fixed. Who owns the initial tokens. Whether later minting or burning is allowed. Sale price, caps, minimums, and private-sale arrangements. Who should custody the proceeds. Whether team tokens are locked. Whether the issuer has a board or owes holders fiduciary duties. Whether investors have redemption, liquidation-preference, or information rights. Identity checks. Exchange listings. Market making. Whether the project's code is actually finished.

And whether issuance complies with securities law.

Every item on that list is part of deciding whether to entrust money to someone.

The standard solves a different problem: whether the token can be recognized.

A standard solved recognizability while leaving trustworthiness entirely outside.

That whole list is the remainder in this history.

It was not overlooked. A clearly bounded standard expressly left it outside its jurisdiction. Its authors did nothing wrong: their problem was the interface, and they solved it well.

The problem came later: projects, contracts, and institutions had to take responsibility for the list outside the interface, and did so unevenly. Standardizing the interface did not standardize the protection supplied by those arrangements.

The technical cost of issuing a token must also be divided into three layers.

The first is writing the bare token itself. Before 2017, ready-made tools could already create compliant tokens, and reusable contract implementations existed. The marginal engineering work at this layer was therefore small.

The second is on-chain deployment: a contract-creation transaction costing gas consumed multiplied by gas price. There is no dollar price fixed across projects and dates. A later public example of ordinary deployment consumed approximately 1,311,213 gas, equivalent to about 0.0262 ETH at 20 gwei or 0.0656 ETH at 50 gwei. This is only an illustrative order-of-magnitude calculation, not a retrospective estimate of the average cost in 2017.

The third is a complete issuance: crowdfunding contracts, refunds, caps, whitelists and identity checks, multisignature treasuries, vesting, security audits, websites, and legal arrangements. All fall outside the standard, and can cost orders of magnitude more money and time than a bare token.

The narrower formulation is therefore this:

ERC-20 turned creating a token recognizable and transferable by existing Ethereum tools from protocol research and development into deployment of a standard contract.

It did not standardize the creation of a safe, lawful financing project capable of delivering on its promises.

In 2017, a practical factor erased the distance between those two things: both were called issuing a token. The same term meant deploying a thirty-line contract at one end and raising tens of millions of dollars from thousands of strangers at the other.

The large stretch between them falls outside ERC-20 and needs separate arrangements in each project. What a team chooses to provide is one question; what contracts and existing law require is another. Neither disappears simply because the standard does not prescribe it.

2. Three Sets of Numbers

The scale of this wave has at least three sets of figures using different measurement bases, with samples and periods that do not fully coincide.

By year, a media outlet's own tracker listed 43 projects and $256 million in 2016, and 343 projects and more than $5.4 billion in 2017. A data provider listed 913 attempts and 435 successes in 2017, the successful projects raising $5.6 billion. Another database put 2017 at approximately $4.9 billion, explicitly warning that databases differed in their treatment of private sales and recording dates. An academic study's final sample totaled about $5 billion across 306 projects from March 2016 to March 2018, retaining only completed sales raising more than $1 million.

That last figure especially cannot be treated as an industry total.

The same data provider put 2016 at approximately $240 million, against the media outlet's $256 million. Even before the 2017 explosion, with a smaller denominator, that $16 million gap showed that inclusion rules, private-sale treatment, and dollar-conversion dates could each materially change the total.

Quarterly data expose definitional conflicts more clearly, including one contradiction worth preserving.

Reporting on the same media outlet's quarterly material simultaneously gave approximately $36 million for the first quarter, $797 million for the second, and $1.13 billion for the first half.

The first two add up to only $833 million.

This probably reflects tracker updates, cutoff dates, or changed inclusion rules. Without the underlying transaction-level data, however, one should not choose an explanation and erase the source's contradiction on its behalf.

Preserving a mismatch is closer to the facts than supplying a tidy number.

Likewise, another source's 297 sales ending in the fourth quarter cannot be directly compared with the media outlet's 343 funded projects for the whole year. One counts sales ending during that quarter; the other uses a different tracker's annual project definition.

That explains why counts of 2017 ICOs range from over 300 to over 900.

913 were attempts; only 435 succeeded.

The 478 between them are among the least discussed participants in this history. They published white papers and opened sales, but failed to reach minimum targets or canceled midway.

They too spent money on websites, documents, and advisers, yet those costs entered none of the tables. They appear neither in success statistics nor in scam statistics.

Fundraising denominators usually count only successful projects. Every proportion calculated by funds raised therefore omits those 478 from its denominator.

This research found no authoritative aggregate reconstruction of currencies and quantities actually received. The reasons are concrete: private-sale addresses were often undisclosed, while non-Ethereum receipts such as dollars and bitcoin do not appear in Ethereum transaction records at all.

Researchers consequently inferred investors from token allocations rather than claiming to reconstruct all inflows.

One statement must therefore be exact: approximately 91% of tokens in the 2017 sample ran on existing blockchains, usually Ethereum. That is a share of issuance technology stacks. It cannot become “91% of funds raised were ETH,” which is a currency share and a different quantity.

Several large sales make the currency issue clear.

Block.one's EOS sale ran for nearly a year, from June 26, 2017, to June 1, 2018. It publicly sold 900 million tokens for ETH and reserved another 100 million for founders, at an average sale price of about $4.40 per token. Multiplying 900 million by $4.40 gives roughly $3.96 billion, but the SEC order did not declare that arithmetic an exact dollar principal received. It said the ETH proceeds were then worth billions of dollars.

Filecoin's public phase ran for about four weeks, August 10 to September 7, 2017, accepting dollars, ETH, BTC, and ZEC. Valuing each investment when made, the project ultimately calculated $205.8 million, including a previous $52 million adviser round. The same assets had been worth approximately $253 million on September 2 and about $233 million at the close; a later data provider listed approximately $257 million.

Four numbers, the same assets.

Tezos supplies the table's firmest row. Its foundation published native quantities: 65,681 BTC plus 361,122 ETH, raised over 2,000 Bitcoin blocks, roughly 13 days. Contemporary media gave about $232 million, but that number changes with the pricing date and should not replace the coin quantities.

Bancor received approximately 396,720 ETH, worth about $152–153 million at the time.

It took less than three hours.

Three hours and nearly a year. Two sales beginning in the same year, using the same standard, issuing fungible tokens of the same general kind.

At the interface level specified by ERC-20, both could expose the same six functions and two events. That did not make their complete issuance contracts or financing arrangements identical.

Reading this standard interface alone cannot tell us whether a sale took three hours or a year, who the buyers were, or whether a product existed. A project can separately encode timing, whitelists, and lockups. Reading a balance or completing a transfer does not verify those additional arrangements.

The table also shows that the largest sale depends on the definition. Allow sales starting in 2017 but ending in 2018, and EOS dwarfs the others. Require completion within 2017, and other names stand out.

3. Forty-Seven and Thirty-Nine

For allocation structures, the manually reconstructed study is more useful than treating one famous white paper as typical.

Its final sample comprised 306 completed sales raising more than $1 million, from spring 2016 to spring 2018.

This was therefore a sample of larger, more successful projects. Many small projects and those whose materials had disappeared were excluded from the outset.

Within it, 68% had held private sales. Public sales offered an average of 47% of total supply; private investors already held an average of 11% of future supply at public-sale time; founders held 39%; miners just 2%.

White-paper labels for foundations, advisers, and ecosystem reserves varied too widely to produce a reliable market-wide average.

The neat claim that a typical ICO allocated 70% to the public, 20% to the team, and 10% to advisers therefore lacks sufficient evidentiary support.

The supported version is that successful projects put only about half their supply directly into public sales on average, founders and private investors already held large initial shares, and early and public investors often paid different prices.

That sentence is the foundation for what follows. If purchase prices differ, the same token at the same price on the same day means entirely different things to different people: an unrealized loss for one, twice the investment in profit for another.

Both see the same number on the same screen.

Specific cases are more reliable than industry averages. EOS allocated 900 million versus 100 million: 90% publicly distributed and 10% reserved for founders. Monetha's table allocated 15% to the team, 13% to a loyalty program, 12% to bounties, advisers, and issuance costs, and 10% to future company financing. Outside Status's public sale, 20% went to core developers and the team, vesting over 24 months with a six-month cliff; 29% formed future reserves locked for at least a year.

Then there were discounts, no marginal phenomenon.

In the same sample, private investors received an average discount of approximately 34% from the listed price. The earliest or largest public-sale investors received around 17% on average.

Lockups moved in the opposite direction.

Only 14% of projects imposed private-token lockups, averaging about 0.53 years where present. By contrast, approximately 58–59% imposed vesting on at least some issuer and founder tokens, with a weighted average of about 1.1 years.

Together, discounts and lockups form a concrete chain.

Private investors obtained discounted tokens first, usually without lockups.

Public-sale investors supplied more capital at higher marginal prices.

After exchange listing, private investors had room to cash out whenever the secondary-market price exceeded their discounted cost.

This is not merely an inference. The study observed on-chain that large private investors sold earlier when discounts were higher.

The protection figures also deserve to be set out.

Only 18% of projects gave investors any governance rights, often limited or nonbinding votes.

Only 4% released raised funds in milestone-based tranches.

Only 4% placed funds under an independent custodian's control.

Only 51% had a product or prototype at the time of sale.

Nearly half, in other words, had nothing to show people when they received the money.

Together, these four figures can be compared with the second essay’s three conditions. Calculability, choice, and exit at the mining-pool layer help assess how risk is allocated; their presence alone does not guarantee fairness.

Choice existed: buyers could decline to participate. Exit still depended on lockups, listing, and liquidity. First consider calculability: under an arrangement with no milestone releases, independent custody, or governance rights, and no product yet, buyers had very little they could actually verify and calculate.

Tracing both ends is straightforward. Project teams usually gained control of funds before products matured. Buyers paid more, usually without the liquidation preferences, board seats, follow-on financing protections, or residual cash-flow rights conventional equity investors often receive.

4. Twenty-Five

The allocation and lockup proportions in the previous section were written in white papers.

Whether they were written into code is another question.

A legal study comparing white-paper promises with actual contract code item by item produced one of this history's firmest findings.

Of 36 projects promising lockup restrictions in its sample, 25 had no identifiable implementation hard-coding the restriction into a smart contract.

Likewise, corresponding code restrictions could not be observed for more than 20% of supply-limit promises and about 35% of burning promises.

That does not mean all 2017 lockups were paper promises. Cases differed enormously; these four almost form a classification table.

Monetha was the most concrete tier: the team's 15%, loyalty program's 13%, and future financing's 10% were all expressly set to be locked automatically by smart contract for 12 months.

BAT was automatic on-chain too, but in a different place: vesting sat in a separate second contract rather than the main token contract, and the tokens had been transferred there before the sale.

Storj was a hybrid: it also had a separate vault contract, but researchers found that the team manually transferred tokens into it, rather than the sale contract doing so automatically, and unlike BAT this was not completed before the sale.

For Electroneum, such implementation was impossible: its entire sale took place off-chain before its own network launched, so its sale contract could not automatically enforce those promises at the time.

Four projects, four different degrees of encoding.

Yet buyers could distinguish them by only one visible thing: the wording in the white paper. Across all four, the wording was almost identical.

To tell them apart required reading the contracts. Most 2017 buyers did not, and could not.

On paper, they looked like the same sentence.

The remainder appears here for the second time. First came matters the standard expressly did not govern; now come matters explicitly promised but never encoded.

Their positions differ, but their shape is the same: both lie outside machine execution, while everyone acts as though they have already been executed.

Pause here.

This domain's greatest selling point was that code executes.

The previous essay described one failure of that promise: code executed, but the result was not what participants wanted.

Here is its opposite failure: what people wanted never entered the code, while everyone assumed it had.

The same promise, two failures: one executed too thoroughly; the other was never executed at all.

Only a year separated them, and both point to the same gap. “Code executes” means code will execute once written. It never said that what people promise will necessarily be written into code.

No one took responsibility for the intermediate step.

One of the previous essay's four rule systems is missing here. There, at least four procedures remained: EVM execution, participants' rejection, nodes' voting, and the market's choice of sides. Here even the first was unfinished: the promises never reached the place where they would execute.

The second essay's three conditions must also be recalculated.

Calculability therefore needs a discount. White-paper discounts, allocations, and lockups appeared calculable, but many lacked automatic enforcement in code. Any contractual or other legal constraints still had to be examined; a number on paper could not simply be treated as assets already locked.

Choice was real. One could decline to buy.

Exit was conditional. Listed tokens could be sold, provided someone still wanted to buy.

In markets where some early holders obtained tokens at lower cost, that condition was more fragile than it looked.

Exit always exists on paper. Its practical condition is that others have not exited first.

5. Fifty Thousand to Three Million

Listing fees were among this history's most important and least transparent costs.

An industry interview in March 2018 reported that some exchanges demanded $50,000–$1 million for listing. Sources included named and anonymous participants, who would not identify which exchanges charged the fees.

Another widely cited contemporaneous estimate gave a range of $1–3 million.

Both ranges should be preserved, rather than choosing one as the listing fee in 2017.

The first came from a reporter's interviews with several industry participants, the second from another industry estimate. Both mainly described quotations or demanded commercial terms, not exchange-by-exchange audited payment records.

The nature of this layer matters. Charging a listing fee is not automatically unlawful, but neither does it place issuance and trading outside existing law. What is visible here is a commercial condition between institutions, without a uniform public fee schedule that can be checked item by item. One party controls a gateway through which the other's token can reach large numbers of buyers.

The price of that gateway thus became the only cost in the chain that was both crucial and entirely absent from any record.

The consequences were tangible. Raising funds did not ensure access to a liquid trading venue; whether buyers could sell, and at what price, remained unsettled. Listing's commercial terms were usually not fully visible on-chain or in the white paper, while applicable legal duties needed separate analysis. This step could profoundly affect whether earlier promises became usable.

If no liquid exchange would list a project that had raised tens of millions of dollars, its tokens remained complete, verifiable, and immutable on-chain, while no one could exchange them for anything else.

This was also the link most resembling the old world: a private gatekeeper charged an undisclosed sum for access, something never mentioned in white papers proclaiming the removal of middlemen.

This research found no primary material sufficient to construct an official, public, uniform fee schedule for major 2017 exchanges. Anonymous quotations cannot establish that a particular exchange definitely charged a particular project a particular amount.

Liquidity arrangements were not always neutral market developments either.

Filecoin's investor FAQ expressly discussed reserving tokens to trade its own tokens on exchanges, supply liquidity, stabilize prices, and correct miner incentives. Contemporary Bancor material also reported that some raised ETH was earmarked for price stabilization.

Trace both ends of every link, and the economic shape becomes clear.

Exchanges received listing or trading revenue and commercial terms.

Project treasuries could use raised assets to support their own tokens' markets.

Private investors obtained discounted tokens and earlier exit windows.

Public-sale buyers acquired a tradable token, generally without conventional equity investors' control rights or downside protections.

On-chain reconstruction supplied the final link: an average of 49.3% of investors sold at least some tokens within 90 days of the sale ending; net sales equaled roughly 41.8% of allocated tokens.

“Investors” here still uses addresses as proxies for individuals.

Read this alongside the previous sequence: private-sale investors acquired discounted tokens, public sales supplied most funding, and within 90 days of the sale ending, on average almost half the investor addresses in the sample had sold some tokens.

Who sold to whom is visible on-chain. The price at which the seller originally bought is not.

That price was not an on-chain transaction, but a line in an off-chain agreement.

6. Six Legal Documents

The US regulatory path cannot be summarized as the SEC declaring all ICOs illegal in 2017.

It proceeded through documents of fundamentally different kinds.

The July 25, 2017, DAO investigative report arose under Exchange Act Section 21(a), was not a court judgment, and led to no SEC enforcement action in that matter. It classified those tokens as securities under that factual structure.

Munchee on December 11, 2017, was a settled administrative cease-and-desist order, not a court judgment. After SEC intervention, the company stopped the sale and returned funds. It consented without admitting or denying the findings; the SEC imposed no additional penalty.

Munchee is often reduced to “the SEC said utility tokens are securities too.” More accurately, a claim of utility is no safe harbor: economic reality at sale time matters. The SEC emphasized that the product had not yet developed a usable token economy, the issuer marketed to digital-asset investors, discussed appreciation as the ecosystem grew, and sought secondary-market trading.

Block.one on September 30, 2019, was also a settled administrative order. It found the year-long sale to be an unregistered offering and required a $24 million civil penalty, without admission or denial by the company.

It was neither a fraud judgment nor a court ruling.

Telegram on March 24, 2020, was a federal district court preliminary injunction, not a final liability judgment after trial. The question was whether the SEC had shown a substantial likelihood of success on the merits.

Its analysis deserves attention: the court held that the 2018 private purchase agreements and future token resales could not be separated into isolated transactions. Under the overall economic reality, fundraising, network construction, delivery of discounted tokens, and their distribution by initial buyers to the public formed a larger distribution arrangement.

The case ended in June 2020 with a settlement: return of more than $1.2 billion and an $18.5 million penalty.

Those were settlement terms, not a $1.2 billion damages award after trial.

Kik on September 30, 2020, was a merits liability decision. The court granted the SEC's summary-judgment motion in full, finding an unregistered securities offering. Final judgment followed: a permanent injunction against further violations of the relevant provisions, advance SEC notice for certain digital-asset offerings over three years, and a $5 million penalty.

Ripple on July 13, 2023, produced the sequence's most interesting result.

The same token and issuer received different legal conclusions in different sales settings.

Institutional sales were held to be unregistered offers and sales of investment contracts. Programmatic sales were not, because they were blind bid/ask transactions: buyers did not know whether their money went to Ripple or another holder, so the court did not find in that setting that buyers reasonably expected profits from Ripple's efforts.

Pause on that.

In this ruling, whether buyers knew whom they were buying from was one factor in distinguishing transaction settings. Legal characterization still depends on the particular facts and economic relationship; it cannot be reduced to a single test for every token.

This matters across the domain. Securities law concerns the shape of a relationship, not an object's physical attributes. The same token on opposite sides of a signed agreement and in an anonymous matching queue is not the same thing.

Six cases, documents of several different kinds: an investigative report, settled administrative orders, a preliminary injunction, a settlement, summary judgment, and final judgment.

Calling all of them “SEC courts ruling ICOs illegal” erases their procedural character.

Other jurisdictions did not use a single model either.

Switzerland's regulator in September 2017 neither gave a green light nor imposed a blanket ban. It expressly said there were no ICO-specific rules and existing financial-market laws were technology-neutral. It later classified tokens by economic function as payment, utility, or asset tokens. A pure utility token escaped securities treatment only if it already provided its digital access function at issuance and did not also serve an investment function economically.

Singapore's official explanation was that offers of digital tokens constituting regulated capital-market products were subject to the relevant securities rules. That was not a blanket ban on all ICOs.

China's seven-agency announcement of September 4, 2017, was markedly stronger: it characterized token fundraising as unauthorized illegal public financing, ordered all such fundraising to stop immediately and completed projects to arrange refunds, and prohibited platforms from exchanging fiat and tokens, acting as central counterparties, or providing pricing and information-intermediation services.

The announcement also used the term “suspected” in relation to unlawful token issuance, illegal securities issuance, illegal fundraising, financial fraud, pyramid schemes, and other offenses.

“Suspected” must be retained.

It marks exactly what every preceding section has marked: where an institution's judgment, made on a particular day from particular evidence, sits between suspicion and determination.

7. Mean and Median

The key to returns is not the average.

First clarify the study's sample: all 306 completed projects had to raise more than $1 million; secondary-market prices were available for 276; 250 ultimately entered the 270-day return calculation, excluding thin-trading observations with daily volume below $1,000.

In this sample clearly biased toward large projects that successfully raised funds, the results were as follows.

The equally weighted mean total return over 270 days was 118.7%; weighting by public-sale proceeds gave 105.7%.

Subtracting contemporaneous ETH returns left equally weighted excess returns of just 0.7%, or 38.4% when weighted by proceeds.

Median absolute returns and median ETH-adjusted returns were both negative.

That sentence contains the whole section.

A few big winners pulled the arithmetic mean sharply upward.

This is not unique to the domain. Early-stage venture returns have that shape too. The difference is that a few hundred institutions bear the venture distribution, whereas this time thousands of addresses bore it, most without knowing the distribution they had entered.

High average 2017 ICO returns and a poor experience for the typical investor can therefore both be true. It depends on mean versus median, dollars versus ETH as opportunity cost, and whether failed, small, and never-listed projects enter the denominator.

Earlier studies gave much higher figures. One sample of 570 projects reported 362.21% raw returns and 92.08% bitcoin-adjusted returns over 180 days; another sample of 293 found 430.9% raw and 242.5% adjusted returns over the same window.

The later study explicitly described its own results as more conservative and suggested that its sample of large successful projects might be less affected by market microstructure or price manipulation.

There is therefore no method-free answer to the return earned by ICO investors.

“Investor” itself also needs qualification.

Of its 306 projects, the study found 264 running on Ethereum and identified token contracts for 247. Only 98 ultimately had enough pricing and initial-allocation information for the granular investor sample. Those 98 raised more than $2.3 billion.

An address is not a person. One person may control several addresses, and a custodian or exchange address may represent many people. Researchers expressly identified treating an address as an investor as a key identification assumption.

An average of approximately 4,700 contributors per project is therefore a valuable participation indicator, not a count of 4,700 identifiable individuals.

Survival likewise has several nonequivalent proxies.

A website remains online. Social accounts still update. The token still trades. The code repository has recent commits. The mainnet still produces blocks. The company exists. A usable product has been delivered.

Those seven things are not equivalent.

The study found that only 51% of its successfully funded sample had a product or prototype at sale time; 12 tokens in the 250-project return sample were delisted during observation.

The first measures product maturity, the second market-trading status. Neither alone equals a project death rate.

Another study measured subsequent success through repositories, trading liquidity, and company-level operating outcomes. One finding was that a longer interval since the last code commit correlated with weaker liquidity.

That is a staleness indicator, not an industry half-life.

Together, this section makes one point: returns, survival, and participation can all look precise to a decimal place, while each rests on a denominator and definition easily dropped in retelling.

Those denominators are not hidden. They are clearly stated in each paper's methods, and the authors themselves are often the most cautious. Distortion happens through citation: a number accompanied by four lines of qualifications becomes, after three retellings, just the number.

To date, no study has followed the same projects' repositories monthly for years to estimate a unified decay curve. A single number for the percentage decline in ICO code activity after one year is therefore not presently warranted.

8. The Thinnest Side

The most widely circulated figure is that eight in ten ICOs were scams.

Its direct source was neither a court, the SEC, nor a peer-reviewed fraud census. It came from a 2018 Satis Group industry study.

By project count, that report classified roughly 78% as identified scams, 4% as failed, 3% as dead, and 15% as reaching trading.

Dollar weighting produced an entirely different result.

The report itself estimated that approximately $1.3 billion, or 11% of funds raised, entered its identified-scam category; about $1.7 billion, or 14%, went to failures; approximately $624 million, or 5%, to dead projects; and more than $8 billion, about 70%, to projects subsequently traded on exchanges.

It also noted that much of the money in the scam category was concentrated in a few exceptionally large projects.

More importantly, “identified scam” was the firm's own classification, not a judicial fraud conviction. Its method included projects deemed not to have fulfilled or intended to fulfill development duties, or identified as scams in community material. Courts had not established fraudulent intent case by case.

The claim therefore needs at least four qualifications.

“Eight in ten ICOs were frauds” must become: one firm's classification placed approximately 78% of projects in an identified-scam category, not that courts convicted 78% of fraud.

“Eight in ten dollars were stolen” does not follow. The dollar-weighted share was about 11%.

“Eight in ten investors ultimately lost their principal” does not follow either. Project counts, proceeds, investor counts, and realized losses are four different denominators.

Nor was this an academically established fraud rate.

It remains important contemporaneous evidence of project-quality problems, but cannot be rewritten as a judicially established industry fraud rate.

Now for the real-world section.

Only three off-chain facts can be measured relatively firmly.

The first is the range of listing fees, although the quotations themselves are opaque.

The second is that China's 2017 policy caused large-scale closures and refunds among domestic token issuers and trading platforms. In 2018, regulators said the identified platforms had largely exited without risk. That was the regulator's account of its own enforcement results; public material supplied no corresponding credible causal estimate of net employment, household wealth, or social welfare.

The third is that entry thresholds could indeed be low: about 4,700 contributors per project on average in the sample, with relatively small contributions by the median participant.

But wallets are not people. Famous sales such as Filecoin also restricted participation to accredited investors.

Technical global accessibility cannot therefore be unconditionally rewritten as fully democratized financing.

This research found no verifiable aggregate figure for individual investors' actual net losses.

That is not simply missing data. The object of measurement presents four difficulties.

First, addresses dominate on-chain observation and cannot reliably be consolidated into individuals.

Second, investors may have paid in ETH or BTC, so dollar losses require an opportunity-cost choice. If a token falls from 1 ETH to 0.7 ETH while ETH doubles against the dollar, dollar and ETH calculations yield opposite assessments.

Third, gross proceeds flowing into scam projects are not individuals' realized losses. Refunds, litigation recoveries, forks, and secondary-market sales alter the net amount. Telegram's obligation to return more than $1.2 billion is an obvious counterexample: capital due to be returned should not first be counted as permanent investor loss.

Fourth, a token's 90% fall from issuance price is a mark-to-market loss, not proof that every participant lost 90%. Private buyers averaged a discount of about 34%, and many investors had already sold.

Taken together, this reveals the most important evidentiary boundary of the history.

Funds raised, token prices, and on-chain wallets are the easiest aspects of the wave to quantify.

Whether projects delivered products, individuals' ultimate net losses, net jobs created, and real economic costs reduced lack long-term data of comparable quality.

Then something happens.

The strongest conclusions about the wave often cluster precisely where the evidence is thinnest.

It was a scam. It democratized financing. It ruined a generation of retail investors. It proved a new form of capital formation.

Each requires the second kind of data, which is exactly the kind least available.

This is the remainder's final shape in this history, different from its previous two appearances.

Previously, something was left outside machine jurisdiction. This time it is left outside measurement. Not because no one wanted to measure it, but because the system's design removed the necessary conditions: no identities, no roster, no registry to ask.

Return to the first section's interface.

ERC-20 specifies six functions and two events. Compatible wallets, exchanges, and contracts can reuse the same interface to read balances, process transfers, and manage approvals. Actual support for a particular token still depends on their implementations and choices.

It succeeded remarkably. Ten years later, most on-chain tokens still use the interface.

The list it does not prescribe—who receives how much, lockup periods, custody, enforcement of promises, and value—does not fill itself in because the interface is standardized. Some projects add contracts and institutions; others leave words on paper. Their degree of protection varies greatly.

Every item later depended on a white paper, an announcement, a team's self-restraint, or a settlement order years afterward.

The issuer supplied the first three; the last required waiting years.

The standard solved interoperability.

It never intended to solve trustworthiness.

In 2017, many people used the former as the latter.

This was not entirely gullibility. An entire apparatus appeared to perform that second function: white papers resembled prospectuses, adviser lists resembled boards, audit opinions appeared on official websites, and exchange listings resembled stock-market flotations. Each imitated a counterpart in an established institution without assuming that counterpart's responsibilities.

Wallets reading balances, exchanges supporting tokens, and contracts calling one another still operate widely. That shows the value of a shared interface—not that every implementation is error-free, or that promises outside the interface have been fulfilled.

What the issuer promised, whether it entered the code, and whether it was ultimately fulfilled: no on-chain field records that.

The chain is still settling, but there is still no consensus on what is being settled.
