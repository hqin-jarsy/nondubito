He Sets the Table, Then Leaves

他把桌子摆好，然后走了

Sep 4, 2026Han Qin (秦汉)Independent English edition中文原作 · 简 / 繁

Edition note: An independent English rewriting for readers of The Intouchables. It preserves the Chinese argument while reorganizing its sequence for an English-language essay. Full-film spoilers.

He Sets the Table, Then Leaves

For six months Philippe has written to Éléonore. On paper she encounters his mind—his wit, his poems, his carefully chosen sentences. She does not encounter the wheelchair.

When Driss asks why he will not call, Philippe's answer is revealing: writing allows him to communicate more fully. Beneath that claim lies a fear. In letters he can control when and how his body enters the relationship. A meeting would end that control.

Driss takes the telephone and dials.

A useful intrusion

The call goes well. Soon Philippe and Éléonore speak regularly and exchange photographs. They arrange to meet in Paris. Philippe goes to the restaurant and waits, but panic overtakes him; he leaves before she arrives.

Driss has opened a door, but he cannot walk Philippe through it. The first call worked because an intrusive act created a possibility Philippe then accepted. The failed meeting establishes the limit: another person cannot manufacture courage on your behalf.

That limit becomes important at the end.

Before then, trouble in Driss's family enters Philippe's house. Driss's younger relative is entangled with violent people, and the household that raised him needs him. Philippe tells Driss that he should not spend his whole life pushing a wheelchair. He releases him from the job at precisely the moment Philippe has come to depend on him most.

Professional caregivers replace Driss. Their technique is better, but Philippe withdraws. He stops shaving, avoids going out, and loses interest in the world. Yvonne eventually calls Driss back.

Driss shaves him—first turning his beard into a sequence of ridiculous shapes—dresses him, and drives fast enough to revive their old conspiracy. He takes Philippe to a seaside restaurant, arranges the table, and announces that someone else will join him for lunch.

Éléonore enters as Driss leaves.

Care, interference, and the right to refuse

It is tempting to describe this finale as perfect care: Driss creates the opportunity and then withdraws so Philippe can decide what happens next. There is truth in that reading. Driss does not sit at the table, speak for Philippe, or dictate the outcome. Éléonore and Philippe must make the encounter their own.

But the scene is not neutral. Driss deliberately conceals the plan because Philippe has already fled one meeting. He places a man with limited mobility in a situation that is harder to leave. Affection does not erase that coercive edge.

The film asks us to enjoy the trespass because Driss knows Philippe well and because the meeting appears welcome. In life, good intentions and a happy outcome would not be enough to establish a general right to override another person's refusal.

What makes the ending persuasive is narrower. Driss has learned the difference between doing everything for Philippe and returning a choice to him. He takes care of transport and physical preparation, then removes himself from the conversation. Philippe retains the ability to speak, fall silent, reject intimacy, or allow it to grow—even if Driss has improperly chosen the setting and timing.

Making room without owning the result

Philippe has performed a related act for Driss. He offered responsibility without demanding gratitude, introduced him to new forms of art without prescribing who he should become, and ended their employment when the job threatened to consume the rest of Driss's life.

Neither man simply “saves” the other. Each uses power at times, crosses boundaries, and makes guesses about what the other needs. Their friendship is valuable not because it is pure, but because both gradually learn to stop treating the other's life as a project whose result belongs to them.

The film closes before the lunch can be evaluated. We do not hear the conversation or receive a romantic guarantee. Driss looks through the glass, sees that the meeting has begun, and walks away.

That withdrawal matters. Helping can prepare a room, arrange a ride, make an introduction, or restore an option fear has closed. It cannot supply the words that must be spoken across the table. The other person's future begins where the helper's plan ends.

The title cards then return the comedy to its real-life inspiration: Philippe Pozzo di Borgo remarried and had two young daughters; Abdel Sellou built a business and a family; the two remained close. Those facts are not proof that every intrusion was justified. They are evidence that the relationship did not end when one man's usefulness to the other did.

菲利普和埃莱奥诺尔通信半年。在纸上，她先遇见他的机智、诗句和经过选择的措辞，不必同时面对轮椅。菲利普说书信让他表达得更完整，话里却藏着另一个意思：只要不见面，他就能决定自己的身体何时进入这段关系。德瑞斯拿起电话，替他拨出了那个号码。

一、有用的闯入仍然是闯入

电话带来了愉快的交谈，两个人开始频繁联系，也交换照片、约定见面。可菲利普到了餐厅，仍在对方出现前因恐惧离开。德瑞斯推开了一扇门，却不能替他走过门后那段路。

第一次通话之所以有用，是因为德瑞斯的越界制造了一个菲利普随后愿意接受的可能。它不能因此变成一般原则：知道一个人害怕，不等于获得了替他决定的权利；结果不错，也不会让所有强迫自动正当。

二、勇气不能由别人代做

这次失败没有被影片立刻修复。菲利普重新躲回熟悉的生活，德瑞斯的家庭麻烦也闯进豪宅。两人的关系开始面对一个比“互相改变”更难的问题：帮助到什么地方必须停下？

一个人可以替你拨电话、准备交通、打开原本关闭的选择，却不能替你承受被看见后的回答。若连犹豫、拒绝和退回去的权利都被帮助拿走，帮助就接管了本该属于你的生活。

三、菲利普也必须让德瑞斯离开

德瑞斯家里的年轻亲属卷入麻烦，养大他的家庭需要他回去。此时菲利普已经十分依赖德瑞斯，却告诉他不该一辈子只替别人推轮椅，让他离开这份工作。

这句话既有雇主替雇员作决定的成分，也有真正的放手。菲利普没有因为自己需要照料，就把德瑞斯的全部人生固定为“我的照护者”。他承认这份关系很重要，也承认德瑞斯在关系之外还有需要自己处理的生活。

四、海边餐桌上的边界

后来菲利普换了更专业的照护者，却逐渐封闭自己。德瑞斯回来替他剃须、穿衣、飙车，再把他带到海边餐厅。直到桌子摆好，他才告诉菲利普会有另一个人来，然后在埃莱奥诺尔进门时离开。

这个安排很动人，也并不完全无害。德瑞斯故意隐瞒计划，把行动受限的菲利普放进一个更难退出的场景。影片让我们相信他足够了解朋友，而且结果似乎受欢迎；现实里，善意和成功都不足以给人一张可以无视拒绝的通行证。

五、替他准备可能，不替他占有结果

结尾成立的范围其实更窄：德瑞斯处理了交通、穿着和开场，随后退出谈话。他没有替菲利普介绍自己，没有留在桌边推动关系，也没有要求一个浪漫结局。菲利普仍可以开口、沉默、拒绝，或者允许关系继续。

菲利普也曾为德瑞斯做过相似的事：给出责任和新的经验，却没有规定他最后必须成为什么。两个人都越过边界，也都逐渐学会不把对方的人生当作自己负责完成的项目。

帮助可以把一张桌子摆好，却不能替桌边的人说完接下来的话。 电影停在相遇刚刚开始的地方。德瑞斯隔着玻璃确认菲利普没有独自坐着，便转身离开。另一个人的未来，正从帮助者的计划结束之处开始。

本文所依据的版本为二〇一一年法国电影《触不可及》；片尾字幕提及的真人经历只作为创作背景，不作为电影中每一次越界都正当的证明。
