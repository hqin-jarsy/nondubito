No Paper Can Prove He Exists

他证明不了自己是谁

Sep 4, 2026Han Qin (秦汉)Independent English edition中文原作 · 简 / 繁

Edition note: An independent English rewriting for readers of Capernaum. It preserves the Chinese argument while reorganizing its sequence for an English-language essay. Full-film spoilers.

No Paper Can Prove He Exists

A doctor shines a light into a boy's mouth and studies his teeth. The court needs to know how old the child is, and this is the best evidence anyone can produce.

About twelve, the doctor says.

That small examination contains the whole argument of Capernaum. Zain is old enough to work, to be arrested, and to watch adults bargain away his sister. Yet before the state can hear his complaint, someone has to infer his age from his body. No birth certificate records the day he entered the world. No official file can say who he is.

The lawsuit that sounds impossible

When the judge asks why he wants to sue his parents, Zain answers: because they gave birth to me.

It is easy to hear this as a child's extravagant accusation. Plenty of unhappy children have wished, for an hour, that they had never been born. But the film patiently removes the exaggeration from Zain's sentence. “They gave birth to me” names a sequence of decisions, not a passing mood.

His family has more children than it can feed or even reliably count. None attends school. Zain carries gas cylinders, makes deliveries, works in the landlord's shop, and sells drinks on the street. When a neighbor mentions that school provides meals and supplies, education enters the parents' calculations chiefly as another source of goods. A child who might have had a future remains valuable for what he can bring home today.

This is not a portrait of comfortable people choosing cruelty for sport. Poverty has collapsed the family's horizon. Rent is urgent; food is urgent; the next day is almost an abstraction. The film never asks us to pretend those pressures are minor.

It does ask us to notice who pays for them.

What Zain understands before the adults do

Zain's eleven-year-old sister Sahar gets her first period. He immediately grasps what it means in this household. He helps her wash, steals sanitary pads, and teaches her how to hide them. He begins collecting supplies so that the two of them can run away.

He is trying to protect not an idea of childhood but one particular child's body.

The adults discover the blood anyway. Soon Sahar is dressed, made up, and sent to marry Assad, the landlord's son, in an arrangement tied to rent and two chickens. She cries. Zain clings to her, kicks, bites, and chases the departing car. The scene is almost unbearable because the transaction has been normalized by everyone with enough power to complete it. Only another child behaves as though something impossible is happening.

Afterward, Zain leaves home.

Now his line in court becomes more precise. His parents brought children into the world without securing their civil existence. They used one child as labor and allowed another to become an item in a household bargain. Zain is not merely alleging that they failed to love him properly. He is asking what it means to create a life without making room for the person who must live it.

A person before the document

A birth certificate does not make Zain human. Long before any official recognizes him, he feels pain, protects Sahar, cares for a stranger's baby, and understands injustice with frightening clarity.

But institutions see through records. Without documentation, school, medical care, travel, and legal protection become harder or impossible to reach. The paper does not create the person; its absence lets the world behave as if the person can be overlooked.

That distinction matters. The family's lack of documentation is not presented as a simple case of parental laziness, nor does the film clearly establish Zain's parents as foreign migrants in the way it later establishes Rahil's status. They are poor people living beyond the reliable reach of registration and public services. Their circumstances explain why many ordinary routes are closed.

Explanation, however, cannot be the final sentence. It does not answer Sahar's question: why may my body be exchanged for the household's survival? It does not answer Zain's: why did you bring me here without preserving even the basic facts of my arrival?

Rights before recognition

There is a cruel reversal in the courtroom. Zain wants the state to recognize what has been done to him, but the state must first establish that there is a “him” to hear. His teeth have to testify where the registry is silent.

A child's claim to care should not begin when an official accepts a document. Registration is valuable because it makes society answerable for seeing the child. When registration fails, the child's standing should not fail with it.

Zain has no paper that can prove he exists. The film supplies a different kind of proof. Before forms, files, and adult excuses, there is already someone here—someone who cannot rightly be reduced to labor, rent, or another problem to be managed.

The next essay turns to Rahil, whose undocumented life is made explicit. Her choices do not offer a neat moral contrast to Zain's parents. They sharpen a harder question: how far can desperate circumstances explain an injury, and where does explanation cease to be enough?

一间诊室。一个瘦小的男孩坐在椅子上，张着嘴。

医生打着手电看他的牙，然后给出一个估计：大概十二岁。

这是法庭确认赞恩年龄的方式。

他没有出生证明

没有任何一份文件能回答这个孩子多大。他没有出生证明，没有身份登记，医院里也找不到他的出生记录。父母只记得一个大概，连具体月份都说不清。

于是只能看牙。

法官问他：你为什么要起诉你的父母？

他说：因为他们生了我。

这句话听上去像一句赌气。可电影用了两个小时，让我们看清它指向的并不是一种笼统的怨恨，而是一串十分具体的事情。

这个家怎样过日子

赞恩家里有很多孩子。孩子们不上学，年纪稍大一点就要干活。赞恩送货、扛煤气罐、在店里帮工，也在街上卖自制的饮料。

邻居曾劝父母把孩子送去学校，说那里有免费餐食，还会发东西。父亲首先想到的是少了一个劳动力，母亲后来动心，想的则是那些可以带回家的物资。

这并不说明他们天生冷酷。它说明贫困已经把一家人的视线压低到今天：今天吃什么，房租从哪里来，明天还能不能留在这间屋子里。教育属于更远的将来，而他们几乎没有将来可以支配。

可一个孩子的生活，不能只有大人眼前的今天。

萨哈

萨哈是赞恩的妹妹，十一岁。

赞恩发现她第一次来月经，没有告诉父母。他带她去洗干净，偷来卫生巾，教她怎么用、怎么藏。他知道，大人一旦发现她“长大了”，房东阿萨德就会来把她带走。

他甚至开始准备逃跑。

可是母亲还是发现了。交易很快谈妥：萨哈嫁过去，房租的问题会好办一些，家里还得到两只鸡。

母亲给她换衣服、涂口红。萨哈哭，赞恩死死抱住她。他喊、踢、咬，父亲把他扯开，母亲把妹妹拖上车。

赞恩追着车跑。

那天以后，他离开了家。

“因为他们生了我”

现在再回到法庭上的那句话。

生下一个孩子，却没有替他登记，于是他在制度面前无从证明自己的年龄和身份；生下他，却让他从小承担成年人的生计；生下他的妹妹，又把她当作可以交换房租的东西送走。

赞恩控诉的不是“我的父母不够爱我”。他控诉的是：你们让一个人来到世上，却从未真正为这个人的存在留下位置。

一张纸之前的人

身份文件当然不是人的来源。没有出生证明，赞恩也已经会疼、会怕、会保护妹妹，会在别人需要时伸手。

可是制度要通过文件看见一个人。没有那张纸，他很难上学、就医、出行，也很难主张那些本来就属于他的权利。问题不在于纸创造了人，而在于一个人若没有被写进纸里，世界就可以假装没有看见他。

电影并没有把赞恩父母拍成轻松生活里的恶人。他们贫穷、受困，也缺乏获得身份与公共服务的路径。那些处境解释了许多事：为什么孩子要工作，为什么教育总被推迟，为什么一家人挤在一间屋里。

但解释并不是句号。

它不能替萨哈回答：为什么我的身体可以被拿去换房租？也不能替赞恩回答：为什么你们把我带到这里，却连我是哪一天来到这里都没有留下？

权利不该从证明开始

赞恩站在法庭上时，有一个几乎荒谬的倒置：他要向世界控诉自己遭受了什么，世界却先让他证明自己是谁。

他的牙齿替文件作证。

可一个孩子不该等到有人看过他的牙，才获得被认真对待的资格。出生登记的意义，正是让社会承担“看见”他的责任；而在登记缺席的时候，人的资格也不应一同消失。

赞恩没有一张纸能证明自己存在。但整部电影都在证明另一件事：在纸张、手续和成人的解释之前，他已经是一个不能被当作劳力、租金或麻烦处理的人。

下一篇，会出现另一个没有合法身份的人。她的困境与赞恩一家不同，却让电影里最难的问题变得更清楚：处境能够解释到哪里，又从哪里开始不再足够。
