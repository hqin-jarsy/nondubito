第一篇：不情愿的一篇

Essay 1: The Reluctant Treatise

Han Qin (秦汉)

有些文章是想写的，有些是不得不写的。这一篇属于后者。

Self-as-an-End 这个框架这些年一路往外铺，从生命写到意识，从经济写到法律，从教育写到方法论。每一篇的朝向都是同一个——让主体性获得释放，让人作为目的被承认。战争不在这个朝向里。战争是这套框架必须处理、但最不希望发生的那件事。为它专门写一篇，哪怕只是为了把理论架构补齐，也像在一间刚打扫干净的房子里，专门空出一个角落来放伤疤。

但不写不行。

因为沉默本身就是一种立场——而且恰好是这套框架自己要批判的那一种。对战争保持沉默，等于把这个场域整个让给别的话语去主宰，等于默认另一套战争逻辑在这里拥有最终解释权。沉默的理由可以很充分，沉默的代价却是：当下一次冲突真的发生时，身处其中的人如果想用"人是目的"作为行动的判准，他会发现手里没有现成的语言可用。

一、这一篇是写给谁的

不是写给发动战争的人。也不是写给隔岸观火的人。

是写给那个不得不进入战争、却仍然希望自己不滑离"人是目的"的人。

这个位置很具体。它不是道德高地，是一种窘境——你已经在里面了，退不出去，而你还想在出来的时候仍然认得自己。整个系列的判准都是为这个位置准备的。

二、真正的主角不是战争

在进入任何具体判准之前，必须先立下一件事：这个系列不是"关于战争的哲学"。

它是"涵育如何在战争情境下不被战争本身吞噬"。

涵育是这套框架的原理地基。它指的是主体性在时间中持续深化、展开、互相成长的那个过程。它不是一种静态的状态，是一个正在进行的生长；它也不属于某一个具体的主体，而属于所有主体在一起持续进行的那个结构。一个文明的涵育、一个社会的涵育、一段关系的涵育、一个人一生的涵育——每一个都是一个正在长的东西。

发展、互相砥砺、主体性的深化、文明整体的持续生长——这些全都是涵育的具体形态。目的王国的一切正面工作，都是涵育的展开。

战争在这张地图上的位置很低：它是涵育需要处理的一个极端态。当涵育被某种系统性目的的扩张打断、当主体性在这种压制下无法继续生长、当所有谈判和周旋的路都走尽了，战争才作为兜底出现，目的是让涵育有机会恢复。

战争是手段，涵育是目标。战争唯一合法的存在理由是——通过它，涵育重新获得了继续进行的条件。

这句话决定了后面所有篇章的读法。后面会立四条定理，它们不是在给"战争"立规范，是在给"涵育在战争中不被战争本身吞噬"立保护机制；后面会分析三种结束形态，那也不是在给"战争的结果"分类，是在问"战争结束之后，涵育怎么回来"。

涵育贯穿全篇。每一个判准都是为它服务的。

三、战争和法是同一层的东西

这个定位有一个立刻可用的参照：战争和法在这套框架里占据完全同构的位置。

法是手段，目标是使涵育成为可能——它处理常态碰撞，让主体之间的日常冲突不至于打断彼此的生长。战争也是手段，目标同样是使涵育成为可能——它处理极端态碰撞，让涵育在已经被打断的时候仍有恢复的机会。

两者都是涵育的保护性机制，都不是涵育本身。

这个同构不是新发现，它在人类思想里出现过很多次。1625年，格劳秀斯在三十年战争打得最不像话的时候写《战争与和平法》，做的正是这件事——把战争重新塞回法的语言里，让它至少有一个可以被追问的形状。他的动机在结构上和这里是一样的：不是要论证战争有多好，是要让某种东西在战争中活下来。

把这一层看清楚，会得到一个直接的推论：应当尽一切努力，让碰撞停留在法可以处理的层级，不要让它滑到战争的层级。不是因为战争"不道德"，是因为法处理碰撞不需要中断涵育，而战争处理碰撞必然伴随涵育的中断——哪怕它的目标正是让涵育恢复。这是两者代价上的根本差别。

四、地基不是正义

这里必须停下来澄清一个极容易被误读的地方，否则后面每一句都会被读歪。

这个系列的所有推导——包括"战争保护涵育"、"不打输而不是打赢"、"四条定理"——都不是从"保护涵育是正义的"出发的。

Self-as-an-End 里没有"正义"这个概念。

那推导依据是什么？是这套框架自己的公理：主体性不可被消灭，涵育是主体性的生长形态，涵育不得不继续。

这是一个结构陈述，不是一个价值陈述。"涵育不得不继续"不是因为它"应当继续"，是因为在结构上它就是在继续。压制涵育的行为会失败——被压下去的主体性会以各种形式返回：反抗、分裂、自我重建、在下一代身上重新浮现、作为创伤记忆存续几十年然后转化成行动。这不是道德律令，这是结构观察。

所以推导链不是"涵育应当被保护，所以保护涵育的战争是正义的"；而是"涵育作为主体性的生长是结构事实，压制它必然触发返回力，这个系列要做的是让这种返回力在合格的约束下运行，不让它自己也去打断涵育"。

两种地基的差别不是文字游戏。挂在道德判准上的地基是流动的——任何道德判准都可以被另一个道德判准挑战，而这种挑战永远不会结束。挂在公理上的地基是稳的：它不需要外部的道德证成。

这个澄清决定了后面所有句子的读法。当后面说"某场战争在这套判准下合格"，意思不是"它在道德上正确"，而是"它在结构上没有打断涵育"；当后面说"不合格"，意思不是"它在道德上错误"，而是"它在结构上已经在打断涵育"。合格与不合格是结构判断。

五、判准向内用，不向外用

这个地基有一个后果，值得单独说：它不审判历史。

孙子在两千三百年前做的工作，是人类智慧的真实成就。他没有读过康德，"人是目的"这个语言在他那里根本不存在。这个系列不是要说他错了，是要说他停在了他能到达的地方——而接下来的工作，需要一套后来才出现的语言才能完成。这是涵育的立场，不是审判的立场。

同样，历史上每一场战争里的每一个人，都在他所处的位置上做了他当时能做的选择。很多时候他们并不知道自己在做什么——他们以为自己在捍卫家园、捍卫荣誉、捍卫某种正义，而在结构上，他们捍卫的往往是涵育的某一个具体形态。他们没有"涵育"这个词，但他们服务的就是它。

更要紧的是第二层：这套判准首先是给主体自己用的。它是让那个不得不进入战争的人，能够回头审视自己的行动是否还在涵育的方向上。

判准向内用，是反思的工具。判准向外用——拿去审判别人——就在那一刻滑向了它本来要反对的那种东西。同一句话，朝向不同，性质完全不同。

六、哲学的轻，执行者的重

还有一条边界必须先划下来，而且它是最重要的一条。

这个系列会立四条定理、三种结束形态、一条手段约束——这些都是方向性的原理。但是具体的判断点不在这里：什么时候算所有非战路径真的走尽了？什么时候算涵育真的被逼到临界？这一次扩大是不是必要的？现在是不是该停战？某一个具体手段在这个具体情境下，是不是已经封死了对方战后重新站起来的可能？

这些判断属于军事家和政治家。

承认这条边界是诚实的必要条件。哲学论文在书斋里写成，不在前线做决策。它不面对具体的士兵，不面对当下的死亡，不面对几个小时之内必须做出的、关乎几千几万人命运的判断。哲学可以花几个月打磨一句话；在那个位置上的人常常只有几分钟决定一件事。

他们背负的重量，是哲学家承担不起的。哲学家能做的，是在很远的地方、很安静的房间里，把方向想清楚，然后交出去。

这不是谦虚，是清醒。哲学如果越过这条边界去做定量指导，就是既做不好——因为它没有执行者所处情境的信息——又对执行者不敬：它在假装自己能替别人承担别人的重量。

方向由哲学给，决策由执行者做，责任由执行者担。

七、这个系列站在哪一层

最后一件预先声明的事，免得读者带错期待。

战争在对象上属于主体间碰撞的极端态——两个或多个主体之间的冲突走到了暴力对抗的地步。具体的战术、装备、编制、后勤，全都在那一层。

但这个系列的处理不在那一层。因为一场战争之所以能够发生、持续、扩大、变质，核心机制在更上面——在于某种系统目的（国家利益、意识形态、民族叙事、正义话语、战争伦理）如何吞噬或者保护涵育。战争打起来的时候，怎么打是一个问题；这场战争是否仍然在为涵育服务、是否已经变成打断涵育的工具，是另一个问题。

所以这不是一本兵法。兵法那一层的工作，孙子他们已经做得非常深入了。这个系列站在兵法之上、"人是目的"之下的那一层。期待它教你怎么打仗的读者会失望；它要回答的是另一个问题——

战争作为手段，什么时候仍在为涵育服务，什么时候已经变成打断涵育的工具？

关于实例的一点说明

学术原文有一条很硬的纪律：正文里不出现任何具体的人类战争事件，所有实例都放在一个虚构的场域里完成。理由有三层——历史上大部分具体战争是两个扩张型系统之间的碰撞，本来就不在这套判准的射程内；涉及具体事件会让判准从"给自己用的反思工具"滑向"给外人用的审判工具"；而哲学家在书斋里评述别人在几小时内做出的决断，本身就是对那份重量的不敬。

散文是另一层。原文自己留了这个口子：教育性或应用性的文本可以触碰具体事件，只要它明确承认自己已经进入教育层，不再是原理层的工作。这个系列就站在那一层，所以它会用历史实例——但只用时间距离足够远的，只用来让抽象结构变得可以看见，不用来判断谁对谁错。

这个区别要在第一篇就说清楚，因为它本身就是前面那条"判准向内不向外"的一次应用。

下一篇

孙子说"上兵伐谋"。这句话在中文世界里被引用了两千多年，通常被当作战争智慧的顶点。

但它不是顶点。在它上面还有四级。

而更要紧的是：战争根本不在那条序列里。

Some essays get written because someone wants to write them. Others get written because there is no way around it. This one is the second kind.

The Self-as-an-End framework has spread outward for years now — from life to consciousness, from economics to law, from education to methodology. Every one of those pieces faces the same direction: release subjectivity, recognize persons as ends. War does not face that direction. War is the thing this framework has to handle and least wants to happen. Writing a treatise for it, even just to complete the architecture, feels like clearing out a corner of a freshly cleaned house to keep a scar in.

And yet not writing it was not an option.

Silence is itself a position — and precisely the kind of position this framework criticizes elsewhere. To stay silent about war is to hand that whole field over to other discourses, to concede that some other logic of war holds final interpretive authority there. The reasons for silence can be excellent. The cost of silence is this: when the next conflict actually arrives, anyone inside it who wants to use "persons as ends" as a standard for action will find there is no ready language to use.

1. Who This Is For

Not for the people who start wars. Not for the people watching from a safe distance.

It is for the person who has no choice but to enter a war, and who still hopes not to drift away from "persons as ends" while inside it.

That position is quite specific. It is not a moral high ground; it is a predicament. You are already in it, there is no exit, and you would still like to recognize yourself on the way out. Every standard in this series is built for that position.

2. The Protagonist Is Not War

Before any specific standard, one thing has to be settled: this series is not a philosophy of war.

It is an account of how cultivation avoids being swallowed by war itself.

Cultivation is this framework's foundational principle. It names the process by which subjectivity deepens, unfolds, and grows through and alongside other subjectivity over time. It is not a static condition but an ongoing growth, and it does not belong to any single subject — it belongs to the structure all subjects sustain together. A civilization has cultivation; so does a society, a relationship, a single life. Each one is something currently growing.

Development, mutual sharpening, the deepening of subjectivity, the sustained growth of a civilization as a whole — all of these are concrete forms of cultivation. Every positive project in the Kingdom of Ends is cultivation unfolding.

War sits very low on this map. It is one extreme state that cultivation has to deal with. When cultivation is interrupted by the expansion of some systemic purpose, when subjectivity can no longer grow under that suppression, when every route through negotiation and maneuver has been exhausted — only then does war appear as a backstop, and its purpose is to give cultivation a chance to recover.

War is the means. Cultivation is the end. The single legitimate reason for war to exist is that through it, cultivation regains the conditions to continue.

That sentence governs how everything downstream should be read. Later essays establish four theorems; those theorems are not norms for war but protective mechanisms for cultivation, so that war does not consume the thing it was called in to protect. Later essays analyze three ending forms; that is not a taxonomy of outcomes but an inquiry into how cultivation returns once the fighting stops.

Cultivation runs through the whole series. Every standard in it serves that.

3. War and Law Occupy the Same Layer

This placement comes with an immediately useful reference point: war and law hold structurally identical positions in this framework.

Law is a means whose end is to make cultivation possible. It handles ordinary collisions so that everyday conflict between subjects does not interrupt their growth. War is also a means with the same end. It handles extreme collisions, so that cultivation still has a route back when it has already been interrupted.

Both are protective mechanisms for cultivation. Neither is cultivation itself.

This isomorphism is not a new discovery; it has surfaced repeatedly in human thought. In 1625, while the Thirty Years' War was at its ugliest, Grotius wrote De Jure Belli ac Pacis — and what he was doing was exactly this: pushing war back into the language of law so it would at least have a shape that could be questioned. His motive was structurally the same as the one here. He was not arguing that war is good. He was trying to keep something alive inside it.

Seeing this layer clearly yields an immediate corollary: every effort should go toward keeping collisions at a level law can handle, rather than letting them slide down to the level of war. Not because war is "immoral," but because law can process a collision without interrupting cultivation, while war necessarily interrupts it — even when its entire goal is cultivation's recovery. That is the fundamental difference in cost between the two.

4. The Ground Is Not Justice

Here we have to stop and clear up something very easy to misread, or every sentence that follows will land wrong.

None of this series' derivations — "war protects cultivation," "not losing rather than winning," the four theorems — begins from the claim that protecting cultivation is just.

Self-as-an-End has no concept of justice.

So what does it derive from? From its own axiom: subjectivity cannot be eliminated; cultivation is the growth-form of subjectivity; cultivation cannot not continue.

That is a structural statement, not an evaluative one. Cultivation continues not because it ought to but because structurally it does. Acts that suppress it fail. Suppressed subjectivity returns in one form or another — as resistance, as fracture, as self-reconstruction, as something resurfacing in the next generation, as traumatic memory that persists for decades and eventually converts into action. This is not a moral imperative. It is a structural observation.

So the chain of reasoning is not "cultivation ought to be protected, therefore a war that protects it is just." It is: "cultivation as the growth of subjectivity is a structural fact; suppressing it necessarily triggers a returning force; the work of this series is to keep that returning force running under adequate constraints, so that it does not interrupt cultivation in its own turn."

The difference between the two grounds is not verbal. A ground anchored in a moral criterion is fluid — any moral criterion can be challenged by another moral criterion, and that challenge never ends. A ground anchored in an axiom is stable, because it needs no external moral justification.

This clarification determines how every later sentence should be read. When a later essay says a war is "adequate" under these standards, it does not mean the war was morally right; it means the war did not, structurally, interrupt cultivation. When it says "inadequate," it does not mean morally wrong; it means the war is already interrupting cultivation. Adequacy is a structural judgment.

5. The Standard Points Inward, Not Outward

This ground has a consequence worth stating on its own: it does not put history on trial.

What Sunzi accomplished twenty-three centuries ago is a genuine achievement of human intelligence. He had not read Kant; the phrase "persons as ends" simply did not exist for him. This series is not saying he was wrong. It is saying he stopped where he could reach — and that the work past that point requires a vocabulary that only appeared much later. That is a posture of cultivation, not of judgment.

The same applies to every person in every war in history. Each made the choice available from the position they occupied. Often they did not know what they were doing — they believed they were defending a homeland, an honor, some version of justice, when structurally what they were defending was one particular form of cultivation. They had no word for it. They were serving it anyway.

The second layer matters more: these standards are primarily for the subject's own use. They exist so that the person who has no choice but to enter a war can look back at their own actions and check whether those actions still point toward cultivation.

Pointed inward, a standard is an instrument of reflection. Pointed outward — used to pass sentence on others — it becomes, in that moment, the very thing it was meant to resist. Same sentence, different direction, entirely different nature.

6. Philosophy Is Light; the Executor Carries the Weight

One more boundary has to be drawn first, and it is the most important one.

This series will establish four theorems, three ending forms, and one constraint on means. All of these are directional principles. But the specific judgment calls are not here. When have all non-war routes genuinely been exhausted? When has cultivation genuinely been pushed to its limit? Is this particular escalation necessary? Is this the moment to stop? Does this specific method, in this specific situation, foreclose the other side's ability to stand back up afterward?

Those judgments belong to soldiers and statesmen.

Acknowledging this boundary is a condition of honesty. A philosophy essay is written in a quiet room, not at a front line. It does not face actual soldiers, actual deaths, or a decision that must be made within hours and will determine the fate of thousands. Philosophy can spend months polishing a single sentence. People in that other position often have minutes.

The weight they carry is not a weight a philosopher can bear. What a philosopher can do is sit somewhere far away and very quiet, get the direction clear, and hand it over.

This is not modesty. It is clarity. If philosophy crosses this boundary into quantitative guidance, it does the job badly — it lacks the situational information the executor has — and it disrespects the executor by pretending it can carry someone else's weight for them.

Philosophy supplies the direction. The executor makes the decision and bears the responsibility.

7. Which Layer This Series Stands On

One last thing to declare in advance, so no one arrives with the wrong expectations.

As an object, war belongs to the extreme state of inter-subject collision — a conflict between two or more subjects that has reached armed confrontation. Tactics, equipment, force structure, logistics: all of that lives on that layer.

This series does not work on that layer. Because the reason a war can begin, persist, expand, and degrade lies further up — in how some systemic purpose (national interest, ideology, a story a nation tells about itself, a discourse of justice, an ethics of war) either swallows cultivation or protects it. Once a war is underway, how to fight it is one question. Whether it still serves cultivation, or has already become an instrument that interrupts cultivation, is a different question entirely.

So this is not a book of military strategy. That layer has been worked deeply already, by Sunzi and others. This series stands above the art of war and below "persons as ends." Anyone expecting to be taught how to fight will be disappointed. The question it answers is a different one:

War as a means — when is it still serving cultivation, and when has it become the instrument interrupting it?

A Note on Examples

The academic original holds a hard line: no specific human war event appears anywhere in its main text, and every illustration is worked out inside a fictional setting instead. The reasoning runs three layers deep. Most historical wars were collisions between two expansionist systems and fall outside these standards' range to begin with. Touching specific events tends to slide the standard from an instrument of self-reflection into an instrument for judging others. And a philosopher commenting from a quiet room on a decision someone else made in a matter of hours is, in itself, a failure of respect for that weight.

Essays are a different layer. The original leaves this door open deliberately: educational or applied writing may touch specific events, provided it acknowledges that it has moved into the educational layer and is no longer doing the work of a treatise on principles. This series stands on that layer. So it will use historical examples — but only ones far enough away in time, and only to make an abstract structure visible, never to rule on who was right.

That distinction belongs in the first essay, because it is itself an application of the rule stated above: the standard points inward, not outward.

Next

Sunzi wrote that the highest use of arms is to attack the opponent's strategy. That line has been quoted for two thousand years, usually as the summit of war wisdom.

It is not the summit. There are four levels above it.

And more to the point: war is not on that sequence at all.
